Extinction Bursts

Things get worse right after you set limits

Station 33 of 44 on the Guilt, pressure and fear line. Code G 33

What is Extinction Bursts?

You hold a limit for the first time, and things suddenly get worse, not better. The pressure that used to work gets turned up because it stopped working.

It can sound like

  • You’ve never done this to me before. What has gotten into you?
  • Eleven missed calls and a stream of texts the evening after you said no to a request.
  • Fine. If that’s how you feel, I’ll just stop trying. Is that what you want?

Signs it might be this

  • The worsening starts within hours or days of your holding a line and not before.
  • The new behavior is clearly bigger than anything you saw while you were still going along.
  • The pressure falls away the moment you give in, and returns when you do not.
  • Each time you hold a limit, the response is heavier than the last.
  • The demand itself never changes; only the force behind it does.

Signs it is probably something else

  • The reaction peaks in the first few days and then settles, even though you have not given in.
  • They name their feelings and ask for time, and do not increase contact or demands.
  • Your boundary changed something large in their life, and the upset is about that change.
  • They accept the limit on some points while grieving it, and do not test it again.
  • The surge also appears in places unrelated to your refusal, which points to something else going on for them.
  • The strongest reaction comes in the first days and then eases while the boundary stays in place.
  • They can say, even awkwardly, that they are struggling with the change and still respect it.
  • Others who have set limits with them report a similar short adjustment that passed.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Did the increase begin after I held a line, or was it already building before?
  • Does it ease when I give in and return when I do not?
  • Is the new behavior the same method turned up, or a different one that appeared when the first stopped working?
  • Over several weeks, is the response to my limits getting softer, or heavier?
  • Would I describe this as upset about the change, or as an effort to reverse it?

What it is

The term comes from behavior science: when a habit that used to pay off stops paying off, it often gets louder before it fades. In relationships, this can look like more messages, more anger or bigger promises right after you hold a limit for the first time.

A spike after a new boundary can come from hurt or shock and does not show what anyone meant. It is more worrying when each limit you hold is met with something heavier than before.

Why it matters

The first boundary that actually works is the one that costs the most. It can teach you that holding a limit makes things worse.

Giving in then starts to feel like the only way to get peace again.

How it shows up

  • A jump in volume, frequency or intensity that begins right after you refuse or hold a line, not before.
  • The same method as before, only louder: forty messages instead of four, a longer silent treatment, or a bigger display of hurt.
  • Sometimes a brand-new method appears, such as guilt where there had been anger, once the old one stops working.
  • A shape that is only visible in time order: what happened, what you did differently, and what came next.

What you can do

Pick what fits. You do not owe anyone a confrontation.

Words you could use

“My answer has not changed, and I understand you are upset. I will reply to messages once this evening. Please do not call again before then.”

Ask about it gently

Describe the sequence once, without blame: ‘I noticed that after I said no, the messages went from a few a day to dozens. Can we talk about what is going on?’

Set a clear limit

Hold the same limit and shorten your replies. Repeating one calm sentence is enough: ‘My answer is the same as yesterday.’

Step back

Mute the thread and answer only at set times, such as once in the evening. Let the volume pass without chasing it.

Get support

Tell a friend before you hold the limit, so someone knows what to expect. They can check in the day after, when the surge tends to peak.

At work, school or with services

Keep every message after your refusal and reply in writing with one line. If the pressure keeps rising, give the thread to a manager or HR.

Using this label fairly

  • Calling a person’s hurt reaction an ‘extinction burst’ can dismiss real feelings about a change that did affect them.
  • Treating every spike of anger as a tactic can turn a normal adjustment period into an accusation.

How it differs from look-alikes

An Ultimatum sets a fixed deadline with a stated cost; a burst has no new demand, only more force behind the old one. Urgency Manufacturing invents time pressure from the start, while a burst appears only after resistance.

False Dilemma narrows your choices to two; a burst may offer no choices at all, just rising volume until you give way.
