Triangulation

Bringing in other people’s opinions against you

Station 3 of 19 on the Turning people against you line. Code P 03

What is Triangulation?

Instead of saying what they think, someone keeps telling you that other people agree with them. A two-person problem starts to feel like you against a group.

It can sound like

  • Even your mother thinks you were out of line.
  • Dana told me what you said about me at lunch.
  • I’ve talked to the whole team and none of them see it your way.

Signs it might be this

  • Other people’s views are announced to you as a verdict rather than offered as one perspective.
  • The quoted third person, when you ask them, says they never said it or were told something different.
  • The speaker avoids saying what they personally want and keeps pointing to what others think.
  • You learn a mutual friend or relative has been given a one-sided account before you were told there was a problem.
  • Two people who used to get along start distrusting each other after both talked to the same go-between.

Signs it is probably something else

  • They name the person, say what was actually said, and invite you to ask that person yourself.
  • The third person raised the concern on their own, and the speaker is passing it along because it matters.
  • They state their own view first and mention another’s only as a side note.
  • The other people involved are openly present and speaking for themselves.
  • It was a single mention during a heated moment and was not repeated or used again.
  • The speaker is happy for you to speak with the people they mention.
  • Other people’s views come up rarely and as context, not as proof you must accept.
  • When it turns out a quote was wrong, they correct it and drop the point.

Questions to ask yourself

  • If you asked the third person directly, would they confirm what was said and how it was meant?
  • Does the speaker ever say what they themselves think and want, or only what others think?
  • Were those other people invited into the matter, or recruited after it began?
  • Does the outside opinion arrive as one view among several, or as the final word?
  • Has a relationship with a mutual friend or relative cooled because of things relayed through someone else?

What it is

Triangulation turns a disagreement between two people into a contest with an audience. The speaker quotes a mother, a coworker, a friend or an unnamed ‘everyone’ as backing.

The other person then faces a crowd instead of a single complaint. The third person may be real, exaggerated or made up, and often does not know their words are being used.

One mention of someone else’s view means little. It becomes a habit when outside voices keep arriving in place of the speaker’s own position.

Why it matters

You end up arguing with people who are not in the room and cannot be questioned. It becomes hard to know what anyone actually said.

Relationships with the people being quoted can sour without a word being exchanged with them.

How it shows up

  • Citing a named person’s opinion of you as proof in an argument, such as ‘Even your sister thinks so’.
  • Reporting what a third person said about you in a way that sets the two of you against each other.
  • Using an unnamed crowd (‘people are saying’, ‘everyone has noticed’) that cannot be checked or asked.
  • Carrying messages back and forth so that two other people form a view of one another through the middle person.

What you can do

Pick what fits. You do not owe anyone a confrontation.

Words you could use

“I want to hear your concern, but I can’t answer for what other people think. Tell me what bothers you, and if someone else’s view matters here, I’ll ask them myself.”

Ask about it gently

Ask for the speaker’s own position: ‘I’d like to hear what you think, separate from what other people said. What is bothering you?’

Set a clear limit

Decline to argue against people who are absent: ‘I’m not going to debate what someone else supposedly said. If it matters, I’ll ask them directly.’

Step back

Pause the conversation when the list of other people’s opinions begins. Return to it only if the speaker is willing to talk about their own view.

Get support

Check a quoted statement by asking the person it came from, in a neutral way. Confirming or correcting it for yourself restores your footing.

At work, school or with services

At work, ask that feedback be given directly and in writing, with specific examples, instead of ‘the team feels’. Ask who is raising the concern.

Using this label fairly

  • Labeling any mention of a friend’s opinion as triangulation can shut down normal advice-seeking. The label fits when outside voices replace a person’s own position. It does not fit whenever others are consulted.
  • Assuming a quoted third party is lying, without asking them, can create the very distrust the pattern is known for.

How it differs from look-alikes

Flying Monkeys are third parties who act, such as pressing or watching you, often without knowing why. Triangulation uses their words as evidence against you.

Forced Teaming claims a shared ‘we’ that binds you and needs no third voice at all. Concern Trolling uses worry as the vehicle.

Triangulation stands apart because the argument is carried by someone who is not in the room.
