Projection

Accusing you of what they are doing

Station 7 of 31 on the Dodging blame line. Code B 07

What is Projection?

You say something is happening, and you are told you are the one doing it, even though the record fits the other person more.

It can sound like

  • You’re the one who’s controlling, said by someone who has just told you whom you may see.
  • I’m not angry, you’re angry, said loudly.
  • You’re always accusing me of lying. You’re the liar here.

Signs it might be this

  • The charge matches what you said almost word for word.
  • It arrives only after you name something, never on an ordinary day.
  • The behavior charged is visible in that same conversation, in the person making the charge.
  • No example, date or message is given for the charge.
  • When you offer to check the record, the subject shifts.

Signs it is probably something else

  • They can point to a specific time you did it, and you recognize it.
  • They add ‘and I do it too.’
  • The concern was raised before you named anything, as part of something they were already worried about.
  • When you show them the record, they accept what it shows.
  • A third person who knows you both finds the charge fair.
  • The charge is specific enough to check, and it holds up on some points.
  • You can both name a shared habit without it turning into a contest.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Does the accusation fit what the record shows about them, about me, or about both of us?
  • Did it arrive after I named something, almost like an echo?
  • Could I give a specific example of my behavior that matches it, and could they give one of theirs?
  • When I offer to look at messages or dates together, does the subject change?
  • Would someone who knows us both recognize the charge as true of me?

What it is

A classic idea in psychology is that people can put their own feelings or impulses onto someone else. They often do it without realizing it.

In everyday conflict this looks like an accusation that fits the accuser better than the accused. An example is ‘You’re so controlling’ said in the middle of a list of instructions.

It does not show what anyone meant, and people can do it unknowingly. One mismatch is ordinary.

The pattern is a repeated one, where the charges track whatever you just pointed out.

Why it matters

You spend the conversation defending yourself against the very thing you came to name. That makes the source of the harm hard to see, and harder to describe to anyone else.

The conflict ends in a tie instead of an answer.

How it shows up

  • An accusation that hands your complaint back in nearly the same words: ‘You never listen’ answered with ‘No, you never listen.’
  • Often arrives right after you name a behavior, not at some random time.
  • Can be sincere and unaware: someone may truly not see the behavior in themselves while seeing it clearly in you.
  • Judged by the record, not the tone: dated messages, who said what, and whether the charge matches what is on the page.
  • Different from fair mutual criticism, where both people can say ‘yes, we both do that.’

What you can do

Pick what fits. You do not owe anyone a confrontation.

Words you could use

“I’m open to hearing whether I do that, and I will listen when we get there. Right now I’m asking about what happened on Tuesday. Can we finish that first?”

Ask about it gently

Acknowledge what might be true, then go back to your point: ‘I might do that sometimes. I’d still like to talk about what happened on Tuesday.’

Set a clear limit

Keep to one thing at a time: ‘Let’s finish my concern, then I will listen to yours properly and give it the same time.’

Step back

When the same words keep coming back to you, stop trading charges. Move to something written, where wording cannot be echoed back in the moment.

Get support

Describe the exchange to someone who knows you both and ask whether the charge sounds like you. Check it on a calm day, not in the middle of an argument.

At work, school or with services

Keep the facts in a dated email: what happened, when, and what you are asking for. A reply that engages with it, or one that charges you back, becomes part of the record.

Using this label fairly

  • Saying ‘you’re projecting’ to someone’s honest complaint is itself the move described in Pseudo-Insight as Shutdown. It ends the conversation instead of describing it.
  • The word names an accusation that fits the accuser, which you can only judge from the record. Using it because a charge stung is not enough.

How it differs from look-alikes

Blame-Shifting moves responsibility for an event onto you, while projection hands you a specific trait or motive, often the one you just named. DARVO packs a denial, an attack and a role reversal together.

Projection can be only the echo. Pseudo-Insight as Shutdown uses psychological-sounding terms to stop the talk, whereas projection keeps the talk going, in a circle.
