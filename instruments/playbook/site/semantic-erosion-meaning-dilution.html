Semantic Erosion / Meaning Dilution

Overusing serious words until they mean nothing

Station 30 of 43 on the Twisting what is real line. Code T 30

What is Semantic Erosion / Meaning Dilution?

A serious word gets used for so many small things that when you need it for something serious, it sounds like exaggeration.

It can sound like

  • If that’s harassment, then so is asking someone to pass the salt.
  • Everyone is traumatized these days. It’s just a word people use.
  • I’ve been called abusive for burning toast. The word means nothing anymore.

Signs it might be this

  • The term gets used loosely right when someone has used it for a real situation involving the speaker.
  • A request to keep the term for serious cases is met with a joke or a comparison to the silliest use.
  • The widening of the word helps the person who would otherwise be described by it.
  • You notice you hesitate to use the right word for your own situation because of how it has been used elsewhere.
  • The same group or person repeatedly uses the term in a way that makes it harder to take seriously.

Signs it is probably something else

  • The word changed because many unconnected people began using it that way. It has no link to a particular dispute.
  • They are debating where the line falls, and are open to hearing your reasons.
  • They take your use of the term seriously, even if they disagree about whether it fits.
  • The loosening is in an area where the speaker has nothing to gain or lose.
  • They use the word loosely for themselves as often as for others.
  • The wider use of the word is general and not tied to a conflict you are in.
  • They are willing to talk about where the boundary of the term lies.
  • You can still use the accurate word with them and have it understood.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Did the word’s weakening come from many unconnected speakers, or from a few who gain by it?
  • Is the loose use showing up at the moment someone is using the word accurately?
  • Am I finding it harder to use the accurate term for my own situation, and is the cause something specific?
  • Is there a real conversation about where the word applies, or just a dismissal?
  • Would the person who dilutes it accept the same loose use being aimed at them?

What it is

Semantic erosion and meaning dilution look at one change from two sides. Semantic erosion describes a word wearing thin through overuse.

Meaning dilution describes the effect, where a strong word is spread so widely that it no longer carries force. Words drift all the time, and no single speaker decides it.

The version that matters here is when one person’s use of a term makes it harder to name what happened to someone else. The change in the word is easy to see.

The reason for it is not.

Why it matters

You lose the accurate word for your own situation, so describing it plainly now sounds like exaggeration, to other people and often to you. Without the word, it can be harder to find support or to be believed.

How it shows up

  • A strong term, such as abuse, trauma, harassment or fraud, applied to minor events until listeners grow tired of it.
  • Words such as ‘gaslighting’ or ‘bullying’ once ended an argument. Now they get a shrug or an eye roll when someone uses them about something serious.
  • Often visible as a joke or exaggeration, which is part of why it spreads without anyone planning it.
  • A comparison used to shrink a real claim: ‘if that counts, then everything counts’.
  • A slow change in a community’s vocabulary, and in some cases a push by someone who gains if the word loses force.

What you can do

Pick what fits. You do not owe anyone a confrontation.

Words you could use

“I understand the word is used for a lot of things. I’m using it for what happened on Tuesday, and I’d like you to respond to that. We can talk about the vocabulary afterwards.”

Ask about it gently

Separate the word from the event: ‘I know that word gets used loosely. I’m using it for this specific thing, and here is what happened.’

Set a clear limit

Decline to defend the vocabulary: ‘We can argue about the word another day. Right now I’m telling you about what happened, and I’d like you to hear that.’

Step back

Drop the debate about the word. Describe the events without it, and come back to the label later with someone who will take it seriously.

Get support

Tell someone you trust, or a support service, what happened in plain, factual detail. Ask which word they think fits, instead of choosing one yourself.

At work, school or with services

In a formal complaint, use the policy’s own defined term and quote its definition. Then describe the events in dated, factual language.

Using this label fairly

  • Someone may use a word more widely than you would. Saying they are eroding it can shut down real changes in how language is used. Words change, and not every change is aimed at you.
  • Using ‘dilution’ as an accusation in a dispute about vocabulary may end a conversation that could have helped both people find clearer words.
