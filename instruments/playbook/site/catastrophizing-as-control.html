G

Catastrophizing as Control

Station 19 of 44 on the Guilt, pressure and fear line. Code G 19

Safety first

Predictions of ruin become a safety concern when they come with monitoring, or with control of money, papers, phones or transport. They also do when you are told you would lose children or housing if you left.

Those can be signs of coercive control, where someone gradually takes charge of your choices through fear and dependence. One next step is to talk through your real options with a domestic abuse advocate or legal aid service before anything changes.

They can help you plan privately.

What is Catastrophizing as Control?

When you try something on your own, they predict you’ll end up broke or alone. Over time, managing alone starts to feel impossible.

It can sound like

  • If you take that job, this family falls apart.
  • You’ll end up alone and broke without me.
  • No one else will put up with you the way I do.

Signs it might be this

  • The predicted outcome is far worse than the actual risk, and nobody explains how it would come about.
  • The warning appears when you take a step toward independence and rarely otherwise.
  • Your plans, savings and backup are brushed aside without being examined.
  • The prediction casts the speaker as the only safeguard.
  • You are told that no one else would help you or accept you.

Signs it is probably something else

  • They go through your plan with you, point out gaps, and then say the decision is yours.
  • Their worry is about specific, checkable risks.
  • They are just as anxious about their own changes.
  • They help you prepare for the risks they name.
  • A trusted third person independently sees the same risk.
  • Warnings include specifics you can check and a way to reduce the risk.
  • The speaker is as willing to hear that the plan might work as that it might fail.
  • Support continues after you decide, even if they would have chosen differently.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Is this an account of a specific risk, or a general sense that I cannot cope?
  • Has anyone outside this relationship seen my plan and the risks?
  • Do these warnings come only when I move toward something of my own?
  • Have earlier predictions that came true been as bad as they were described?
  • What would I think if a friend were told the same thing about their plan?

What it is

Catastrophizing as control means painting the future in worst-case terms. It happens whenever the other person is about to make a choice of their own.

That might be a job, a move, a friendship, or an end to the relationship. The predictions come as facts, not worries.

They usually cast the speaker as the one thing keeping disaster away. Worry about someone you love is normal, and a frank warning can be a kindness.

The pattern is predictions that are bigger than the real risk. They come back at each step toward independence.

They also leave you less able to trust your own plans.

Why it matters

Fear of the future can keep a person in place more firmly than any rule. When every independent step is described as the start of ruin, you may stop trying and stay where you are.

You may also lose confidence that you could cope.

How it shows up

  • Forecasts of disaster delivered as certainties: ‘you will lose the house’, not ‘I am worried about the house’.
  • A prediction that you personally will fail without the speaker, whatever the plan is.
  • Disaster linked to many different choices: a job, a course, a move, a friend, a decision to leave.
  • A forecast that does not change when you show a plan, savings or support.
  • Sometimes the message comes through other people (‘your mother is frightened for you’). Sometimes it is backed by chosen stories of people who failed.

What you can do

Read the safety note at the top first. If speaking up could make things worse, skip the wording below and talk to someone you trust or a local support service.

Words you could use

“I’ve heard that you’re worried, and I want to understand the real risks. Can we write down what could go wrong and what would help with each one? I want to talk it over with my sister as well.”

Ask about it gently

Ask for the details: ‘What exactly do you think would go wrong, and what would help?’ A specific worry can be checked. A vague one tells you something too.

Set a clear limit

Say how the predictions land: ‘When you tell me I will fail, I feel less able to think. I’d like us to look at the facts instead.’

Step back

You do not have to answer every prediction. A short ‘I’ve heard you’ lets the conversation move on, and you can work on your plans privately with someone you trust.

Get support

Show your plan, with the figures, to someone outside the relationship, such as a friend, a financial counselor or an advocate. Ask for an honest read of the risks. Keep a copy with a person the other party does not control.

At work, school or with services

At work, a colleague or manager may forecast disaster whenever you apply for a role or take a project. If so, ask for the specific risk in writing. Seek an outside view from a mentor or HR.

Using this label fairly

  • A frank warning about a risky plan is not control. Labeling honest caution as a tactic can lead you to ignore real risks.
  • Do not use this label to dismiss an anxious relative who is trying, clumsily, to say that they care.

How it differs from look-alikes

Emotional Blackmail attaches a penalty the speaker will impose, such as withdrawn love, if you do not comply. Catastrophizing as control predicts a disaster that will arrive on its own.

Guilt-Tripping looks back at blame for a harm already done. This tactic looks ahead to a ruin that has not happened.

Pity Plays make the speaker the one who is suffering. Here you are the one forecast to fall.

The difference is direction: a forecast about your future, offered as fact.
