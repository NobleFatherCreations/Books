The Memory Mixer-Upper

A Guide to the “That Didn’t Happen” Trick

Picture This

It’s Thursday afternoon. Shaela is at her locker, and she is one hundred percent sure of what happened on Tuesday.

On Tuesday, Furbie borrowed her favorite purple gel pen — the one with the glitter ink that Shaela got for her birthday. Shaela handed it over because Furbie said he just needed it for one second to sign a paper. That was three days ago. The pen did not come back.

Shaela walks over to Furbie at the water fountain.

“Hey — can I get my purple pen back?”

Furbie turns around with a look on his face like she just asked him something in a foreign language. “What pen?”

“My glitter pen. You borrowed it Tuesday.”

Furbie shakes his head slowly, like he’s really trying to remember. “I don’t think that happened. I don’t even use gel pens.”

Shaela blinks. “You literally wrote your name with it in front of me.”

“I don’t know what you’re talking about.” Furbie shrugs. “Maybe you lent it to someone else and forgot?”

And just like that, Shaela is standing at the water fountain wondering if she’s going crazy.

The Wait a Minute Feeling

The thing about what just happened is that Shaela didn’t feel angry right away. She felt confused.

That’s the feeling. That sinking, spinning, wait, did I make that up? feeling. Like the floor shifted a little under your feet.

When someone tells you that something you clearly remember didn’t happen — or that you misunderstood it, or that you made it up, or that you’re “too sensitive” — your brain does something strange. Instead of saying “that person is wrong,” it starts questioning itself. Am I sure? Was it Tuesday or Wednesday? Maybe I’m misremembering…

This confusion is the trick working. That’s important to understand. The confusion isn’t a sign that you’re wrong. It’s a sign that the trick is happening.

🧠 This chapter’s Feeling Word: CONFUSED

Confused is what happens when someone tries to put a fog between you and what you actually know.

What’s the trick?

This move is called the Memory Mixer-Upper.

Here’s how it works: someone does something — borrows your stuff, says something mean, breaks an agreement, tells a lie — and then, when you bring it up, they tell you it never happened. Or that it happened differently. Or that you’re remembering it wrong.

It’s not about the pen. It’s about making you doubt yourself. Because a person who doubts their own memory is much easier to push around than a person who trusts it.

The Memory Mixer-Upper shows up in different ways:

  • “That’s not what I said.” (when it is exactly what they said)
  • “You’re so sensitive — I was just joking.” (when it wasn’t a joke)
  • “I never promised that.” (when they absolutely did)
  • “Everyone else thinks you’re overreacting.” (even when nobody actually said that)

The trick works best on kind, self-aware people — the kind who are always willing to consider that maybe they got something wrong. If you’re the kind of person who asks “wait, am I sure?” before you push back, the Memory Mixer-Upper is aimed at you specifically.

Their Moves & Your Power-Ups

Move #1: The Denial — “That didn’t happen / I never said that.”

The Truth Lock

The TRUTH LOCK is simple: you don’t have to convince them. You just have to stay connected to what you actually know.

When the denial happens, your job is not to win the argument. Your job is to stay anchored to your own memory like a ship anchored to the harbor floor. The waves can splash. The anchor holds.

Say this

“I remember it differently. My memory is that [say exactly what happened].”

That’s it. You’re not saying they’re lying (even if they are). You’re not getting pulled into a debate about who has the better memory. You’re just stating what you know.

Move #2: The Feeling Dismiss — “You’re overreacting / being too sensitive.”

The Feeling Protector

This move happens right after the denial. When you say you’re upset about something, they turn the whole conversation into a question about your feelings instead of their behavior. Your feelings become the problem.

The Feeling Protector says: your feelings are always real. You don’t have to defend having them.

Say this

“I’m not overreacting — I’m letting you know how I feel. The feeling is real even if we remember things differently.”
Move #3: The Fog Throw — “Maybe you lent it to someone else and forgot? / I think you’re confused.”

The Memory Vault

This is the sneaky one. Instead of just denying, they suggest an alternative reality. Maybe you did something wrong. Maybe you made a mistake. They plant a seed of “wait, what if I’m wrong?” in your brain and then walk away, leaving you to water it.

The MEMORY VAULT is a tool you use in private, after the conversation. Write it down.

Say this

(in your head or your journal): “Here is what I know: [the event]. Here is when it happened: [day/time]. Here is who else was there: [names]. I trust my memory.”

Writing it down makes it solid. It’s very hard to Memory Mixer-Upper a piece of paper.

Trickster Levels

🌱Level 1 — Accidental

Someone genuinely misremembers something. They said they’d meet you at 3, but in their brain it was 3:30. No trick happening — just two different memories. Usually these people are open to talking it through: “Hm, I thought it was 3:30 — I’m sorry, you might be right.”

🔥Level 2 — Occasional

Someone doesn’t want to be wrong or get in trouble, so they fudge the story a little. This happens and it’s not okay, but it’s usually not a whole strategy — it’s embarrassment or self-protection.

💀Level 3 — The Full Trick

This is when someone uses the Memory Mixer-Upper regularly, on purpose, to make you doubt yourself and stay in control. If someone keeps doing this — not once, not twice, but as a pattern — that is the full trick. And the pattern is more important than any single incident.

📖 STORY TIMESHAELA’S STORY

Zill found Shaela standing at the water fountain looking at the floor.

“What happened?”

“Furbie said he never borrowed my pen. The purple one. But he absolutely did.”

Zill thought about it for exactly two seconds. “Do you have proof?”

“I mean — it was right there. I gave it to him. He wrote something with it.”

“Then you’re not imagining it.” Zill said it the same way he’d say “two plus two is four.” Just a fact. “What did you say to him?”

“I kind of just… went quiet. I didn’t know what to say.”

“Okay.” Zill pulled her backpack strap. “Here’s the thing my grandmother says: the truth doesn’t need you to shout it. It just needs you to hold onto it. You know what happened. You don’t have to convince Furbie that you know. You just have to keep knowing it yourself.”

The next day, Shaela went back to Furbie. She wasn’t angry. She wasn’t loud. She said: “Hey — I remember you borrowing my purple pen on Tuesday. I’d like it back or I’d like you to replace it. That’s all.”

Furbie did the memory thing again: “I really don’t think—”

“I remember it differently,” Shaela said. “And I just need the pen back.”

Furbie looked at her for a second. Something in her voice was different from the day before — steadier. He reached into his bag and dropped the pen on her desk without another word.

The TRUTH LOCK had held.

Traffic Light

Green One memory disagreement, no pattern. Use your TRUTH LOCK and MEMORY VAULT. Hold your ground calmly. You don’t need help for this one yet. Yellow This is the third or fourth time the same person has done this to you. Your memory is being questioned regularly. Write down what’s happening and share it with a parent, teacher, or counselor. Red An adult is doing the Memory Mixer-Upper — a teacher, family member, or someone with authority over you. When grown-ups do this to kids, it’s too big for Power-Ups alone. Please tell someone you trust.

Grown-Up Corner

The Memory Mixer-Upper is gaslighting — one of the most psychologically damaging forms of manipulation that exists, and one that doesn’t become less common in adulthood. It becomes more sophisticated. Children who are taught to trust their own memories early, and to use language like “I remember it differently” rather than “you’re lying,” develop a foundation of self-trust that protects them across their entire lives.

The reason the Memory Mixer-Upper works so well on kind children is that kind children are genuinely open to being wrong. That openness — which is a gift — gets weaponized. Helping your child identify the pattern (not just the single incident) is the key. One misremembering is human. A consistent pattern of “that never happened / you’re imagining it” from the same person is something else.

Ask your child: “Has anyone ever made you feel like you couldn’t trust your own memory? What did that feel like? What do you wish you’d said?”

Your Quest!

This week, practice the MEMORY VAULT. When something happens that matters to you — something you want to remember clearly — write it down.

Not a long entry. Just: What happened. When. Who was there.

Do this three times this week. Not because you think someone is going to Memory Mixer-Upper you — but because getting in the habit of trusting and recording your memory is a Power-Up you’ll use for the rest of your life.

Level Up Together

Parent and child: Think of a time when you were sure about something — a memory, a promise, something that happened — and someone told you that you were wrong. How did it feel? Did you stay confident in what you remembered, or did you start to doubt yourself? What do you wish you’d done?
