Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that being understood quickly means being known truly, and a reflex to share deeply and promise yourself before you have checked who someone really is.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • A pull toward people who seem to like everything you like, and a rush of trust when they match your pace.
  • An automatic sentence after a few days of talking, “it feels like I’ve known them forever.”
  • A habit of telling your secrets soon after someone tells you theirs, as if it were only fair.
A point in a life
The first year of running a company.
Where it happens
An investor mixer in a hotel.

You won’t be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You’re a deep-tech spin-out CEO, forty-one, a former university researcher who has spent fifteen years in a lab and eight months running a company, and you have never raised money. You have a patent, a licence from the university, and four employees. You also have a hole where your doctoral supervisor used to be. He died in the spring, and he was the person who would have told you what to do next.

Tuesday, a hotel ballroom

The investor-founder matchmaking mixer has round tables, lanyards, and a coloured dot on each badge for stage. Yours is green: early, with nothing to show. You stand by the coffee urn and feel like a visitor. Everyone around you seems to know someone who knows someone.

A man in a good jacket stops beside you and reads your badge before he reads your face. “Deep tech,” he says. “Good. Most people here sell apps.” His own badge says Partner, growth fund, a name you do not know. He tells you he left a university department years ago after a fight over credit, and that he still misses the smell of warm solder in a cold lab. It is almost your story. You laugh, and something in your neck lets go.

When you ask, lightly, about the fund, he answers at once. He says the fund is small and discreet by design, and that it backed a spin-out from your own university last year. You open the website on your phone while he fetches you a cup. The page is clean and says very little. The co-investor he named has no page at all, and his surname brings up nothing. You text a friend in the licensing office at the university. Her reply comes before the speeches: she has never heard of that spin-out. You tell yourself that a person with a story like his does not need a profile. When he comes back he says he would like to hear more, over dinner tomorrow, and you say yes before you have decided.

Wednesday and Thursday, the hotel restaurant

At the first dinner he listens in a way people seldom do. When you speak slowly, he speaks slowly. When you speed up on the patent, so does he. “You’re not pitching,” he says. “You’re describing something you love.” He agrees with your doubts about the scale-up and then, gently, tells you that you are being too modest. Between the starter and the main he has moved your round from next spring to this autumn, and you have nodded at every step. Before the plates are cleared he has booked a second dinner for the following night, at the same table. Your shoulders drop for the first time in months.

On Thursday, over dessert, he looks at the tablecloth. He had a mentor who died before his first company was funded. He says he has never said this to a founder before. You tell him about your supervisor. You have not told anyone. You say more than you meant to, including that you are not sure you are the right person to run any of it. He says, “I know. I’ve been you.” Then he says, “The funds that fit you are not the ones at that mixer.” Your eyes sting, and you let him pay.

Then, casually, he asks about the cap table: who holds what, whether the university has a stake, who else is looking. He says he can introduce you to people who will make the round easier, and all he needs is a little context so he can “help properly.” You send it before the coffee arrives: a summary, with the university’s terms and the names of the two other funds.

The following Tuesday, your office

The letter arrives by email, two pages, courteous, headed “Non-binding letter of intent.” It gives his fund a “right of first look” at your round for ninety days, so that, he writes, “we can work together without distraction.” It says other funds are showing interest and that he wants to avoid a crowded process. There is no valuation, no size, no named partner beyond his first name. One line says you will not talk to other investors about a priced round in that time.

You call to tell him you have read it. He is kind. “You’re not a process person, I could tell the first night,” he says. He tells you that you are someone who values loyalty, and that he knew it the first night. He says this is how serious investors show commitment, and that the letter is non-binding. He says a man who has trusted you with the story of his mentor is asking for nothing but a little loyalty in return. You hear yourself say you will sign by Friday.

Three months later, your kitchen

It is a Sunday in January and the kettle has gone cold beside you. The ninety days ended on a Friday. No valuation came, and no call. His replies arrive a day late now, one line each, still warm. The two funds you stopped answering have closed their rounds for the year; their last emails sit in your inbox, bold and unread.

On Tuesday a new adviser wrote to you about your patent. He drinks the same terrible coffee, hates slides, and writes at your hour, long past midnight. He mentioned his brother’s debts. By Wednesday you had told him what you pay your four employees and what the university’s terms say, and by Thursday you had told him about your supervisor’s last email. You feel yourself warm at each new message before you have read it. You have not looked him up.

You say “We’re in a great place” to the lab, to your sister, to the bathroom mirror. At night you reread your supervisor’s last email and cannot hear what he would say. And you do not do what he would have done, which is ask who the man is.

Your four employees are paid until March. The funds that would have asked you questions have stopped answering you.

The Name

That’s The Mirror Hook.

Definition

The Mirror Hook is the sequence where a persona that fits you arrives, matches your pace and then sets it, offers curated disclosures that invite yours, and asks for exclusivity before you could know who they are, using Catfishing, Pacing and Leading, Premature Vulnerability / Trauma Dumping, Premature Trust Extraction, Premature Exclusivity, and Identity Hooking to convert the wish to be understood quickly into the loss of the time you needed to check who someone is before trusting them. In corporate and fundraising settings it concentrates in matchmaking events, introducer-led dinners, family office approaches and early-stage rounds, where a first-time founder or principal is short of peers and runway. It is hard to detect because warmth, fit and enthusiasm are what real investors also offer. What can be lost is a window of time, confidential information, other investors’ attention and a clean fundraising process.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Catfishing

    Idealize

    A story that mirrors yours, and a claim that does not check out: a fund “small and discreet by design” said to have backed a spin-out from your university that its licensing office has never heard of, a co-investor you cannot find, a surname that appears nowhere. This functions by presenting a fit that is too good to be checked, and making verification feel like a lack of faith. Concern rises when the person’s role, fund, track record or co-investors cannot be confirmed through independent, public and verifiable sources.

  2. 2

    Pacing and Leading

    Idealize

    He speaks slowly when you do, speeds up when you speed up on the patent, agrees with your doubts about the scale-up and then calls you too modest, and books the second dinner for the next night before the first ends. This functions by building rapport that is real to the target and then using it to steer decisions. Concern rises when the agreement stops at the point where the target would normally take time.

  3. 3

    Premature Vulnerability / Trauma Dumping

    Idealize

    Over dessert he looks at the tablecloth and says he has never told a founder this: his own mentor died before his first company was funded; you answer with your supervisor, whom you have told no one about, and your doubts about running the company. This functions by creating intimacy that outruns knowledge of the person. Concern rises when the disclosure is unusually matched to the target’s own history and is followed by a request.

  4. 4

    Premature Trust Extraction

    Idealize

    Casual questions about who holds what on the cap table, whether the university has a stake and who else is looking, with an offer of introductions if you give “a little context”; you send a summary with the university’s terms that night. This functions by converting a feeling of closeness into confidential information. Concern rises when the questions are about leverage, such as who else is looking, and not about the technology.

  5. 5

    Premature Exclusivity

    Hook

    A two-page letter within the week grants his fund a “right of first look” for ninety days “without distraction,” with no valuation, no size and no partner named beyond his first name, and bars priced talks with other investors. This functions by closing off alternatives before anyone could verify the person who asked. Concern rises when exclusivity is requested before valuation, size, terms and identity are clear.

  6. 6

    Identity Hooking

    Hook

    On the phone he tells you that you are “not a process person” and are someone who values loyalty, adds that a man who shared his mentor with you asks only for loyalty and that the letter is non-binding, and you say you will sign by Friday. This functions by tying the exclusivity to who the person believes they are. Concern rises when a request for checking is described as out of character.

Why This Order

Catfishing is first because the persona must exist before anything else is possible. A fit is what makes the target relax, and relaxation is what makes everything else look reasonable. The persona need not be false in every detail; it only has to be plausible and uncheckable. It has to come before the matching of your pace, because he can only match you once the story about his fight over credit has made you relax around him.

At a party you meet someone who loves the same obscure band, grew up in your town and is just as nervous as you. You relax, and it does not occur to you to check any of it. Flip it: if the person at the party had matched your every move and mood before telling you anything about themselves, you would ask a simple question you could check.

Pacing and Leading makes the persona feel like a person. It matches the founder’s speed first, which is experienced as understanding. Only then does the pace change: the second dinner follows so quickly that the timeline is already being set. It has to come before the confession over dessert, because his slowing when you slow is what lets you lean in and answer his story about a dead mentor with your own.

A friend who walks at your speed on a hike is easy company. Then, a little at a time, you notice you are walking at hers. Flip it: if your friend had poured out a deep secret on the first mile before ever matching your speed, you would listen kindly and keep your own stories for later.

Premature Vulnerability follows, because a disclosure is the quickest way to create intimacy that has not been earned. It invites a matching disclosure, and a founder who has just lost a mentor is unusually available. It has to come before the request for the cap table, because the confession about your supervisor is what makes handing him the university’s terms feel like help.

On a long train journey a stranger tells you about a family quarrel. Before you know it, you are telling them about yours. Flip it: if the stranger on the train had asked for your address before telling you anything personal, you would just say no.

Premature Trust Extraction uses that intimacy. The founder is not interrogated; they are helped. The cap table, the university terms and the competing interest are volunteered inside a conversation that feels like care. What leaves the room is leverage. It has to come before the letter of intent, because the letter only makes sense for his fund once your summary has told him what the university holds and which two other funds are writing.

A new friend who has just told you something sad asks for your phone passcode “to help find your lost photos.” Saying no feels rude. Flip it: if the new friend had asked you to drop your other friends before sharing anything sad, you would say no and keep your friends close.

Premature Exclusivity is the conversion point, and it is placed after the extraction because the persona now knows what the founder has and who else is interested. The letter arrives within a week of the dinners, while the warmth is still fresh and before any background could be checked. It has to come before the talk of loyalty, because the call about loyalty needs the ninety-day letter lying on your desk to give the word something to ask of you.

On the third date someone says, “I’d like us to stop seeing other people.” It sounds lovely. You still do not know where they live. Flip it: if your date had told you “you’re the loyal type, I can tell” before asking for anything, you would laugh it off and still ask where they live.

Identity Hooking supplies the closure: refusing the letter would mean betraying the person the founder has been told they are. It has to come last, because the words about loyalty land only once the ninety-day letter is on your desk and your hesitation to sign gives him something to answer.

Someone tells you that you are the kind of person who never lets a friend down. Later, when they ask for something big, saying no feels like proving them wrong about you. Flip it: if someone asked you to keep them as your only friend right after saying you never let people down, you would say no and keep your other friends.

What would weaken it is a request for independently verifiable identity, a pause of a week before any signature, independent counsel on the letter, and sharing no cap table information before checking. The sequence depends on speed; time is its enemy.

Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A matchmaking mixer puts strangers in a room for a few hours with a badge as the only credential. Money is scarce and a founder’s runway is short, so everyone is in a hurry. And the paperwork of early funding, a short letter that gives one party time before anyone else, is a normal form that few people read twice.

Catfishing comes first because the room has no way to check anyone. A badge, a card and a clean website are all the identity a mixer can offer, and a founder short of time does not spend a week on background searches. A story that fits is enough to let a person relax.

Pacing and Leading comes second because the evening runs on a schedule. A dinner booked for tomorrow is how anyone in a hurry sets the pace, and matching a nervous founder’s speed is simply how a good conversation begins. The rhythm then passes from the founder to the investor without anyone deciding it should.

Premature Vulnerability comes third because a story shared at the speed of the first dinner asks for another in return. A sad story told across a table is hard to leave unanswered, and a founder who has recently lost a supervisor has an answer ready.

Premature Trust Extraction comes fourth because a round cannot move without information. Who holds what, what the university’s terms say and who else is interested are the facts any investor needs, and they are handed over most easily at a moment that already feels like care.

Premature Exclusivity comes fifth because investors’ time is scarce too. A short letter giving one fund first look at a round is a common form, and it can only be written once the fund knows the numbers and the competition. It lands in the week when the warmth of the dinners has not yet cooled.

Identity Hooking comes last because a founder who hesitates over a letter needs a reason to sign, and the cheapest reason on offer is the founder’s own self-image. Loyalty costs nothing to say and is hard to argue with in a small industry where reputation is currency.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: be believed, be matched, be confided in, be informed, be bound, and be convinced that signing is who you are. It turns a careful researcher into a founder who feels that checking would be disloyal.

On Purpose

In the story you could not see whether anyone meant it. Here, someone does. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a new buyer at a client company. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Leona Brenford was three weeks into her first job as head of purchasing at a mid-sized logistics firm when a supplier’s representative, Anders Calford, sat down beside her at an industry lunch. He had grown up in her home town, studied at her university and disliked slide decks as much as she did. His company had a brochure and a mobile number, and she could find nothing else about it. Over two coffees he spoke at her speed, and when she hurried, he hurried; by the third, they were working to his calendar. At the end of the month he mentioned, quietly, that he had been passed over at his own firm and was thinking of leaving, and Leona heard herself say she was not sure she deserved her own job. He asked, as a kindness, for her budget range and the names of the two other bidders, so he could “pitch it properly,” and she sent them from her phone that evening. A week later a one-page agreement arrived naming his company as her only supplier for a year. A line said she would not request other quotes in that time. When she hesitated, he said he had always thought of her as someone who keeps her word. She signed on Friday. Anders told a former colleague, over a drink, that new buyers were always the easiest people in a building to keep close.

From the inside, the person in the middle can’t tell the difference, and doesn’t need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The person may find that they have shared more than they would normally share with a stranger, and may be unsure how that happened. Over time, they may stop approaching other investors, feel embarrassed to ask who the person is, and read every doubt as disloyalty to someone who has been kind.

They may feel that the connection was real, which it may partly be, and find it hard to separate the feeling from the facts. They may be reluctant to tell counsel, since it sounds like an admission of being taken in.

Institutional Echo

Matchmaking events, introducer networks and online investor directories rely on self-description. Titles, fund names and track records are often presented rather than checked, and a founder with no peer group has limited ways to test them. Non-binding letters of intent that include exclusivity, a right of first look or a standstill can be presented as routine, though whether a given clause binds depends on its wording and jurisdiction.

The same shape appears in family office approaches, where a principal without a team can be courted by an advisor or co-investor whose standing is described rather than shown.

The honest reading is that most investors who are warm are simply warm, that many real funds are small and private, and that a letter of intent is an ordinary tool. Intent would require additional evidence, and the discriminator is verifiable identity gaps, not warmth: a person who cannot be found in any independent, verifiable source, whose fund cannot be confirmed, and who objects to checking.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that the right investor just gets you, and that chemistry is the best due diligence. Around it sits the belief that sophisticated founders move fast, that checking someone’s background is distrust, and that a handshake is worth more than paper.

The value being exploited is genuine: people do find real partners through shared experience, and wanting to be understood is especially strong after a loss.

The language that makes the pattern sound normal is the language of fit: “I get you,” “we’re built the same,” “this is how we do things.”

The public story is the founder who found a believer. The private loss is the weeks in which nobody checked.

Observable Indicators

  1. The person’s role, fund or track record cannot be confirmed through independent public sources.
  2. Their personal story closely mirrors yours, including specific details of loss or conflict.
  3. Intimate disclosure arrives within the first one or two meetings.
  4. The pace of meetings accelerates as soon as you show hesitation.
  5. Questions concern who else is investing, your cap table or the university’s terms before any term sheet.
  6. Requests for confidential information are framed as helping.
  7. An exclusivity or “right of first look” letter arrives within days, before valuation, size or named partners.
  8. Co-investors or references are named but cannot be reached or are vague.
  9. A request to check background is described as unnecessary, or as out of character for you.
  10. Pressure rises around other interested funds that you cannot identify.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • Screenshots and saved copies of the person’s website, profile and any directory listing, with the date.
  • Every message, including personal disclosures, with dates.
  • A list of what confidential information you shared, when, and in what form.
  • The letter of intent in every version, with the date and time of receipt, and any redlines.
  • Your own notes on questions asked about the cap table, the university and other investors.
  • Identity checks you carried out and what you found or could not find.
  • Names of any introducer or mutual contact, and what they confirmed.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder’s fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity “to be papered.”
  • Deadline records: allocation, “final close,” offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Real investors are often warm, often share their own stories, and often ask for some exclusivity. Many genuine funds are small and private. The discriminator is verifiable identity combined with the order of requests. A real person can be checked and welcomes it. The pattern asks for commitment before checking is possible and treats checking as an insult.

Other benign lookalikes:

  • Genuine shared history. A mentor’s death and a university fight are not unusual among researchers, and a real investor may have lived through the same.
  • Standard exclusivity. A no-shop period after a term sheet, with real terms and a real valuation, is ordinary.
  • Discreet funds. Some funds keep a minimal public footprint for good reasons.
  • Warm business courting. Enthusiasm and fast follow-up are normal in a competitive round.

Concern strengthens when: the person cannot be verified through any independent source; the exclusivity request precedes any valuation or terms; and a request to check or take advice is met with reproach.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or “not needed at this stage.”
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or “cleanup of the cap table” serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as “just formalities.”
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side’s calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Chain Breakers

  • Slow the timeline by one week, in writing. “Thank you for the letter. I will respond after my counsel has reviewed it, by the end of next week.”
  • Verify before you share. Look for independent, public and verifiable sources: registrations, filings, prior deals, and reference calls with people you find yourself, not those they provide.
  • Ask the introducer or event organiser. “What do you know about this fund, and how do you know it?” Treat a vague answer as information.
  • Share nothing confidential before an NDA you have had reviewed. Give summary-level information until identity is confirmed.
  • Get a lawyer to read any exclusivity letter and explain exactly what it prevents and for how long.
  • Ask for the missing terms. “Could you send the proposed valuation, round size, and the name and role of the partner who would lead?”
  • Keep other conversations alive until a real term sheet is in hand.
  • Tell a peer or the university’s technology transfer office, who can sanity-check the approach.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A persona whose identity cannot be verified; an exclusivity request before terms; confidential information requested early; a story that closely mirrors yours.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    You have shared confidential data and cannot confirm who holds it; the letter is signed and the person now resists a clean exit; payments, fees or “expenses” are requested up front; the person claims to represent funds or co-investors you cannot find. Relevant routes may include a securities or corporate attorney, the university’s technology transfer or legal office, a securities or financial services regulator where an offering or a finder’s arrangement is in question, and law enforcement where fraud is suspected.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Any threat, blackmail using what you disclosed about yourself or your supervisor, requests for money transfers, intimidation, or a statement of self-harm by anyone involved. Contact emergency or crisis services, qualified counsel and law enforcement. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete messages. If grief is overwhelming, speak to a doctor or counsellor.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call (“To confirm what we agreed today...”). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
  • Mediation or arbitration services, where a contract points to them or both sides agree.
  • A professional licensing or disciplinary body for lawyers, accountants, or registered financial advisers, where the person involved holds such a license.
  • A securities, financial services or investment regulator, where an offering, allocation or finder’s arrangement is in question.
  • An employment or labor regulator, or a whistleblower channel, where retaliation or a compliance concern is involved.
  • A consumer or small-business protection authority, where an advisory or consulting service has been sold to you.
  • A family office principal’s independent fiduciary, trust counsel, or trusted family advisor who has no stake in the engagement.
  • A therapist, peer support group or founder community for the personal toll, which is separate from the legal and commercial questions.

Do not make public accusations before you have evidence and advice. Statements in group channels, to investors or to references can create their own exposure, including defamation risk.

URGENT — safety first. Threats, blackmail, extortion, stalking, intimidation, fraud in progress, physical danger, self-harm statements by anyone involved, abuse of a dependent person (including an elderly or vulnerable family office principal), child or sexual exploitation, illegal confinement, or severe retaliation. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact local emergency services, crisis lines, and qualified counsel or law enforcement as appropriate. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence. Tell someone you trust right away. Where self-harm is mentioned by you or by someone else, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately.

Safer Response Language

  • “I appreciate the letter. I’ll respond after my counsel has reviewed it.”

  • “Could you send your fund’s registration details and the name of the partner who would lead?”

  • “Before I share more, can we agree an NDA my lawyer has reviewed?”

  • “I’m talking to a few funds, and I won’t agree to exclusivity before there is a term sheet.”

  • “I would like to speak to a co-investor from your last round. Could you introduce me?”

  • “Who introduced us, and what do they know about the fund?”

  • “I’m happy to take a few days. If the timing is a problem, that’s useful for me to know.”
