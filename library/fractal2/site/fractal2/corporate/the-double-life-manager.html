Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that what you saw in private cannot be real if everyone else sees someone kind, and a reflex to doubt yourself rather than compare stories.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • An automatic thought when someone praises a person who hurt you, “maybe I’m the one who saw it wrong.”
  • A habit of keeping different friends apart, and of telling each only their own part of your life.
  • A wariness of anyone who is charming in company, and a struggle to trust what you notice behind closed doors.
A point in a life
Midcareer, three years under one boss.
Where it happens
A client-facing consulting firm.

You won’t be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You’re a client relationship lead, you’re thirty-five, and tonight you are wearing the dress you bought for the awards dinner, standing in a hotel corridor with your phone in your hand. You want nothing more than for the evening to be ordinary. You have worked under the senior partner for three years. He hired you. In meetings with clients he is generous to a degree that has become part of the firm’s reputation: attentive, funny, quick to give credit. A client once told you he was the most gracious senior person she had ever met.

Before the dinner, the corridor

The internal email arrived forty minutes ago. The subject line is “Q3 Account Review.” The body says, in full: “I am concerned about the standard of your work on this account. Your recent client communications have been careless and your follow-through has been poor. I expect a written remediation plan by Monday. This is not the first time this has come up.” You read it standing under a framed print of the city skyline. Your hands go cold. The carpet is the color of wet slate, and from the ballroom comes the sound of a hundred glasses being set down.

It is sent to you alone, with two of his deputies copied. It is not copied to the account team, or to the client, or to anyone who sat in the March meetings. You think of the client at table six, who is saving you a seat.

It has not come up before. You check, scrolling through your own records with your thumb. There is no earlier mention. There is a note from the March incident, in which he thanked you. There is a one-to-one summary from August where he said, “You’re doing a great job.” The email names no date and no client. “Careless” and “poor” have no example behind them. “I am concerned” could be care. One of the copied deputies passes you and says, easily, “Don’t read too much into it. It’s quite measured.” You cannot put your finger on a sentence. You go back in and sit down.

The awards dinner, table six

You are in your seat beside a procurement head you have worked with for four years, with your phone face down in your clutch. The speeches start. “I’ll tell you something about this woman,” he says into the microphone. “When the project nearly went sideways in March, she didn’t flinch. She is the reason this partnership works.” The client at your table squeezes your arm. Three hundred people applaud. He raises his glass to you across the room, and the light catches his smile, which is a very good one. During the main course he leans across and says, kindly, “You look tired. Are you looking after yourself?” You say you are fine.

Monday, his office

On Monday you stand in his doorway and ask him about the email. He frowns, the way a man frowns at an unfamiliar word. “I don’t recall it being that harsh,” he says. “I think you’re reading it in a particular way. We’ve always been very supportive of you.” When you ask whether he said at the dinner what he said, he laughs: “Of course I did. I meant every word.” You read him the first line from your phone. He listens with his head tilted and says, “I really don’t see what you see.”

That week, your desk

He says he is sure your deputy directors will agree the tone was balanced. They are the same two he copied, and the next one-to-one invitation arrives with both of them on it. Later that week the client calls you directly, delighted, to say she told her board that you are the heart of the relationship. She quotes the speech back to you almost word for word.

You start to keep a folder. Praise in public, a sentence in the awards program. Criticism in private, a sentence in an email. When you describe it out loud to someone who was at the dinner, it sounds like nothing. A kind speech. A sharp email. Anyone might. You have stopped eating in the office kitchen, where the deputies talk. You reread both documents at your desk after everyone has gone. The hardest part is not the email. It is that you can no longer say, with the confidence you had in February, which one of them he is.

Two years later

It is a Saturday in April, and you are hosting a dinner at your apartment, a table of six, and your new neighbor, a retired judge, is telling a story that has everyone laughing. He is warm, quick, interested in each person in turn. He asks about your work and remembers your answer. Someone says, “Isn’t he wonderful?” and the table says yes, and you hear yourself say yes with them.

While the others pass the bread, he leans toward you and says, low and pleasant, that your friend’s dress is “a brave choice, isn’t it?” Then he smiles and turns back to the story. You hold the butter dish. The remark was so quiet that you tell yourself the dishwasher drowned half of it. You pass the butter.

Your phone buzzes against your knee: two friends who have never met each other, each asking separately how the evening is going. You write each of them a different, tidy account, each missing a piece the other has. You keep the two of them out of the same room now. It is easier. You have not told anyone what he said at the table. They would ask what he was like, and you would have to say he was the most charming man there, and you would not be able to say which of the two sentences was true.

The Name

That’s The Double Life Manager.

Definition

The Double Life Manager is the sequence where two sharply different versions of a person are presented to separate audiences, kept apart, and defended with ambiguity, so that anyone who sees both is led to doubt the one that is harder to prove. It can use Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde, Compartmentalization, Plausible Deniability, Performative Public Affection, Gaslighting and Information Control to convert the natural trust that the version of someone we see is the whole person into the loss of a consistent, verifiable understanding of who someone actually is. It is hard to detect because public warmth is often sincere, private criticism can be legitimate, and most managers behave differently in different settings. Intent would require additional evidence; the pattern is the gap between audiences, not the existence of a harsh email.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde

    Idealize

    The two men: generous and public with clients, and a private email saying “I am concerned about the standard of your work” and “This is not the first time this has come up.” This functions by giving each audience a convincing and incompatible picture. Concern rises when the gap is large, recurs, and tracks who is watching.

  2. 2

    Compartmentalization

    Isolate

    The separate rooms: the email goes to you alone with two deputies copied, not to the account team or the client, and the people who see one version never see the other. This functions by preventing the two pictures from meeting. Concern rises when you notice that the people who see one version do not see the other.

  3. 3

    Plausible Deniability

    Isolate

    The email that can be read two ways: no earlier mention in your records, no date, client or example behind “careless” and “poor,” and a deputy telling you it is “quite measured.” This functions by making the private version hard to pin down. Concern rises when explanations are available for every instance and none for the pattern.

  4. 4

    Performative Public Affection

    Idealize

    The speech with an audience: “She is the reason this partnership works,” the client squeezing your arm, three hundred people applauding, and a raised glass across the room. This functions by creating a visible record that conflicts with your account and makes it harder to describe the private conduct. Concern rises when public praise is greatest around the same time as private criticism.

  5. 5

    Gaslighting

    Several stages

    The Monday answer: “I don’t recall it being that harsh,” “I think you’re reading it in a particular way,” and “I meant every word” about the speech. This functions by shifting the question from his conduct to your perception. Concern rises when your accurate recollection is repeatedly described as a misreading.

  6. 6

    Information Control

    Several stages

    Who is in the loop: he says your deputy directors will agree the tone was balanced, they are the same two he copied, they are on the next one-to-one invitation, and the client has heard only the speech. This functions by keeping audiences from comparing notes. Concern rises when your attempts to share the full record with a neutral party meet resistance.

Why This Order

The two Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde personas have to exist first, because the rest of the sequence is a maintenance system for a gap that has already been created. A manager who is the same in every room would have nothing to protect. It has to come before the email going only to you and two deputies, because a private email this harsh can be kept from the client’s view only if it goes to you and two deputies alone.

Picture a neighbor who is the star of the street party and snaps at his children the moment the door closes. Flip it: if the neighbor had always kept his street friends and his home life carefully apart before any party, you would notice the gap and mention your worry to a trusted adult.

Compartmentalization then keeps the gap safe. The point is not that the two sides are secret but that they never share an audience. It has to come before the deputy’s “quite measured,” because the email being seen only by you and two deputies is what makes their reading the only second opinion.

Think of a teenager who keeps school friends, home friends and online friends in three separate boxes. Nobody holds the whole picture. Flip it: if the teenager’s messages were already vague enough to mean anything before the friend groups were split, you would ask plainly what each one meant.

Plausible Deniability protects the private side by making it ambiguous, so that when it is seen, it can be read generously. It has to come before the speech at the dinner, because a mild-sounding “I am concerned” and a deputy’s reassurance leave you nothing to show anyone when he praises you in front of three hundred.

A friend texts “fine, whatever you want.” It could be warm or cold, and if you complain, it was “only a text.” Flip it: if your friend made a big show of loving you in front of everyone before any chilly text arrived, you would still ask face to face what the text meant.

Performative Public Affection protects the public side and adds weight: the more convincing the warmth, the less likely an observer is to credit the private account. It has to come before his “I don’t recall it being that harsh,” because the applause and the raised glass are what make your account of the email sound unlikely.

Picture a parent who hugs you warmly at the school gate every morning. If you later say what was said at home, nobody believes you. Flip it: if the parent had already said “that never happened” about home before any hug at the gate, you would write down what you remember and tell a trusted adult.

Gaslighting is the repair step. When the two versions collide, it is applied to the person who noticed, so the contradiction is resolved in the way that costs the manager nothing. It has to come before the one-to-one with both deputies, because once he has said you are reading it in a particular way, the only people left to ask are the two he copied.

You say “you shouted at me yesterday” and hear “I don’t remember that. Are you sure?” Soon you are checking your own diary. Flip it: if the people who saw yesterday had already been told what to say before you mentioned the shouting, you would trust your diary and talk to someone outside it.

Information Control then closes the loop by managing who gets to hold the whole picture, so that the next collision is less likely. It has to come last, because naming those two deputies as the readers settles who may hold the email and the speech together, which is the one thing that could show the contradiction.

Picture a family where one parent decides who tells whom what. Each sibling ends up with a different story. Flip it: if each sibling already held a different story and only then the parent said your memory was wrong, you would compare notes with your siblings yourself.

The conversion point is the moment you start doubting your own record. What would weaken the sequence: your own dated files, a neutral third reader who has both documents, and a pattern of written follow-up that makes private statements part of the record.

Why the System Produces This Order

A professional services firm produces this order without a plan. Three facts about the setting do the work. The firm’s reputation is built on how its senior people look to clients, so public warmth is an asset. Private feedback travels by email to small lists, so the audience for each message is chosen. And the people who might compare the two sides, the client and the account team, are on the other side of a door.

Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde comes first because the rest of the sequence maintains a gap that has to exist already. A senior partner who is the same in every room would have nothing to protect.

Compartmentalization comes second because the gap has to stay safe, and it stays safe when the two sides never share an audience. An email copied to two deputies and no one else reaches exactly the people who will not contradict it. Nobody needs to forbid comparison; the lists do it.

Plausible Deniability comes third because the private side is safest when it is ambiguous. “I am concerned” could be care, and a deputy can say it is measured. When it is seen, it can be read generously.

Performative Public Affection comes fourth because the public side needs weight. The more convincing the warmth in the ballroom, the less likely any observer is to credit the private account.

The step that makes you doubt your own account comes fifth because the two versions will eventually collide, and the collision has to be resolved in the way that costs the senior partner nothing. It is applied to the person who noticed: you are reading it in a particular way.

Information Control comes last because the next collision is less likely if someone manages who holds the whole picture. The same two deputies who were copied sit in your next one-to-one, and the client quotes the speech.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a firm that rewards a gracious face in public and a hard hand in private. It turns the person in the middle into someone who keeps a folder and no longer trusts their own record.

On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A firm that prizes a gracious face in front of clients, email lists that choose their own audiences, and a ballroom full of applause did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a junior colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Yusuf Banerjee, an associate at a consulting firm, opened an email from his director, Adelaide Torley, forty minutes before the firm’s annual dinner. It said that his client work had been “careless” and that this was “not the first time,” with no example. It was copied to two of her deputies and no one on his project team. In the corridor outside the ballroom a deputy passed him and said, “It’s quite measured, don’t read too much into it.” At the dinner she praised him from the stage as “the reason this client stays.” On Monday he asked Adelaide about the email; she said she did not remember it being that harsh, and that he was reading it in a particular way. His next review meeting was scheduled with the same two deputies. The client called him that week, delighted, quoting the speech. Adelaide was never unsure what she was doing. She told a friend that Yusuf would believe the stage over the screen.

From the inside, the person in the middle can’t tell the difference, and doesn’t need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt which version of the manager is real, and to second-guess their memory of an exchange. Over time, they may stop describing the private conduct to others, because it sounds minor out loud. They may become hypervigilant about emails, re-read messages, and prepare for each interaction as if it were evidence. They may feel flattered and wary at once, and find it hard to explain to a client or a friend why a warm public moment makes them uneasy.

Institutional Echo

Client-service environments reward visible warmth and keep internal feedback in separate channels, which can leave the two disconnected. Performance feedback delivered by email with a small circle of copied managers can create a record that the recipient has not seen the basis for. Awards, recognition and client-facing praise can be genuine and still sit next to a very different internal assessment.

The honest reading is often that managers vary their register, that severe feedback may be warranted, and that the same person can be generous and demanding. Intent would require additional evidence: whether other direct reports describe the same gap, whether the criticism had any earlier history, and whether the manager acknowledges and corrects it when shown the record.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that character shows in public, and that someone who is gracious to clients must be gracious. We trust the person who is charming in front of others, and we take a private complaint to be the exception. Around it sits the belief that good managers keep criticism private, which is often right, and that the person who challenges a charming leader is the one with the problem.

The value exploited is trust in what we can see. The public story is a gracious leader who celebrated his people. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a person whose accurate perception is the one thing the firm treats as unreliable.

Observable Indicators

  1. Public praise and private criticism are given in a short period and differ sharply in substance.
  2. The criticism has no earlier written history.
  3. The harsh message is sent to you with a small number of senior people copied.
  4. Different audiences, such as clients and colleagues, describe markedly different experiences of the manager.
  5. The private conduct is described as a misreading when raised.
  6. The manager states he does not recall, or that the tone was balanced.
  7. Written feedback is requested to be replaced by a conversation.
  8. Your attempts to share the full record with a neutral party are discouraged.
  9. Praise is given in public after a private request that you do not repeat the criticism.
  10. Others who witnessed both versions express doubt about the private one.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • The harsh email, with headers, recipients and the date and time, kept as received.
  • Earlier reviews, one-to-one summaries, thank-you notes and positive messages from the same period.
  • Awards programs, speeches or client messages that describe your work, as lawfully obtained.
  • Your written follow-up summaries after any conversation about the email, and the responses.
  • A dated list of who was copied on feedback, and who was not.
  • Names of colleagues or clients who saw both versions, and what they said.
  • A short note written the same day, separating the quoted words from your interpretation.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder’s fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity “to be papered.”
  • Deadline records: allocation, “final close,” offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Managers legitimately praise in public and criticize in private, and one email can be a genuine response to a genuine problem. Memory differences between two people are ordinary.

The discriminator is whether the gap is consistent, one-directional and denied. A manager with sincere concerns can name them, give examples and hear a response. This pattern shifts when examined.

Other benign lookalikes:

  • Real performance concerns. The email may reflect a real issue that you have not yet seen clearly.
  • Different registers. Public and private communication styles often differ.
  • Honest recall. The manager may truly not remember the exchange.

Concern strengthens when: criticism has no earlier history or examples; the manager’s account changes when shown the record; and other reports describe the same gap.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or “not needed at this stage.”
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or “cleanup of the cap table” serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as “just formalities.”
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side’s calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Chain Breakers

  • Keep both records together, in one dated folder: public praise, private criticism and your notes.
  • Respond to feedback in writing, asking for specifics: “Could you give me two examples of the communications you mean?”
  • Send a short summary after every conversation: “To confirm what we discussed today...”
  • Ask a neutral third party, such as HR or a senior colleague you trust, to read both documents.
  • Ask for the standard. “What does a good remediation plan need to show, and who will review it?”
  • Keep a witness list of clients or colleagues who saw both versions, and do not ask them to take sides.
  • Do not forward confidential client material to prove your point.
  • Do not make public accusations before evidence and advice.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A harsh message with no history; a denial when you raise it; your accurate recollection described as a misreading. Start the folder and send written summaries.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    A remediation plan or review that relies on the criticism; the same pattern described by others; pay or role at stake. Relevant routes may include HR or employee relations, a skip-level or independent senior sponsor, an employment lawyer or union representative where available, and an ombuds channel.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Threats, retaliation after raising the issue, harassment, or anyone describing self-harm. Prioritize safety and emergency support, contact qualified counsel, and do not confront the person or delete evidence.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call (“To confirm what we agreed today...”). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
