Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that anything you report in private will reach the wrong people, and a reflex to back down when friends and neighbors start gently telling you to let it go.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • A habit of telling nobody about a concern, even people sworn to keep it private, because last time it traveled.
  • A pull to give way when several people you know, one after another, suggest you are causing trouble.
  • An automatic thought, "everyone already knows, so I may as well drop it," whenever a complaint feels necessary.
A point in a life
The week after filing an ethics report.
Where it happens
A company with an ethics hotline.

You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You're a senior accountant, you are thirty-six, and you are the person who notices when numbers do not walk the same path twice. For two quarters a set of expense approvals in one division has been landing in your queue with the same signature and the same round figures. You have asked your manager, who said it was handled. You have asked once more.

Sunday night, your kitchen table

So on a Sunday night you do the thing the policy invites you to do. You open the ethics hotline portal, you read the confidentiality statement twice, and you file. Your tea is cold beside the laptop, and you leave the window open for a minute after you click submit. The confirmation gives you a case number and a sentence: "Your report will be handled in confidence and shared only with those who need to know." You write the number on a sticky note and put the note in a drawer. You feel strangely calm, the way people do after they have done a clean, correct thing.

Monday to Thursday, your desk

On Monday at nine an email arrives from the compliance coordinator. "Apologies, a routing slip: a summary of your intake was copied to a wider list for a few minutes. It has been recalled. No action needed." You read it twice. Accidents happen. Your stomach goes tight anyway, and you tell it to stop.

On Tuesday a colleague you have known for years stops by your desk, smelling of the lemon hand cream she has used for as long as you have known her. "Heard you've been having a hard time lately," she says, tilting her head. You haven't told anyone. You say you are fine. "Someone mentioned you might want a friendly face," she says, and she does not seem to know why she is saying it. "Well, if you ever want to talk, I'm here."

On Wednesday someone from two floors up, a person you like, sits on the edge of your desk. He is kind. He tells you that "people are worried," that "you've been under a lot of pressure," and that he hopes "you're not doing anything you can't take back." He doesn't say what. On Thursday a former manager, who left the company two years ago, rings your mobile to say she "heard a rumor" and that she "just wanted to look out for you." She says, warmly, that this kind of thing "has a way of following people." Three days, three people, and you can no longer treat any of them as a one-off.

Friday, the office kitchen

On Friday three more people find you at the kettle, one after another, each with a kind face. One asks how you are sleeping. One says she has been thinking of you. One just stands beside you while the water boils, and says, "We all want what's best." None of them says the word complaint. None says hotline. Not one of them mentions the person you reported.

The next Monday, your desk

You call the compliance line and ask whether your report has been shared outside the review team. A calm voice tells you the process is confidential and that nobody involved has any record of a disclosure. You find your Tuesday colleague and ask who it was that mentioned you. She frowns. "Nobody, really. I just care about you." You believe them. You believe they are telling you the truth as they understand it. That is almost the worst part.

By lunch another friend stops by and asks if you have thought about "what this does to the team." Another asks, "Is it worth it?" You catch yourself picturing the people on your floor, and what a case number might cost them.

A third touches your arm and says, "It might be better to let it go before it gets bigger." You count. Six people since Tuesday, the same message in six voices. Each of them is someone you like. Each is genuinely, visibly concerned. Then you notice you are drafting a message to withdraw the report, and the draft is already polite.

You have not slept properly in a week. You have stopped opening the expense queue first thing, because the signature is in it. You eat lunch at your desk so nobody can stop by. You think about how you would explain to anyone that nobody did anything, and you cannot find the first sentence.

Two years later, a new office

Two years later it's a Thursday in May, and you're at a new company, at a desk by a window, looking at a payment that has gone out twice. The amount is small. Your first thought is of the sticky note, which you kept, and which is in the wallet in your bag.

You open a message to your manager. You type, "Quick question about invoice 4410." You delete "Quick." You delete the rest. The cursor blinks in an empty box. The company has a hotline, and its confidentiality statement is on the intranet, and you open neither. You have not said the words ethics hotline aloud since that Sunday.

Your manager walks past and asks if there is anything you want to flag. "Nothing," you say. The analyst at the next desk leans over to ask if you are sure about the number you mentioned in passing this morning. A second colleague stops with her coffee and says the same. Before the third arrives, you say, "You're probably right, I'll drop it," and the payment stays where it is.

You are thirty-eight. You are still the person who notices when numbers do not walk the same path twice. You have stopped telling anyone.

The Name

That's The Flying Monkey Network.

Definition

The Flying Monkey Network is the sequence where a confidential complaint is passed to people who then press the complainant on someone else's behalf, so that pressure arrives without institutional fingerprints, using Complaint Leaking, Proxy Recruitment, Social Pressure Layering, Plausible Deniability, Community Reframing, and Withdrawal Pressure to convert the trust that a confidential complaint will stay confidential into the loss of control over who knows about the complaint and when. It may benefit whoever is the subject of the report, or whoever prefers it did not proceed. It exploits the way reports pass through human hands, and the way colleagues act from concern. It is hard to detect because each messenger is sincere and no instruction can be shown. In this sector it can cost a person their report, their standing, and the sense that the process was safe to use.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Complaint Leaking

    Confuse

    An accident notice: on Monday the compliance coordinator writes that a summary of your intake was copied to a wider list by mistake for a few minutes, was recalled, and needs "no action." This functions by activating a social network before the process has done anything. Concern rises when colleagues refer to your situation days after you filed, though you told nobody.

  2. 2

    Proxy Recruitment

    Isolate

    Colleagues carry the message without knowing it: a friend of years says "Heard you've been having a hard time lately," and that someone mentioned you might want a friendly face, though you told nobody. This functions by moving pressure from the institution to relationships, where it is harder to name. Concern rises when people who rarely discuss your work all approach you in the same week.

  3. 3

    Social Pressure Layering

    Confuse

    Contacts pile up across days and settings: a kind lunch on Wednesday ("people are worried," "you can't take back") and a phone call from a former manager on Thursday about a rumor that "has a way of following people." This functions by making the cumulative effect much stronger than any single remark. Concern rises when the messages are different in tone but identical in meaning.

  4. 4

    Plausible Deniability

    Isolate

    Everyone disclaims it: the compliance voice says nobody involved has any record of a disclosure, and the colleague from Tuesday says "Nobody, really. I just care about you." This functions by ensuring there is no one to hold responsible. Concern rises when a direct question about disclosure is answered with a general statement of confidentiality.

  5. 5

    Community Reframing

    Confuse

    The matter is recast as harm to the team: a friend asks if you have thought about "what this does to the team," and another asks "Is it worth it?" This functions by moving your report from an act of integrity to a threat to relationships. Concern rises when conversations center on the team's feelings and never on the content of the report.

  6. 6

    Withdrawal Pressure

    Extract · Control

    The same quiet message in six voices: "It might be better to let it go before it gets bigger," with no one naming the report, until you find yourself drafting a polite message taking the report back. This functions by making withdrawal feel like the kind choice. Concern rises when you find yourself drafting a withdrawal, and nobody has actually asked you to.

Why This Order

Complaint Leaking comes first because the whole pattern depends on information arriving where it should not. Without it, the network has nothing to act on. It also needs no malice: a report can be shared carelessly, or by someone who believes they are helping. It has to come before the friendly visits, because a colleague can only say she heard you are having a hard time if your intake summary has already reached a wider list.

Picture telling one trusted person a private worry, and later hearing it repeated by someone who says, "Oh, it just came up." Nobody meant it to get out. It did. Flip it: if someone you rarely hear from asked about your worry before you had told anyone, you would ask how they came to know.

Proxy Recruitment follows because direct pressure from the subject or the institution would be visible and, in many places, prohibited. A friendly colleague is not subject to the same scrutiny, and carries credibility the institution cannot. This is also the stage at which the pattern is hardest to separate from real concern, because colleagues do worry about each other. It has to come before the three people at the kettle, because the Tuesday colleague, the man on your desk and the former manager are what make the next three feel like a pattern.

A friend you haven't heard from in months texts to ask how you are. They don't say why. Perhaps they were asked to, or perhaps they heard something. Flip it: if half a dozen people had already asked, "You okay with your choice?" before this friend texted, you would ask the friend straight out who sent them.

Social Pressure Layering is what converts concern into force. One conversation can be set aside; six in five days cannot. The layering also isolates you, since each person looks like a friend and the total looks like a campaign only from the inside. It has to come before your call to compliance, because you only ask who has been talking once so many people have said the same thing that you need to know where it began.

One person saying "are you sure about that?" is easy to answer. Six in a week, in the lift, over lunch and by text, is not. Flip it: if you first heard a rumour about you that nobody would own, you would ask the next person who questions you where they heard it.

Plausible Deniability protects every link: the messengers meant no harm, the institution had no part, and the leak cannot be traced. It has to come before the questions about what this does to the team, because that care only sounds like their own idea once nobody can say who started the talk.

A rumour goes round the office, and everyone you ask says, "I just heard it somewhere." Nobody started it, and nobody meant any harm. Flip it: if colleagues first asked whether raising things was "fair on the rest of us," you would say fair questions help everyone and still ask where the rumour began.

Community Reframing then supplies the moral frame: the cost of the report is now borne by people you like. It has to come before the drafted withdrawal, because you only start to write it after people you like have told you what your report might cost them.

You mention that the shared fridge is always full of someone else's lunch, and hear, "Do you really want to make this a thing for the whole floor?" Flip it: if everyone kept sighing about how calm the floor used to be before you mentioned the fridge, you would still raise the fridge and ask for a fair shelf.

Withdrawal Pressure is the closing step, and the conversion point is when you start drafting the withdrawal yourself. It has to come last, because it needs your colleagues' kindness to have already carried the same message six times, so that the withdrawal you write looks like your own idea.

Nobody tells you to drop it. Everyone keeps mentioning how pleasant things were before, until you find yourself writing the message that takes it back. Flip it: if you had already started writing the message that takes it back and then someone asked whether it was worth the fuss, you would stop and leave your concern in place.

What would weaken the sequence: stating clearly that you will not discuss the report; asking compliance in writing who has had access; taking each contact as information, not as a verdict; and bringing in an independent adviser before you decide anything.

Why the System Produces This Order

A company ethics process has three features. A report passes through intake, routing and review, and each handoff is another place a summary can land. Confidentiality is promised, but "those who need to know" is a wide phrase in a large company. And the people on a floor are friends, with real care for each other and a strong wish to keep the peace. Nobody designed a way to talk a reporter out of a report. The setting has the parts for one.

Complaint leaking comes first because a report has to be routed, and routing means copies. A summary that reaches a wider list for a few minutes is usually an accident, and an accident is all that is needed: the information is out.

Proxy recruitment comes second because once the information is out, the people who hear it care about the person it concerns. They do not need to be asked. They reach out because a friend seems troubled, and their kindness is real.

Social pressure layering comes third because a friendly floor passes concern along. Each person adds a visit or a text, and each visit looks harmless alone. Six of them in a week read, to the person at the center, as the whole office.

Plausible deniability comes fourth because nobody in the chain has done anything wrong. The coordinator made a slip, the colleagues were kind, and the compliance line truthfully holds no record of anything. Everyone can say so, and each of them is right.

Community reframing comes fifth because workplaces talk about the team more than the case. A question like "what does this do to the floor?" is a real concern, and it moves the cost of the report from the company to people the reporter likes.

Withdrawal pressure comes last because no one has to ask for a withdrawal. Reminders of how pleasant things were are enough, and a person who has heard them often enough writes the message.

Put it together and you get the order this setting needs: a leak that looks like an accident, friends who reach out, a crowd of them, no one to blame, a cost put on people you like, and a withdrawal that feels like your own idea. Nobody had to plan it. The structure rewards the peace of the floor, and it turns a person who noticed a problem into one who stops saying so.

On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. An intake process, a wide idea of who needs to know and a friendly floor did the work, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Nadia Petrov, a bookkeeper at a regional firm, sent a note to the internal ethics line about a pattern of identical invoices. The next morning a coworker told her that a summary of her note had gone to the wrong list by mistake. That afternoon a friend from another team said she had heard Nadia was having a rough time. A man from accounts payable sat on the edge of her desk and said people were worried. A former manager rang her mobile and said that this kind of thing has a way of following a person. By Friday five more people had stopped by or messaged, all kind. When Nadia rang the ethics line, she was told nothing had been shared. When she asked the friend who had said anything, the friend said nobody had, she just cared. In the second week a colleague asked whether she had thought about what this did to the floor, and another asked whether it was worth it. Nobody ever said the word report. On the Thursday Nadia began writing a note to take it back. The head of finance, Graham Varton, told a friend over drinks that it had all stayed very friendly, and that this was the point.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The person may begin to wonder who knows, and to look at friendly faces with suspicion they do not want. Over time, the person may feel guilt for the strain on colleagues, and doubt whether the report was worth it.

They may stop eating lunch with others, rehearse refusals, or lose sleep. They may be tempted to explain themselves, which gives the pressure more material. They may also feel isolated by the fact that nobody did anything specific, and that the institution they trusted to protect them may have been the first place the information moved.

Institutional Echo

Confidential reporting channels depend on the discipline of the people who handle them. When case information passes through several hands, such as intake staff, investigators, line managers and legal, the chance that it will travel increases. Where access logs for a case file are not available to the reporter, there may be no way to check.

Where "need to know" is defined informally, a manager asked to "support the team through a sensitive time" may learn more than intended. Whether any particular disclosure breaches a duty or an anti-retaliation rule depends on jurisdiction and facts. The honest reading is that leaks can be accidental, and that colleagues may simply have heard a rumor. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully the timing of the first contact relative to the filing, who had access, and whether the contacts used shared language.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good team never makes trouble, and that loyalty to colleagues comes before loyalty to a process. Alongside it sits the belief that if something were really wrong, it would not need a hotline.

The social value being exploited is care for the people around us. The language that makes it sound normal includes "I'm just looking out for you," "is it worth it," and "let's not make this bigger." The public story is a close-knit company. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is a reporter who learns that confidentiality was a promise nobody could keep.

Observable Indicators

  1. Colleagues refer to your situation shortly after you filed, though you told no one.
  2. People who rarely contact you approach you in the same week.
  3. Different people use similar phrases, such as "is it worth it" or "let it go."
  4. None of the contacts names the report, but all point to its consequences.
  5. Contacts include former colleagues or people outside the reporting line.
  6. The messages emphasize the team's feelings, not the subject of the complaint.
  7. Compliance answers a question about disclosure with a general statement of confidentiality.
  8. The status of your case does not change while the social pressure increases.
  9. A friendly person offers help in exchange for dropping or softening the report.
  10. You find yourself considering withdrawal for reasons unrelated to the merits.
  11. Colleagues become cooler or more distant if you decline to discuss it.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • The hotline confirmation, case number, date and time of filing, and the confidentiality statement you were shown.
  • A dated, factual note of every contact after filing: who, when, where, and what they said, as close to verbatim as you can lawfully recall.
  • The date you first told anyone about the report, if ever.
  • Your written question to compliance about disclosure, and the reply.
  • Messages or calls from people who contacted you, preserved as they stand.
  • Names of anyone else who received similar approaches.
  • Any change in your assignments, access or treatment after the filing, with dates.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder's fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity "to be papered."
  • Deadline records: allocation, "final close," offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Colleagues often notice stress and care. Rumors circulate in offices for reasons unrelated to any report, and a leak can be accidental. A friend who asks whether you are all right may only be that.

Specific lookalikes include:

  • Sincere concern. One person asks once and respects your answer.
  • Unrelated rumor. The contacts predate your filing, or concern a different matter.
  • Accidental disclosure. A mistake in handling, acknowledged and corrected.
  • Legitimate process contact. An investigator contacting you through the proper channel.

Concern strengthens when: several people approach you within days of filing; their language is similar; the approach is about dropping the matter, not about you; and the institution cannot or will not say who had access.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or "not needed at this stage."
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or "cleanup of the cap table" serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as "just formalities."
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side's calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Chain Breakers

  • Keep a dated, factual log of every contact, written the same day, and apart from anything you send.
  • Use one neutral line with everyone: "I'm not able to discuss it, but thank you for checking in."
  • Do not confide in the people who approach you, however kind. Treat each contact as information.
  • Ask compliance in writing who has had access to your report and whether there has been any disclosure outside the review, and keep their answer.
  • Ask for the confidentiality policy and the process to raise a suspected breach.
  • Find an independent adviser with no tie to the company before deciding to withdraw, narrow or continue.
  • Do not confront the suspected source or accuse colleagues. You may be wrong, and it can create exposure.
  • Do not withdraw while under pressure. Take time and take advice first.
  • Tell one trusted person outside the company, such as a friend or relative, so you are not carrying it alone.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A colleague refers to your report soon after filing; two or more people deliver similar messages; compliance gives only a general answer on disclosure.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    Proxy contacts continue or intensify; your assignments, ratings or access change after the filing; the investigation focuses on you more than the complaint; or the report appears to have reached the person it concerns. Relevant routes may include your own attorney, an independent director or audit committee, a company ombuds or independent compliance officer, an employment regulator or whistleblower channel, and a professional licensing body where relevant.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Threats, intimidation, stalking, blackmail, a suggestion of harm to you or your family, fraud in progress, or self-harm statements by you or anyone involved. Prioritize safety and emergency support, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call ("To confirm what we agreed today..."). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
  • Mediation or arbitration services, where a contract points to them or both sides agree.
  • A professional licensing or disciplinary body for lawyers, accountants, or registered financial advisers, where the person involved holds such a license.
  • A securities, financial services or investment regulator, where an offering, allocation or finder's arrangement is in question.
  • An employment or labor regulator, or a whistleblower channel, where retaliation or a compliance concern is involved.
  • A consumer or small-business protection authority, where an advisory or consulting service has been sold to you.
  • A family office principal's independent fiduciary, trust counsel, or trusted family advisor who has no stake in the engagement.
  • A therapist, peer support group or founder community for the personal toll, which is separate from the legal and commercial questions.

Do not make public accusations before you have evidence and advice. Statements in group channels, to investors or to references can create their own exposure, including defamation risk.

URGENT — safety first. Threats, blackmail, extortion, stalking, intimidation, fraud in progress, physical danger, self-harm statements by anyone involved, abuse of a dependent person (including an elderly or vulnerable family office principal), child or sexual exploitation, illegal confinement, or severe retaliation. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact local emergency services, crisis lines, and qualified counsel or law enforcement as appropriate. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence. Tell someone you trust right away. Where self-harm is mentioned by you or by someone else, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately.
