Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the tool you depend on can change its rules whenever it likes and you must accept, and a sense that leaving has become too costly to even consider.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • A habit of putting up with worse terms and higher prices, because moving everything elsewhere feels impossible.
  • An automatic sentence, "we're stuck with it now," said about a tool that once arrived as a gift.
  • A pull to accept the next free offer without asking what it will cost to leave later.
A point in a life
The middle years of a career.
Where it happens
A mid-sized professional services firm.

You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You're an IT director at a mid-sized professional services firm, you're forty-five, and you are the person who said yes to the free one.

Six years ago, a partners' meeting room

The firm has maybe ninety people, a shared drive nobody trusts, a contact list living in four partners' personal spreadsheets, and a managing director who wants "one place where the client relationship lives." The room smells of toast and cold coffee, and the whiteboard still has last quarter's bids on it. The vendor's offer is a workspace suite with a built-in CRM, free for the first tier, no credit card, no procurement cycle.

You have run a thirty-day pilot. You have compared it, sincerely, against two paid options. Neither comes close at zero dollars. Nothing needs approving, so nobody asks for a security review or a legal one. You hand round a one-page recommendation, the partners nod, and it is the easiest decision of your career. One of them taps the table twice and says, "Good. Done." Your shoulders drop as you send it. That afternoon a welcome email arrives, with a cheerful subject line: Your workspace is ready.

Two years on, the open-plan floor

Then it grows, the way good tools do. The sales team asks for pipeline views, so the CRM module goes live. Finance wants approvals routed through it. The bid team moves their templates into the shared workspace. Someone connects the calendar, then the e-signature add-on, then the client portal. Each step is small, each is free or close to it, and each time you think, correctly, that this is just good use of a tool you already have. A consultant hired for process redesign draws the new operating model on the glass wall, and the boxes on the diagram carry the platform's own feature names.

By then every deal, every note, every proposal and every client contact history lives on the vendor's servers, in the vendor's formats. When you try to picture where it is, the only picture you get is a login screen. A partner asks where last spring's pitch went, and you say, "It's in there," and he nods as if that were an address.

Last year, your office

Last year you scoped a migration project and shelved it as "not now." The sheet is still in your drawer, printed, folded in three. Proprietary formats. Every integration rebuilt from scratch. Retraining for ninety-odd people. Weeks of parallel running, during which the bid team cannot stop bidding. The total is a number you do not take to the partners. You close the drawer with the sheet inside and tell yourself you will revisit it after the busy season.

A Tuesday in March, your office

An email arrives titled "Important updates to your agreement." Your finance lead forwards it with one line: "Can you look at this? Not sure who owns it." Forty-one pages. Page nine has a new schedule titled Data Portability and Export Services. Bulk export of workspace records is now a metered service, priced per record and per attachment gigabyte, with a minimum charge, available to customers "in good standing on a current paid plan." Plan pricing is revised upward from the first of the quarter. The free tier you started on was retired two years ago, in a message you do remember skimming. The updated terms take effect in thirty days, and continued use constitutes acceptance. You read that last clause three times.

You do the arithmetic on a legal pad, because you want to see it. Two hundred and some thousand records. Attachments. The number is not a catastrophe, and next to the sheet in your drawer it is small. You get the account manager on a call. He is friendly. He says they are "so grateful for the partnership," that the changes reflect investment in the platform, and that most customers find the new structure better value. You ask whether the export fee applies if you leave. The answer is warm and very long, and when it ends you realize it did not contain a yes or a no. Your pen has stopped moving.

Your finance lead puts her head round the door at five. "Should we be looking at other options?" you ask. "Other options for what?" she says, kindly. "It's just where we work." That evening you open the strategy deck from two years ago, the one you wrote. Slide four is titled "Platform as foundation." You remember being proud of that slide. You search the deck for the word "vendor," and it finds nothing. You close the laptop, and the sentence that comes is not about money. It is: when did we stop being able to say no?

Two years later, a Monday in January

Two years later it is a Monday in January, and the managing director is running the stand-up with the wall screen behind her. Last week's invoice is on it, bigger again, and you signed it on Friday without opening the schedule. At five to nine a free analytics add-on lands in your inbox with a green Accept button. You press it while standing up, on the way to the meeting, and do not scroll down.

"One place where the relationship lives," the managing director says, as she says most Mondays, and the room smiles. A new analyst, three weeks in, puts up her hand and asks whether anyone has ever priced a move to something else. You hear yourself say, "That's one for another year." Nobody looks at you for long.

You have stopped drafting the proposals you used to write, the ones that start with a blank page. Every idea you have begins with a question to the vendor's roadmap, and you notice you have not had one of your own since the autumn. At home your partner asks what you want from the year, and you say, "Quiet," and mean it. You are the person who found the better tool once. Now you are the person who renews it.

The Name

That'sThe Platform Lock-In.

Definition

The Platform Lock-In is the sequence where a free or low-cost platform, adopted without the scrutiny a paid purchase would receive, grows into the firm's operating infrastructure, accumulates years of its records on the provider's systems, and makes leaving expensive enough that later changes to price, terms and data access can land on a customer with no practical alternative. It can use Free Adoption, Ecosystem Embedding, Data Accumulation, Switching Cost Inflation, Terms Modification and Institutional Dependency Normalization to convert the ordinary attraction of free tools into lost leverage and the lost option to switch later. It is hard to detect because every step looks like good use of a tool already in hand, and the dependence shows only at the moment of trying to leave. A great deal of ordinary software has this shape with no one designing it; the question is which features are structural and which were engineered.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Free Adoption

    Idealize

    The zero-dollar offer: a free first tier with no credit card and no procurement cycle, so nothing needed approving and nobody asked for a security or legal review, and your one-page recommendation went through on a nod. This functions by removing the moments at which a firm would test price, exit rights and data ownership. Concern rises when the decision memo compares features but never asks what the terms say about export, termination or future pricing.

  1. 1

    Ecosystem Embedding

    Idealize

    The tool that kept spreading: the CRM module, approvals, bid templates, calendar, e-signature and client portal, then a consultant's operating model whose boxes carry the platform's own feature names. This functions by turning one replaceable tool into a web of dependencies, so each addition raises what would have to move together. Concern rises when process documents and job descriptions begin using the platform's feature names as the firm's own concepts.

  1. 1

    Data Accumulation

    Idealize

    Six years of memory elsewhere: every deal, note, proposal and client contact history since the first week, in the vendor's formats, on the vendor's servers. This functions by making the stored history the firm's most valuable and least portable asset. Concern rises when nobody has tested a full export, or the export drops attachments, relationships or history.

  1. 1

    Switching Cost Inflation

    Idealize

    The sheet in the drawer: the migration project marked "not now," with proprietary formats, every integration rebuilt, retraining, and weeks of parallel running while the bid team cannot stop bidding. This functions by raising the price of the alternative until staying looks rational even as terms worsen. Concern rises when the cost of leaving is not discoverable until you ask, or export is priced as a premium service.

  1. 1

    Terms Modification

    Idealize

    The email about your agreement: forty-one pages, export now metered per record and per attachment gigabyte, plan pricing raised, and new terms taking effect in thirty days with continued use counted as acceptance. This functions by letting the provider move the arrangement at its own pace once the customer's alternatives have narrowed. Concern rises when changes cluster after major embedding milestones or the notice period is shorter than any realistic review or migration.

  1. 1

    Institutional Dependency Normalization

    Idealize

    The word nobody says: slide four of your strategy deck reads "Platform as foundation," and nobody, you included, has called the platform a vendor in years; it is just where the firm lives. This functions by moving the question from "should we be here?" to "how do we make the best of being here?", which closes the exit conversation before it begins. Concern rises when raising an exit plan is treated as disloyal or unrealistic.

Why This Order

Free Adoption has to come first because it is the only step cheap enough to skip scrutiny. A tool that costs nothing is approved by whoever needs it, and the review that would ask about export rights, price caps and termination never happens. Every later step rests on that missing early comparison. It has to come before the extra connections, because every later one is judged against a tool already installed at no cost, with no security or legal review behind it.

Picture a neighbor offering to keep your bike in their shed, no charge. You never think to ask about house rules, because nothing was agreed. Flip it: if your bike, your ladder and your garden chairs had each ended up in the neighbor's shed one at a time before any offer was made, you would stop and ask whose shed it now was.

Ecosystem Embedding turns a decision into a habit. A pilot is reversible; an operating model built from the tool's own parts is not, and because each addition is small and sensible, no single approval triggers a review. It has to come before the records piling up, because every proposal and client note only goes into the workspace once the bid team and finance already work inside it daily.

Think of a new relationship where your toothbrush, your books and your post slowly end up at their flat. None of it was a decision. Flip it: if years of your letters and diaries already sat at their flat while your toothbrush was still at home, you would see at once how much of you was there, and ask why.

Data Accumulation follows because embedded use produces records, and records make leaving expensive without any clause saying so. The firm builds the lever itself, one note at a time. It has to come before the migration sheet, because the rebuilding and retraining on it only add up to so much once the firm's deals and client histories sit in the vendor's formats.

Think of ten years of photos in one phone app. No rule traps them there, but every new photo makes leaving heavier. Flip it: if the app saved your pictures in a format no other app could open from the very first photo, you would notice on day one, with little at stake, and could still choose another.

Switching Cost Inflation is where the structure becomes visible to anyone who looks, but by then what is being switched away from is the firm's memory. Part of the cost is structural, since proprietary formats and rebuilt integrations are expensive by nature, and part is a choice, because export mechanics and their pricing are decisions someone made. It has to come before the agreement update, because a charge on export is only safe to add once your own sheet has shown leaving is too big to take to the partners.

Picture wanting to move house and finding every box is a size no other van will take. Flip it: if the van company raised its price while your boxes were still empty and could be packed to suit any van, you would simply ring another company and pack to its sizes.

Terms Modification is safe for a provider only after this point: a price change imposed on a customer who can walk costs the provider the customer, while the same change on a customer who cannot costs nothing. It has to come before your finance lead's answer, because the platform only sounds like simply where everyone works once the new export schedule has made leaving a price nobody wants to pay.

Picture a landlord raising the rent after you have unpacked and enrolled the children at the local school. "It's better value," they say, and you have nowhere to take the argument. Flip it: if the landlord kept saying nobody here ever moves out, and only then raised the rent, you would notice the talk had settled you in, and ask who gains.

Institutional Dependency Normalization closes the loop and supplies the plausible deniability. Once the platform is "where we work," no one owns the dependence and the question of whether the firm could leave feels like a question about the firm's identity. It has to come last, because it needs everything before it: a platform nobody calls a vendor, forty-one pages of terms to accept, and a sheet in the drawer showing leaving is out of reach.

Picture a family that says "we're a Saturday-at-the-same-café family." Nobody remembers choosing it, and suggesting somewhere else feels like disloyalty. Flip it: if the family was already calling itself a Saturday-at-the-same-café family, and only then did the café raise its prices, you would see the label had come first and ask whether anyone chose it.

The conversion point of the combo is the first time terms move after exit has been priced out of reach. What weakens the sequence is anything that restores the comparison: a tested export, an exit clause negotiated early, a second system kept alive for one critical function, a yearly review that asks the vendor question aloud.

Why the System Produces This Order

A software platform is built around a few structural facts. A free first tier removes the one gate that normally slows a purchase, since nothing needs a budget line. The vendor earns its money later, from renewals and add-ons, so its business grows with every feature a customer adopts. Records live in the formats the vendor chose, and agreements can be revised by notice, with continued use counting as acceptance. A small firm, meanwhile, has one IT lead, a lean budget and no time for a second review.

Free Adoption comes first because zero cost is the only price that gets a decision made in a month. Procurement, security and legal reviews are triggered by spending, and a free sign-up spends nothing. Nobody skips the review; it is never asked for.

Ecosystem Embedding comes second because the vendor's pricing depends on use spreading. Each new connection is small and sensible, and each is offered at little or no cost, so no single one reaches the threshold that would prompt a second look.

Data Accumulation comes third because every working feature produces records. Deals, notes and proposals are saved where the work is done, and the work is being done inside the platform. Nobody sets out to build a store; it fills as the firm does its job.

Switching Cost Inflation comes fourth because the size of the store and the number of integrations only become visible when someone scopes a move. Some of the cost is natural, since proprietary formats and rebuilt connections are expensive. Some is a design choice, because export is a feature that was built and priced by someone.

Terms Modification comes fifth because a change that would send a customer elsewhere costs the vendor nothing once the customer cannot go. The same notice sent to a customer on day thirty would have been read very differently.

Institutional Dependency Normalization comes last because, once the cost is settled, the firm needs a way to live with it. Calling the platform where we work stops the question from being asked, and the question starts to sound like a challenge to the firm's identity.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a gift that skips the checks, then a habit, then a store, then a bill for leaving, then new terms, then a story that makes it all ordinary. What it turns the person in the middle into is someone who maintains the platform that was once a choice.

On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A free tier, a renewal target and a form agreement did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a client. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Odette Farrow ran operations at a small logistics firm when a consultant, Calvin Yates, offered her a reporting dashboard at no charge. There was no contract, only a sign-up page, and she approved it herself that afternoon. Within a few weeks he had linked it to her rota, then her invoicing, then the supplier portal, each time saying it would take five minutes and cost nothing. By spring her drivers' hours, her invoices and her customer notes were all stored in his system, in a layout her old software could not read.

When she asked what a move would involve, he sent a careful list of what would have to be rebuilt and retrained, and the total made her put it away. In June a letter came saying that exporting her records would now be charged, and that the new rates began in thirty days. Calvin rang to say how much they valued working with her firm, and that most clients found the new rates good value. Her staff had started calling it "the system," and when she suggested trying another, her deputy asked, "Why would we do that? It's where everything is."

Calvin was never confused about what he was doing. He told a colleague that the free month was never about the month.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The person who chose the tool may begin to feel the dependence is their personal responsibility, because they signed off on it. They may find themselves defending the platform in meetings, partly out of habit and partly because criticizing it means criticizing their own judgment. Over time they may stop mentioning exit options, having met impatience or a project nobody can fund.

Institutional Echo

Free and low tiers that convert to paid plans as usage grows are a widely used commercial model. Terms letting a provider change pricing or conditions by notice are common in standard-form software agreements, though what any agreement allows, and whether it binds a given customer, depends on the text and the jurisdiction. Limited or separately priced export can function as a switching cost whether or not it was intended as one.

Inside the firm, procurement thresholds that exempt zero-cost tools let the dependency grow with no decision point. Implementation partners paid to build on a platform may have reasons to deepen its use, which is worth asking about and not assuming.

The honest reading is often that providers run ordinary businesses, portability is genuinely hard, and fees reflect real cost. Intent would require additional evidence: whether export was tested and promised at adoption, whether changes landed after lock-in across many customers, and whether comparable customers received different terms.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that free is a gift and that the market always offers somewhere else to go. Around it sits an admiration for pragmatism: a good operator uses the best available tool and does not waste money.

A second story says infrastructure is neutral: "the cloud" and "the platform" sound like weather, so a change in terms feels like an event and not a negotiation. The value exploited is a good one: the instinct to use what works. Nothing here argues against adopting useful software. The public story is a firm that moved fast and did more for less. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a firm that can no longer say no.

Observable Indicators

  1. A free or low-cost tier was adopted without a documented comparison of exit terms, data ownership or price-change rights.
  2. The platform's feature names appear in the firm's process documents and training as though they were the firm's own concepts.
  3. A full export has never been run, or was run and omitted attachments, relationships, history or custom fields.
  4. Export or data portability is offered as a separately priced service, with minimums or plan conditions.
  5. Terms or pricing changes arrive by notice, with continued use treated as acceptance, in a window shorter than a realistic review or migration.
  6. Changes cluster after embedding milestones such as a CRM roll-out or client portal.
  7. A free tier was retired through a general mailing and not a direct conversation.
  8. No one can say who owns the contract, the renewal date or the notice window.
  9. A question about exit gets a long, warm answer that says neither yes nor no.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • The original adoption memo, pilot notes and any comparison of alternatives, with dates and approvers.
  • The agreement, pricing page and export policy as they stood at adoption, and every later version, kept as received with the date of notice.
  • Each "important updates" notice, with how it reached the firm and when it was read.
  • Results of any test export, noting what was and was not included and the formats produced.
  • Your questions to the provider about export, exit and pricing, with the replies.
  • An internal migration cost estimate with assumptions, kept separate from anything sent to the vendor.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder's fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity "to be papered."
  • Deadline records: allocation, "final close," offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Real platforms legitimately integrate, grow with a firm and charge for data services that cost money to provide, and deep use of a good tool is often exactly the right call.

The discriminator is whether exit stayed testable and affordable while the firm became more embedded. A benign arrangement has a documented export, a stable or capped price path, proportionate notice and a provider that answers the exit question plainly.

Other benign lookalikes:

  • Genuine cost recovery. Large exports consume real compute and support.
  • Legitimate tier restructuring. Free tiers are retired for sustainability, ideally with a clear migration path.
  • Chosen standardization. A firm may knowingly run everything on one platform for security and consistency, and review that choice regularly.

Concern strengthens when: export cannot be tested or is priced as a premium; terms change soon after major embedding milestones; and the provider will not say plainly whether leaving is possible on the stated terms.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or "not needed at this stage."
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or "cleanup of the cap table" serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as "just formalities."
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side's calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Recognition Cue Cards

Spot it

The Export Test

Ask"Has anyone run a full export of our data and opened it somewhere else?"

Concern rises ifnobody has tried, or the export drops attachments, relationships or history.

Spot it

The Exit Price Test

Ask"What would leaving cost in money and weeks, and how much of that is the provider's own fee?"

Concern rises ifthe answer is unknown, or the provider's fee is the largest part.

Spot it

The Notice Test

Ask"Is the notice period long enough to review changes with counsel and plan a migration?"

Concern rises ifthe review window is shorter than the migration window.

Spot it

The Owner Test

Ask"Who owns this contract, and who approved each module we added?"

Concern rises ifnobody can say, or everyone assumes someone else does.

Chain Breakers

  • Run a test export now. Pull a representative slice, open it elsewhere, and write down exactly what is missing.
  • Name an owner for the contract, with authority over renewal dates, notice windows and escalation to a partner.
  • Read the notice against the prior terms with counsel or a qualified adviser, and list what changed, what it may cost and the deadline.
  • Ask the exit question in writing: "If we terminated on current terms, what is the export format, timeline and total cost?" A vague reply is itself information.
  • Raise export rights, price caps and notice periods before renewal, while the provider still wants a long relationship.
  • Keep one credible alternative for a critical function, or a mirrored copy of core records in a neutral format.
  • Do not withhold payment or access systems outside your authority as protest.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A terms notice with a short window; a tested export found incomplete; a vague reply to the exit question; no named internal owner.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    New fees or conditions on export or data access; changes that appear to affect data ownership or the confidentiality of client information; a notice period shorter than a realistic migration; a provider declining to say in writing whether data can be retrieved. Relevant routes may include the firm's counsel, an independent technology or procurement adviser, the insurer or compliance function, a data-protection or consumer authority, and the contract's dispute process.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Data access cut or threatened as leverage; threats or payment demands tied to release of records; suspected unauthorized access to client information; or anyone describing self-harm under the pressure. Prioritize safety and emergency support, use the firm's incident process and qualified counsel, and do not confront, negotiate under threat, or access systems you are not authorized to use.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call ("To confirm what we agreed today..."). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
  • Mediation or arbitration services, where a contract points to them or both sides agree.
  • A professional licensing or disciplinary body for lawyers, accountants, or registered financial advisers, where the person involved holds such a license.
  • A securities, financial services or investment regulator, where an offering, allocation or finder's arrangement is in question.
  • An employment or labor regulator, or a whistleblower channel, where retaliation or a compliance concern is involved.
  • A consumer or small-business protection authority, where an advisory or consulting service has been sold to you.
  • A family office principal's independent fiduciary, trust counsel, or trusted family advisor who has no stake in the engagement.
  • A therapist, peer support group or founder community for the personal toll, which is separate from the legal and commercial questions.

Do not make public accusations before you have evidence and advice. Statements in group channels, to investors or to references can create their own exposure, including defamation risk.

URGENT — safety first. Threats, blackmail, extortion, stalking, intimidation, fraud in progress, physical danger, self-harm statements by anyone involved, abuse of a dependent person (including an elderly or vulnerable family office principal), child or sexual exploitation, illegal confinement, or severe retaliation. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact local emergency services, crisis lines, and qualified counsel or law enforcement as appropriate. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence. Tell someone you trust right away. Where self-harm is mentioned by you or by someone else, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately.

Safer Response Language

  • "Before we accept the updated terms, can you confirm in writing what a full export includes, in what format, and at what cost?"

  • "Could you send a redline against the previous agreement so we can review the changes properly?"

  • "Our review needs more than thirty days. Can the effective date move, or can we agree a transition period?"

  • "Who on your side owns our data portability question, and when can we expect a written reply?"

  • "Is there a price cap or notice commitment we can agree for the next three years?"

  • "Please confirm under which clause the export fee applies if we terminate during the term."
