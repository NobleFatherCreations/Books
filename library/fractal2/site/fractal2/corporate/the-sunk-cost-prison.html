Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that what you have already put in is a reason to keep going, and a tired sense that trying to change course has never worked and never will.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • A habit of staying with projects, plans or people because of the years behind them, not the ones ahead.
  • A pull to trust the next promise each time the finish line moves, hoping that this time you will finally arrive.
  • An automatic sentence, “there’s no point trying, it won’t change anything,” that arrives before you have tried.
A point in a life
Midlife, nine years into running a company.
Where it happens
A mid-sized company and its strategic advisor.

You won’t be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You’re forty-eight, the chief executive of a company you have run for nine years, and four months ago you brought in a strategic advisor. The agreement has a deliverables list on page three: operational restructuring, a market analysis, an investor pipeline, a quarterly execution roadmap. You can recite it. There is a folder on your second monitor for what he has delivered. It holds a calendar export and a note called “brainstorm 2.”

You have not been careless. You moved your Tuesday leadership meeting to fit his calls. You introduced him to your two biggest customers and a former board member. You told your team, in a staff meeting, that he was going to help you see the next two years.

A Tuesday in May, the small conference room

The whiteboard is full again. Arrows, circles, the word “flywheel” underlined twice. It is the end of another three-hour session, and the room smells of marker pens and the sandwiches nobody finished. Out in the corridor a vacuum cleaner starts, stops, starts. Forty hours of this so far, good conversation, and no document.

You say the word “ending” for the first time. Lightly, as a question.

He does not argue. He counts on his fingers. “Forty hours. Your two best customers. Your old board member’s time. Walk away now and all of that was for nothing.”

You look at his fingers. You think of your customers’ faces when you introduced him. You say you were only thinking aloud.

After he leaves you stay in the room and open the agreement on your laptop, because you want to know where you stand. It renews automatically for twelve months unless you give sixty days’ written notice. The retainer is described as “guaranteed,” and you are not sure whether that means owed in full even if you end it. Termination for cause needs the cause documented, and the scope is vague enough that you cannot picture what a failure would look like on paper. You signed every page. You initialed the renewal clause yourself, in March, in a hurry, between two calls.

The one firm thing you do that afternoon is ask finance to hold back the second retainer installment. You feel slightly ashamed of it, as if you had hidden his coat.

The end of June, your office

The quarterly review is in your office, at the round table by the window. He is warm and unhurried. He has brought pastries, and the box sits open between you with one left in it.

“I know it’s been slower than you hoped,” he says. “The foundation is solid. Next quarter is when it clicks.”

You ask about the investor pipeline. “That’s about trust,” he says. “I’m warming three conversations. The paper comes after.” You nod. You notice that you are nodding.

You ask where the roadmap is. He tilts his head. “The sessions are the roadmap. You can’t put what we’ve been building into a deck. Not yet.”

The agreement is open on your monitor. Page three. It says roadmap. You do not point at it. Over the next weeks, at the same round table, the market analysis becomes “embedded in our conversations.” The restructuring becomes “a mindset shift.” Each time, you leave feeling that you have missed a subtlety, and that perhaps the fault is in how you read the list.

Late July, your office

At a session in late July, at that round table, you say the word “dates” out loud. He sets down his coffee.

“I’d hate for this to come apart now, with everything we’ve built. After all the time we’ve both put in. I’ve given this my evenings, and I’d hate to think it was wasted. And you told your team I’d help them see the next two years. I’d hate for them to watch that fall over.” Then, quieter: “And if it ends badly now, you’re the one who explains the retainer to your board.”

You cannot tell which part to answer. The team, his evenings, the board. They arrive as one thing. You say, “Of course. Let’s keep going.”

October, your office, before anyone is in

You stopped asking where the dates are sometime in September. You know what the question brings: a new word for the same list, and the sense that you misread it. You stopped opening the folder on Fridays. When he says “next quarter,” you nod before he finishes.

On a Wednesday in October, at seven in the morning, you open the agreement to check something else and see the notice clause. You count back on the calendar. The sixty-day window for the renewal closed eleven days ago, while you were waiting for next quarter.

You do not feel angry. You feel almost nothing. You close the file.

The following spring, the leadership meeting

A year later the advisor is still on the calendar, and you have stopped thinking of that as a decision.

At the Tuesday meeting your head of product proposes closing a small service line that has lost money for three years. You hear yourself say, “We built that line with our own hands. Let’s give it one more year.” Nobody argues. You notice that nobody argues with you much anymore.

A new software vendor says the integration will be live “by summer, at the latest.” It was going to be live by Christmas. You feel the old lift in your chest anyway.

Then your operations lead puts up a slide about changing how invoices are approved. It is a good slide. Before she reaches the second bullet you say, gently, “Leave it. Ideas like that get buried.” She closes her laptop. You see it land on her face, and you recognize the look, because you have worn it.

Your board chair asked once whether the advisor was “earning his keep.” You told him these things take time. You heard yourself say it, and it sounded like someone else’s sentence. Now it just sounds like you.

You wake at five most mornings and recite page three until it is light. The folder on your second monitor is still called “brainstorm 2.” You spent a year paying for next quarter, and somewhere in it you stopped expecting your own effort to count.

The Name

That’s The Sunk Cost Prison.

Definition

The Sunk Cost Prison is the sequence where prior investment becomes the argument for further investment, using Sunk Cost Exploitation, Commitment Traps, Future Faking, Moving Goalposts, FOG, and Learned Helplessness to convert a natural aversion to writing off the past into the loss of clear-eyed evaluation of the future. It is hard to detect because complex strategy work genuinely takes time, scope does evolve, and an unproductive engagement is not necessarily a designed one. The concern is not slow delivery; it is delivery that is continuously redefined while the cost of leaving rises.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Sunk Cost Exploitation

    Hook

    The tally read back to you: forty hours, your two best customers, a former board member and your Tuesday meeting are counted, and he says that walking away now makes all of it nothing. This functions by making past investment the reason to continue, whether or not it produced anything. Concern rises when the main argument for staying is what has already been spent.

  2. 2

    Commitment Traps

    Hook

    Clauses that bind you in writing: automatic twelve-month renewal unless sixty days’ written notice, a “guaranteed” retainer, and termination for cause that you must document. This functions by pricing exit above continuation. Concern rises when exit terms were not flagged or discussed when the agreement was signed.

  3. 3

    Future Faking

    Hook

    A hope with no paper behind it: in the meeting he says “Next quarter is when it clicks” and “I’m warming three conversations. The paper comes after,” and you nod. This functions by keeping the payoff just out of reach so that hope substitutes for delivery. Concern rises when promised dates keep moving without a written plan.

  4. 4

    Moving Goalposts

    Devalue

    A list whose lines keep changing meaning: asked for the roadmap, he says “the sessions are the roadmap,” and the market analysis becomes “embedded in our conversations” and the restructuring “a mindset shift.” This functions by redefining missed deliverables as achieved. Concern rises when the definition of success changes after the deadline.

  5. 5

    FOG

    Several stages

    Three pulls at once: at the word “dates” he says he’d hate to think his evenings were wasted, recalls your promise to your team about the next two years, and warns you’d be explaining the retainer to your board. This functions by making a simple evaluation emotionally heavy. Concern rises when you cannot compare scope with output without feeling disloyal.

  6. 6

    Learned Helplessness

    Several stages

    A habit of not asking: you stop asking where the dates are, stop opening the folder, nod at “next quarter,” and let the sixty-day window pass unnoticed. This functions as the end state, in which the person stops looking for the door. Concern rises when you notice you have stopped asking.

Why This Order

Sunk Cost Exploitation comes first because it is already there. Four months of time and trust exist whether or not the advisor does anything, and the pattern simply makes them feel heavier than they are. It has to come before the renewal clause you read afterward, because you only look for the notice period once you have been told that walking away wastes forty hours and your customers’ time.

You drive an hour to a restaurant and find it is awful. Someone says, “We came all this way,” and you sit down and order anyway. The drive is over either way; eating there will not bring it back. Flip it: you tick a small box on the restaurant’s booking page that adds a fee after noon, and only then drive an hour and find the food awful; you notice the fee and the drive are two separate things.

Commitment Traps must precede Future Faking, because the traps establish the cost of leaving. Once exit is expensive, even a thin hope becomes rational. It has to come before the promises of next quarter, because the sixty-day notice and the guaranteed retainer are what make waiting cheaper for you than leaving.

Picture ticking a small box when you join a club. A year later you find that the box renewed it, and the window to cancel closed last week. Flip it: before you tick the box when you join the club, someone promises it is easy to cancel any time, definitely; you ask for that in writing and notice the box says otherwise.

Future Faking must precede Moving Goalposts, because there has to be an original promise before it can be moved. It has to come before the redefined deliverables, because the page-three list only gets restated once you have been given next quarter and have agreed to wait for it.

A friend who owes you money says “next payday, definitely.” You stop mentioning it, because next payday is so close and asking feels rude. Flip it: your friend first says the money depends on one more thing, then on a different thing; only afterward do they say “next payday, definitely,” and you ask them to write the conditions down.

Moving Goalposts is where the contract meets the conversation: the deliverables list stays on page three, and the meaning of each line shifts. It has to come before the speech about his evenings, because you only say “dates” out loud once the list has been restated enough that you ask for something firm.

You are told the job needs one more form. When you bring it, you learn it needs a different one. Each time you were close, and each time the rule had moved. Flip it: before any form is mentioned, the person at the desk says they would hate for you to leave and have done so much for you; then comes one more form, and you notice you are answering feelings, not forms.

FOG is the weather that results from the combination, and it need not be induced separately. It has to come before you stop asking, because the speech about his evenings, your team and your board is what makes you answer “Of course” instead of repeating the question.

A friend says, “I’d hate for us to fall out, I’ve done so much for you, and I’d feel awful if it was all for nothing.” You cannot tell which of the three you are answering. Flip it: you stop mentioning the money after being waved off again and again; then your friend says “I’d hate for us to fall out,” and you notice you cannot bring yourself to answer any part of it.

Learned Helplessness cannot be installed directly; it is the cumulative product of enough cycles of hope, disappointment and renewed commitment inside a structure that penalises exit. It has to come last, because you only stop asking after the forty hours, the renewal clause, the promises, the softened list and the speech about his evenings have all been lived through in the same room.

Ask the school office about a form three times and get three different answers. By the fourth term you stop asking and just hope. Flip it: you ask the school office about a form, get different answers, and stop asking; then the clerk says they stayed late over your form and would feel terrible if it all came to nothing, and you cannot tell which part to answer.

The conversion point is the missed notice window, after which the structure holds without anyone pushing.

Whether this was deliberate or an overstretched advisor in over his head cannot be told from the sequence alone; intent would require additional evidence. What weakens it: a written deliverables schedule with dates, a calendar reminder for the notice window set on day one, and a scheduled review where fees are compared with delivered artifacts.

Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. An advisory engagement is billed monthly against a deliverables list written in broad terms, because the work is mostly conversation. The agreement renews itself unless someone gives notice by a date that is easy to forget. And the business runs in quarters, so there is always a next one to point to. A retainer, a notice clause and a calendar: those three facts of the setting produce the order below.

Sunk Cost Exploitation comes first because it is already there. Months of meetings, introductions to customers and staff time exist whether or not anything has been delivered, and any question about ending touches them at once.

Commitment Traps comes second because the agreement was signed before the work started, and its small print is read only when someone asks about leaving. A renewal clause initialed in a hurry in March is a fact of the setting, not a plan.

Future Faking comes third because the calendar offers a promise at no cost: next quarter. With exit already priced, a thin hope becomes the sensible choice, and each review can honestly say that the foundation is nearly done.

Moving Goalposts comes fourth because broad deliverables can be restated in conversation. A market analysis can become something embedded in sessions; a roadmap can become the sessions themselves. Nobody edits page three, so it never says anything is late.

FOG comes fifth because a question about dates from a leader who has introduced the advisor to customers and told the staff is a question with feelings already attached: hours given, a team watching, a board waiting. The three arrive together because all three are true at once.

Learned Helplessness comes last because it cannot be installed in one meeting. It builds from cycles of hope, a new word for the same list and a renewed agreement to wait, until the person stops raising the question at all.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs. The investment has to exist before it can be cited, the contract has to hold before the promise can be rolled forward, and the conversation has to blur before anyone stops asking. It turns a chief executive who wanted dates into one who waits for the next quarter without being asked.

On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A monthly retainer, an agreement that renews itself and a calendar that runs in quarters were enough, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newly appointed manager. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Priscilla Anwar had been running a regional logistics office for four months when a consultant, Linus Hargreave, was engaged to coach her. By the end of April they had held forty hours of sessions and produced no written plan. When she mentioned stopping, Linus listed the hours, the executives she had introduced to him and the favor of her time, and said that ending now would waste all of it. She read the engagement letter afterward and found it renewed itself unless she wrote sixty days ahead. At the next review he told her that the foundation was solid and that results would come next quarter, that three conversations were warming. When she asked where the written plan was, he said the sessions were the plan. Over the following weeks her staffing review became “embedded in discussions” and her process audit became “a mindset shift.” In July she asked for dates. Linus put his coffee down and said he would hate for it to fall apart after the evenings he had given, that her own staff were watching, and that her manager would ask her to explain the fees. She said, of course. By fall she had stopped asking, and the sixty-day date passed without anyone marking it. Linus told a friend that his best clients were the ones who stopped asking for dates.

From the inside, the person in the middle can’t tell the difference, and doesn’t need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The CEO may start to defend the engagement to their own team, because stopping it would mean admitting the earlier statements were wrong. Over time, the person may lose confidence in their own judgment about what counts as delivery. They may stop checking the folder, feel embarrassed to raise the matter with the board, and keep postponing the decision until a deadline passes.

Institutional Echo

Retainer agreements with automatic renewal and written-notice windows are common in professional services. Deliverables written at a high level can be redefined in practice, and an agile or exploratory engagement may legitimately evolve. Whether a guaranteed retainer is owed on termination, or what counts as cause, depends on the wording and on contract law that varies by place. Most often a poorly scoped engagement drifts, and nobody designed the trap. Intent would require additional evidence: whether deliverables were ever scheduled in writing, whether the same redefinition pattern appears with other clients, and whether notice windows are often missed.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that persistence is a virtue and quitting is failure. People are taught to see things through, that good things take time, and that abandoning a project wastes what was put in. All of this is partly true, which is why it works. The language that makes it sound normal is patience, trust the process, and “we’re building something.” The public story is a CEO who invested in her future. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is renewed commitment to something that has not delivered.

Observable Indicators

  1. Deliverables listed in the agreement have not been produced after months of fees.
  2. Sessions, calls or conversations are described as the deliverable.
  3. Promised dates move without a written revised plan.
  4. Requests for a specific artifact are answered with a reframing of what the artifact is.
  5. Automatic renewal and notice windows were not discussed at signing.
  6. The retainer is described as guaranteed without a clear termination mechanism.
  7. The advisor refers to time already invested when you question progress.
  8. Introductions made by you are used to show momentum.
  9. Notice windows pass without anyone mentioning them.
  10. You have stopped asking about the deliverables.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • The agreement and its deliverables list, with the date of signing and every draft.
  • A table you keep of each deliverable, its promised date, and what was delivered.
  • Invoices and payment records against deliverables received.
  • Calendar entries and notes for sessions, with hours spent.
  • Messages where a deliverable is redefined or a new date is given.
  • The notice date for automatic renewal, and any reminder you set.
  • Introductions you made on his behalf, with dates.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder’s fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity “to be papered.”
  • Deadline records: allocation, “final close,” offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Strategy work is often exploratory, scope does evolve, and a slow quarter does not mean a trap. The discriminator is whether delivery is ever defined in writing and measured against payment. A genuine engagement can show its artifacts, or agree a written change order. This pattern meets a request for artifacts with a redefinition.

Benign lookalikes:

  • Exploratory work. Early months may genuinely produce discussion before documents.
  • Agile scope. Change orders agreed in writing are normal.
  • Overstretched advisor. Missed milestones can come from incompetence.
  • Standard renewal terms. Automatic renewal is common and not suspect in itself.

Concern strengthens when: the same deliverable is redefined more than once; written requests for artifacts are answered with discussion; notice windows pass while promises are renewed.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or “not needed at this stage.”
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or “cleanup of the cap table” serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as “just formalities.”
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side’s calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Chain Breakers

  • Put the notice date in your calendar on day one with a thirty-day warning.
  • Ask for a written deliverables schedule with dates and acceptance criteria.
  • Hold a dated review comparing fees paid with artifacts delivered.
  • Ask the “fresh start” question: “If I were deciding today, would I sign?”
  • Use counsel to read the renewal and termination terms before the window closes.
  • Withhold payment only as the agreement allows, and take advice before doing so.
  • Tell a board member or peer, so the decision is not yours alone.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A deliverable is redefined after its date; the notice window is within sixty days; you cannot produce a deliverable list against payment.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    The advisor disputes termination, claims fees owed beyond the notice terms, or refuses to produce artifacts after written requests. Relevant routes may include a commercial attorney, an independent accountant to reconcile fees, mediation if the agreement provides for it, and a small-business protection authority.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Threats, blackmail or intimidation follow a termination request, or anyone speaks of self-harm. Contact local emergency services, crisis support and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person, and do not delete evidence.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call (“To confirm what we agreed today...”). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
  • Mediation or arbitration services, where a contract points to them or both sides agree.
  • A professional licensing or disciplinary body for lawyers, accountants, or registered financial advisers, where the person involved holds such a license.
  • A securities, financial services or investment regulator, where an offering, allocation or finder’s arrangement is in question.
  • An employment or labor regulator, or a whistleblower channel, where retaliation or a compliance concern is involved.
  • A consumer or small-business protection authority, where an advisory or consulting service has been sold to you.
  • A family office principal’s independent fiduciary, trust counsel, or trusted family advisor who has no stake in the engagement.
  • A therapist, peer support group or founder community for the personal toll, which is separate from the legal and commercial questions.

Do not make public accusations before you have evidence and advice. Statements in group channels, to investors or to references can create their own exposure, including defamation risk.

URGENT — safety first. Threats, blackmail, extortion, stalking, intimidation, fraud in progress, physical danger, self-harm statements by anyone involved, abuse of a dependent person (including an elderly or vulnerable family office principal), child or sexual exploitation, illegal confinement, or severe retaliation. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact local emergency services, crisis lines, and qualified counsel or law enforcement as appropriate. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence. Tell someone you trust right away. Where self-harm is mentioned by you or by someone else, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately.
