Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that other people can explain your life better than you can, and a reflex to treat your own feelings as signs of a problem rather than information.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

  • An automatic reply when asked what you think, "I don't really know anymore," even about your own life.
  • A difficulty telling the story of what happened to you in order, with gaps where your own view should be.
  • A pull toward people who confidently explain you to yourself, and a quiet relief at handing over the job.
A point in a life
Midlife, six years into a peer forum.
Where it happens
A closed forum of agency owners.

You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.

Cold Open

You're a managing director, running a twenty-five-person creative agency. You're forty-two, you built the place from a two-desk studio, and for six years you've belonged to a peer forum of eight owners who meet monthly, keep each other's confidences, and say things they can't say to their staff. The forum is run by a chair, a former agency owner a little older than you, warm and sharp, who once drove two hours to sit with you the week your largest client left. You've said, more than once, that it's the one place you can think out loud.

In March a deal collapsed. On the last call your side asked who would own the work product if the client brought it in-house. The client's counsel went quiet, then cold, and the deal died within the week. The call notes are in a folder on your laptop, dated, plain, a little dull: the question is typed on the agenda, and counsel asked for a day and never came back. In April you told the forum in confidence that you asked a reasonable question, that the other side was evasive, and that you'd lost sleep over whether you should have pushed less. The chair said he would reflect on it. You went home lighter.

June, a rented house

At the retreat, in a rented house with a long table and coffee in jugs, the chair opens the afternoon by saying he has written you a letter. It's a gift, he says, and he'd like to read it aloud, because the group has been part of the story. You say, lightly, that you weren't expecting that. He smiles. "That's rather the point."

He reads. Two pages, in the first person, with the grace of a man who has done this before. It says the deal did not collapse over work product. It says you did not actually ask a question on that call. It says you were warned in the autumn, which you don't remember, and that you have since "rewritten" the story, which he has noticed in the forum before. You go through the autumn in your head and find nothing, and the nothing is no comfort.

He asks you to say, in your own words, what happened on that call. You begin. After two sentences he nods and finishes it for you, and what he supplies is smoother than what you were saying. By the fourth sentence you're saying "yes, that's it" about a call you were on and he was not.

The letter goes on. It says you have a long-standing pattern of backing away from closeness at the moment of commitment. It says you "went into a protective posture" and "performed a concern," that your sensitivity to control is "a wound from earlier in your life," and that your account of the other side being evasive is you putting your own fear onto them. It ends with love: he's only saying this because he believes in you. You look around the table. People are nodding. Someone's eyes are wet. A founder you've known for years, who sat in your kitchen one bad Christmas, says quietly that it was brave of you to hear that.

You start to say that you have the call notes, that the question was on the agenda, that counsel asked for a day and never came back. The chair raises a gentle hand. He says he can hear that you're not in a good place to take this in, and that perhaps what matters now is not the facts but what the facts are protecting. A woman who runs a consultancy across the table says, kindly, that your voice has gone up. You notice that it has. Your face burns.

That evening, the car

You drive home with the letter in your bag. At the second set of lights you try to tell yourself what happened, in order, and the order has moved. Was it the autumn conversation that went wrong? Did you actually ask the question? Which version did you tell the forum, the one in your notes or the one you remember? You can't say how you got to the retreat, except as a man being gently shown who he is.

Four months later

Four months later it's a Sunday evening, and your partner, at the kitchen table, asks what you remember about the call. You begin, "Well, the letter said," and you hear it. You try again from the start and lose the thread by the second sentence, and start again from the middle. In meetings you've stopped saying "I think." You say "apparently" and "I was told." When your head of studio asks whether the new brief feels right to you, you ask what she would do. At a conference last month a consultant you had just met told you, over coffee, what you were really afraid of, and you felt your shoulders come down. You thanked him. You haven't called the founder from that Christmas.

Your notes are open on the laptop, dated and plain. The letter is in your bag by the door. You have read it more times than you have read your own notes.

The Name

That's The Perspective Eraser.

Definition

The Perspective Eraser is the sequence where a trusted person's account of what happened gradually replaces your own, using Gaslighting, Perspecticide, Weaponized Therapy-Speak, Tone Policing and Collapse of Narrative Continuity to convert trust in the people who explain you to yourself into the loss of a coherent account of your own life. In this sector it concentrates in peer forums, executive coaching, mentoring and board relationships where personal and commercial matters are discussed together. It is hard to detect because it arrives as care, and because honest feedback can sound identical. What may be lost is confidence in one's own read of events, standing among peers, and a story one can act on. Intent would require additional evidence, and ordinary developmental feedback is common and legitimate.

Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

  1. 1

    Gaslighting

    Several stages

    The letter reframes the record: "you did not actually ask a question on that call," you were "warned in the autumn," and you have "rewritten" the story, though your dated call notes show the question and you remember no warning. This functions by contradicting the person's own memory with a confident alternative, so they turn to the speaker for the truth. Concern rises when a stated account contradicts dated notes or agendas, and the notes are not consulted.

  2. 2

    Perspecticide

    Confuse

    The chair has you tell the call in your own words, finishes your sentences with a smoother version after two, and by the fourth you are saying "yes, that's it" about a call you were on and he was not. This functions by repeatedly treating their reading of events as a symptom instead of a view, until they stop offering one. Concern rises when your account is described as a "story" while the other account is described as "the reflection."

  3. 3

    Weaponized Therapy-Speak

    Confuse

    The letter names your differences as wounds and projections: a "protective posture," a "performed" concern, sensitivity to control as "a wound from earlier in your life," and your account of evasive counsel as "a projection." This functions by moving a business disagreement into the person's psychology, where the facts cannot be argued. Concern rises when personal history, which you shared in confidence, is used to explain a commercial judgment.

  4. 4

    Tone Policing

    Devalue

    When you raise your notes, the chair says he can hear you are "activated," and a participant says your voice has gone up, and it has. This functions by making any objection proof of the diagnosis, so a protest confirms the label. Concern rises when a manner is raised whenever a specific fact is offered.

  5. 5

    Collapse of Narrative Continuity

    Several stages

    In the car you can no longer put the autumn, the question, the notes and the retreat in order, cannot say how you reached the retreat except as a man being shown who he is, and cannot read your own dated notes as your own. This functions by leaving the target with fragments, so a coherent counter-account cannot be built and any later account sounds unreliable. Concern rises when you cannot say in order what happened, and the other version is the only complete one.

Why This Order

Gaslighting has to come first because the sequence needs an opening in the person's trust in their own memory. It begins with something small and checkable, a date, a warning, whether a question was asked. If that opening is created, the rest does not have to be proved. It only has to be offered. It has to come before the chair finishing your account, because you only let him supply your sentences once you have lost your footing about the autumn and about the question.

A friend insists you told her you would be away that weekend. You are sure you did not. By Sunday you are scrolling back through your own messages to see. Flip it: if your friend had always told you what you "really" think before any mix-up about the weekend, you would trust your own messages and say so.

Perspecticide follows because it turns a single contested fact into a standing habit. When the person's view is repeatedly described as an account to be assessed and never as a view to be heard, they begin to defer in advance. It has to come before the letter's verdict on your past, because you have already handed him your account of the call, so you are in no position to argue with what he says it means.

Every time you give an opinion, your parent says, "That's one way to see it," and then tells you the right way. After years, you start asking "What should I think?" before you speak. Flip it: if your parent explained every opinion you gave as "just your mood from school" before any talk of the right way, you would say your view still stands.

Weaponized Therapy-Speak then supplies the vocabulary. It is the most effective step in this sector because it sounds caring, because it can be spoken in public, and because it is very hard to refute: a person who says they are not projecting sounds like they are. It has to come before the remark about your voice, because once the letter has named your doubts a wound, a raised voice can be cited as proof that the name fits.

Your sister says, "You're only upset because of what happened with Dad." Now a complaint about the dishes is about your childhood, and saying it isn't makes it more so. Flip it: if your sister had told you to "lower your voice" before you had said a word about the dishes, you would calmly raise the dishes again.

Tone Policing locks the door. Once the label is in place, every defense is evidence of the label, and the person's rising voice is cited as confirmation. It also changes the audience, because those watching see an upset person where the evidence says something else. It has to come before the drive home, because the table, watching your face burn, has settled on its account of you, and you leave with nobody's version but his.

In a meeting you say, "That number is wrong," and a colleague says, "Let's all stay calm." The room now looks at your face, not the number. Flip it: if the room had already retold what you meant by the number before you had finished speaking, you would write down your own point straight away.

Collapse of Narrative Continuity closes the loop. The person has been told what happened, what they were like, and what their feelings mean, and cannot tell any longer which parts came from them. The other account, by then the only complete one, stands. It also gives plausible deniability, because every step was framed as help. It has to come last, because by the time you are in the car you have heard the chair's account of the call, of you and of your feelings, and have nothing of your own to set beside it.

After a long family argument where everyone told you what you said and what you meant, you try to retell it that night and find holes where your own memory should be. Flip it: if you already had holes in your memory of the argument when someone told you to calm down, you would jot down what you remember and check it with someone you trust.

What would weaken the sequence: dated contemporaneous notes; a second reader outside the group; an agenda or minutes of the call in question; and a listener who asks, "What did you observe?" in place of "What does this say about you?"

Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. It falls out of what a closed peer forum is built to produce. Members hold each other's confidences, so the forum knows things about you that nobody else does. A chair holds the trust of the room and usually speaks last. And the meetings happen at a long table and a retreat, where what is said aloud counts and the notes in your own folder stay at home.

The step that makes you doubt your own account comes first because a forum has no record of its own. Nothing said in confidence is written down for everyone, so a small, checkable point, a date, a warning, whether a question was asked, can be put in doubt by anyone with standing. Once that opening exists, the rest only has to be offered.

Perspecticide comes second because a chair's role is to help members put their thoughts into words. Finishing a sentence for someone is an ordinary kindness, and when the finished version is smoother, the member agrees to it. Repeated, it turns into a habit of waiting to be told what you think.

Weaponized Therapy-Speak comes third because the forum's own language is the language of care. Words about wounds, protective postures and what the facts are protecting are the register of the room, they sound loving, and they cannot be answered without sounding defensive.

Tone Policing comes fourth because eight people are watching one face. Once the account has been labeled, a raised voice becomes the visible evidence, and the table, which cannot see the notes, sees an upset person.

Collapse of Narrative Continuity comes last because the member has by now been told what happened, what they are like and what their feelings mean. The only complete account in the room is the chair's, and when the member leaves, it is the one they carry.

Put it together and the order is the one a trusted, closed room needs: open a doubt, take over the telling, name the feeling, point at the voice, and leave a single account standing. What it turns the person into is someone who checks other people's version of their life before their own.

On Purpose

In the story you could not see whether anyone meant it. Here, someone does. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a junior colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Felix Armitage, a manager at a consulting firm, told his mentor, a partner named Odette Morwell, in confidence that he had lost a proposal after asking the client for a decision date. Odette said she would think about it. At the next away day she handed him a printed note and said she would like to read it to the team, as a gift. It said the proposal had not been lost over a date. It said Felix had not actually asked the client anything, and that she had warned him back in January, which he did not remember. When she asked him to describe the call, he began, and after two sentences she completed it for him, in smoother words, and he found himself agreeing. The note said his unease with senior clients was a wound from earlier in his life, and that blaming the client was him putting his own fear onto them. It closed by saying she was only writing it because she believed in him. Felix said he had the emails. Odette lifted a hand and said she could tell he wasn't ready to hear it. A director across the table said, kindly, that his voice had risen. He noticed it had. On the train home he tried to put the proposal in order, and the dates kept sliding.

Odette was never confused about what she was doing. She told a colleague she preferred her managers unsure of their own story.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

Individual Level

The person may begin to preface their own account with "I might be wrong." Over time, they may stop trusting their notes, apologize for emotion, and avoid raising the matter with anyone outside the group. They may re-read old emails to check what they believe. The person may begin to withdraw from decisions, or to seek the chair's reading of events before forming their own. They may feel grateful and diminished at once. Some report that the account they tell themselves has gaps they cannot fill. They may also lose sleep and become tearful, which can be mistaken for confirmation of the label.

Institutional Echo

Peer forums, executive coaching, mentoring programs and board relationships can operationalize this pattern without anyone planning it. Confidentiality norms mean there is rarely a neutral record, so the chair's account may become the account. Reflection letters, 360 summaries and coaching memos that mix commercial judgments with personal characterizations can turn a business disagreement into a description of a person. Reading such a letter aloud to a group adds social pressure to accept it. Where the facilitator is not a licensed clinician, psychological language carries authority without the safeguards that clinical practice involves. The honest reading is that many forum chairs and coaches act in good faith and sometimes give accurate, hard feedback. A pattern is more credible when the account diverges from the written record, recurs across members, and is delivered where it cannot be answered.

Civilizational Echo

The myth is that self-awareness is the highest professional virtue, and that anyone who resists feedback lacks it. Around it sits the idea that founders carry unexamined wounds that explain their decisions. The social value exploited is growth: the willingness to be told something hard by someone who loves you. The language that makes it sound normal is "I'm saying this because I care," "that's not about the facts," and "what is this protecting?" The public story is a tender, courageous group. The private loss is one person's ability to say what happened and be believed, including by themselves.

Observable Indicators

  1. A reflection or feedback document states that an event did not occur as the person recalls, without reference to the notes.
  2. The document attributes a business outcome to the person's character or history rather than to specific, observable actions.
  3. Private disclosures made in confidence reappear as explanations in a group setting.
  4. Clinical or psychological terms are used by someone who is not the person's clinician.
  5. The person's raised voice, emotion or objection is cited as evidence for the characterization.
  6. Specific facts offered in reply are set aside as "not the point."
  7. The group is asked to validate the characterization, and dissent is read as lack of insight.
  8. The person's account is called a "story" or a "narrative," while the other account is called a "reflection."
  9. The person begins to ask the chair, in place of their own notes, what happened.
  10. The person's own written account of events changes over successive versions.

Evidence To Preserve — Additions

  • The reflection letter, in your hands in any lawful form, and a dated note of what was read aloud.
  • Your contemporaneous call notes, agendas, and emails about the event discussed, with dates.
  • Forum charters, confidentiality agreements, and any rules about feedback and what may be read aloud.
  • Messages in which the chair or others state the characterization, and your replies.
  • A record of what you disclosed, to whom, and in what setting, written the same day.
  • Your own written timeline of the event, made before reading anyone else's account.
  • Names of attendees and what they said, noted by you shortly afterwards.
  • Counter-party emails or counsel messages that bear on what the other side actually said.

Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper is the relationship. Most of what matters sits in a few documents and in the gaps between them and what was said aloud. Keep, in dated and unaltered form where you lawfully hold them:

  • Engagement papers: signed engagement letters, master services agreements, advisory agreements, statements of work, scope appendices and change orders, and every draft and redline, including the versions you did not sign.
  • Money records: retainer schedules, success-fee and revenue-share provisions, invoices, payment records, and finder's fee waivers or acknowledgments.
  • Delivery records: deliverables received (dated and stored), kickoff and status decks, strategy memos, milestone and acceptance emails, and sign-off forms.
  • Capital and equity records: term sheets, side letters, subscription documents, limited partnership agreements, capital call notices, distribution statements, cap table versions with dates, option grant letters, board approvals, vesting schedules, and offer letters that mention equity "to be papered."
  • Deadline records: allocation, "final close," offer-expiry and signing-deadline emails, with timestamps and a note of who set the date and what diligence you asked for.
  • Employment records: separation drafts and release language, performance improvement plans, 360 feedback reports, calibration notes, performance reviews with dates, and HR complaints with responses and timelines.
  • Governance records: board decks and minutes you are entitled to hold, signing-authority lists, delegation documents, communications protocols, and access-change notices.
  • Conversation records: calendar invites and agendas, emails, texts, DMs, group chat and workspace channel statements (preserved by screenshot with date and channel visible), introducer emails, and your own written summaries of verbal conversations, sent the same day where possible.
  • Access records: data room indexes and access logs you can lawfully see, access requests and denials, and version history.
  • People and timeline: names of witnesses and of anyone else who received the same promise, and a dated timeline of commitments versus delivery.

A short, factual note to yourself written close to the event is worth more than a long account written later. Keep it separate from anything you send.

Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal. Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not authorized to access.

Recording, monitoring and confidentiality rules differ by jurisdiction and by agreement. A confidentiality clause may limit what you can share with others, but whether and how it applies to you needs qualified review.

False-Positive Check — Specific

Honest peer feedback can be painful, and real self-deception exists. A forum chair may be right that someone shut down a deal for reasons of their own. The discriminator is whether the account can be checked against the record and whether the person's reply is heard. Genuine developmental feedback cites specific, observable actions, welcomes correction and leaves the person's own account intact beside it. This pattern replaces the account and treats the reply as proof.

Other benign lookalikes:

  • Accurate hard feedback. The question on the call may in fact have been poorly timed.
  • Good-faith vocabulary. Some people use therapeutic words from their own experience without intent to label.
  • A genuine memory gap. A person may truly forget an earlier conversation.
  • A request for reflection. A chair may invite members to consider their role.
  • Real stress. A person may in fact be upset, and it may be appropriate to say so gently.

Concern strengthens when: the account contradicts dated notes and the notes are not looked at; personal history shared in confidence is used to explain a commercial decision; replies are labeled as symptoms; and the same approach is described by other members.

Universal Benign Explanations

Before reading a pattern into any situation, rule out the ordinary explanations. In this sector they usually sound like this:

  • Scope and change. An agile engagement can legitimately evolve, and change orders agreed in writing are normal. A vague scope may reflect an early-stage problem that is not yet well defined.
  • Weak delivery. A consultant, fractional executive or advisor may simply be overstretched, underqualified for the task, or unclear about priorities. A failed deliverable is not, on its own, a designed pattern.
  • Real market pressure. Allocations can be truly limited, rounds can close on real dates, and a leadership change can create a genuine signing window.
  • Standard commercial terms. Retainers, success fees, exclusivity tied to real consideration, notice periods and auto-renewal are common features of professional engagements, though levels and norms vary and should not be assumed universal.
  • Legal and administrative delay. Papering equity, cleaning up a cap table, and completing fund administration can legitimately take time, and outside counsel, administrators and accountants have their own queues.
  • Confidentiality and privilege. Duties to other clients, other investors, a fund, or an active investigation can genuinely limit what someone is able to tell you.
  • Disclosed monitoring and metrics. Monitoring of work systems under a disclosed policy, consistent scoring in a calibration process, and access controls on a data room are ordinary risk management and privacy protection.
  • Legitimate performance management. A performance improvement plan or critical feedback may reflect a real performance or communication problem. Founders and executives can also be abrupt or unfair, and feedback on tone may be sound.
  • Strategic disagreement. Two competent people can disagree about direction without either acting in bad faith.
  • Role overlap in small ecosystems. One person acting as friend, introducer, investor and coach can be normal and disclosed in a small market.
  • Personal disclosure. An advisor sharing a hardship, a marriage, a childhood or a faith may be sincere. It is observable behavior, not proof of motive.
  • Fair settlement terms. Mutual non-disparagement and releases in a settlement can be balanced and agreed with advice.
  • Ordinary human factors. Poor communication, disorganization, administrative overload, technology failure, cultural mismatch, poor training, crisis conditions, resource scarcity, human error, and clumsy but good-faith management.
  • Genuine mentorship or sponsorship, where autonomy and independence grow over time.

Intent would require additional evidence. A poor engagement can come from incompetence, overreach or changed circumstances without any design behind it.

Concern Strengthens When

The pattern becomes more worrying, though not proven, when several of these appear together or repeat:

  • The same kind of gap between promise and paper recurs across deliverables, fees, equity or terms.
  • Requests for written terms, scope definitions, fee calculations, cap table versions, waterfalls or data room access are resisted, delayed, or answered only verbally.
  • Outside review is discouraged: counsel, a second advisor, a co-investor, a co-founder, a peer, or the introducer is described as uninformed, biased, disloyal, or "not needed at this stage."
  • Written clarity is avoided. Commitments are made on calls and in person but not in follow-up emails, and summaries you send are not confirmed or are disputed.
  • Questions about fees, attribution or missed milestones trigger consequences, such as coldness, withdrawn access, reputational hints, or being called difficult.
  • Explanations shift, such as the reason for a delay changing each time, or "cleanup of the cap table" serving as a standing reason.
  • Rules are applied unequally. Others receive papered equity, access, or flexibility that you were told was not possible.
  • Leaving is punished. Notice, resignation, termination or withdrawal is met with guilt, delayed figures, notice disputes, or talk of references and reputation.
  • Witnesses, other founders, other employees or other investors describe a similar sequence independently.
  • Verbal promises contradict the documents, and the documents are described as "just formalities."
  • Dependency grows over time: more of your capital, strategy, operations, introductions, or revenue runs through one person.
  • Deadlines are arranged around the other side's calendar and leave no practical time for counsel or diligence.
  • Confidential information you shared is later used to shape conversations with you or about you.
  • Retaliation follows boundary-setting, such as asking for scope, declining an exclusivity, raising a compliance concern, or questioning a score.
  • Reputation is raised as a reason to stay quiet, with comments about a small industry, a bad reference, or what investors will hear.

Concern Weakens When

The picture becomes more reassuring when:

  • You are given real time to review, and a deadline moves when counsel asks for more time.
  • Terms are clear and in writing before work, payment or exclusivity begins, with benefits and obligations both specified.
  • Mistakes are acknowledged and corrected, including by the more senior or more trusted party.
  • Independent advice is encouraged, including from your own counsel, accountant, or other advisors, and a competing view is welcomed rather than discounted.
  • Policies and fee mechanics are applied consistently to everyone in a comparable position.
  • Saying no costs nothing. Declining an allocation, an exclusivity, a side letter or a scope item does not change how you are treated.
  • Exit is clean. Notice is acknowledged, handover is orderly, and figures arrive on time.
  • Accountability is accepted. Someone is named as responsible for the fee, waterfall, deliverable or decision, and answers follow-up questions.
  • Cap tables, waterfalls, access logs and methodology are available to the people they affect, within real confidentiality limits that are explained.
  • Oversight is transparent. Approvals, delegations and communication protocols are documented, chosen by the principal with full information, and reversible.
  • Others with comparable dealings describe similar, fair treatment.

None of these settle the question alone, and none replaces independent advice on a high-stakes agreement.

Recognition Cue Cards

Spot it

The Notes Test

Ask"Does what is being said about the call match the notes made at the time?"

Concern rises ifthe notes are set aside as unimportant, or nobody has looked at them.

Spot it

The Label Test

Ask"Is this describing what I did, or what I am?"

Concern rises ifthe document uses terms such as "pattern," "wound" or "projection" without naming a single observable action.

Spot it

The Reply Test

Ask"If I answer with a fact, is the fact discussed?"

Concern rises ifthe answer is treated as evidence of the label, or my tone is raised each time I offer one.

Spot it

The Story Test

Ask"Can I still tell, in order, what happened?"

Concern rises ifthe sequence has moved, and the only full version is someone else's.

Chain Breakers

  • Write your own timeline first, dated and plain, before reading anyone else's account of the event.
  • Separate observation from interpretation. "What did I do or say, specifically?" is a question the letter should be able to answer.
  • Ask for the letter in writing, and take time to read it away from the room.
  • Decline to respond in real time. "I would like to read this and reply in writing."
  • Show your notes to someone outside the group, such as an independent adviser, an old colleague, or a counsel, and ask them to read the facts and nothing else.
  • Keep one relationship outside the forum where you can say what happened out loud.
  • Ask what a different explanation would look like. Offer one, calmly, in writing.
  • Check confidentiality terms before deciding whether to leave the forum, and plan the exit with care.
  • Talk to a qualified professional about the personal toll, separately from the business question.
  • Do not make public accusations. Group statements can create their own exposure.

Escalation Triggers — Specific

  1. LOW→MODERATE

    A document or remark states something about an event that contradicts your notes; a personal disclosure is cited to explain a business decision; or your replies are described as symptoms.

  2. MODERATE→HIGH

    The characterization affects your standing, relationships or business; it is repeated to people outside the forum; or you cannot reconstruct the sequence of events and are losing confidence in your work. Consider an independent attorney, an adviser with no tie to the forum, a professional licensing body if the person holds a clinical license and has acted as your clinician, and an ombuds or board contact where one exists.

  3. HIGH→URGENT

    Any statement of self-harm by you or anyone involved, threats, blackmail using what you disclosed in confidence, or stalking or intimidation. Contact local emergency or crisis services immediately, and tell someone you trust. Do not confront the person or negotiate under threat.

Escalation Tiers For This Sector

The four tiers are defined above. Which bodies exist, what they can do, and whether any step is available to you depends on jurisdiction and on the agreements you have signed.

LOW — clarify and document. Ask for the scope, fee calculation, or equity status in writing. Send a short follow-up email summarizing each call ("To confirm what we agreed today..."). Keep your own dated timeline. Ask for the governing policy, the signing authority, and the process for changes. State boundaries calmly, such as needing time to review or wanting a second reader on any signature.

MODERATE — slow down and get independent help. Engage your own counsel or a qualified adviser who is not connected to the other party before signing, renewing, or agreeing to an exclusivity, side letter, release or waterfall. Tell a trusted peer, co-founder, board member or support person what is happening. Compare alternatives. Request cap table versions, data room access, or methodology in writing. Slow the timeline and decline to sign under a deadline you have not been able to test. Preserve records lawfully.

HIGH — appropriate outside support. Where money, equity, standing or career are materially at stake, or where documents and promises clearly diverge, consider the following kinds of bodies, chosen to fit the facts and jurisdiction:

  • A qualified attorney with relevant contract, corporate, securities or employment experience.
  • Independent board members, a lead director, an audit or compliance committee, or a company ombuds or compliance officer, where they exist and are independent of the person involved.
  • Internal HR or employee relations, and where relevant a union, works council or employee representative.
  • A forensic accountant or independent fund administrator or auditor, where fees, waterfalls or cap table entries are in question.
  • Mediation or arbitration services, where a contract points to them or both sides agree.
  • A professional licensing or disciplinary body for lawyers, accountants, or registered financial advisers, where the person involved holds such a license.
  • A securities, financial services or investment regulator, where an offering, allocation or finder's arrangement is in question.
  • An employment or labor regulator, or a whistleblower channel, where retaliation or a compliance concern is involved.
  • A consumer or small-business protection authority, where an advisory or consulting service has been sold to you.
  • A family office principal's independent fiduciary, trust counsel, or trusted family advisor who has no stake in the engagement.
  • A therapist, peer support group or founder community for the personal toll, which is separate from the legal and commercial questions.

Do not make public accusations before you have evidence and advice. Statements in group channels, to investors or to references can create their own exposure, including defamation risk.

URGENT — safety first. Threats, blackmail, extortion, stalking, intimidation, fraud in progress, physical danger, self-harm statements by anyone involved, abuse of a dependent person (including an elderly or vulnerable family office principal), child or sexual exploitation, illegal confinement, or severe retaliation. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact local emergency services, crisis lines, and qualified counsel or law enforcement as appropriate. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence. Tell someone you trust right away. Where self-harm is mentioned by you or by someone else, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately.
