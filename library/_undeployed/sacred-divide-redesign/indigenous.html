A day inside

Before you read

One ordinary day, told from inside. The person is a composite built from documented patterns, not a real individual. The point is to make the structure visible at human scale: phrases like "removal mechanism" or "giving record" are easy to read past, but a Tuesday organized around them is not.

Tanya · A Tuesday in berry season · A First Nation in northern Canada, and the town an hour south

Up at six, coffee, and she watches the light come over the water where her grandmother's grandmother's people watched it come over the water, and there is no sentence in English that gets this right so she has stopped trying.

She teaches language at the school. Eleven kids in her Tuesday group. Two of them have grandparents who were at the school in Fort Alexander and who do not speak the language because it was beaten out of them, and Tanya is teaching their grandchildren the words back. She would do this for free. She nearly does.

At ten she takes a call about the records. Fourth call this year. The archdiocese has responded to say the material is under review. It has been under review since 2019.

At noon she sees an ad on her phone: a wellness retreat, four hours south, non-Indigenous facilitator, $2,800 for four nights, using her nation's word for the ceremony in the headline. She screenshots it. She has a folder of these. The folder has forty-one screenshots in it.

In the afternoon a girl of about fifteen stays behind after class and doesn't say anything for a while, and then talks about her uncle, who is also a ceremonial helper, and who everyone respects, and who she doesn't want to be in the room with. Tanya listens. She knows exactly who. She knows there is no one to report to, because the nearest thing to a body is a circle of people who are all related to him, and because the last time someone raised something about a respected man it turned into a story about the person who raised it.

At night she sits on the step. She thinks: everything that nearly destroyed us came from outside, and that is the truest thing about my life. And then she thinks about a fifteen-year-old who cannot say a name out loud. And she understands that both of those are the same fight, that they have always been the same fight — protecting our children — and that almost nobody will let her say them in the same sentence.

If nothing felt familiar, ask whether that's because it isn't your life, or because the patterns have become normal to you.

Moments in the room

1883–1951 — The Potlatch and Sun Dance bans

Canada and the United States criminalised central ceremonies for decades. People were imprisoned and regalia confiscated; some of it sits in museums still.

Why it matters. This page's inversion of the codex's usual question. The apex here was imposed from outside, by statute, and its harms are the documented ones.

1996 and 2015 — The last school closes; the Commission reports

Canada's last residential school closed in 1996. In 2015 the Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented the system and named it cultural genocide, documenting more than 3,200 child deaths; the national memorial register listed 4,037 names in 2019 and added more than 1,100 in 2025, and potential unmarked graves have been identified since 2021.

Why it matters. The most thoroughly documented case in this codex of religious institutions operating a state programme against children — and the only page where the codex's usual accountability question points outward at churches and governments rather than inward.

1993 and after — The Declaration of War against Exploiters of Lakota Spirituality

Lakota leaders issued a formal declaration against non-Native sale of ceremony. It has been reissued and reaffirmed since, and remains routinely ignored by the retreat industry.

Why it matters. Where authority in this tradition actually spoke, in public, in its own name. That page's compel column defers to it — because the communities' own repudiations are the authority that matters.

Genealogy

Before you read

Rules with a history. Each card asks the same three questions: what was this rule originally for, has that reason expired, and who benefits from the rule continuing anyway?

Missionary and residential schooling

Origin. Church-run residential and boarding schools operating from the 1870s in Canada, the U.S., and Australia — the last closing within living memory. GOVERNMENT REPORT · Canada's TRC

What it was for. Stated as civilizing and Christianizing; in practice, the explicit destruction of language, ceremony, and family.

Why that reason expired. This is the codex's clearest case of religion used as a weapon by outsiders. Deaths, abuse, and unmarked graves are documented by national commissions. It has not expired in effect: the trauma, the language loss, and the child-welfare overrepresentation are present-tense.

Who benefits now. Historically: colonial states and the churches contracted to run the schools. Now: the institutions that have still not fully disclosed records or paid what they promised. GOVERNMENT REPORT

Criminalization of ceremony

Origin. Potlatch and Sun Dance bans, the U.S. 'Religious Crimes Code'; American Indian Religious Freedom Act only passed in 1978. OFFICIAL POLICY

What it was for. Suppression of political and economic structures that colonial administration could not control.

Why that reason expired. Legally, yes. But the interruption is why so much transmission is broken and so much authority is now unverifiable — which is precisely the vulnerability that outside appropriators exploit.

Who benefits now. The appropriation economy, which fills a vacuum that suppression created.

Unaccountable individual authority

Origin. Oral, kinship-based transmission in which the elder or healer is also relative, doctor, and community authority. PATTERN OBSERVED

What it was for. In an intact community, that concentration was checked by everyone knowing everyone — accountability was total precisely because the community was small and permanent.

Why that reason expired. Where colonization broke communities, dispersed families, and severed knowledge chains, the checks broke with them. What remains is authority without the community structure that used to regulate it — and abuse by a ceremonial figure is nearly impossible to report when he is also your uncle and the only healer for two hundred miles.

Who benefits now. Specific individuals, in specific communities. Naming this is not colonial; refusing to name it protects the same people the boarding schools protected.

'Plastic shaman' commercialization

Origin. Expanded from the 1960s counterculture onward; Lakota elders issued a formal declaration against exploiters in 1993. OFFICIAL POLICY

What it was for. None. This is theft with incense.

Why that reason expired. It was never legitimate, and Indigenous nations have said so in writing. Untrained outsiders sell ceremony at Western prices — in one documented case with three deaths and a criminal conviction. COURT RECORD

Who benefits now. Non-Indigenous entrepreneurs, retreat operators, and the wellness industry. Source communities generally see none of it.

The expired-reason test works on any rule. Take one you follow or enforce, name what it was originally for, and ask whether that reason still exists. If it doesn't, and the rule survives anyway, the section's last question is the one that matters to you: who benefits from it continuing?

Reach

Before you read

How far the institution's authority extends into three private areas: what members may read and hear, how children are raised and schooled, and decisions about their own bodies.

Information

  • Sacred knowledge is legitimately restricted by initiation and kinship — this is genuine intellectual sovereignty, not information control, and the codex distinguishes them.
  • The control risk is that the same secrecy can shield a specific abusive elder from community accountability. PATTERN OBSERVED
  • Outsider extraction is the larger documented problem: researchers, tourists, and self-appointed shamans publishing and selling restricted knowledge without consent. INVESTIGATIVE REPORT
  • 'You'll dishonor the ancestors' can suppress internal questioning — an outsourcing of authority to people who cannot be consulted. PATTERN OBSERVED

Children

  • Initiation and coming-of-age rites are typically communal and consensual within tradition; the practice at issue is where they are conducted without the young person's real ability to decline. VARIES BY COMMUNITY
  • The overwhelming documented harm to Indigenous children in this profile was inflicted by outside religious institutions: residential and mission schools, with confirmed deaths and unmarked graves. GOVERNMENT REPORT
  • Female genital cutting occurs in some traditional contexts and is defended in cultural terms; it is a documented harm to children regardless of framing. GOVERNMENT REPORT · WHO
  • Child witchcraft accusations in parts of West and Central Africa — often at the intersection of traditional belief and Pentecostal deliverance ministries — have led to abandonment and violence. GOVERNMENT REPORT / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT

Bodies

  • Many traditions were and are matrilineal, with women holding ceremonial and political authority — the colonial imposition of patriarchy is itself a documented harm. ACADEMIC SOURCE
  • Two-spirit and third-gender roles were recognized in numerous nations and were specifically targeted by missionaries. ACADEMIC SOURCE
  • Where control patterns do appear internally: arranged or early marriage, menstrual restriction, and elder authority over women's choices in some communities. VARIES BY COMMUNITY
  • Abuse by ceremonial leaders is documented and structurally hard to report where the leader is also kin, healer, and community authority. FORMER MEMBER TESTIMONY
  • Missing and murdered Indigenous women is the overwhelming gendered harm here, and its source is external. GOVERNMENT REPORT

1 · Love Bombing

Overwhelming affection on arrival to create emotional dependency before terms are revealed.

How it shows here

  • People may be drawn through ceremony, ancestry, land, elders, songs, healing rituals, initiation, and the promise of returning to what colonization or modernity stole.
  • The emotional hook is powerful because it touches identity deeper than belief: blood, soil, memory, tribe, ancestors, survival.
  • Love becomes coercive when elders, shamans, family systems, or communal authorities use ancestry to demand obedience, silence abuse, enforce gender roles, or punish departure as betrayal of the people.

The strongest defense. These traditions were oppressed; criticizing them repeats colonial disrespect.

The counter. Oppression by outsiders does not make internal control sacred. A wounded tradition can still wound people. Respecting survival does not require pretending every elder is safe, every ritual is harmless, or every demand made in the name of ancestors is love.

Evidence grade. Cultural Ceremony and kinship welcome are communal and, unlike most of this codex, offer something that is genuinely the reader's inheritance.

2 · Weaponized Generosity

Giving help that installs unspoken obligation.

How it shows here

  • Indigenous, folk, and ancestral traditions may offer ceremony, healing, elder guidance, songs, land connection, initiation, family belonging, and recovery of identity after displacement or colonization.
  • The generosity can be profound because it restores what modernity, migration, or historical violence severed: ancestry, place, ritual, memory, and communal meaning.
  • But that same restoration can become leverage when elders, shamans, family authorities, or community leaders demand obedience, secrecy, money, sexual access, silence, or loyalty in the name of tradition.

The strongest defense. Sacred traditions require secrecy because they were exploited. Elders protect knowledge. Outsiders cannot judge what they do not understand.

The counter. Secrecy becomes PLAUSIBLE DENIABILITY when it protects unsafe authority. Elder guidance is not above consent. Protection from outsiders must not become protection from accountability to insiders.

Evidence grade. Cultural Authority attributed to ancestors who cannot be consulted, in an oral tradition with no text to check against. (sourced)

The loops

Before you read

How the techniques connect into cycles. A single practice can look harmless on its own. The loops show how one step feeds the next, so that, for example, a newcomer's welcome turns into an obligation.

1 · Money to Doctrine to Money

Where internal control appears, a gatekeeper prices access to culture that is the person's own inheritance.

2 · Fear to Dependence to Fear

Misfortune attributed to neglecting ceremony supplies the fear; the gatekeeper supplies the relief.

3 · Children to Members to Children

The loop was broken violently from outside: children removed to destroy transmission, and the damage is present-tense.

4 · Aid to Legitimacy to Leverage to Aid

The external version runs hardest here — churches contracted to run schools received both funding and legitimacy.

5 · Unpaid Labor to Assets to Power to More Labor

Retreat and appropriation economies extract labor, knowledge, and revenue with nothing returning to source communities.

6 · Scandal to Removal to Reform Theatre to Continuity

Institutions apologized, individuals were named, and records remain unreleased.

7 · Persecution to Solidarity to Insulation to Unaccountability

This is the one tradition where the persecution loop's premise is overwhelming — which makes naming internal harm hardest, and no less necessary.

Why this matters to you

The loops explain why good people stay in arrangements they would never sign up for from scratch. The more you have already put in, the harder it is to walk away. Researchers call this the sunk-cost effect (Arkes & Blumer, 1985), and it works on time, money and relationships alike. Ask yourself: if you were starting today, knowing what you know, would you join?

The questions

Before you read

The six questions the documented record could not resolve. Each is expanded below: why it is being asked, and a concrete example already on this page that it points back to.

Question 1 of 6
Which church ran the school your family was taken to, and has it released its records in full? If not, ask what it is protecting after a hundred years.

Why this is asked

Records held by the churches that ran the schools are records of what happened to specific children. The question asks whether they have been released in full, and if not, what is being protected.

Example already on this page

The cases section records that the residential school system was "largely operated by churches under government contract," and that Catholic entities were released from a $25 million fundraising pledge in 2015 after raising under $4 million.

Question 2 of 6
If a ceremonial leader harmed a young person, ask who in your nation is empowered to hear it — and if the answer is nobody, ask whether that is tradition or damage.

Why this is asked

If nobody in a nation is empowered to hear a complaint against a ceremonial leader, young people have nowhere to go. The question asks whether that absence is tradition, or damage.

Example already on this page

The glance table's unanswered question: "Protection from outsiders is owed and overdue. Who protects the insider from the elder?"

Question 3 of 6
Ask a non-Indigenous person selling your ceremony to name the community that authorized them. Then ask that community.

Why this is asked

A ceremony sold by an outsider either has a community's authorization or it doesn't. The question asks for the name of that community, and then asks the community itself.

Example already on this page

The cases section records James Arthur Ray's sweat lodge for paying participants, where three died, and notes that Lakota leaders had condemned the sale of their ceremonies in 1993 89.

Question 4 of 6
If a cut, an early marriage, or an accusation is defended as tradition, ask which elder will state it publicly in their own name.

Why this is asked

A harmful practice defended as tradition should be defended by name, in public. The question asks which elder will do so.

Example already on this page

The who-gets-hurt table lists girls subjected to cutting or early marriage "in specific contexts."

Question 5 of 6
Ask who receives the money from a retreat run on your practices. Follow it all the way to the account.

Why this is asked

Retreat money is traceable. The question asks you to follow it to the account and see whether it reaches the community whose practice is being sold.

Example already on this page

The glance table records ayahuasca tourism, retreat industries and appropriated ceremony sold at Western prices, "revenue frequently bypassing source communities."

Question 6 of 6
You may have been taught that internal criticism helps the colonizer. Ask honestly whether protecting an abuser has ever helped your nation survive anything.

Why this is asked

The argument that internal criticism helps the colonizer has real historical weight. The question asks whether protecting an abuser has ever actually helped a nation survive.

Example already on this page

The law table records the U.S. Code of Indian Offenses (1883), which outlawed ceremonies, and the laws that reversed it in 1978 and 1990.

In closing

Your traditions are the only ones in this codex that were nearly exterminated by another religion's institutions, and the accounting for that is nowhere near complete. Nothing on this page reduces that, and anyone who quotes it to excuse what was done to your family has misread it deliberately.

And still: sovereignty means governing yourselves, which includes the authority to hold your own accountable. Protection from outsiders and protection from insiders are not opposites — nations that have built their own protocols for ceremony, research, and safeguarding have shown that. The most anti-colonial thing available is a community that does not need anyone's permission to protect its own children.
