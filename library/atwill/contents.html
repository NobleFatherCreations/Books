I. The frame

  • Most of your waking life happens hereThe hours add up to one of the highest-leverage rooms this library could cover, and it has been the most neglected.
  • “We’re a family”Families don’t fire each other. A workplace that wants the obligations of family without its protections should worry you.
  • Where the power actually sitsMap who can fire whom, who needs the job more, and who has savings — before assessing anything anyone said.
  • Passion as a wage discount“Do what you love” frequently means “we pay you in meaning instead of money.” You can love the work and still be underpaid for it.
  • A bad job versus a coercive oneThe same gradient as everywhere else in this library. Bad management is not automatically coercive control.

II. The moves

  • The moving targetGoals that change once you meet them. It reads as your inadequacy. It is their design.
  • Manufactured urgency as a permanent stateAsk how long the crisis has lasted. A permanent emergency is a management style, not a description of reality.
  • Recognition instead of payA title, a shout-out, employee of the month — a real reward, delivered in the wrong currency.
  • IsolationA reorg, a remote reassignment, a shift change. Same mechanism as everywhere else, aimed at your allies.
  • The performance review as an instrumentSometimes an assessment. Sometimes a record being built to justify a decision already made.
  • The improvement plan that is a terminationA PIP is sometimes real support and sometimes a documented exit, and the markers tell them apart.
  • Information asymmetryThey know your market rate, your replacement cost, and the budget. You usually don’t.
  • The loyalty debtSomething they did for you once, invoked forever. Gratitude is not an unlimited, permanent debt.
  • Overwork as identityWhen the hours become who you are, saying no to more of them starts to feel like an identity threat.
  • The one who left and was erasedWhat happens to the person who raised something and then vanished is a warning aimed at everyone who stayed.

III. The machinery

  • HR is not your friendTheir job is to protect the company from liability. Sometimes that helps you. Know which situation you’re in.
  • The NDAMost employees overestimate what it actually covers.
  • Non-competesFrequently weaker than they read, and enforceability varies enormously by where you are.
  • The arbitration clause you signedSigned at hiring, rarely read, and it may have already decided how a future dispute gets resolved.
  • Contract, classification, and the gig lineBeing classified as a contractor changes what you’re owed substantially. Misclassification is common.
  • Algorithmic managementThe Loop’s argument, compressed: coercive control with an API and no human to appeal to.
  • SurveillanceKeystrokes, cameras, tone analysis, location. Routine now, legal in many places, worth knowing about.
  • Wage theft in its ordinary formsUnpaid overtime and rounded time cards are the most common financial harm at work and the least often named as theft.

IV. Who is doing it

  • The founderA specific, documented pathology where mission and charisma substitute for structure.
  • The manager who is also trappedUsually squeezed from above. That changes your options. It doesn’t excuse the harm.
  • The peer who enforcesThe colleague who polices the culture without being asked to.
  • The board and the money behind itWhat lands on you often started several floors up, in a target nobody on your floor set.
  • NobodySometimes an incentive produced the harm and no person chose it. That changes the remedy, not the reality.

V. What you’re actually owed

  • What a contract should containA checklist to run your own against.
  • Hours, breaks, and the law where you areMinimums exist almost everywhere. Checking yours takes minutes.
  • What a manager may not requireA job can require a great deal. It can’t require everything.
  • Discrimination and harassmentPlain-language definitions, no law degree required.
  • Health and safetyPhysical and, increasingly, psychological obligations exist and are enforceable.
  • If you’re dismissedProcess, notice, and what typically makes it unlawful. What to do in the first 48 hours.
  • Where to checkWhich body in your specific country can actually compel an answer.

VI. Your options

  • Documentation, starting todayThe same instrument as everywhere else in this library. Most people in a bad situation have none.
  • Raising it internallyThe naming test, applied at work — and the real retaliation risk, named honestly.
  • The grievanceA formal process that exists in many workplaces and triggers specific, useful things.
  • Collective actionHistorically the most effective response there is. It starts smaller than a union.
  • The regulatorUsing an external body is not “going nuclear.” It is frequently the intended, ordinary route.
  • Legal advice with no moneyFree and low-cost help exists for exactly this. Most people don’t know to look.
  • Leaving wellHow you leave affects references and sometimes legal claims. There’s a right way even from a bad situation.
  • WhistleblowingReal legal protection exists. Real retaliation also happens. Both facts, stated plainly.

VII. The ledger

  • Am I the manager doing this?The Weighing’s standard, applied to anyone in this audience who also manages people.
  • What a good workplace actually looks likeThe reference sample. You cannot recognise a coercive workplace with no baseline for a decent one.
  • What this book might have wrongPublished rather than buried — what varies by jurisdiction more than this book can promise to know.
  • What changes at scaleDocumentation and internal escalation help individuals. Regulation and organising change the structure.
