The Drop

“The RV”

The cold open

📍 FESTIE POV

His name is Jordan and he needs to re-up and the row 14 situation is probably fine.

This is the internal logic at 10:52 p.m. on Saturday at a four-day festival in the Central Valley where it is still eighty-nine degrees because the Central Valley has decided, as a region, that evening is a suggestion rather than a meteorological event. Jordan is twenty-three. He has been at this festival since Thursday. He is a reasonable, functional, not-stupid person who has made one specific error in judgment that he is about to pay for in a way that will feel like a fever dream and look, in retrospect, like the most legible possible outcome of the steps he took to get here.

The steps he took to get here:

Step one: ran out of what he brought, which was enough for the weekend but which was not quite enough for the weekend when you share freely because the scene runs on generosity and you forgot that the scene runs on generosity until you were out.

Step two: asked a friend. The friend did not have. The friend, however, had a number. The number belonged to a friend of the friend — someone the friend had met at a regional last year, someone the friend described as “solid, he’s out there, just text him.” The friend had not seen this person since the regional. The friend would not be able to pick this person out of a lineup. The friend meant well. The friend’s “solid” is based on one encounter approximately fourteen months ago at an event where everyone seemed solid because everyone was having a good time and good times produce the illusion of vetting.

Step three: Jordan texted the number.

The response came in two minutes: Meet me by row 14 RVs. Two minutes. No name. No camp. No context. Just the row and the time and the implicit premise that this is how things work out here, which it sometimes is, and which is the entire operating premise of Jordan’s current situation and also the operating premise of the two men in the beige RV.

The beige RV has its curtains drawn. This is information. At 10:52 p.m. at a festival in the Central Valley, with the temperature still at eighty-nine degrees and the party fully operational all around, the curtains of a beige RV being drawn tight against the ambient light of the festival is either someone’s very committed effort to sleep — which is not the vibe at this hour in this location — or it is a space that has been prepared to not be visible from outside.

Jordan does not think about the curtains. Jordan has not been taught to think about the curtains.

Come inside man, AC’s on, water’s cold.

The AC is real. The water is probably cold. These are genuine inducements. An outside temperature of eighty-nine degrees is a genuine argument for accepting the invitation to step into a space where the temperature is controlled and the water is cold and two men are waiting who texted back in two minutes because they were already here, already waiting, already with the curtains drawn.

Jordan steps up.

The door shuts.

The click is not the door.

The click is one of the two men racking a slide on something that Jordan’s nervous system identifies before his brain does, in the specific nanosecond between the sound and the understanding. The understanding arrives clean and total and without the usual softening of gradual comprehension. There is a gun in the beige RV with the curtains drawn and the cold water and the AC and the two men who texted back in two minutes, and Jordan is inside it, and the geometry of the space — four feet between him and the door, two men in the space between — has just become the entirety of his available universe.

Everything in the pockets.

Jordan’s pockets contain: his phone — a 2023 iPhone with a cracked lower corner from a stage-adjacent incident at a show in Oakland last spring that he never got fixed because he kept meaning to get fixed. His wallet — forty dollars cash, which is not what he was going to spend tonight, and his debit card and his driver’s license and a loyalty card for a sandwich place in Sacramento with six punches on it, two away from a free sandwich, which is a detail his brain produces with the strange specificity of a nervous system trying to catalog everything that is currently leaving his possession. His car keys. The four hundred dollars he was going to spend — cash, folded, separate from the wallet, in the back pocket where he put it for this specific meeting.

He puts everything on the beige RV’s small table.

We’ll mail ’em back, swear. One of them laughs. The laugh is not cruel exactly — it is the laugh of someone for whom this is a logistical situation rather than an emotional one, someone for whom Jordan is a resource that has been successfully accessed and is now being efficiently processed. The laugh is the worst part because it is not angry. Anger would make this feel like something Jordan could respond to. The laugh is just: business.

They take his hat for some reason.

He doesn’t know why. He will never know why. The hat is a New Era fitted in a colorway he drove forty minutes to get. It has been on his head since Thursday and has accumulated the specific patina of a hat that has been at a festival for three days, which is its own distinct texture and which means nothing to the men in the RV except that it’s a hat and taking the hat was apparently part of the plan or a whim or something in between those two things that Jordan will spend an uncomfortable amount of mental energy on over the following weeks.

You’re good. Go.

He walks out of the RV at 11:14 p.m. with: no phone. No wallet. No keys. No cash. No hat. No recourse.

The no recourse is the thing that is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is the thing that makes this chapter different from the others in the practical-danger section.

In Chapter 11, the counter-drop involves calling for help. In Chapter 12, there are rights you can invoke. In Chapter 13 and 15, there are protocols and policies and legal structures that imperfectly but meaningfully exist.

In this chapter, Jordan has been robbed at gunpoint and his available options are:

Option A: Tell festival security he got robbed while attempting to purchase illegal substances from two strangers he found via a friend-of-a-friend text chain, in a beige RV on row 14, at 10:52 p.m. on a Saturday. Festival security will be sympathetic in the way that people are sympathetic to someone who has stepped on a rake they were warned about, will take his name and description, will almost certainly not find the men in the RV, and will file a report that will be useful to nobody and that may trigger questions Jordan would prefer not to be the subject of.

Option B: Tell the county sheriff’s department the same story, with the addition that he can give them the number he texted, which is either disposable or will lead to an investigation in which Jordan is very likely a cooperating witness in a narcotics matter given the circumstances of the encounter, which is not the role he was planning to occupy this weekend.

Option C: Sit on a tire in the next row over and try to remember what his car looks like from this angle until he can figure out what to do next, which is what he does, which is what most people in this situation do, which is what the people in the beige RV are counting on.

He sits on the tire.

The word he keeps landing on is geometry. Four feet to the door and two men between him and it. He is twenty-three and he has never once had to think about the geometry of a room, and now he is going to think about it in every room for years, which is the part of tonight that actually follows him home.

The festival is continuing. The festival does not know what happened. The festival is producing bass, ambient light, the smell of festival food, the specific sound of four thousand people having a better night than Jordan is having right now. A group of people in elaborate costumes walks past him on the way to the main stage and someone in the group says “I need water” and someone else says “there’s a station by the big art thing” and they go on and that is the full extent of their awareness of the man sitting on a tire trying to remember what his car looks like.

His camp is on the other side of the festival.

Sunrise is six hours away.

He has no phone.

He has no keys.

He does not have the hat.

He is going to sit on the tire until something occurs to him, which will take approximately twenty-two minutes, and what will occur to him is that he knows the approximate location of his camp and walking toward it is a thing he can do with his feet, which still work, and that someone in his camp will lend him a phone, and that the conversation he is going to have with his camp at twelve-thirty a.m. is a conversation he would very much like to skip but cannot.

The chapter is not about the crime.

The chapter is about row 14.

About the RV with the curtains drawn.

About the friend-of-a-friend chain and the two minutes of response time and the click that was not the door.

About every decision point between “I need to re-up” and “everything in the pockets.”

Not because Jordan deserved what happened to him. He did not. You do not deserve to be robbed for trying to buy something. The robbery is on the robbers. Full stop. No asterisk.

But the geometry of the RV was a choice that happened before the robbery, and that geometry could have been a different shape, and that different shape is the whole chapter.

The Soundboard Quote

“The deal in the private space with the person you’ve known for eleven minutes is not a deal. It is an arrangement in which all of the risk is yours and all of the leverage is theirs and the only variable is whether they decide to exercise the leverage tonight. Most of the time they don’t. The times they do are why this chapter exists.”

— Patchbay Alvarez, forty-one, stage manager, destroyer of clipboard frauds, and the only person in the scene who has successfully predicted three separate RV situations based solely on proximity to row 14, curtain position, and the specific quality of the “come inside” offer. Has a personal policy: all deals happen in open daylight with witnesses present and a phone camera rolling as a deterrent. Has explained this policy to every stage crew person she has trained. Has also explained it to her nephew twice, both times after incidents that did not involve a gun but could have, and that she is keeping track of. Recorded backstage at a regional on Saturday afternoon while eating a sandwich and watching the RV row from a comfortable distance that she has described as “professional curiosity and personal insurance.”

The Real F*cking Setlist

What this chapter is actually about

This chapter is about the geometry of bad situations — the spatial, informational, and social architecture that has to be in place before any of the violence or theft or no-recourse scenarios can happen. Most of what makes the RV possible was constructed before Jordan walked to row 14: the friend-of-a-friend chain that diluted the trust to fiction, the convenience framing that made “come inside” feel like hospitality rather than a trap, the no-witness situation that removed the deterrent that keeps most of these situations from escalating. Five Tracks. All preventable before you leave your camp. The chapter ends with the Safety Appendix that contains the specific mechanics of doing all of them right.

The Tapers’ Section

Situational Awareness · Target-Hardening · The No-Witness Problem

Brief. Then the practical content.

Situational awareness in security contexts means: reading the environment for indicators that precede bad events rather than responding to bad events after they happen. In festival language, this means: drawn curtains in daylight, response time too fast for a genuine casual arrangement, enclosed private space offered before any trust is established, AC and cold water as a lure rather than an amenity. None of these are individually definitive. Together, in a ten-second read, they are a complete picture that Jordan did not have the training to read.

Target-hardening is the concept that makes a potential victim less attractive than the next potential victim — harder to rob, harder to work, more costly to engage. In RV-row terms, target-hardening looks like: doing business in the open, with witnesses, at a distance that allows you to run. Most opportunistic crime requires low-cost, low-risk conditions. Witnesses are a cost. Open space is a cost. A running distance is a cost. Add the costs and most opportunistic criminals go find someone cheaper to rob.

The no-witness problem is what produces the Crime With No Recourse. When Jordan enters the RV, he loses every structural protection he had outside it: witnesses, running distance, visible distress, the ambient social safety net of a public festival environment. The door closing is not just a door. It is the removal of every deterrent simultaneously.

The festival is the safest it can be when deals happen in the open.

The festival is most dangerous when people go inside.

You are in.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Geometry Trap

(confined-space coercion / spatial control)

The Behavior

The deal needs to move inside.

The reasons are various and they arrive dressed as hospitality. AC because the Central Valley is still eighty-nine degrees. The back of the RV because there is something to show you. The tent because “I don’t want to do this out in the open.” The car because “my stuff is in here.” The trailer because “it’s loud out there.” The yurt. The van. The dark corner behind the generator. The stage-left corridor where the sound isn’t as bad. The storage unit that one camp has behind the art car.

Every one of these spaces has in common: once you are in it, your options are different from what they were outside it.

Outside, you can call for help with your voice. Outside, witnesses exist. Outside, running is a thing. Outside, the social fabric of the festival is ambient and available. Inside, all of those things require either your host’s cooperation or a level of physical confrontation that the Geometry Trap was specifically designed to make feel disproportionate.

The move inside does not have to be aggressive to be effective. It just has to happen. The gun does not have to come out until the door shuts. The Geometry Trap is the door shutting. Everything after is consequences.

The Read

Ask: why does this conversation or transaction need to happen inside?

For most legitimate transactions, the answer is: it doesn’t. A person who has something to sell you does not need you inside their space to sell it. A person who wants to show you something could show you it outside. A person who is offering you hospitality in good faith does not require the hospitality to be accepted in a private space as a condition of the offer.

The “come inside” is a spatial move disguised as a social one. The space is the play. The hospitality is the frame.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Open-Space Requirement

One rule, zero exceptions:

All transactions happen in the open, with witnesses, at a distance I can walk away from without asking anyone’s permission.

Step 1 — When “come inside” arrives: “I’m good out here.”

Step 2 — If the transaction requires you to be inside: the transaction is not happening.

Step 3 — If the offer persists or escalates: “I’m going to head out.” Walk away. The convenience of the inside offer is not worth the geometry it creates.

Step 4 — If you are already inside and something feels wrong: “I need to step out.” Use that sentence before anything is on the table. Move toward the exit. A physical exit from a space is always more available at the beginning of a bad situation than it is at the end of one.

Vibe Check

Some inside conversations are fine. Some people offer hospitality in good faith from inside their RV. The rule is not “never go inside” in the context of festival social life. The rule is “all transactions happen in the open.” Transactions and social situations are different categories. You can go inside for dinner. Do not go inside to buy.

Mirror Set

Have you ever moved a conversation or a transaction inside specifically because the inside offered you an advantage — privacy, control of exits, reduced witness presence? This applies not just to robbery but to any situation where the enclosed space reduces the other person’s options. Notice if you have moved people inside.

Refractions

Touring Artists: The green room, the after-bus, the backstage corridor after a set — all of these are enclosed spaces where the power dynamics are already uneven. The Geometry Trap runs in professional contexts as readily as in lot contexts. The rule applies.

First-Timer: You don’t know the layout of the festival well enough to map your exit routes. That is a specific vulnerability in any geometry situation. Know where the medical tent is, the main thoroughfare, the nearest food court — any space with ambient foot traffic. Those are your safety spaces.

Runs in Every Direction

The Geometry Trap gets set by the tour bus regular who suggests moving somewhere quieter, the camp acquaintance with the drawn curtains, the friend-of-a-friend who’s “just right over there,” and anyone who’s learned that a private space changes what’s possible. The gender of the person running it is irrelevant. The geometry is the play. It runs equally regardless of who constructed it.

Sober Tuesday

The salary negotiation that the manager wants to have in their office — not the conference room, their office, with the door closed — rather than in a neutral space. The geometry shifts the power. The offer is not better because you are surrounded by their things.

Track 02: The Friend-of-A-Friend Chain

(trust transitivity dilution)

The Behavior

Your friend knows someone.

That someone is “solid.”

Your friend’s assessment of “solid” is based on an encounter fourteen months ago at a different event where everyone seemed solid because the event was good. Your friend cannot pick this person out of a lineup. Your friend has not verified anything about this person since the encounter. Your friend’s “solid” is not an assessment of current behavior, supply chain safety, propensity to run a stick-up operation, or general trustworthiness across contexts. It is a vibes-memory from a year-plus ago in a setting that produces positive vibes-memories almost universally.

This is not your friend’s fault. This is the trust-transitivity error from Chapter 3 applied to personal safety at two removes. Trust does not travel through two links unchanged. Trust is attenuated by each link. Your friend trusts them, and that trust is based on limited information and time passage, and you are now being asked to take that already-attenuated trust and stake your phone, wallet, car keys, four hundred dollars, and physical safety on it.

You are being asked to trust a stranger on the recommendation of a friend who barely knows them.

That is the same as trusting a stranger.

The Read

Map the chain. How many links between you and the person? When was the last contact between the linking friend and the source? Under what circumstances did the trust form?

Two links with a fourteen-month gap and a festival encounter as the basis: that is not a reference. That is a coincidence presented as a recommendation.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Direct Verification Protocol

Step 1 — Before following any friend-of-a-friend to any meeting: ask the linking friend: “How well do you actually know this person? When did you last interact? What’s their actual name and camp?”

Step 2 — Require specifics. Not “they’re solid.” Name. Camp. When last seen. How the original connection was made.

Step 3 — If the answer is vague: do not proceed with the referral. Your friend cannot vouch for someone they cannot describe.

Step 4 — If you can verify the source independently — through a camp network, a mutual connection who has more recent contact, a profile with actual traceable scene roots — verify before you walk to row 14.

Step 5 — If you cannot verify and you still want to proceed: do the transaction in the open, with a witness, with the geometry of a parking lot or a main thoroughfare rather than a beige RV with curtains drawn.

Vibe Check

Friend-of-a-friend connections are the basis of most of how festival communities function. Most of them are fine. Most of the people at row 14 are not running stick-ups. The chain breaks most dangerously in contexts where the transaction requires either physical privacy or significant financial exposure, because that is where the gap between “vibes-solid” and “verified-solid” costs the most.

Mirror Set

Have you ever passed along a friend-of-a-friend referral as “solid” based on limited contact? Have you ever said “just text them, they’re good” about someone you haven’t seen in over a year? You may have inadvertently constructed a chain that someone else is walking to the end of.

Refractions

Long-Tenured Festies: Your name carries significant vouching weight in the scene. The informal references people drop with your name attached — “[you] said they were solid” — may bear no resemblance to anything you have actually said. You have limited control over this. You can explicitly tell your network: “If anyone uses my name to vouch for a meeting or a transaction, have them check with me first.”

Runs in Every Direction

The chain dilutes the same way whether it passes through the tour crew’s usual guy, the camp’s newest addition vouched for by someone half-remembered, the festival hookup’s roommate, or the friend of a friend everyone agrees is “basically family.” The vibe-memory problem is universal. The verification protocol is also universal.

Sober Tuesday

The babysitter referred by a neighbor who met her once at a block party two summers ago and describes her as “so sweet.” The “sweet” is a five-minute impression. The hire is unsupervised time with your kids. The gap between the data and the decision is the chain.

Track 03: The Witness Rule

(third-party deterrent effect)

The Behavior

The witness is the cheapest crime deterrent in existence and costs nothing but the social friction of saying “let me bring someone.”

The Witness Rule is not about having a defender. A witness doesn’t have to do anything in a bad situation. A witness is a deterrent to the bad situation happening in the first place. The presence of a third person raises the cost of any predatory action because: the third person is a second victim, a second witness, a second person who can call for help, a second person who can run, a second person whose description of events will corroborate the first, a second body to manage if the plan involves coercion. Most opportunistic criminals in festival environments prefer single-target interactions because the math is simpler. A second person changes the math.

The men in the beige RV did not approach a group. They texted one number. The one number texted back one person. That person showed up alone because that was the arrangement and showing up with a witness to a transaction you arranged via a stranger’s text would feel weird and paranoid.

It would also have been fine. It would have been fine for the witness to be there. The awkwardness of the witness is significantly preferable to the click that is not the door.

The Read

For any meeting with someone you don’t know well, in any location, for any reason: bring someone.

Not because you expect violence. Because the witness changes the cost calculation for the person who might be considering violence, and most people who might be considering violence make the rational calculation that it is not worth it when the cost calculation changes.

The witness does not have to know the full context of why they’re there. “Come with me for a few, I’ve got a thing” is sufficient.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Plus-One Protocol

Step 1 — For any meeting with a stranger or near-stranger, in any setting, for any transaction: bring one person.

Step 2 — Brief them briefly: “I’m meeting someone I don’t know well. Just be there with me.”

Step 3 — The witness stands where they can see you. Not inside. Not right next to you. Visible, outside, with a sight line.

Step 4 — The witness has your description of the location, the person’s number, and the time. If you don’t come out in fifteen minutes: the witness has the information.

Step 5 — If the transaction “requires” you to come alone: that requirement is the red flag. Legitimate transactions do not require you to leave your safety infrastructure behind.

Vibe Check

Most meetings with strangers at festivals are completely benign and the witness protocol will produce nothing except a mildly puzzled witness. This is a fine outcome. The protocol costs you a slightly awkward “hey, come with me for a sec” and produces dramatically different outcomes in the small percentage of situations that are not benign.

Mirror Set

Have you ever asked to meet someone alone when you knew that a witness would change the dynamic you were going for? The Witness Rule as a counter-drop has a mirror: have you ever benefited from the absence of witnesses in a way you would not have benefited from in their presence?

Refractions

Solo Festies: If you’re attending alone, the witness protocol requires building it from the environment: a stranger from the neighboring camp whose name you learn, a DanceSafe volunteer you introduce yourself to, the camp adjacent to yours with a lead you exchange numbers with. These are not relationships. They are structural witnesses.

BIPOC Festies: Traveling with a witness is a safety protocol that has a longer and more serious history in communities for whom moving through public spaces alone has historically carried specific risks. The festival context adds a layer to something that may already be an existing practice.

Runs in Every Direction

The witness deters the touring vet with a reputation to protect, the camp lead who’s careful in front of others, the stranger who’s only opportunistic when nobody’s watching, and anyone across every predatory gender and identity configuration. The witness is not protection against ideological violence. It is protection against opportunistic material crime, which is what this chapter addresses.

Sober Tuesday

The HR conversation that you take a note-taker into. The meeting with the landlord that you bring a friend to. The car dealership negotiation where you bring the friend who knows what things cost. The witness changes the power geometry. The lesson applies everywhere power geometry matters.

Track 04: The Camp Got Hit

(opportunistic theft and target-hardening)

The Behavior

While everyone is at the main stage for the headliner, the camp has a ghost population of exactly zero and a portable speaker, three phones charging from a power bank, someone’s wallet in a mesh pocket of a bag they left on a chair, a laptop in a day bag with the zipper facing outward, a cash stack under a rolled sleeping bag, and a GoPro clipped to a camp table as if it is an ambient fixture rather than a three-hundred-dollar camera that is cordially inviting any passing raccoon — literal or figurative — to take it.

The camp-hit scenario is not a sophisticated crime. It requires exactly the following assets: the ability to walk, peripheral awareness, and the two minutes that a gap in ambient camp presence provides. The perpetrator does not need a master plan. They need a scan of an unattended camp, a pocket large enough for a phone, and the confidence that a crowded festival with a headliner on the main stage is not going to produce a witness within the relevant time window.

The relevant time window is about ninety seconds.

Everything that is not secured or on a body is available in ninety seconds.

The Read

The camp-hit is predictable in its timing: headliner set, full-camp migration to main stage, transition windows between stages. These are the windows when camp population drops to zero and the camp’s material contents are ambient and accessible.

The read is not about identifying the predator. The read is about identifying the window and building the target-hardening before the window opens.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Camp Hardening Protocol

Step 1 — Before the event: identify what is in the camp that cannot be replaced (medication, prescription glasses, keys, passport, ID, significant cash, irreplaceable personal items). Those go in a lockbox or on a body. No exceptions.

Step 2 — Phones charging: in a lockbox. Not on a camp table. Not in an open pocket. A lockbox bolted to a tent stake or a cooler hasp.

Step 3 — Cash: body-carried in the smallest amount needed for the session. The rest in the lockbox. The lockbox is not a hotel safe — it is a physical deterrent that raises the cost of the theft above what most opportunistic thieves are prepared to spend. Most opportunistic thieves will skip the lockbox for the next camp that doesn’t have one.

Step 4 — Camp rotation: if the camp has more than four people, someone stays. Not as a punishment rotation. As a standing policy. One person at camp during every main-stage window. They get their phone charged and their dinner eaten and their introvert time logged and the camp does not lose three hundred dollars of gear.

Step 5 — GoPro: in the bag. In the locked car. Not on the table. The table is not a display case. It is a festival table. It belongs to nobody and everything on it belongs to whoever walks by with the right pocket size.

Vibe Check

Festival theft is statistically uncommon relative to the size of the crowds. Most people are not there to steal. The target-hardening is not about paranoia. It is about making the camp a less attractive target than the camp next door, which is the whole game. Opportunistic theft is a math problem. Change the math.

Mirror Set

Have you ever taken something from a camp that was not yours because it seemed unattended and you needed it? A lighter. A bottle of water. The charger that you returned but took for three hours first. Small thefts are the Studio Debut version of the Camp Got Hit. Notice the shape.

Refractions

Vendors: Your booth at a festival is a camp-got-hit target of the first order. Cashbox protocols, card reader security, and booth-sitting coverage during adjacent-stage transitions are not paranoid. They are professional practice.

Artists with Gear: Your equipment at a festival is the most valuable thing in the camp and the least secured thing in the camp because you trusted the vibe of the space and the vibe of the space has, historically, not been a cable lock. Cable lock everything that can be cable-locked.

Runs in Every Direction

Whoever hits the unguarded camp isn’t a specific archetype — the opportunistic vendor row neighbor, the festie passing through at 4 a.m., the person who’s been casing tents since Thursday, the stranger who just noticed nobody locked the trailer. They come in every gender, identity, and presentation. The target-hardening works regardless.

Sober Tuesday

The office where nobody locks their computers because “we’re all colleagues here.” The bike locked with a cable that requires one pair of wire cutters and twenty seconds. The apartment building with a lobby door that doesn’t fully latch. The target-hardening is not paranoia. It is the cost comparison between a cable lock and a replacement bike.

Track 05: The Crime with No Recourse

(illegality-induced reporting suppression)

The Behavior

Jordan cannot call the police.

Or rather: Jordan can call the police, and the call will produce a conversation in which Jordan is asked to explain the circumstances of the robbery, and the circumstances of the robbery include his purpose in walking to row 14, which was to purchase a controlled substance, which is information that Jordan would strongly prefer to not be the subject of a police report that might become evidence in a subsequent proceeding.

The Crime With No Recourse is the structural feature of any criminal enterprise that operates against people who are themselves doing something illegal: the victims cannot report without self-implicating, which means the perpetrators have the reporting suppression built in as a design feature. The men in the beige RV do not need to worry about Jordan going to the police. They know Jordan is not going to the police. Jordan sitting on a tire in the next row over is the expected outcome. Jordan sitting on a tire is the business model.

This is not unique to drug transactions. The Camp Got Hit has the same structural feature — the person who had substances at camp is going to think very carefully about whether to involve official security in documenting what was stolen. The Geometry Trap scenario applies any time the victim’s presence in the situation involves activity they’d prefer not to explain.

The design of the no-recourse scenario is why prevention is the only meaningful counter-drop. There is no satisfying response to the beige RV once Jordan is inside it. The response had to happen before row 14.

The Read

Before any situation that creates the no-recourse structure: ask, explicitly, “If this goes wrong, what are my options?” If the answer is “limited, because of what I’m doing” — that information should factor into the risk calculus of whether and how to proceed.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Festival Safety Report

For crimes that occurred at festival, the reporting options are more nuanced than the binary of “police or nothing”:

Step 1 — Festival safety staff. At most festivals, festival safety operates separately from county law enforcement and is empowered to document incidents, remove people from the festival grounds, and share incident reports with law enforcement only at their discretion. Reporting an armed robbery to festival safety does not automatically produce a county sheriff’s investigation of your activities.

Step 2 — Document everything immediately. Time, location, description of the men, description of the RV, the license plate if visible, the number you texted. Write it. Voice memo it. Text it to yourself. The documentation exists for your use — for an insurance claim, for a civil matter, for your own memory which will be less reliable in two days.

Step 3 — Harm-reduction volunteers are not law enforcement and are not required to report. They can connect you to resources. They can sit with you. They can help you find your camp. Use them.

Step 4 — The camp network. Tell your camp lead. Your camp lead can spread the information through the camp’s network without involving your name — “there’s a situation happening near row 14 RVs, heads up.” The information protects others without requiring you to file anything.

Step 5 — The subreddit, the Discord, the scene’s informal information networks. Information about specific locations, specific descriptions, specific patterns of predatory behavior at specific festivals travels fast and saves people. The privacy of the reporting is in your control. The protection it provides is real.

Vibe Check

Festival safety staff and harm-reduction teams are almost universally non-judgmental about the circumstances of a situation. Their job is safety, not compliance. Most of them have heard everything and are specifically not interested in using it against you. The fear of judgment is often larger than the actual judgment you’d receive.

Mirror Set

Have you ever taken advantage of someone’s inability to report against you? Have you ever exploited the no-recourse structure — even in a context less extreme than an armed robbery — to do something you knew the other person couldn’t easily protest? The no-recourse design is not unique to the RV. It runs in every context where the victim’s situation makes formal reporting costly.

Refractions

BIPOC Festies: The fear of law enforcement contact is higher and more grounded in BIPOC communities, which means the Crime With No Recourse hits harder — the threshold for even festival safety reporting can feel higher when the safety team includes or is adjacent to police. Know which safety resources at your specific festival operate independently of law enforcement before you need them.

Undocumented Festies: The same issue applies with additional stakes. DanceSafe, Zendo, and peer harm-reduction networks are not connected to immigration enforcement. Use them.

Runs in Every Direction

The no-recourse structure gets built by anyone who’s identified a category of victim who can’t easily report — the undocumented festie, the person on probation, the minor who snuck in, the person whose ID doesn’t match who they told the officer they were. It is created by and benefits people of any gender or identity. It is not gendered. It is opportunistic. The protection is the prevention.

Sober Tuesday

The manager who gives inappropriate assignments to the employee who is on a visa and cannot easily leave the company or escalate without risking their status. The no-recourse structure runs in every space where someone’s ability to report is compromised by their own situation. The design is the same. The exploitation is the same.

Tales From the Lot — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Dev Acharya is twenty-three, and he is in a situation that is almost the same as Jordan’s and will end differently, and the distance between the two outcomes is the specific width of the phrase “hold on, let me grab my friend real quick.”

Dev has run out of what he brought. He texts a friend. The friend does not have. The friend, however, has a number — someone solid, camps with the northern bloc near the water towers, reliable, friend’s name is Marcus. Dev texts the number. The number texts back in two minutes: Row 14, come through.

This is the watermelon moment. Not a dramatic one — more like the chapter 4 version, where the body knows something before the brain catches up. Dev is reading the text and there is a specific quality to the two-minute response time — the readiness of it, the way the directions arrive with the efficiency of someone who has been waiting for this exact text — that produces, in his chest, the mild cooling sensation he has come to associate with information he doesn’t yet fully have.

He walks to the edge of the camp.

He does not walk to row 14.

He stands at the edge of the camp and he looks at the text again and he asks himself the question he has learned to ask from a conversation with his older sister, who has been going to festivals for twelve years and who gave him three rules before his first one: don’t go inside with someone you don’t know, don’t go anywhere without a witness, and if anything feels even slightly off, it’s off.

He texts his sister: “got a ref to a stranger, row 14 RVs. slightly off feeling. you up?”

His sister is asleep in Oakland. It is 10:54 p.m. She texts back in six minutes: “Row 14 at a festival at 10:54 pm from a stranger = no. I’m up, call me.”

Dev calls her. They talk for four minutes. She says the specific things she has said before, in the particular register of someone who has seen the row-14 situation in different configurations at different festivals and knows what the drawn curtains mean and knows what the two-minute response time means.

She says: “Do you have a buddy who can go with you if you still want to go?”

He finds his friend Nabila. Nabila has been to enough of these to know the Plus-One Protocol without it being named. “Row 14, give me two minutes.” She is there in ninety seconds.

They walk to row 14 together. They find the beige RV. The curtains are drawn.

Dev says: “Hey, this is my friend Nabila. She came with me.”

There is a pause on the other side of the RV door that is neither welcoming nor brief. The pause is the sound of a calculation being run that produces a different result when the input is two people rather than one.

The door opens slightly. The man inside looks at Dev and Nabila. Nabila is looking at the drawn curtains. Nabila is looking at the row. Nabila has her phone out and is typing something that might be the location or might be a text to someone who knows where they are or might be nothing in particular, but which communicates, to the man calculating through the slightly open door, that she is specifically and visibly tracking this moment.

The man says: “Actually, my guy doesn’t have anything tonight. Sorry, bad info.”

He closes the door.

Dev and Nabila walk back to camp. The transaction did not happen. The thing Dev needed to re-up for he did not get. This is a mildly annoying outcome that will resolve itself by tomorrow when he finds a vetted source through a more established chain.

Nabila says: “Curtains were drawn.”

Dev says: “Yeah. I noticed.”

They go back to the main stage. The headliner is still going. The night is still good.

Nobody’s hat is taken.

The Soundcheck

The Row 14 Prevention Drill

Before the event. Sober. With your crew.

Three questions. Out loud. Everyone answers.

Question 1: Where is your lockbox? What’s in it? Where is it secured?

If the answer is “I don’t have one” or “it’s just in my bag” — fix that before Friday. The lockbox costs thirty dollars and prevents the Camp Got Hit.

Question 2: What is your policy on following strangers to private spaces for transactions?

State the policy out loud: “Transactions happen in the open with a witness.” Every crew member says it. The policy is established before you need it.

Question 3: If someone gets the “slightly off” feeling about a referral or a meeting — what do you do?

Establish the protocol: the crew member with the slightly-off feeling says it out loud, to the crew, and the crew runs the Plus-One Protocol. No exceptions. Slightly off is off enough.

Three questions. Five minutes. Prevents the hat situation.
