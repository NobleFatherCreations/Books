The Drop

“The Eight-Ball”

The cold open

📍 WOOK POV

I bring an eight-ball to every party.

Not to sell. That’s the first thing I want you to understand. I’m not a dealer. I have a real job. I lease commercial property in three neighborhoods in Los Angeles, which is a job that does not require I explain myself to anyone, and I have done it long enough that I am comfortable, and comfortable means I can afford to be generous. I can afford the eight-ball. I can afford the festival ticket, the proper hotel forty minutes from the site because I am thirty-eight and I am not sleeping in a tent anymore, I have earned the right to sleep in a bed. I can afford to be the person at the party who says here, take some, which is — I want you to understand this — genuinely what I am. I am generous. I have always been this person. This is not a strategy. This is a personality trait.

That is what I tell myself driving down from the hotel at nine p.m. This is who I am. I am generous. I like parties. I like music. I like people who are open. I am a person who is drawn to people who are open and I want to facilitate their openness and the eight-ball is how I do that. Nothing sinister. Just chemistry. Just a man who has good stuff and likes to share.

I clock the room in about forty-five seconds.

Three hundred people in a converted warehouse space east of the arts district. The ceiling is three stories of raw rebar and it does something beautiful with the fog machines — the fog collects at the top and makes the lights look like they’re underwater. I’ve been at enough of these to know which vantage point gives you the full room. I go there first. I always go there first.

Of those three hundred people, I am interested in maybe forty. Not in the way that sounds — not all at once, not necessarily that way at all. Just: forty is the number that passes the basic read. Open face. Here for the music. Young enough to be enthusiastic, not so young I am not doing that. Alone or with one friend, which is the good number, big groups are logistics. Came for real, not for the Instagram story, you can tell by whether they face the stage or face the crowd. Someone in the room at the full-length mirror.

Of those forty, maybe ten tonight. That’s the work.

I see her at eleven-fifteen.

Black mesh top. Glitter on the collarbones. Earrings that came from a festival vendor, the kind where you can tell by the weight. She does not know anybody — you can read this by the way she navigates, the particular caution of someone who has not yet mapped the social terrain, who has not yet located the friendly node in the room that makes everything else relax. She came with someone. That someone is not here. She has checked her phone three times in eight minutes, which is the signal, which is the window. She is almost ready to recalibrate and leave. She is thirty seconds from leaving.

That’s my window. The almost-leaving. I know exactly what almost-leaving looks like and I know it doesn’t close the same way a fully-leaving does.

I do not approach.

I never approach first. First approach is too visible, too obviously directional. Instead I get into her sightline. I find the two girls from the earlier part of the night — I already helped them through something small, already spent twenty minutes making sure they felt good, which is the loop, helping is the loop — and I am laughing with them in a way that is genuine and also located directly in her sightline, and I am being warm in a way that any person who is standing alone and recalibrating can receive as: this group of people is having a safe good time and I could be near that.

She drifts over. Of course she does. They always drift.

I make space. I do not make a production of making space. I just: open the geometry of the group slightly, the way you open a door by stepping to the side rather than turning the handle, and she comes in. I am warm. Not flirty. Not obviously anything. I am the older brother who knows the DJ, which is a character I have been playing since I was twenty-four and which I have gotten very good at. The older brother who knows the DJ is not a threat. The older brother who knows the DJ is a resource.

I pull out the bag without asking. This is important. Asking makes it a decision. Not asking makes it a gift. I bump the two girls first — friends first, always friends first, this is the structure, watch how it works — and then I look at mesh-top and I shrug.

You good?

Like it’s optional. Like I don’t care.

She nods. She takes it. And then, before she has even registered the hit, she says the sentence I am farming the whole night for.

Oh my god thank you, you’re so nice.

There it is. That’s the sentence. I am so nice. Once she has said that sentence — once she has named the relationship as a generous-person/grateful-recipient relationship — the whole frame is set. She has told herself a story about me. The story is: he is generous and warm and has been at these a long time and knows the DJ and gave me something nice. That story is now doing structural work. It is holding doors open that her critical faculty would normally close.

I have not pushed. I have not touched without invitation. I have not said anything that could be cited in any account of this night. I have been, by every operational metric, nice.

By midnight I know her name, her neighborhood, that she moved to LA from St. Louis eighteen months ago, that her friend bailed and she was about to leave, that she had a bad experience at a party last year that she describes with the specific economy of someone who has said the sentence enough times that it has become compressed, and that she does not usually take coke from strangers.

I am not a stranger. I am the nice guy. I established that before the bag came out.

By one a.m. her serotonin is doing what serotonin does and she is loose and warm and the music has become a full-body experience and I am the only person in the room she knows and she has decided I am safe, which is — and I know this — not a decision she made. It is a decision the chemistry made on her behalf.

By two a.m. I suggest the kitchen, it’s quieter, we can actually hear each other. She agrees. The kitchen is not quieter in any meaningful sense but it is separated from the main room and separated from her ability to locate any other person she might know and separated from the ambient witnesses that a crowded dance floor provides.

There’s always one more bump. I don’t have to offer it. She asks. They always ask at that point because the first one told her body something and her body wants the sentence to continue. I take it out. I don’t measure. Why would I measure? We are past measuring. We established the frame two hours ago. I am nice. She is grateful.

By three-thirty she is sitting on the counter and I have my hand on her knee and she has laughed three times at things that were not that funny and she has mentioned her ex in a way that told me everything I needed to know about what kind of open she is tonight and I have been warm, consistent, undemanding, older-brother who knows the DJ, the safe port in the strange party.

There is an older word for what she is. My uncle ran a room like this out of a bar in Reseda for eleven years before the liver took him, and he would have called her the mark. I do not use that word. I have never used that word. I want that on the record, in whatever record this is.

I have used it twice tonight, in my head, and corrected myself both times.

I have not pushed once.

I have not had to.

The structure does the pushing. The chemistry does the pushing. The original frame — I am nice, she is grateful — does the pushing. And she is making choices inside that structure that she believes are hers because they feel like hers and they are coming from her body and her voice and her hands.

I sleep great. I sleep so great.

I am not like those guys. Those guys who grab, who insist, who make it obvious, who leave a trail of something that looks like what it is. Those guys are predators.

I’m just generous.

I bring an eight-ball to every party.

Here is the thing I need you to know, sitting in this chapter, four beers or two bumps or a half-tab in or completely sober, on the rail or in the hammock or reading this in your tent with a headlamp because you couldn’t sleep:

That man slept great.

He woke up and made coffee and felt fine.

He did not experience what happened as harm. He experienced it as a natural outcome of a generous night. His accounting of events is internally consistent and morally comfortable for him.

He will be at the next one.

And the next one.

And the next one.

And the sentence he farms — you’re so nice — will be spoken fifty, a hundred, two hundred more times, by people who came with someone who bailed and were almost ready to leave and found a warm port in the strange party and did not know that the warmth had a structure and the structure had a price.

The price was their yes before they knew they were saying it.

Yes is a sober word.

Even when you are not sober. Even when the music is doing what it does. Even when the bag comes out without asking and the frame is already set and the older-brother character has been running for an hour before the question becomes a question.

Yes is a sober word in the sense that it must be made by the part of you that can still see the exit.

A body shape is not a yes. A laugh is not a yes. A sure through chattering teeth is not a yes. Gratitude is not a yes. Being alone in a kitchen with someone who has been nice all night is not a yes.

That is the chapter.

That is every chapter.

It doesn’t have to ask because by the time the bag comes out, the asking already happened — it just happened in a framing conversation forty minutes ago that she didn’t know was a framing conversation. The Real F*cking Setlist

What this chapter is actually about

This chapter is about how Wooks weaponize altered states. Not about whether you alter your state — that is your business, full stop, no lecture forthcoming. This chapter is about the moment someone decides your altered state is a door into your body, your wallet, your phone, your camp, or your week. We are covering seven tracks a Wook runs around capacity, and we are building four tools you keep regardless of where you fall on the substance spectrum: The Capacity Spectrum, the Bystander Interruption Kit, the Morning-After Reckoning, and the one sentence that is the whole chapter condensed to a line you can use at two a.m. with a bag in your face. Yes is a sober word. The part of you that can still see the exit has to be the part that answers. If that part is not running, the answer is not a yes yet.

The Tapers’ Section

Harm Reduction · Capacity Theory · DanceSafe Lineage

A little theory. Then back to the dust.

Harm reduction is the idea that people will use substances regardless of moralizing, so the goal is keeping them as safe as possible. Test kits. Hydration. Cool-down spaces. Trusted information. Accurate dosing. Drug interaction awareness. Crisis support without punishment or criminalization. In scene language: Zendo Project, DanceSafe, Wharf Rats, the medical tent, the psychedelic peer support volunteer with a clipboard and a fanny pack with stickers and Narcan in it. These organizations have been building the language this chapter uses for decades. We are not inventing it. We are translating it into the register of the PLURth Angel at four a.m. with a flashlight under the chin.

Capacity theory is the legal and ethical idea that consent requires a person who can understand, weigh, and freely choose. A person who is blackout, unconscious, dissociated, at psychedelic peak, in physical crisis, or inside a coercive emotional frame cannot give meaningful consent regardless of what their body or mouth does. In scene language: a body shape is not a yes. A nod is not a yes. A sure through chattering teeth is not a yes. A laugh is not a yes. Gratitude is not a yes. Being alone with someone in a kitchen at three-thirty a.m. is not a yes.

The DanceSafe and Zendo lineage also gives us this: a capacity-aware crew tests their substances, sets a dose plan, has a sober anchor, agrees on check-ins, and treats consent as something that must be re-asked when the chemistry shifts. A capacity-disrespecting Wook treats your altered state as a green light, your we already started as a contract, and your blackout as a mystery instead of a crime.

That is enough. Put your harm-reduction sticker back on the cooler. Let’s walk.

You are in.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Dose Creep

(incremental boundary erosion)

The Behavior

You agreed to a small thing.

Half a tab of lucy. One bump. A microdose. A two-finger pour. A short ceremony. A light cup of tea. An edible labeled starter dose by a man who is not a labeling authority and whose certification, if asked, is “I’ve been doing this for years.”

Then the math starts moving.

“That batch runs weaker, you’ll want another.” “This is basically the same as what you already did.” “You’re tolerating it well — you can handle a little more.” “That dose is for tourists.” “You’re not feeling it because you’re blocking.” “Just lick the rim.” “This is basically just kava.”

The Dose Creep is not always coercive in tone. Sometimes it is friendly, paternal, maternal, spiritual. The voice is calm. The pace is slow. The escalation looks like generosity. But the contract you agreed to is not the contract being run. You said yes to half a tab of lucy in a tested batch from a known source. You did not say yes to the follow-on that the other person decided, inside their own head, was the logical extension of your original yes.

The Read

Watch for math that changes after consent. Did the dose described match what was offered? Did the follow-on offer come before or after you hit your original ceiling? Are they tracking your dose or losing track on purpose? Are they refusing to name milligrams, grams, drops, or duration? Are they using the word “intuitive” to mean “I’m not measuring”?

Vague dosing is not mysticism. Vague dosing is plausible deniability.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Pre-Set Dose Plan

Set the plan before the lasers start. Before the event. Before the camp opens. Before the cooler is touched. Not at eleven p.m. with a man named Atlas pouring tea.

Step 1 — Decide your ceiling sober. Write it down. “Tonight: one tab, tested, nothing else.” “Tonight: one bump at midnight, water after, that’s the set.” “Tonight: sober. Cacao only. No exceptions.” The note lives in your phone where your altered self can read it.

Step 2 — Tell one anchor. The anchor knows the plan. The anchor is allowed to say “that’s not the plan” and the pre-agreement is that you listen when they say that.

Step 3 — Refuse renegotiation in altered states. “I don’t update my dose plan after I’ve started dosing.” Or the shorter version: “Future me already voted.”

Step 4 — Treat every upsell as data. If someone keeps pushing past your stated ceiling, they are not being friendly. They are trying to lower your capacity. That is a Track, not a vibe.

Vibe Check

People can offer you things kindly. You can decline kindly. The test is what happens after the third no. Green: accepts and redirects. Yellow: keeps the option warm. Red: escalates, reframes your no as your problem, finds a new angle.

Mirror Set

Have you ever pushed someone past their stated dose because you wanted company at the peak? Have you ever told someone “you’re not feeling it” because their slowness was inconveniencing your timeline? Be honest about which side of this you have been on. Then stop running it.

Refractions

First-Timer: A veteran’s tolerance is not your floor. What takes them to baseline will take you somewhere else entirely. Set your ceiling based on your body and your experience level, not theirs.

Sober and Recovery Lane: “Just a sip” is not a small ask. It is the whole ask. Your sobriety is not an inconvenience to the group’s chemistry plan. You do not owe anyone a modified version of your sobriety to make the night more comfortable for them.

Ceremony Lane: A facilitator who says the dose is determined intuitively and cannot be specified in advance is not running a ceremony. They are running a dose-accountability bypass. Real ceremony practice has named protocols. Ask for them before you sit down.

Runs in Every Direction

The Dose Creep is run by male producers who pour generously, femme facilitators who say “the medicine knows what you need,” nonbinary elders whose ceremonial authority is used to override questions, touring veterans whose confidence is the credential, and camp leads who treat their kitchen supply as a social tool. The pour is not gendered. The push past no is not gendered.

Sober Tuesday

If a waiter refilled your wine glass twice after you said you were fine and then described the third refill as “your body wanted it,” you would leave a different tip. The restaurant does not get a pass because the candles were nice.

Track 02: The Sober-Friend Skim

(capacity-asymmetry targeting)

The Behavior

Your sober anchor is the entire safety architecture for the night.

The Wook knows this. So they remove it.

They get the anchor talked into “just one.” They flirt with the anchor. They pull the anchor into a forty-five-minute conversation that is genuinely interesting and also precisely as long as it needs to be. They tell the anchor someone else is checking on you. They invite the anchor on a side mission that leaves you alone in the bass crowd at peak. They convince the anchor that everyone is “in good hands” — which is the specific phrase that means no one has that specific job anymore.

The Sober-Friend Skim doesn’t always look like seduction. Sometimes it looks like recruitment: “You should come sit by the fire.” “Your friend is fine, she’s with my crew.” “Come to the afters, we’ll meet up with her later.” Suddenly the only person whose job was to track you is somewhere else, untracking.

The Read

Is one person consistently pulling your anchor away from their anchor role? Is the anchor being convinced their job is unnecessary? Is the anchor being told you are “with someone” when you are not? Watch the geometry: the Wook needs your anchor removed before moving to the next Track. The skim is often visible from outside as a sequence.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Two-Anchor System

One anchor is a single point of failure. Two anchors is a system.

Step 1 — Designate two anchors before the event. Anchor A and Anchor B. They both know the plan, both have check-in windows, both have a code word.

Step 2 — Stagger their availability. If A is dosing tonight, B is sober or lower. If both want to dose, stagger the timing so they are never both at peak simultaneously.

Step 3 — The code word is a sentence that means “I need you now, no questions.” Text it. Say it. It does not require context. The anchor responds immediately with location and movement toward you, not with “are you okay?”

Step 4 — The anchor’s job is not to be fun. Their job is structural. Be very clear about this before the event. Anchor is a role, not a hangout.

Step 5 — If the anchor disappears: locate a DanceSafe or Zendo volunteer, a harm-reduction tent, a ranger, a staff member. These are your community anchors. Know where they are before you need them.

Vibe Check

Anchors are human and can genuinely lose track. Someone pulling the anchor into a long conversation may not be running a play. The test is pattern: does it happen consistently, with the same person or type, at the same phase of the night?

Mirror Set

Have you ever kept someone’s anchor occupied while something else was happening? Have you ever told a sober person their friend was “fine” before checking? Have you ever recruited someone away from their care role for your own evening’s convenience?

Refractions

New to the scene: You may not have an established anchor network yet. DanceSafe, Zendo, and harm-reduction volunteers at any festival with safety infrastructure are your structural anchor. Locate them on the festival map before your first dose.

Queer/Trans Festie: In spaces where your full identity may not be safe with a random security guard, build your anchor network from your specific community before the event. The anchor should understand your context, not just your chemical plan.

Disabled and ND Festies: Capacity asymmetry compounds with any pre-existing condition that affects processing speed, executive function, or social boundary-reading. Your anchor needs to know your specific tells — not generic altered-state tells, yours. Brief them before you go in.

Runs in Every Direction

The Sober-Friend Skim is run by men, women, and everyone across the spectrum. It requires no particular gender to execute — only the social skills to occupy an anchor’s attention and the situational awareness to time the occupation correctly. Both skills are distributed equally.

Sober Tuesday

Your work friend who always wants a “quick word” with your mentor right before your performance review. Long enough that the meeting runs short and you don’t get to the hard thing. The anchor was occupied. The floor was left open.

Track 03: The “We Already Started” Pivot

(sunk-cost applied to consent)

The Behavior

“We already started this together.”

“You can’t half-ceremony.”

“It’s bad luck to leave the medicine unfinished.”

“But you said yes to this earlier.”

“After what we’ve already done — you’re going to stop now?”

The “We Already Started” Pivot is one of the oldest moves in coercion. It treats a small yes as a binding contract for every yes after it. It tells you the door closed when you walked through it, so do not bother turning around. Your previous consent becomes a claim on your current capacity. Your earlier presence becomes evidence of your future compliance.

The door is always open. There is no ceremony, no hookup, no commitment, no shared experience, no chemical partnership that closes the door. The door is open at 2 a.m. It is open at peak. It is open in the middle of a set they drove four hours to see. It is open in their tent. It is open after their hands are already on you. The door is always open and the Pivot is a set design that is supposed to make the door invisible.

The Read

Is your earlier consent being cited as evidence you cannot withdraw? Are they framing pause or re-evaluation as failure, fear, weakness, or betrayal? Are they making you feel like slowing down is doing something to them? Is the language about the container — “you agreed to this space” — being used to lock you in the space?

A real container honors the pause. A coercive container punishes it.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Re-Consent Pause

Step 1 — Use the line: “I am stopping. My earlier yes does not cover this.” No further explanation required. No apology. No justification for changed feelings.

Step 2 — Move toward people or light. Wooks lose power in the presence of witnesses. Harm-reduction tents, kitchen lights, crowds, rangers. Move toward them.

Step 3 — Document before the altered state clears. Time, location, what was said, what was offered, what you stopped. Even fragmented. Even voice-noted while walking.

Step 4 — Tell your anchor. Even if you are embarrassed. Especially if you are embarrassed.

Vibe Check

You are allowed to stop in the middle. You are allowed to stop after the hookup has started, after the ceremony has begun, after the dose has hit, after you said yes three times in a row, after the whole night was built around the assumption that you were in. There is no contract. There was never a contract. The only contract that exists is the one you can give with the part of you that can see the exit.

Mirror Set

Have you ever told someone they couldn’t stop? Have you ever used “we already started” to push past discomfort — a partner’s, a participant’s, a friend’s? Have you ever framed a pause as a rejection of you rather than a recalibration for them? If yes, you ran this Track. Name it. Stop running it.

Refractions

Poly and Kink Scenes: Prior negotiation does not override active withdrawal. The negotiated agreement is the floor, not the ceiling. “We already discussed this” does not mean “you already gave consent for all future instances.”

Ceremony and Plant Medicine Lane: “You agreed to surrender when you sat down” is one of the most dangerous sentences in the scene. Surrender in ceremony means releasing the ego’s resistance to the medicine’s work. It does not mean releasing your capacity to stop. These are different things. Any facilitator who conflates them is running the Pivot inside a sacred container.

Runs in Every Direction

The Pivot runs across every gender and every scene. Male ceremony facilitators running it from a position of spiritual authority. Femme partners running it inside a relationship where commitment has been mistaken for consent. Nonbinary scene figures running it inside a community where questioning the container feels like questioning the values. The Pivot is ideologically flexible and will wear whatever costume is most available.

Sober Tuesday

“But we’ve been in this project together for eight months — you can’t pull out now.” Yes you can. Eight months of investment does not obligate the ninth month. The Pivot wears a blazer in the conference room and a poncho in the ceremony space. Same move.

Track 04: The Peak Pickpocket

(consent under cognitive overload)

The Behavior

The peak is when your judgment is most beautiful and least operational.

It is also when Wooks move.

They take your phone “to find your friends” and never give it back, or they scroll, or they text from your number, or they note your passcode, or they read your DMs while you are somewhere else being made of light. They take your bag “to hold it safe” and go through it. They take your wallet “for the ride” and pocket the cash. They take your body. Your kiss. Your photo. Your password. Your story.

They ask you for your Venmo while you are dissolving and you tell them because at peak the brain’s executive function is running at reduced capacity and the social script of answering a question runs on autopilot when the full assessment system is elsewhere. They get you to send a text. Sign a thing on their phone. Agree to a deal. Name a price. Hand over a key. Nod into a camera that is recording. Say yes to the plan that did not exist twenty minutes ago.

Some peak pickpockets are physical. Some are financial. Some are emotional. Some are reputational — a photo you didn’t agree to, a video, a story told to someone important in the scene while you were not able to manage your own narrative. All of them rely on the same fact: at peak, you cannot lock the door the way you normally would.

The Read

Did they wait until peak to ask what they had not asked before? Are they handling your things “for you” in ways that limit your access to them? Did they introduce decisions — housing, money, sex, access, commitment — that should have been made sober? Are they using the peak as the moment to ask for things they have been working toward all night?

The peak is the destination. The building happened earlier.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Peak Perimeter

Build this before you peak, not during.

Step 1 — Establish physical perimeter: bag on body or with named anchor only. Phone in body pocket or with anchor. Cash in separate location from cards. No new transactions during peak — this is a standing policy your anchor enforces.

Step 2 — Establish social perimeter: your anchor has license to say “she’s not available for that right now” on your behalf. Not to speak for you about your feelings. To run interference on requests that require your executive function while your executive function is on leave.

Step 3 — Establish decision perimeter: Nothing that costs money, body, reputation, career, or commitment gets decided at peak. If someone has a “now or never” offer at two a.m. on the bass floor — the answer is never. Real offers survive until morning.

Step 4 — After peak, before sleep: brief survey. Phone intact? Bag intact? What was agreed to? The anchor runs this with you. Four questions. Five minutes.

Vibe Check

Peak states produce genuine insight, genuine connection, genuine beauty. Some of what happens at peak is exactly what it is: real. The test is not did something happen at peak. The test is: did someone wait for your peak to ask for something they had not asked for when you were at baseline? If yes, they chose the timing on purpose.

Mirror Set

Have you ever waited for someone’s peak to have a conversation you wanted to have? Have you ever introduced a question — about intimacy, about money, about a plan — at the specific moment when someone’s ability to assess it clearly was most reduced? If yes, that is the Peak Pickpocket run inside a relationship you thought was clean. Notice the timing.

Refractions

First-Timer: You do not yet know what your peak looks like from the inside, which makes you maximally susceptible to the pickpocket. Your anchor is your entire protection here. Establish them and brief them specifically: “If anyone asks me for money, physical access, or commitments after midnight, please interrupt.”

BIPOC Festie: Reputational pickpocketing — the photo, the video, the story told about you while you were not able to manage your own narrative — lands differently when your image in predominantly white spaces is already managed under additional scrutiny. Your narrative is yours. Build the perimeter around it.

Vendor and Artist Lane: Business agreements made at peak are not binding. If you were at peak when you agreed to a split, a booking, a collaboration, a price — you have grounds to revisit it. The verbal agreement made at two a.m. in a fog machine is not a contract.

Runs in Every Direction

The Peak Pickpocket does not require proximity. The financial pickpocket can be across the room, working the Venmo from a text. The reputational pickpocket can be across the festival, running your story through four people before you have sobered up enough to tell it yourself. The physical pickpocket can be someone your body trusts, which makes the ask feel like a natural continuation rather than a targeted move. The peak is the window. The shape of the pickpocket varies.

Sober Tuesday

The work dinner where someone introduced the partnership terms over the second bottle of wine, after you had declined a third on the basis of a morning meeting, and after you had said yes and sent the email you did not remember sending. The terms were in the email. The email is what the contract cites.

Track 05: The Come-Down Rewrite

(post-event narrative control / DARVO precursor)

The Behavior

Sunday morning. Your brain is in the slow reboot. The synapses are tired. The emotional skin is thin. The memories of the previous thirty-six hours exist in a way that is vivid in some patches and completely absent in others, with no reliable way to distinguish the vivid-accurate from the vivid-constructed or the absent-because-it-didn’t-happen from the absent-because-your-brain-was-elsewhere.

This is when they call.

“Last night was so incredible.” “I’ve never felt that kind of connection.” “You were so present, so open.” “We had such a deep moment — I keep thinking about it.” “That’s wild, you seemed so into everything.”

The Come-Down Rewrite is when someone else interprets the night for you while your brain is still rebooting. The interpretation is always slightly — sometimes massively — favorable to them. The corners they round are the corners you would have wanted to keep. The parts they describe as “incredible” are the parts your fragmentary memory cannot fully confirm or deny. And because humans default to the first coherent version of an unclear event — because the brain hates ambiguity and reaches for narrative resolution — the version they give you has a high chance of becoming yours.

If you challenge it, you are confused. Or ungrateful. Or “stuck in your head.” Or “letting the come-down color something beautiful.”

The Rewrite is also a DARVO precursor. If the night involved something you later name as harm, the Rewrite is the first layer of the defense: she said yes, she was so into it, she was totally present, she was the one who— The Rewrite gets established while your memory is most plastic. It gets established before you can consult your anchor. It gets established before you find out what other people saw.

The Read

Are they the first person to tell you what happened? Are they framing your memory gaps as confirmation of their version — “you were so far gone, you probably don’t remember, but”? Are they discouraging you from talking to other witnesses? Are they framing your discomfort as a misinterpretation of something wonderful?

Memory after altered states is fragile and Wooks know this. So do good friends. The difference is what they do with that knowledge.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Receipt Sweep

Step 1 — Before talking to them, talk to your anchor. Anchor’s version first. Before anyone else’s.

Step 2 — Check your phone independently. Texts you sent. Photos timestamped. Location data. Voice notes you made. Step count. Battery drain patterns. Anything that creates an independent record.

Step 3 — Check with witnesses outside their social circle. The vendor who was nearby. The harm-reduction volunteer. The friend from a different camp. The person sober for the night.

Step 4 — Write your own version before reading theirs. Even if it is fragmented. Especially fragmented. Your fragmented version is still your version and it has standing.

Step 5 — If their version contradicts the receipts, trust the receipts. Not their description of what “clearly” happened. The receipts.

Vibe Check

People can disagree about a shared night without running a Rewrite. Memory is genuinely fallible on both sides. The test is not do we remember it differently. The test is: do they make space for your version, or do they replace it? Do they offer their account as one account, or as the account?

Mirror Set

Have you ever told someone “you said yes” when you were not entirely certain, because the certainty made the morning more comfortable for you? Have you ever been the first voice in someone’s Sunday, soft and sure, interpreting a night when you knew they could not fully check? That is the Rewrite in a relationship that did not feel coercive. It is still the Rewrite.

Refractions

Sober Lane: You are often the only person in the crew with a clean tape. Your version of the night is community infrastructure. Tell it. Do not soften it to protect someone else’s narrative.

Survivor Lane: A Come-Down Rewrite that replaces your experience of harm with someone else’s account of beauty is not just gaslighting. It is the first layer of the coercive-control documentation that will be deployed if you ever name what happened. Document your version before their version becomes the shared memory.

Runs in Every Direction

The Rewrite is run by anyone who benefits from a particular version of the night. It does not require malice — sometimes people genuinely believe their interpretation, which makes it more persistent and harder to counter. The Rewrite runs across every gender because the underlying mechanic — fill the memory gap with the story that serves you — is available to everyone.

Sober Tuesday

The project retrospective where the person who caused the failure presents their version of events before anyone else has spoken, and by the end of the meeting that version is what everyone remembers. The Rewrite runs in every debrief where one person speaks first and longest.

Track 06: The Plug Leverage

(supply-as-coercion in consent)

The Behavior

The plug becomes the relationship.

It starts as access. Then it becomes the only access. Then it becomes the only safe access, because theirs is clean and everyone else’s is suspect, and the story about everyone else’s quality is something they tell you with a frequency that is slightly too convenient to be only about your safety. Then the price changes. Then the price is not money.

“You can crash with me, it’s easier that way.” “I’ll just bring it to your tent — come out after.” “Don’t go to anyone else, I’ve seen what they’re moving and it’s dirty.” “I’ll cover you tonight, we’ll figure it out later.” “You owe me, but not in money.”

The Plug Leverage Wook is not always selling substances. Sometimes they are selling festival tickets, backstage wristbands, ceremony invites, gear loans, campsite space, a vehicle seat on the last caravan out. The pattern is identical. They become the supplier of something you need or want. They make themselves the only clean source. Then they price it in something other than money — in loyalty, in silence, in physical availability, in the suppression of a concern you were about to raise.

A real plug is a vendor. A Wook plug is a landlord.

The Read

Is the supply line tied to favors that grow? Are you being told other sources are unsafe in a way that creates exclusive dependence? Are you being made responsible for managing one person’s goodwill in order to maintain your access? Are you being asked to repay in body, secrecy, labor, or loyalty?

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Supply Independence Plan

Step 1 — Map the dependency. What does this person supply you with? Be specific. Substances, housing, tickets, rides, work, social access. Write the list.

Step 2 — Build two alternates per item. Two test-kit sources. Two ride options. Two housing contacts. Two friends who can spot you financially. The redundancy breaks the leverage.

Step 3 — Refuse non-monetary repayment explicitly. “Money is the only form of repayment I’m doing. If that doesn’t work for you, I need to find another source.” If the deal cannot survive that sentence, the deal was a leash.

Step 4 — Pay forward, not back. If you want to honor a genuine favor, give something equivalent to someone else in the scene who needs it. Wooks are structurally opposed to this because forward payment cannot be collected by them.

Step 5 — Test the relationship by buying nothing for one week. Stop using their supply. Observe what happens to the warmth, the access, the communication. The answer will be clear and it will be fast.

Vibe Check

Real friends in the supply chain exist. They are delighted when you find a better or safer or cheaper source. They want you safe, not dependent. They do not mention what they have done for you during conversations about what you owe them. The distinction between a vendor-friend and a leverage-plug is: what happens to the friendship when you need nothing from them.

Mirror Set

Have you ever held supply over someone’s head — not overtly, just: made yourself the most convenient option and let the convenience do the work of binding them to you? Have you ever priced access in loyalty rather than money because loyalty was what you actually wanted? Name it without theater. Then stop running it.

Refractions

Touring Lane: A tour-bus plug is a tour-bus boss. The person who supplies the whole bus controls the whole bus. Build outside lines before you need them — before you are four dates into a tour and the only option is the person running the leverage.

Recovery Lane: The “I got you” plug and the “I got you” sponsor can both be leverage if the price is silence about the relationship. Being clean of one substance does not automatically clean the leverage structure.

Queer Scene: Supply leverage in small queer scenes can involve social access, community platforms, safe housing, or event invites rather than substances. The mechanism is identical. The thing supplied differs. The price is the same: compliance or exile.

Runs in Every Direction

The Plug Leverage runs equally from men, women, nonbinary people, elders, peers, touring artists, promoters, camp leads, ceremony facilitators, housing providers, and the person with the one van going to the festival. Supply is power. Power is not gendered.

Sober Tuesday

The landlord who controls which unit comes open next and “just happens” to move fastest for the tenants who never call about the leak. The supply is a better apartment. The price is silence. The leverage is called management.

Track 07: The Ceremony Cover

(sacred framing as capacity bypass)

The Behavior

Ceremony has a long, real, documented history of healing. Ayahuasca, psilocybin-assisted therapy, breathwork, sweat lodge, sound healing, kava ceremony, cacao ceremony, and a range of indigenous and neo-indigenous practices are real and have produced real documented benefit in real documented research.

The Ceremony Cover Wook uses the vocabulary and architecture of this tradition to override the consent rules that apply everywhere else.

“In this lineage, we don’t ask — we feel.” “The medicine is the facilitator, not me.” “If you resist the touch, you’re resisting the work.” “This part of ceremony is private, just the two of us.” “Don’t bring your modern boundaries into this space.” “You agreed to surrender when you sat down.” “The plant is telling me to hold you.”

The Cover is built from three materials: a significant dose of a psychoactive substance (to reduce capacity), a sacred framing (to override normal consent questioning — “this is different, this is the medicine”), and an isolated container (to remove witnesses and external accountability). The Wook then operates inside the container with the assumption that nobody will check the work because the work is sacred.

It is not sacred. It is a Track in a poncho. The poncho comes off.

The Read

Is the facilitator named, traceable, and lineage-checkable by a source outside their own community? Is the dose protocol written and disclosed in advance, sober? Are there other facilitators or witnesses present? Is touch consent given sober, specific, in writing? Are integration sessions witnessed or recorded? Can you leave the container at any point without being told the leaving is dangerous, bad luck, or spiritually harmful?

Real ceremony has structure that survives sunlight. If the structure dissolves under plain questions, it is not a structure. It is staging.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Pre-Ceremony Checklist

Do this before you sit in any ceremony involving psychoactive doses, significant breathwork, or any physical touch protocol. Sober. Before you have taken anything.

Step 1 — Facilitator verification: Name. Lineage. Trainers. References from participants you can contact independently. Does your contact exist outside their own social proof bubble?

Step 2 — Protocol clarity: Written dose. Substance name and batch source. Duration. What happens if you want to stop. Who else is there. Where the exit is.

Step 3 — Touch consent: Is touch used? What kind? Where? Is the consent given sober, in writing, with specific body parts named? Can it be withdrawn mid-ceremony?

Step 4 — Witness presence: Who else is in the room? Is there a second facilitator? Is there a record — audio, video, written notes — that a third party can verify?

Step 5 — Exit protocol: Can you leave at any point? Will leaving be treated as a choice or as a failure of the work? Is there a support person available outside the ceremony who is not affiliated with the facilitator?

If any of these five questions produce evasion, spiritual reframing, or offense at the asking — the ceremony is not structured. The questions are the ceremony’s first test. Real ceremony passes the first test.

Vibe Check

Real ceremony practice exists and it is extraordinary and it has changed lives. The Ceremony Cover is the counterfeit. The counterfeit relies on the genuine article’s reputation. The way to honor real ceremony is to insist on the standards that real ceremony holds itself to. Real practitioners welcome the checklist. The Cover flinches from it.

Mirror Set

Have you ever used “the medicine decides” as a way to bypass a question about something you wanted? Have you ever run a ceremony with undisclosed doses, no secondary witness, and touch protocols that were never explicitly negotiated? Have you ever framed someone’s post-ceremony discomfort as their failure to integrate rather than as a problem with the ceremony itself? If yes: the medicine was not deciding. You were deciding. And you decided to use the medicine as cover.

Refractions

Indigenous and Lineage-Held Practitioners: The Ceremony Cover is frequently run by non-indigenous people appropriating lineage vocabulary to fabricate authority. Real lineage practice has real lineage accountability — elders, community, consequences. The Cover has none of these. The appropriation harms both the participants of the Cover ceremony and the integrity of the real practice being stolen.

Queer and Femme Participants in Ceremony: Body-touch protocols in ceremony spaces are disproportionately deployed against queer and femme participants in ways that cite the sacred container to override the body’s clear signals. Your body’s clear signal is not your unhealed ego speaking. It is data.

Runs in Every Direction

The Ceremony Cover is run by men in full regalia, women with extensive networks of positive testimonials, nonbinary facilitators whose social capital is built on genuine previous good work, and first-year practitioners who do not know yet that what they are running is not ceremony. The Cover requires sacred vocabulary and a private container. Both are gender-neutral.

Sober Tuesday

“The company culture here is about radical honesty.” Said by a manager who then uses the radical honesty frame to override your HR complaint. Sacred container, same mechanism, different costume.

Build this in your head. Refer to it during. Refer to it when someone is trying to tell you that a specific state can consent to a specific thing.

Clear baseline: Can consent to anything. Can revoke consent to anything. The standard.

One to two standard drinks / mild cannabis / low-dose edible: Can consent to most things. Judgment is slightly altered. Significant decisions — money, housing, career, new sexual relationships — are better made at baseline. Not emergencies but watch the clock.

Moderate dose — three to four drinks / moderate cannabis / one tested cap of MDMA: Can consent to physical intimacy with a known partner with established consent framework. Cannot reliably consent to new partners, financial agreements, major commitments, or situations involving significant power asymmetry. The executive function is attending a different meeting.

Peak dose — at or above heavy alcohol / psychedelic plateau / K-hole adjacent / significant dissociation: Cannot consent to new physical sexual contact. Cannot consent to financial transactions. Cannot consent to anything that requires executive function assessment. Can consent to: water, rest, presence, care, music, an anchor staying close. That is approximately the full list.

Blackout / unconscious / medical distress: Cannot consent to anything. Anyone who proceeds on the basis that this state is consent is not confused about consent. They are running the play.

This is not a rule imposed by someone who wants to limit your fun. This is a description of how human cognitive function actually works under these conditions. The Wook who tells you that you can consent perfectly well at peak is telling you something that is not true, and they know it is not true, and they are telling you anyway because the alternative requires them to wait until you are capable of a genuine yes.

You don’t have to be certain. You don’t have to know. You don’t have to have evidence that passes any standard of legal scrutiny. You just have to see something that looks wrong and walk over.

The interruption is the magic. The Wook’s momentum requires an unobserved container. Walk over and the container no longer exists.

Three interruption moves. In escalating order.

The Casual Break-In:

Walk up to the person who looks off. “Hey — oh my god, is this you? I’ve been looking everywhere.” Friendly, warm, no drama, physical presence inserted into the situation. You don’t know the person. That is fine. You know what “I’ve been looking everywhere” communicates to the person being targeted: someone came for me. The Wook knows what it communicates too.

The Direct Check-In:

“Hey — sorry to interrupt. Can I check in with her directly for a second?” Not aggressive. Just: insert yourself, create a third presence, and ask the person directly if they are okay. Watch their face. Watch their body. If they are fine and this is a regular conversation, they will tell you and you will leave and nothing will have been lost. If they are not fine, the interruption just became load-bearing.

The Named Escalation:

“I’m with harm reduction. Protocol is I check in with this person directly and I’m going to take it from here.” You do not have to actually be with harm reduction. You do have to say it with the calm confidence of someone who has said it before. Then you stay, you get the person’s location, you get their anchor, you walk them toward staff, medical, or their crew. You hand them off to a safe resource and you document what you saw.

The Bystander’s one rule:

You do not need to be certain. You do not need to be right. You need to see something that looks wrong and walk over. The cost of a false positive is a slightly awkward interaction. The cost of a false negative is what this chapter is about.

This section is here because this chapter is the chapter where some of you are going to recognize yourself on the other side.

Maybe you brought an eight-ball and called it generosity. Maybe you waited for peak to have the conversation. Maybe you told someone a version of the night that was helpful to you before they could form their own version. Maybe you ran the Dose Creep because you wanted company and told yourself it was care.

Maybe you have been doing this for a long time and have never called it what it is.

Here is what is true:

You cannot undo the past instances. You cannot unring the bell. The harm occurred regardless of whether you intended it as harm.

Here is what is also true:

Stopping is possible. Right now, today, next event. The next time you pack the bag, ask yourself whose capacity you are planning to work with tonight. If the answer involves someone being lowered, you have the information you need.

The Morning-After Reckoning does not require a public confession, a letter, a ceremony, or a group process. It requires a private reckoning with your own behavior and a changed behavior at the next event.

If the person you harmed is available and wants contact, that is a separate process that requires their consent and their terms, not your timeline.

If you are carrying this and want to do something with it: DanceSafe, MAPS, and several harm-reduction organizations have resources for people who have caused harm in substance-adjacent situations and want to do accountability work. The resources exist. They are not gentle. They are necessary.

The scene does not get safer by only teaching potential targets. It gets safer when the people on the other side of this chapter also read it and reckon with it.

I bring an eight-ball to every party.

It is eleven-fifteen and I have already done the room. Twenty-two targets in a three-hundred-person warehouse in east Oakland, of which eight or nine are operational tonight depending on how the night develops. Standard distribution.

I find the two women from the earlier hour — the ones I helped through the come-up at nine-thirty, spent real time with, genuine care, that’s always real — and I get myself positioned in the sightline of the one who’s been on her phone. Black crochet top, silver rings, came alone or came with someone who has already migrated toward the main stage. Almost-leaving posture. Good. That’s the window.

She drifts over. Exactly on schedule.

Her name is Kezia. I find this out within four minutes. LA transplant, eighteen months in, works in illustration, came with a friend who “went to find water” eleven minutes ago and has not returned, which is information I file for the approach. She is open, a little unmoored, the particular quality of someone who would like to belong to what is happening around her and has not yet found the way in.

I pull out the bag.

I bump the two women first — always friends first — and then I look at Kezia and shrug.

You good?

She looks at the bag. She looks at me. She says: “What’s in it?”

This is not the first question I expect. Usually the first question is a nod, or a sure, or a laugh that means yes. What’s in it requires an actual answer and the actual answer is either the truth or a lie and the lie is checkable in ways that create downstream problems.

I say: “It’s good coke. Clean. Tested.”

She says: “Tested with what?”

I say reagent.

She says: “Do you have the kit?”

I do not have the kit on me right now. The kit is in the car. I start to explain this and she says “it’s cool, I have one” and reaches into a small crossbody bag and produces a Marquis reagent kit, which is not the right kit for cocaine but is the tell that this is a person who came prepared, and I am recalibrating in real time.

I say: “That’s for MDMA.”

She says: “I know, I have a cocaine reagent in my car. I can go get it, or I can skip it, but I just wanted to know if you had it.”

She is not hostile. She is entirely pleasant. She is asking factual questions in the same tone I use when I ask about a good spot for late-night food. She is just: equipped.

She takes the bump. She says thank you. She does not say you’re so nice. She says “thank you, that was generous.” The difference between those two sentences is not large. It is enormous.

By midnight I have bought her one drink — she asked what she could give me in return and I said “nothing, I like parties” and she said “okay” without debt in her voice, which means the Kandi Debt Racket is not running. She has texted someone twice. The text chain is with someone in the building — not outside, in the building — which means she has an anchor and the anchor is close.

At one-fifteen I suggest the back room, it’s quieter.

She says: “I’m good here. I like the energy.”

I say the back room has better sound isolation.

She says: “The sound is the point.” And then, without adjustment or performance, she turns back toward the stage.

At two a.m. a woman in a green jacket arrives next to Kezia and they hug and the green jacket woman looks at me and gives me a single long read and says “hey” in the tone that carries the word “I see you” inside it. The anchor. Kezia introduces me by name. The anchor knows my name already. That is the tell — Kezia texted my name at some point in the evening, which means someone outside this building has a record of where she is and who she is with.

By two-thirty I am at the bar talking to someone else.

Kezia will have a good night. She will go home at four, sober enough to make the decision to go home, and she will text her anchor when she gets there, and the anchor will send a thumbs up, and that will be the night.

She will not know that anything unusual happened. She will think she had a good conversation with a generous person and went home when she was ready. That is exactly what she will have done.

Her preparation.
