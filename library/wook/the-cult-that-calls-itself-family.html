The Drop

“Strike Day”

The cold open

📍 WITNESS POV

It is Sunday at 11:14 a.m., which is the most honest hour at any festival.

Not because the people are honest. Because the circumstance is. Friday at eight p.m., everyone is their best festival self — intentions stated, outfits intact, the weekend still a blank canvas onto which they are painting the version of themselves they drove three hours to become. Sunday at 11:14 a.m., the canvas has been used. The weekend has happened to people. The party, as an entity, has filed for divorce and is already arguing about who gets the tent poles.

The eyelashes that were a whole personality on Friday night are now a small gray crescent on the camp table next to a half-empty bottle of LMNT, a dead vape pen still being inhaled hopefully like a sad little flute, someone’s kandi cuff that dissolved slightly in the rain on Saturday, and the camp cooler, which has become a small contained ecosystem with its own microclimate and possibly its own spiritual tradition.

One DJ is still going somewhere in the tree line, which is either dedication or a hostage situation.

The porta line smells like a war crime.

Strike has started.

This is when the camp tells the truth about itself. Not in its manifesto. Not in its annual photo in matching colors with good lighting and everyone present for the shot. In who is on their knees at 11:14 a.m. and who is on cushions, and what the distance between those two groups says about how the camp actually runs.

Out by the kitchen, three people are doing work.

Dinah is on her knees breaking down the propane stove. She is forty-one, a structural engineer in her default life, the person who read the load-bearing specs on the shade structure in 2017 and has quietly kept everyone under it safe from a windstorm every year since. She has been at this camp for eight years. She has been on her knees since seven a.m. Her water bottle has been empty for forty minutes. Nobody has refilled it. Not because anyone decided not to — the decision was never made. It just did not occur to the people who are not on their knees that the person on their knees might need water, which is itself a finding.

Hugo is folding the parachute cloth with the focused fury that only genuinely sober people can summon at this hour. Seven years clean. Six full builds. The single person in this camp whose Sunday morning is not also a hangover. He has been working since six-fifteen a.m. He can see the inner-circle cushions from where he stands. He is not looking at them. He is looking at the parachute and he is thinking something specific that he has not yet said out loud but that he will say, at eleven-fourteen a.m., to Dinah, when he puts down the parachute and walks the twelve feet.

Tomás is sorting bins. He joined the camp last year. He has not stopped doing labor since Thursday at nine a.m. He has lost four pounds. He has the specific two-thousand-yard stare of a man who was told this camp is family and is standing at the exact moment he realizes that family, in this camp, is a verb that means do the strike. He has a particular stillness that is not peace. It is the stillness of someone holding a very specific question very carefully and not yet knowing whether it is safe to put it down.

The question is: if this is family, why am I the one carrying boxes.

It is a good question. He will get an answer today. Not from Lorelei. From Hugo, at approximately eleven-fourteen and thirty seconds, when Hugo sits down next to Dinah and says the thing.

Eight feet away, in the last remaining patch of shade on the entire lot, the inner circle is having a Moment.

Five people on cushions. Not build cushions — actual brought-from-home meditation cushions in coordinated earth tones, which were carried to the festival in a dedicated padded bag and which have never touched a tent stake.

Lorelei the camp lead, who founded the camp in 2016, which she will tell you only when it is strategically useful. She is holding a hand-thrown ceramic mug that says HOLD SPACE / NOT GRUDGES in tasteful sans-serif. She does not technically own the truck. She does not technically own the trailer. She does, however, control the group chat — admin since founding, never transferred; the build list; the volunteer schedule; the wristband distribution, which she describes as “intuitive” and which has been the same intuition for nine years running; the relationship with the festival organizers; the camp Instagram; the password to the shared Dropbox where the budget allegedly lives. The budget has not been seen by anyone outside the inner circle in four years. Lorelei calls this “streamlined.”

Brett, her partner, is half-listening while rolling something with the unhurried hands of a man who has not done a meaningful camp labor task since 2019 and who is universally beloved because Brett is “the vibes.” Brett’s actual contribution this weekend was being charming at the Saturday afters and bringing two pints of mango sorbet that he ate himself in the trailer with the door closed. This is known. It is not addressed. It is the kind of thing that gets filed under Brett is Brett, which is the specific cognitive category for beloved people whose account is perpetually overdrawn but whose social credit is inexhaustible.

Marisol, three years in, is nodding emphatically. Marisol identified in year one that proximity to Lorelei is the actual currency in this camp and has been spending it carefully ever since. Marisol does not do dishes. Marisol “holds the emotional container,” which is one of those phrases that consistently means the same three other people will be doing the dishes. Marisol is on the camp’s annual poster, which has required an ongoing and gradually more creative rewriting of contribution history. Nobody has said this out loud. Dinah has said it in her head approximately forty times.

Ash, returning camper, is mostly quiet. Ash has been watching this camp long enough to know that quiet is the only safe posture when the inner circle is convening, especially on Sunday at eleven a.m. when the weekend has happened and the accounting has not yet been done. Ash has watched this exact type of conversation happen in 2022, 2023, and 2024 — always on Sunday morning, always about someone in the middle ring who had started asking questions, always ending with that person not being at the camp next year. Ash has not asked questions. This is not wisdom. It is the survival adaptation of someone who decided that belonging costs a certain amount of visibility and has been paying that toll for four years.

Pippa is first season. She is sitting slightly too straight and laughing slightly too hard at things that are not jokes. She is being onboarded. She thinks she is in. She is, in fact, being assessed. The assessment has been running since Thursday at two p.m. when Pippa arrived and immediately and genuinely started helping with the shade structure, which is how the camp identifies labor capacity in new arrivals. Lorelei noticed. Lorelei noted. The note is in a category that does not get written down but that has been translated, in previous seasons, into specific requests made approximately four months from now when the build planning starts.

The Moment they are having is about Dinah.

Lorelei is saying, in a soft and worried voice, that Dinah has been “a little intense” this weekend. She noticed Dinah seemed to be “carrying a lot of energy” during the build. She is “wondering if maybe this camp isn’t quite the right fit for her next year.”

Dinah is twelve feet away on her knees with an empty water bottle and a propane stove and eight years of structural labor.

Lorelei has not spoken to Dinah about this.

Lorelei will not speak to Dinah about this. The conversation will happen in the inner circle. The inner circle will arrive at a consensus. The consensus will be deployed, eventually, as a gentle and regretful and loving message from Lorelei that uses the word “container” twice and frames Dinah’s departure as a personal growth chapter rather than a managed exit.

Marisol nods. “Honestly, I’ve been feeling that too.”

Brett says: “She just runs hot.”

Brett does not know what “hot” means in this sentence. Brett does not know what most sentences mean. Brett is, however, agreeing, which is his only structural function in the camp, and he performs it reliably.

Ash says nothing. Ash is staring at a fly on a cushion with the philosophical intensity of someone who is watching a small terrible thing happen and has decided not to be the person who names it, at least not today.

Pippa feels her stomach do something. She has known Dinah for forty-eight hours and she watched Dinah fix the generator at two a.m. Friday and intercept a guy named Skip who was walking off with the wrong cooler on Saturday and stay on her knees with the propane stove since seven this morning while the inner circle arranged their matching cushions in the shade.

Pippa is new. Pippa is not going to say anything. The math of her social capital in this camp is still negative — she has spent the weekend building credit and she does not yet have enough to spend it on a public disagreement with the camp lead. This is a rational calculation. It is also exactly how the cartel persists from season to season: by recruiting people who are in the calculation phase before they have enough information to know the math is wrong.

Hugo’s phone buzzes.

He reads it.

It is the camp group chat.

Lorelei has posted: “Just want to hold space for checking in on each other during strike. How’s everyone’s energy? Feeling some intensity in the field and want to make sure we’re tending to each other.”

Hugo reads it once. He reads it again.

He puts his phone in his pocket.

He does not move for a moment. Nine years at this camp and he has watched this conversation happen from a distance three separate times and every time he has told himself it was not his to interrupt. The ninth year is heavy. The parachute is right here in his hands and folding it is a task that would keep him honestly busy for another twenty minutes.

The twelve feet is not the hard part. The twelve feet takes four seconds.

The hard part is the second before it.

He puts down the parachute. He picks up his water bottle, which is still half-full because Hugo planned ahead, which is a habit of people who have been doing the actual work for long enough to know the water runs out.

He walks twelve feet.

He sits down next to Dinah.

He says: “Dinah. Stop. I’ll finish the stove. Here’s my water.”

She takes the bottle. She drinks half of it. She does not say anything for a moment.

He says: “I think you should leave this camp next year. Not because of you. Because of them.”

She cries for thirty seconds. Not performance. The tired kind, the weight-releasing kind, the kind that happens when someone finally says the sentence you have been carrying in your own body for two years.

She says: “Are you leaving too?”

He says: “Yeah. I just decided.”

Tomás is six feet away, still holding a bin. He is looking at them. He is doing the math. He has had enough information now.

Hugo looks at him and says: “Come sit down.”

Tomás puts the bin down.

This is how the cartel ends. Not in lightning. Not in confrontation. Not in a public reckoning or a manifesto rebuttal or a scene in the group chat. It ends in twelve feet and a water bottle and the sentence that has been true for two years being said to the person it needed to reach.

Twelve feet.

That is the distance of the entire chapter.

The Soundboard Quote

— Reggie “Wingnut” Okonkwo, fifty-two, build lead and owner of the actual truck, fifteen years welding shade structures across three states and two countries, current holder of the title “the person who knows where everything is” in every camp he has ever worked with and the one person in every camp whose opinion on the shade structure is sought entirely after it is built. Has worked with fourteen camps. Has identified the Camp Cartel pattern in eleven of them. Has left four camps, one after the conversation with the camp lead and three without. Currently co-running a small build camp called Service Road that has a four-sentence manifesto and a shared spreadsheet where anyone can see the budget. Recorded Sunday morning at strike while on his hands and knees under a frame looking for a clevis pin while three people in matching saffron discussed the “container” eight feet away.

The Real F*cking Setlist

What this chapter is actually about

This chapter is about how camps — the thing this scene invented that is genuinely one of its most extraordinary gifts, the chosen family structure that has held people through divorces and deaths and sobriety milestones and gender transitions and thirty-year friendships — get turned into governance structures by people who would never tolerate being called a government. We are covering seven moves, and before we cover them we are saying clearly: camps are not the problem. This scene would not exist without them. The truck, the trailer, the kitchen, the shade, the generator, the coffee at six a.m. for a stranger — that is real and it is worth protecting. This chapter is for protecting it from the small, mean, quiet version that puts the manifesto on the outside and runs the cartel underneath.

The Tapers’ Section

Janis · Whisper Networks · Power Without Process

Quick translation. Then back to the dust.

Irving Janis named groupthink in the 1970s: the thing that happens when a small cohesive group becomes so committed to its own harmony that it stops being able to process reality. In festival language: when your camp has lost the ability to hear a true sentence because it would inconvenience the vibe, the vibe is now governing. The vibe is the king. Everyone else is a peasant in tie-dye with a parachute to fold.

Whisper networks are informal information channels people build when formal accountability systems fail them. They are sometimes literally lifesaving. They are also, sometimes, run by the exact people who created the conditions that made formal protection impossible. Both can be true. The test is whether the whisper protects the vulnerable or protects the powerful. A whisper that warns first-timers about a known predator is sacred. A whisper that runs “Dinah has been intense” through the inner circle while Dinah is on her knees at seven a.m. is governance in a soft voice.

Power without process is the concept that describes what Lorelei has. She controls the chat, the budget, the wristbands, the relationships, and the narrative — and none of it is written down, transferred, or accountable to the camp’s stated values. The power is real. The process that would make it legible and contestable does not exist. That asymmetry is not an accident. Unwritten power is unchallenged power. The HOLD SPACE / NOT GRUDGES mug is the PR department of unwritten power.

That is enough theory. Put it under the truck.

You are in.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Camp Mom Power Grab

(informal authority capture)

The Behavior

She — or he, or they, but let’s stay with she for this instance because the Camp Mom archetype has a specific gendered flavor in most scenes — started as the person who just “took care of things.” The person who made sure the kitchen was stocked. Who remembered birthdays. Who texted when someone seemed off. Who knew where the extra tent stakes were. Who had the festival organizer’s cell number. Who handled it. The care was real. The competence was real. The centrality that resulted was also real and also, gradually, structural.

The Camp Mom Power Grab is the conversion of informal care-role into informal governance authority. She controls the group chat because she set it up and never transferred admin. She controls the wristband distribution because she handled the relationship with the festival contact and knows the allocation system. She controls the build list because she’s the one who’s always done it. She controls the narrative because she’s been there the longest and people default to her read on things.

None of these controls were seized in a coup. They accumulated through years of competent caregiving in an environment that never developed formal processes to distribute the power the competence generated. The problem is not the competence. The problem is what the concentration of power begins to do: it becomes the mechanism for excluding people who ask inconvenient questions, for managing exits, for controlling the camp’s self-image, for ensuring the cartel’s longevity.

“Lorelei has always handled that” becomes “Lorelei is the only one who can handle that” becomes “questioning Lorelei’s handling is questioning the camp’s ability to function.”

That last one is governance.

The Read

Ask: can Lorelei — or whoever holds the informal authority role — leave the camp for a season and have it run without crisis? If the answer is no, the camp has built a throne instead of a structure. Ask: who inherits the keys, the relationships, the accounts, the permissions if the Camp Mom steps back? If there is no answer, the succession was never planned because succession was never supposed to happen.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Formal Distribution Ask

Step 1 — Make the ask in the context of care, not confrontation: “I want to take some of the load off you. Walk me through the trailer system and the organizer contact so I have it too.”

Step 2 — Ask for the budget. Not accusatorially. As a participant: “Can the full camp see this year’s financials? I want to understand how we’re resourced.”

Step 3 — Request that admin access be shared on the group chat, the cloud folder, the social accounts. Formally. As a proposal.

Step 4 — If all three are refused or deflected: the power is not informal. It is intentional. You now have information about the camp’s actual structure.

Vibe Check

Some camp leads are just good at their jobs and have earned their central role through decades of genuine competence. Centrality is not automatically a Grab. The test is: does the central person try to distribute their power over time, or do they consolidate it? Do they cross-train, or do they make themselves indispensable?

Mirror Set

If you are the central person: when did you last try to give away a piece of power you didn’t have to give away? When did you last cross-train someone in a function you control? If the honest answer is “I haven’t, because it’s easier to handle it myself” — you may have built a throne without noticing. Examine the floor.

Refractions

BIPOC Camp Leads: When the central authority role is held by a BIPOC person in a predominantly white camp, the dynamics of accountability become additionally complex — questioning authority can be racially coded as attack, and the Camp Mom herself may be using her position to protect the community she has built. The structural problem is still the structural problem. Accountability and care for the person can coexist.

Disabled Leads: If the central person also carries a disability that makes certain kinds of labor impossible and for which the camp has no protocol — the authority and the access question are entangled with accessibility. Untangle them separately.

Runs in Every Direction

The Camp Mom Power Grab runs in camps led by femmes, mascs, nonbinary elders, sober mentors, queer organizers, and anyone who started doing care work and was never asked to give the keys back. The grab is available to anyone who is competent enough, present enough, and patient enough to become indispensable before the camp develops the structures that would make indispensability impossible.

Sober Tuesday

The employee who has been doing the job long enough that only they know the password to the critical system, the institutional memory of the vendor relationship, and the location of the files. When they leave, there is a crisis. That is not a tribute to their dedication. That is a management failure that the organization tolerated for years because the crisis was always future.

Track 02: The Wristband Hierarchy

(credential gating / access stratification)

The Behavior

The festival gives the camp a number of artist, staff, or volunteer passes. The camp distributes them. Lorelei decides who gets the upgrade. The decision is made on an “intuitive” basis that, examined over nine years, has produced the same outcome: inner-circle members and new recruits being assessed get the access. Middle-ring members who have been doing structural labor for years get general admission.

The wristband is not just a wristband. At a festival, access is status, belonging, and logistical freedom. The artist pass means you can be where the action is. The staff band means you can navigate the site at three a.m. without being questioned. The volunteer credential means your labor is legible to the festival’s infrastructure as real. The difference between the bands is the difference between being inside the camp and being of the camp.

When the band distribution is controlled by one person and never explained, it becomes a loyalty metric. The people who ask questions get general. The people who do extra labor and don’t ask questions sometimes get access. The people who align with the inner ring get the upgrade. The bands are the camp’s visible reward system for invisible compliance.

The Read

Ask: what is the process for access distribution? If the answer is “Lorelei handles it” or “it’s intuitive” or “it’s based on what the camp needs” — ask who made the decision about need. If nobody can name the criteria, the criteria are not organizational. They are personal. Personal criteria in access distribution are always, eventually, loyalty criteria.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Transparent Access Proposal

Step 1 — Propose a written access-distribution process before the festival: roles, hours, criteria. Transparent to all camp members.

Step 2 — Ask for the camp’s access allocation from the festival in writing. Who has how many of what kind.

Step 3 — Compare the distribution to the labor log. If the people doing the most work don’t have the most access, name that discrepancy specifically: “Dinah ran the build for eight years and gets general admission. The inner circle gets artist passes. I want to understand the criteria.”

Step 4 — If the criteria cannot survive being named out loud, they are not criteria. They are preferences.

Vibe Check

Some camps have genuinely complex access logistics driven by their actual festival function — sound camps need staff bands for their sound crew, art car camps need vehicle passes for their drivers. Functional access is real. The test is whether the function explains the full distribution, or whether the function explains some of it and the rest is relational.

Mirror Set

Have you ever advocated for someone’s access upgrade based primarily on how much you liked them? Have you ever withheld the information about how access gets distributed because knowing the process would empower the people asking about it? The hierarchy is not always conscious. But it always has an architect.

Refractions

BIPOC and Queer Artists: When access distribution in predominantly white scenes follows patterns that consistently favor white inner-ring members over BIPOC and queer artists doing structural labor, the wristband hierarchy is also a racial and identity hierarchy. Name both layers.

First Season: You don’t yet know what the baseline access distribution looks like. Ask before you accept what you’re offered as normal. “What access do returning middle-ring members typically get?”

Runs in Every Direction

The Wristband Hierarchy is managed by whoever controls the relationship with the festival and whoever controls the camp. Both can be any gender or identity. The hierarchy itself is structural and runs regardless of the identity of the person managing it.

Sober Tuesday

The office where some people get the conference room booking system and some people have to ask someone else to book it for them. The functional difference is small. The status signal is large. Access to access is always partly about belonging.

Track 03: The “We Don’t Do That Here” Soft Exile

(norms enforcement as ostracism)

The Behavior

Nobody is kicked out. Kicking people out is messy and documentable and creates the kind of incident that gets named. Instead, the Soft Exile is the withdrawal of the things that make belonging feel like belonging: warmth, the easy inclusion, the invitation to the inner-circle conversations, the access to information that other people seem to have, the sense that the camp is glad you are in it.

Hugo asked about the bank account in year five. He did not accuse anyone of anything. He asked: “Can we all see the camp financials?” The question was reasonable. It was the question of a person who had built the camp’s infrastructure for five years and wanted to understand how the camp was resourced.

The response was not a refusal. A refusal can be contested. The response was a shift in temperature. Lorelei became slightly more formal with Hugo. The invitations to the inner-circle conversations started going to different people. Hugo’s input at the build planning meeting was received and then not referenced. Nothing was said. The exile had no incident. It was conducted entirely through the management of warmth, and by the time Hugo could clearly name what was happening it had been happening for three years.

The Read

Watch the temperature gradient. After a question, a concern, a boundary, or a no — does the warmth level stay the same? Does information still flow to you as easily as before? Do you still get the invitation to the things you used to get invited to? The Soft Exile runs on the hypothesis that you will attribute the cooling to your own behavior — that you will decide you did something wrong and self-correct, or that you will decide the environment is unwelcoming and leave. Either outcome serves the cartel.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Draft Check

Step 1 — Name the temperature shift directly and early: “I’ve noticed things have felt a little different since I raised the financial question. Is there something I should know?”

Step 2 — If the response is “I don’t know what you’re talking about” or “you might be reading into it” — that is information. The exile is confirmed.

Step 3 — Build lateral. The Soft Exile works by making the inner ring’s warmth your primary source of belonging. If you have strong relationships with middle-ring members independently — built without going through the inner ring — the exile has less power.

Step 4 — If the exile continues after the direct conversation: name it to the middle ring. Not dramatically. Factually: “I asked about the budget and the temperature changed. Has anyone else had that experience?”

Vibe Check

Sometimes people genuinely become more distant after a hard conversation, not because they’re running a cartel, but because the conversation stirred something that needs processing time. The test is duration and proportion: does normal warmth return within a reasonable time, or does the cooling become a permanent new baseline?

Mirror Set

Have you ever gotten colder toward someone after they asked a question you didn’t like? Have you ever stopped including someone in the inner conversations after they raised a concern? Have you ever withheld warmth as a governance tool without calling it that? The Soft Exile is the most passive of the cartel Tracks and the one most likely to be run without full awareness.

Refractions

Veteran Festies in Middle Ring: If you have been around long enough to notice the pattern across multiple seasons, you are also in the position to name it to newer members before they lose years to the calculation phase. That conversation — “here is what I’ve seen happen to people who ask that question” — is one of the most useful things an experienced person can offer.

Runs in Every Direction

The Soft Exile can be run by camp moms, camp dads, nonbinary elders, beloved femme organizers, and quiet male camp leads who never say anything explicitly but are the thermometer everyone else reads. The exile is conducted through social temperature. Temperature has no gender.

Sober Tuesday

The work team where someone who raised a safety concern in a meeting is no longer included on the project updates email. Nothing was said. The exile happened in the cc field.

Track 04: The Group Chat Flying Monkey Squad

(proxy harassment / coalition manipulation)

The Behavior

Lorelei does not run the concern about Dinah herself. She raises it in the inner circle. The inner circle distributes it. By Sunday evening, three camp members have independently checked in with Dinah in ways that feel personal and caring but that are carrying the same information — “is everything okay, you seemed like you were carrying a lot of energy this weekend” — because the information was seeded at eleven a.m. on cushions while Dinah was on her knees.

The group chat runs the same way. A message doesn’t have to be an attack to be a governance tool. “Just want to hold space for checking in on each other during strike. How’s everyone’s energy?” is a message that means multiple things simultaneously: a genuine check-in, a soft reframe of the labor situation as an emotional situation, and a signal to the inner circle that the camp lead is observing.

The Flying Monkey Squad is the deployment of the social network as a distributed enforcement and narrative-management tool. Individual members of the squad often do not know they are in the squad. They have been seeded with a read on a situation and they are acting on it in good faith, which is what makes the pattern so hard to contest from the inside.

The Read

When multiple people deliver the same message independently within a short time window — especially after a question was raised or a concern was named — ask: where did this message originate? Did these three people arrive at this concern simultaneously and independently, or is there a source? If there is a source, what is the source’s interest in the message being distributed?

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Origin Audit

Step 1 — When you receive the third version of the same message from three different people: “I notice a few people have said something similar this week. Where is this coming from?”

Step 2 — If the source names themselves, go to the source directly. “It sounds like there’s a concern about me. I want to hear it from you directly.”

Step 3 — If the source does not name themselves, treat the distributed message as a signal about the camp’s governance, not as three independent data points about your behavior.

Step 4 — Refuse to respond to the indirect version. The indirect version is the mechanism. “If there’s a concern, I want to be in the room when it’s raised. I’m not going to process it in a side channel.”

Vibe Check

Communities do genuinely check in on each other. Multiple people caring about one person simultaneously is not automatically a squad. The test is whether the check-ins are carrying the same content — the same framing, the same concern, the same read — which suggests a shared origin, or whether they are varied and independently derived.

Mirror Set

Have you ever seeded a read on someone and then watched it travel through the camp’s social network? Have you ever been the person who “just wanted to check in” on someone because someone you trusted told you something about them before you had your own experience? The Flying Monkey Squad does not require malice. It requires people who trust the source and act on the information.

Refractions

Harm-Reduction Crew: You are particularly susceptible to being recruited as a Flying Monkey because your role legitimizes welfare checks. If you are sent to check on someone by a camp lead, ask yourself what the camp lead’s interest in that check is.

Queer Scene: In small queer scenes, the Flying Monkey dynamic can run with devastating efficiency because social networks are dense and trust is high. A seeded read travels further and faster.

Runs in Every Direction

The Squad is assembled by camp leads, inner-circle members, and anyone with enough social capital to make their read on a situation spread as ambient community concern. The assembler can be any gender or identity. The squad members are usually acting in good faith.

Sober Tuesday

The manager who mentions to three different team members separately that they’re “a little worried about how X has been handling the project,” before X has been given any direct feedback. The performance conversation has been seeded in the culture before it happens in the room.

Track 05: The First-Season Funnel

(newcomer lovebombing)

The Behavior

Pippa arrives on Thursday and is immediately, intensely welcomed. “We’ve been waiting for someone like you.” She is included in conversations that feel like the inner circle. She is given the tour not just of the physical camp but of the camp’s mythology — the time it rained and the generator died and everyone held it together, the year a famous artist stopped by, the founding story told with a warmth that communicates: you are now part of something real.

She is called family by Friday.

She does not know that she is being assessed, not welcomed. She does not know that the warmth is calibrating her labor capacity, her social compliance, her potential as a future middle-ring member. She does not know that the word “family” in this camp’s usage is a verb that means “you have been earmarked.”

By Sunday morning she has done two generator fixes, one shovel intervention, and the full kitchen prep for eighty people. She has not once been brought water by an inner-ring member. She has brought water to inner-ring members twice.

The Funnel converts the genuine longing for belonging — which is most powerful in new arrivals, who have not yet built lateral connections in the scene and are dependent on the camp’s warmth for their entire festival experience — into a labor and loyalty pipeline. The lovebomb is the open valve. The work starts flowing before the person understands the structure of the pipe.

The Read

How fast did you get called family? How much labor have you done versus how much access have you received? What happened to the previous first-season people — are they still here, or did they leave after one season, and what does the official story say about why?

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Season Pace Protocol

Step 1 — Receive the welcome warmly and genuinely. The warmth may be real.

Step 2 — Decline the “family” frame for at least one full season: “I’m really glad to be here. I want to earn that over time.” Watch how this lands.

Step 3 — Observe the labor distribution before you contribute at an intensive level. Who else is doing what work? Are the people who have been here for years doing proportionate or less labor than first-season arrivals?

Step 4 — Ask about the people who left: “Who was in camp in year six? Where are they now?” The answer to this question is camp archaeology.

Step 5 — Talk to someone who has been in the camp for three or more years who is not inner-ring. Middle-ring perspective is the most accurate map of the actual terrain.

Vibe Check

Some camps genuinely are family from the first day and mean it, and the warmth and the structure and the labor and the access all flow in roughly proportionate ways. The test is symmetry: do first-season members have proportionate labor, access, and voice, or is the labor disproportionate to the access and voice while the “family” language is disproportionate to the actual duration and depth of relationship?

Mirror Set

If you are an established camp member: have you personally made sure the first-season arrivals this year had what they needed, or did you observe their labor from a distance while being impressed by their contribution? Have you checked in on their workload as you would check in on a family member who is new to a physically demanding situation? If not, the family framing was one-directional.

Refractions

New to Scene: Your first camp invitation is the most powerful social experience you will have in your first festival year. The intensity of the welcome is not proportionate to the depth of the relationship. Pace yourself. Build lateral connections outside the camp.

Live Painters, Glovers, Wire-Wrap Artists: If a camp recruits you because your craft makes their camp look beautiful in photos, they are also recruiting your unpaid contribution as a brand asset. You are not just a camper. Negotiate your role accordingly.

Runs in Every Direction

The Funnel happens in every kind of camp across every identity composition. Mutual aid camps, sober camps, queer family camps, burner mega-camps, touring crews. The mechanism is: intense early belonging, quiet escalating labor, delayed inclusion in actual power. The mechanism does not care what the manifesto says.

Sober Tuesday

The job that starts with two weeks of your new manager being incredibly warm, enthusiastic about your potential, and then in week three quietly redirecting approximately forty percent of someone else’s workload onto your desk as a “growth opportunity.” The warmth was real. It was also the pipeline being opened.

Track 06: The PLUR Mask, Toxic Camp

(façade management / impression management)

The Behavior

The camp has a manifesto. The manifesto talks about consent, accountability, harm reduction, mutual aid, intersectionality, healing, radical welcome, and the protection of the magic. It was written in 2017 and has been updated once since then, mostly in the paragraph about queer identity.

The camp has a curated Instagram. The bios are luminous. The opening-night speech by the camp lead is about “the world we are practicing.” The annual photo is warm-lit, matching colors, everyone present, shot on Friday before the weekend has happened to anyone.

The vocabulary is fluent. Everyone says “I noticed,” “I’m holding,” “I have a need,” “I want to honor what you’re saying,” the language of careful mutual tending.

And then at 11:14 a.m. on Sunday, the inner circle is on cushions assessing the camp’s structural engineer because she asked about the bank account in year five, while she is twelve feet away on her knees with an empty water bottle, and the three Flying Monkeys are checking in on her with the same read, and the group chat is asking about everyone’s energy, and Pippa who just did two generator fixes is being cataloged for the build coordinator role nobody told her was open.

The mask is perfect. The camp is running.

The PLUR Mask is the most dangerous version of the Cartel because it weaponizes the actual language of liberation against the people the language was supposed to protect. The concern-raising vocabulary is so fluent that anyone who raises a concern will have their concern managed using the same vocabulary, by someone who uses it better. The accountability language is so present that accountability conversations are always available as a theoretical option and never executed in practice. The manifesto is so beautiful that pointing at the gap between the manifesto and the behavior feels like attacking something precious.

The mask is the manifesto’s perfect evil twin.

The Read

Ask two questions. First: When was the last time the manifesto language was used to make someone in power uncomfortable rather than to manage someone without it? Second: If you raised a plain behavioral concern — not a vocabulary concern, a plain sentence about dishes or labor or access or money — what would happen?

The plain sentence test is the mask detector. Masks cannot survive plain language. They respond to plain language with more vocabulary. If the vocabulary escalates in response to plain language, the vocabulary is the wall.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Plain Sentence Protocol

Step 1 — Identify one concern. State it in the most boring factual language possible. “Hugo has been doing the build for six years and doesn’t have a staff pass. Lorelei has had a staff pass since founding. I want to understand the criteria for the distribution.”

Step 2 — Decline to rephrase in vocabulary. If asked to “reframe this in terms of needs,” say: “I’d rather keep it in behavioral terms. Hugo has been doing structural labor for six years without the access that others with equivalent tenure have.”

Step 3 — Document the response. If the plain sentence produces vocabulary management rather than a direct answer to the behavioral question, that response is information about the camp’s actual function.

Step 4 — Decide whether the camp can hear plain language. Some camps can — they take the hit, examine the behavior, redistribute. Those camps are worth staying for. Some camps cannot, because the vocabulary is the architecture and removing it collapses the power structure. Those camps are worth leaving.

Vibe Check

Beautiful manifestos are not the enemy. Real consent culture, real harm reduction, real mutual aid, real accountability practice — all of these are real and they are worth the words. The Track is when the language is consistently used as a weapon against the people the language was supposed to protect. If the camp’s manifesto would be embarrassed by the camp’s actual Sunday morning behavior, the manifesto is mask, not mirror.

Mirror Set

If you wrote the camp’s manifesto: when was the last time the manifesto was applied to a hard situation in a way that made you — not an outsider, not a dissident, you — uncomfortable? A real manifesto is occasionally uncomfortable for everyone in the camp. A mask manifesto is uncomfortable only for outsiders and the freshly exiled.

Refractions

Sober and Recovery Lane: Recovery vocabulary deployed in service of a cartel is specifically toxic because it presents control as health. Watch for camps that say “we hold each other accountable” while running Soft Exiles on anyone who asks about the money.

Activist and Movement Lane: Movement vocabulary deployed in service of a cartel is also specifically toxic. Watch for camps that say “we center the most marginalized” while actually centering the most photogenic.

Runs in Every Direction

The mask is verbal. Anyone fluent enough in the values vocabulary can wear it. Identity is not protection against the mask and identity does not make the mask acceptable. The mask runs across every camp demographic.

Sober Tuesday

The nonprofit board that has a beautifully worded mission statement, a values page on its website, and a grievance process that routes directly back to the executive director who is the subject of the complaint. The vocabulary is real. The structure makes the vocabulary impossible to use. That is the mask in an org chart.

Track 07: The Gift Economy Skim

(sacred labor extraction)

The Behavior

The camp has values. The values include service, mutual aid, contribution, gift economy, leave-no-trace, and “being of use to the magic.” Some of these values are completely genuine and run through the camp’s actual behavior like a spine. And some of them have been quietly turned into a labor extraction engine that benefits the inner ring at the expense of the middle ring and the first-season arrivals.

The pattern is not symmetrical.

Dinah, Hugo, and Tomás do most of the structural work. The inner ring does some visible, photographable work. The people who are adjacent to the inner ring do varying amounts. And the inner-ring members do none of the structural work and are not held accountable for this, because asking them to contribute proportionately would be “transactional,” or “default-world thinking,” or “not in the gift economy,” or “not in alignment with their capacity this season,” which is a phrase that seems to apply to the inner ring exclusively.

When the middle-ring workers are tired, they are told they are “leading from love.” When they ask for help, they are told “the camp is not built on guilt.” When they stop showing up, the camp’s values mysteriously shift to “honoring people’s capacity.” When they leave entirely, the camp produces a beautiful Instagram post about their departure that frames it as personal growth and doesn’t mention the labor distribution.

Then the camp recruits three new workers.

The Gift Economy Skim is the engine of cartel longevity.

The Read

Follow the labor. Who does the build, the kitchen, the strike, the storage run, the post-event cleaning? Then follow the access and the credit. Who gets the artist passes, the annual poster, the year-end acknowledgment, the inner-circle conversations? If the labor names and the credit names are different people, you are watching the skim operate.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Labor Cap

Step 1 — Before the season, set a labor cap. Specific, written, personal: “I’m available for X hours of build and Y hours of kitchen. That’s my contribution ceiling this season.”

Step 2 — Name the cap to the camp lead before the event. Not apologetically. As a logistical fact.

Step 3 — When you hit the cap: “I’ve hit my labor cap for this season.” Full stop. No negotiation in the context of festival conditions where saying no feels like abandoning family. The cap was set sober, in writing, in advance.

Step 4 — Ask for the labor map: “Can we do a quick labor audit before the season starts — who does what, for how many hours, across build, kitchen, and strike?” A camp that resists the labor audit is a camp that knows the audit will be damning.

Step 5 — If the labor map reveals the Skim: “I’m not willing to do more structural labor than inner-ring members this season. If there isn’t a way to equalize that, I need to reconsider my role here.”

Vibe Check

Some people genuinely cannot do physical labor. Disability, chronic pain, recovery from injury, mental health conditions, and caregiving responsibilities all affect what people can contribute physically. That is real and the camp should accommodate it. The test is whether the accommodation applies equally or whether it applies selectively based on inner-ring status.

Mirror Set

Have you ever benefited from an asymmetrical labor structure and told yourself you contributed in other ways without examining whether those other ways were actually equivalent? Have you ever invoked “gift economy” to avoid a direct conversation about who is doing more work than their fair share? If yes: “leading from love” is a sentence that describes someone else’s contribution while you sit on cushions.

Refractions

Artists, Live Painters, Glovers, Hoopers: If your craft is being treated as your camp contribution while you are also being asked to do structural labor, name that. Your performance is not equivalent to build labor. You can do both. You do not owe both.

First Season: You are most vulnerable to the Skim in your first year because the social equation is not yet clear and the lovebomb is still active. The labor cap set before the event is the only protection that is active before you need it.

Runs in Every Direction

The Gift Economy Skim requires people doing the structural labor and people not doing it and a values framework that prevents the discrepancy from being named. All three elements run across every camp identity. The mechanism is available to any camp that has values language, a labor-intensive existence, and an inner ring.

Sober Tuesday

The team where “we’re a startup family” is the reason nobody is paid overtime. The gift economy of startup culture is the Tuesday version of the same skim, with equity instead of artist passes and “mission alignment” instead of “being of use to the magic.”

Have You Been the Wook?

Mirror Check At The Trailer

You have been in Lorelei’s camp, or you have been Lorelei, or you have been Marisol sitting on the cushions while Dinah was on her knees, or you have been Ash watching and staying quiet because the calculation said quiet was safer.

Have you ever called a newcomer “family” before they’d earned it, because naming the family was useful to you? Have you ever controlled a resource — the chat, the budget, the passes, the relationships — and declined to share it, not because sharing would hurt the camp, but because sharing would reduce your leverage? Have you ever felt the warmth toward a camp member diminish after they asked a question you found inconvenient, and attributed the cooling to something about them? Have you ever sat on cushions while other people did the work and told yourself you were contributing in other ways?

The Camp Cartel does not require a villain in linen. It requires a culture that has mistaken the performance of values for the practice of them, a power structure that accumulated quietly over years of competent caregiving, and a social environment where asking about the bank account is treated as a betrayal of the magic.

Most of the people running it are not bad people. They are people who found a structure that worked for them and stopped questioning whether it worked for everyone else.

The question is not “have you ever run one of these Tracks.” The question is: when you are next on cushions while someone is on their knees, do you see the twelve feet between you?

Do you walk it?

The Wook Discog

Studio Debut — A Storage Unit, A Truck Schedule, And Real Love For It

Lorelei in year one, 2016. She genuinely built something. She coordinated the storage unit and the truck and the organizer relationships and the build plan because she was the one who wanted it enough to do it. She genuinely called it family because she genuinely felt it. The control was functional, not political. The power was informal because it was too early to formalize. The first season was real. The studio debut sounds like exactly what it was: someone who loved the scene enough to build a thing and called everyone in it family because they felt like family. That is not a Track. That is a foundation. The Track started when the foundation calcified into architecture.

Live Album — The Manifesto Has A Visual Brand Now

Lorelei in year seven. The manifesto has been updated. The Instagram has a distinct visual brand. The first-season funnel is running with the efficiency of something that has been refined through repetition. The Soft Exile has taken three people. The budget has not been seen by the full camp in four years. The vocabulary is fluent enough that any concern can be managed, any question can be reframed, any discomfort can be held in the container until it stops being inconvenient. Lorelei has not made a single explicit decision to run a cartel. She has made a thousand small decisions to maintain her position, each one individually defensible, collectively constituting a structure that serves her and costs Dinah and Hugo and Tomás years. The Live Album sounds like community. It is governance.

Greatest Hits Compilation — Community. Spelled Governance.

The festival within the festival. The camp that is large enough to have its own infrastructure: its own sound system, its own kitchen operation, its own healing village, its own volunteer coordination, its own social media presence, its own alumni network, its own physical infrastructure worth tens of thousands of dollars. The cartel at this scale is not run by one person. It is run by a board of four people who are all inner ring and none of whom have significant accountability to the three hundred people who constitute the camp’s middle ring and first-season arrivals. The budget is a nonprofit. The founder salary is real. The volunteer labor is classified as “contribution.” The wristband allocation is a staff meeting agenda item that the three hundred people have never been invited to. The Greatest Hits Compilation is profiled in scene media twice a year. The people who left are occasionally described as “not being ready for the container.”

Tales From the Strike Line — The Save

Witness POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

It was the same camp. The same Sunday. The same eleven-fourteen a.m.

Dinah had been on her knees for four hours.

The watermelon moment was this: Hugo picked up his water bottle, walked twelve feet, sat down next to her, and said: “Dinah. Stop. I’ll finish the stove. Here’s my water.”

She takes the bottle. She drinks half. She does not say anything for a moment because she is doing the thing where you receive care after a long period of not receiving it and your body does not know how to do it efficiently. It has to remember.

He says: “I think you should leave this camp next year. Not because of you. Because of them.”

She cries for thirty seconds.

Then she says: “Are you leaving too?”

He says: “Yeah. I just decided.”

Tomás puts down the bin. He comes and sits. Hugo looks at him and says: “We’re leaving. We don’t think you should keep doing this much labor for these people. We’re not telling you what to do. We’re telling you what we’re doing.”

Tomás cries for forty-five seconds. Then he says: “Where are you going to go?”

Hugo says: “We have a year to figure it out.”

They finish the strike. Together, cleanly, without a scene in the chat. They do not confront Lorelei. They do not post. They do not perform. They work the rest of the day and they are warm to the people they are warm to and they leave Sunday night with the full camp load and the knowledge of what they are going to do next.

In October, each sends a single message to Lorelei. Not coordinated timing. Different wording. Same substance.

Hugo: “Hi Lorelei. Thanks for nine years. I’m not going to be at the camp next season. No drama. Just a different chapter. Best to you and Brett.”

Dinah: “Hi Lorelei. After eight years, I’m stepping back. I love the people. The dynamics aren’t working for me anymore and I don’t have the energy to renegotiate them. Wishing you well.”

Tomás: “Hey Lorelei. Stepping back this year. I learned a lot. Going to figure out what I actually want to be in. Thank you for the welcome.”

Lorelei responds within an hour to each. All three responses use the word “container.” All three ask for a call to process. All three suggest the timing is “interesting” and there is perhaps “something coming up” that the leaver has not yet been able to name.

None of them take the call.

Hugo: “I’m not going to do a process call. I appreciate the offer. Goodnight.”

Dinah: “No call. Take care.”

Tomás: 👍

In February, Pippa gets asked to take on the build coordinator role — the role Hugo used to do, on top of her existing contribution, for the same camp dues, with no additional access or decision-making power. She writes to Dinah on Instagram. They have spoken four times in real life. “I’m sorry, this is weird. Can I ask you what happened?”

Dinah writes back: “Yes. Let’s get on a call.”

Over forty minutes, Dinah explains the seven Tracks. Not as a performance. As the information she wished she’d had in year two. She names them in the camp’s specific terms. The Camp Mom and the bank account. The wristband pattern. Hugo’s soft exile after the budget question. The Flying Monkey checks. The funnel and how Pippa was brought in. The manifesto and the gap. The labor distribution over eight years.

Pippa cries for about four minutes.

Then she says: “I just got asked to be the build coordinator.”

Dinah says: “Don’t say yes yet. Don’t even respond. Take a week.”

Pippa takes a week.

Pippa sends her own one-line message in March.

By May, Dinah, Hugo, Tomás, Pippa, and two middle-ring campers who had left in previous seasons and had been watching from the wings, start a small new camp called Service Road.

The Service Road manifesto is four sentences long:

We do labor together. We share access. We talk to each other directly. Brett is not the vibes here.

The last sentence is an inside joke that only makes sense if you were in Lorelei’s camp. They kept it in anyway. It earned its place.

The Fanny Pack

Front pocket — chapter essentials

Chapter 8. Under the cooler.

Camps that say “we don’t have a hierarchy” have a hierarchy. You can tell because the people at the top say it the most.

The Camp Mom Power Grab (informal authority capture): Power accumulated through care-role competence. Diagnose by asking who inherits the keys.

The Wristband Hierarchy (credential gating): Access distribution is a loyalty metric when the criteria aren’t written down. Ask for the criteria.

The “We Don’t Do That Here” Soft Exile (norms enforcement as ostracism): The cooling is the governance. Name the temperature shift early.

The Group Chat Flying Monkey Squad (proxy harassment): When three people deliver the same message independently, ask where it originated.

The First-Season Funnel (newcomer lovebombing): Receive the welcome. Decline the “family” frame for one full season. Observe the labor distribution before contributing at intensity.

The PLUR Mask, Toxic Camp (façade management): Plain language is the mask detector. If vocabulary escalates in response to plain language, the vocabulary is the wall.

The Gift Economy Skim (sacred labor extraction): Follow the labor. Follow the credit. If the names are different, the skim is running. Set the labor cap in writing before the event.

The cartel survives by being the only camp in your life. The remedy is more camps. Better ones. Honest ones.

The Soundcheck

The Camp Cartel Audit

Run this on the camp you are in, thinking of joining, or thinking of leaving. Seven maps. One session. A boring note titled “Camping Checklist” or “Gear List.”

1. The Key Map. Who has: storage unit and trailer key, bank account access, camp socials password, festival organizer relationships, build master file, group chat admin, inventory list, post-festival debrief invite. If most answers are one or two names, the power is consolidated.

2. The Labor Map. Last full season: who did the build, the kitchen, the strike, the storage run, post-event cleaning, conflict management, chat management. Compare to the Key Map. If the labor names and the key names are different people, you have a cartel.

3. The Voice Map. Last season: who got the loudest public thank-you, who got named in the year-end post, whose face was on the Instagram. Compare to the Labor Map. If credit names and labor names are different, you have publicity laundering.

4. The Exit Map. Last three seasons: who left, why according to the official narrative, why according to the people who left. If the gap is large, you have a cartel running Soft Exile and post-hoc rewrites.

5. The Onboarding Map. Newcomers last two seasons: how were they welcomed, how fast were they called family, how much labor did they do in season one, how many are still in year two. High-intensity welcome plus low year-two retention is a funnel.

6. The Vocabulary Map. Last season: how often was manifesto language used to enable accountability versus used to disable a concern. If the language disables more than it enables, the manifesto is a mask.

7. The Plain Sentence Test. Pick one concern. State it in plain language. Name. Behavior. No vocabulary. Observe the response. If the response is vocabulary management, Public Cry Coup, Concern Hostage, or Soft Exile: you have your answer.

The Sunrise Set

Zero slang. Just the true thing.

Camps are one of the most beautiful things this scene has ever made.

They are the chosen family some of us never had in the families we were born into. They are the structure that makes a festival possible. They are the coffee at six a.m. that someone made for a stranger. They are the truck that arrived in the rain. They are the person who was just there at four a.m. on the worst night of your year and was not trying to get anything from you.

That is the real camp. That is what we are protecting.

This chapter is about the version that puts on the costume of the real camp and runs something else underneath. Where care becomes a monopoly. Where inner ring becomes a ladder. Where chat becomes governance. Where welcome becomes harvest. Where gift economy becomes a one-directional extraction that gets called mutual.

You are not asked to become suspicious of every camp.

You are asked to look at your camp and notice whether plain language about behavior produces real change. If it does, your camp is messy and alive and worth your labor and your loyalty and your Sunday mornings on your knees.

If it does not, the most loving thing you can do — for yourself, for the people next to you in the labor, for the next first-season arrival who is about to be told they are family — is to leave honestly and build something better twelve feet away.

The cartel survives by being the only camp in your life.

Build more camps.
