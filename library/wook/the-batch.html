The Drop

“Forty A Cap”

The cold open

📍 WOOK POV

I’m not a dealer.

I want to be clear about that before anything else, because the word dealer has a specific set of associations that do not apply to me and I am not going to let those associations land on a situation that is, when described accurately, something different. I am a guy who knows a guy. I am a guy who has friends who want to have a good time and who happen to live in a city where the prices are higher and the quality is worse and who are going to be at this festival and who I am helping out. That is not dealing. That is friendship with logistical awareness.

My name is —

Actually we’re going to skip the name. Doesn’t matter. Call me what you want.

I am twenty-seven. I have been coming to this festival for four years. I know the layout. I know which stages run late. I know which porta quadrant smells the least like a moral failing. I know the Bunk Police booth is near the north entrance, I know the DanceSafe table is across from food court row, I know these things the way I know anything useful — through experience, through paying attention, through being a person who takes the scene seriously.

I fronted eight hundred dollars for eighty caps from a source I have worked with for two years.

My source has never burned me. Two years, a dozen transactions, consistent quality, nothing bad. I know people who have been burned by sketchy sources. I know what that looks like. This is not that. My source is a real person with a real reputation and two years of clean history and the only people in the scene who have a problem with him are people who don’t actually know him, which is a category of criticism that I do not take seriously.

I marked it up to forty a cap.

This is the part where I want to be careful about how I explain it, because the explanation matters.

The markup covers: my risk, my time, my fronted capital, my gas, the two hundred dollars my festival ticket cost that I am not asking anyone to share, the three text conversations I have had with my source this week managing the logistics of this transaction. Forty a cap is not gouging. Forty a cap is fair. You would pay sixty in the city and you would not know the person you were paying sixty and you would not have the two-year relationship I have with my source as a quality guarantee. Forty a cap is me absorbing the risk and passing the savings forward. The markup is the market being rational.

I have said this, or a version of this, to myself approximately eleven times since Thursday morning. The repetition is not a sign that I am unconvinced. It is a sign that I am thorough.

The Bunk Police line Thursday afternoon was long.

I am not going to act like the line was a surprise. The line was not a surprise. The Bunk Police booth at this festival gets busy on Thursday afternoon because everybody arrives Thursday and everybody has the same idea at the same time and the booth is one booth. The line was maybe twenty people. Twenty minutes at most.

I had things to do.

I had people waiting on me. I had thirty caps I was planning to move before sunset and they are not going to move themselves and the window between arrival and the first night is the best window and I was not going to spend twenty minutes in a line when I have a source I have been working with for two years who has never once given me reason to doubt him.

I’ll test the rest of it Sunday.

The thought was: I will test the rest of it Sunday, after the first night, once the product has spoken for itself, which it will, because it always has.

What I actually mean when I think “I’ll test it Sunday” is: I’ll test it when the information would be comforting rather than useful. I don’t know this about myself yet. I will know it by Saturday afternoon.

I just need to move some before tonight.

It is Saturday at 2:07 p.m. when the first text comes.

It is from Deon. Deon is twenty-three and he is at this festival because I told him it was the right festival to start with and I have been right about that every year for four years and he trusts my recommendations. He is a friend of a friend who became a friend and he came because I said it would be good and I gave him one of the eighty caps at cost, which is to say I gave him one at what I paid my source, because Deon is a friend and not a customer and there is a difference.

The text says: Bro something’s wrong with Maddie.

Maddie is Deon’s girlfriend. I have met her three times. She seems fine. She seems like someone who came here to have a good time and has been having one.

Something’s wrong with Maddie is not specific enough for me to know what it means yet. Something’s wrong could mean a lot of things. The festival produces a lot of things that look like something is wrong. I send back: You good? What’s happening?

Twenty-two minutes pass.

The second text is not from Deon.

The second text is from a different number, a number I recognize as Theo, who is at the festival with his crew and who I sold twelve caps to on Thursday night at forty dollars each and who I have known for about eight months. The text says: Dude, Cole is in the medical tent.

I look at this text for a moment.

Cole is in Theo’s crew. I have met Cole twice. Cole is fine. Cole is a twenty-five-year-old who goes to festivals and takes molly and is fine.

Cole is in the medical tent.

I send: What happened?

No response.

At 2:46 p.m., three texts arrive in the span of four minutes, from three different people, none of whom know each other, none of whom are in the same social circle:

Are you around? People are getting hurt. Was your shit clean?

Hey man you need to call me right now.

Where are you camping. Someone needs to talk to you.

I am sitting in my tent. My tent is a good tent — a four-season North Face that I bought with adult money and which has never once let in rain. I am sitting in this good tent and looking at my phone and I am still, at 2:46 p.m. on Saturday, in the mental space of probably something went wrong with one person and it probably has nothing to do with me because my source is solid and two years is a long relationship.

The screenshot arrives at 3:14 p.m.

It comes from a number I do not have saved. The message is a screenshot of a group chat I am not in, a chat that appears to have roughly eighty members, most of whom I do not know. The screenshot shows a single message with my name in it.

Don’t take anything that came from [name]. People are seizing at the bass stage. The Bunk Police booth has the batch. Stay away.

My name. In a screenshot. Going around a group chat of eighty people that I am not in, being shared by a number I don’t have saved, having presumably been shared out of the eighty-person group chat to other group chats, having presumably been shared out of those group chats to other group chats.

My name is not in one screenshot. My name is in a cascade.

I am deleting Telegram threads. I am doing this while knowing it will not work — the threads live on the other people’s phones, you cannot delete a sent message from someone else’s storage, the archive is distributed and you cannot unarchive it from your end. I am deleting them anyway because my hands need something to do that is not shaking.

My hands are shaking.

My source, who has been solid for two years, has apparently given me a batch that contains something that is making people seize at the bass stage.

The thought arrives very clearly, separate from the panic, like a text from a different part of my brain: I did not test the batch.

The second thought: I told people it was clean.

The third thought, which arrives last because it is the one my brain has been protecting me from since 2:07 p.m.: I told people it was clean and it was not clean and I told them it was clean because I did not test it and I did not test it because I told myself I was going to test it Sunday and Sunday was going to be fine and my source is solid and I have been saying my source is solid for two years and my source is not solid.

My phone buzzes.

The number is unfamiliar. The message is from the medical tent volunteer line — someone gave them my number, someone in the cascade, someone who has a name and a phone and a decision to make about what to do with the information they have.

The message says:

Hey. We have a young woman here who’s unconscious. She told her friend before she lost consciousness that the cap came from you. We need to know what was in it. We’re not cops. We just need to know what was in it.

I look up the name.

I know the name.

The unconscious girl is my ex-girlfriend’s little sister. She is nineteen years old. I met her at Christmas two years ago. She was wearing a Santa hat and arguing with her mother about veganism at the kitchen table in her mom’s house in Fremont, and she was funny about it, she was genuinely funny, she had a good sense of humor for a seventeen-year-old in a Santa hat who was losing an argument about cheese to her mother.

I did not know she was at this festival.

I did not know she was the kind of girl who took molly.

I did not know anything about her situation tonight except that she is nineteen and unconscious and somebody gave the medical tent my name as the person whose cap she took.

Two Rangers and a Bunk Police volunteer show up at my tent at 3:47 p.m. The volunteer has a kind face. The kind face has been doing this for a long time. The kind face has seen many situations like this one and has learned, through repetition, the specific expression that communicates I am not here to punish you, I am here to get the information that might help the person in the medical tent, and the speed at which you give me that information is the only variable that still has meaningful effect on what happens next.

I tell them what I know. Which is: I do not know what was in it. I do not know what my source cut it with. I did not test it. I have the source’s number. I give them the number.

I ask: Is she okay?

The Bunk Police volunteer looks at the ground.

That is the answer.

That is the whole answer.

Here is what this chapter requires you to know about the person sitting in the good tent at 3:47 p.m.:

He is not a villain in the way villains work in fiction. He did not intend this. He did not set out to hurt anyone. He did not know the girl in the Santa hat was going to be at this festival. He did not think “I will skip the test because I want someone to die” — he thought “I’ll test it Sunday” because “I’ll test it Sunday” is a sentence that preserved his access to the forty-a-cap window and cost him nothing until Saturday.

He is also responsible for what happened. Both of those things are true simultaneously, without canceling each other out, which is the hardest sentence in the chapter to hold.

The discernment hook for this chapter is not the source. Sources can be wrong. Sources that have been solid for two years can produce one bad batch. The hook is the test. The test was there. The test takes three minutes. The Bunk Police booth was twenty minutes away and the line was twenty people. The test would have answered the question that the Santa hat girl is now answering in the medical tent in a different way.

The test was skipped because the test was inconvenient and the convenience of skipping it was worth more, in the calculation, than the certainty it would have provided.

That calculation is the chapter.

That calculation is the Wook.

The Soundboard Quote

“The sentence ‘my guy has been solid for two years’ is the sentence that precedes the mass-casualty batch. Every time. Not sometimes. Every single time I have sat with the person after the batch, that sentence was in the days before it. I don’t know why people think two years of clean product is a reason to stop testing. Two years of clean product is a reason to be grateful and test anyway. The test takes three minutes. The batch takes the rest of your life.”

— Bear Tevaru, forty-four, soundman, harm-reduction advocate, and the person who has sat with the person-after-the-batch more times than any other recurring cast member of this book. Thirty-two years in the scene, currently FOH at a regional in the high desert and simultaneously maintaining a volunteer coordination role with three harm-reduction organizations because the sound work and the safety work have become the same job at his level of tenure. Has never once said “you should have known better” to the person sitting in the good tent. Has always, always said: “Tell me what was in it. We can still help people if we move now.” Recorded in a tent. At 4:30 a.m. After a night that had ended worse than it started.

The Real F*cking Setlist

What this chapter is actually about

This chapter is about self-deception as the manipulation turned inward — the place where the Wook pattern runs against the self rather than against a target. The five Tracks are not moves deployed against strangers. They are moves deployed against the self’s own capacity to accurately assess risk. The “I’m not really a dealer” identity laundering, the “my guy is solid” trust transitivity, the “I’ll test it Sunday” deferral, the “forty a cap is fair” moral licensing — these are not threats. They are reasoning errors made in conditions of motivated cognition, which is a fancy phrase for: your brain constructed the version of the situation that let you keep doing what you were already doing. The chapter is about how to see through your own version of the situation before the batch provides the correction.

The Tapers’ Section

Harm Reduction · Supply Chain Accountability · Moral Licensing

Quick theory. Then the practical content.

Supply chain accountability is the principle that everyone in the distribution chain of a substance bears partial accountability for the harm downstream. The source, the mid-level, the person who fronts a few for friends — all of them are nodes in the chain, and the chain produces outcomes at the end of it regardless of how the nodes feel about their role. This is not a legal analysis. It is a harm-reduction framework. In harm-reduction terms: you are accountable for what you distribute, regardless of what you call yourself.

Moral licensing is the psychological phenomenon where one positive self-perception licenses a subsequent failure of care. I am generous, therefore my product is good. I have been doing this for two years without incident, therefore this instance will also be without incident. The good history becomes the permission slip for the next decision. The license is not valid. The license is also very convincing to the person holding it.

The test-kit imperative is not a suggestion in harm-reduction practice. It is the baseline. No batch, no matter how solid the source, no matter how long the relationship, no matter how inconvenient the moment, is safe to distribute without testing. Not because sources lie — because chemistry is unpredictable, because the supply chain above your source has unknown links, because fentanyl contamination has reached substrates that were previously considered clean. Because the test takes three minutes. Because the girl in the Santa hat takes the rest of your life.

You are in.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Not-Really-a-Dealer

(identity laundering / role distancing)

The Behavior

The internal narrative requires a specific self-image to function. The self-image is: I am not a dealer. I am a guy who helps out friends. I am a logistics person. I am a generous person with connections who happens to be able to bring good stuff at a fair price to people I care about.

The self-image functions as a permission structure. A dealer has responsibilities — to test, to disclose, to be accountable for the downstream effects of their distribution. A guy who helps out friends does not carry those responsibilities, because the relationship is defined as friendship and friendship does not have a liability protocol.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the fiction that the social definition of the transaction changes its legal and ethical content. It does not. Distributing a substance for profit — even at forty dollars, even to friends, even with a two-year source relationship — is distribution. The legal definition does not care about the friendship framing. The harm-reduction accountability does not care about the friendship framing. The girl in the medical tent does not care about the friendship framing.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the first Track because it is the one that makes all the other Tracks possible. If you are a dealer, you test. If you are just helping out friends, you skip the line.

The Read

The question is not: “Am I a dealer?” The question is: “Am I distributing a substance for profit or access?” If the answer is yes — the responsibilities of distribution apply regardless of the relational framing.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Role Audit

Step 1 — Name the transaction accurately. Not “helping out” or “bringing some for friends.” What is the transaction?

I am acquiring a substance from a source.

I am transporting it.

I am distributing it to people.

I am charging money.

Step 2 — Ask: what are the responsibilities of someone who does those four things? Testing. Accurate disclosure about contents. Capacity to address harms downstream. Those are the responsibilities. They apply regardless of what the relationship is called.

Step 3 — Decide whether you are willing to take those responsibilities seriously. If yes: run the protocols. If no: stop distributing.

Vibe Check

Not everyone who gives a substance to a friend is a dealer in any morally meaningful sense. Sharing your own supply, at no markup, with full disclosure and testing, within a small group of people who all consented to the information — that is a different category from what this chapter describes. The Track is not “has ever given a friend something.” The Track is the systematic distancing from accountability through identity reframing.

Mirror Set

Have you ever called yourself “not a dealer” specifically to avoid the responsibilities that come with that role? Have you ever used the friendship framing to skip a step you would have taken if you thought of yourself as running a distribution operation? The framing does not change the content of the transaction.

Refractions

Scene Veterans: The longer you have been doing this, the more convincing the “this is different from dealing” narrative becomes. The tenure makes the identity feel more real and the accountability feel less necessary. Both effects are inversely correlated with actual safety.

Touring Artists and Crew: The tour-bus supply situation has a specific version of the Not-Really-A-Dealer narrative: “we just bring enough for the run.” The run is distribution. Test it.

Runs in Every Direction

The Not-Really-A-Dealer shows up as the guy who “just knows a guy,” the femme distributor with the spreadsheet and the Venmo, the touring crew member who’s been doing this since he was nineteen, and the camp lead who fronts for the whole group. The narrative is gender-neutral. The batch doesn’t care who’s holding it.

Sober Tuesday

The investment firm that does not consider itself a bank and therefore does not apply banking regulations to its deposit-taking activities. The regulatory arbitrage works until it doesn’t. The framing does not change the function.

Track 02: The My Guy Is Solid

(trust transitivity error / chain-of-trust fallacy)

The Behavior

Your source is solid. You have two years of evidence. Nothing bad has happened in two years. Therefore: the current batch is solid.

This is the trust transitivity error. It treats the source’s historical track record as evidence about the current batch, when the current batch is a separate object with its own contents that your source’s history cannot speak to. Your source’s two-year record is real data about your source’s two-year period. It is not data about the batch you received on Thursday.

The reason it is not data: your source has a supply chain. Your source’s supply chain has unknown links above it. The chemistry of a batch can change based on factors that have nothing to do with your source’s intentions or character. Fentanyl contamination has been documented in MDMA, cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin, counterfeit pills, and multiple other substances that were previously considered clean. The contamination often comes from above the source in the chain — from the synthesis, the cutting, the pressing — and it is invisible to your source unless your source tests, which your source may not do for the same reasons you don’t: because the source’s source has been solid, and the chain of trust extends upward without interruption until the batch provides the interruption.

The My Guy Is Solid is not evidence about the batch. It is evidence about your relationship. Both are real. One of them is useful for predicting today’s product. That one is the test.

The Read

The test. Every time. Not sometimes. Not when you are suspicious. Not when you have a reason to doubt your source. Every time. Because the absence of a reason to doubt is not evidence that nothing is wrong — it is the default state that the test is supposed to update from.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Chain Audit

Step 1 — Ask your source, every transaction: “Have you tested this batch?” Not rhetorically. Literally: what kit, what result?

Step 2 — Verify with your own kit regardless of what they say. Not because you distrust them. Because the chain above them is unknown to both of you.

Step 3 — Marquis for MDMA: purple-black is correct. Yellow, orange, or no reaction: do not distribute.

Step 4 — Fentanyl strip: always. On everything. Regardless of substrate. The positive result is one line. One line: destroy the batch. Do not distribute a single unit of a batch that tests positive for fentanyl.

Step 5 — Document the test. Photo of the result. Timestamped. In your camera roll. So that “I didn’t know” can also be “and here is the test I ran.”

Vibe Check

Good sources exist. Long-term reliable supply relationships exist. Trust, built through evidence over time, is real and useful. None of that replaces the test. The test is what turns trust into information.

Mirror Set

Have you ever told someone something was clean based on your source’s reputation rather than a test? Have you ever passed on the “my guy is solid” reassurance as a substitute for the test result? If yes: you transferred the fiction of certainty without the substance of it.

Refractions

Harm-Reduction Volunteers: The most common sentence preceding a serious batch incident in your intake is “someone told them it was tested.” The sentence was not “here is the test result.” The sentence was “I trust the person who told me it was tested.” Those are different sentences.

Runs in Every Direction

The chain-of-trust error runs through the tour crew’s usual connect, the camp’s two-year relationship with a specific source, the femme distributor whose reputation has never once been questioned, and the friend of a friend everyone vouches for without having met him. It doesn’t care about the quality of the human relationship. It cares about the quality of the batch, which only the test can establish.

Sober Tuesday

The audit firm that signed off on the financial statements because the client had been reliable for twelve years. The twelve-year track record was real. The fraudulent statements were also real. The audit was the test. The audit was not run with sufficient scrutiny. The twelve years became the permission slip. That is the My Guy Is Solid running in accounting.

Track 03: The Skipped Test

(availability heuristic override of risk assessment)

The Behavior

The Bunk Police line was twenty people. Twenty minutes.

He had thirty caps to move before sunset.

The availability heuristic is the cognitive shortcut that treats the ease of imagining a bad outcome as a measure of its probability. The bad outcome — someone getting hurt from an untested batch — was not easy to imagine in Thursday’s context, because the context was busy and good and his source had been solid for two years and twenty minutes in a line is a real cost against a hypothetical risk.

The hypothetical risk was not hypothetical.

The hypothetical risk had a name and a Santa hat and a mother in Fremont who was going to receive a phone call.

The Skipped Test is not a single decision. It is the accumulation of smaller decisions — the line was long, the moment was busy, I’ll do it later, it’s probably fine, my source is solid — each of which is individually defensible in the moment and collectively produced the decision to distribute an untested substance to people who believed it was clean.

The Skipped Test is the chapter’s central safety content. Not because testing is complicated — because it is not, it takes three minutes, the kits are available, the protocol is simple. But because the decision to skip it is made under exactly the conditions that make every other shortcut in this book appealing: momentum, social pressure, the window, the belief that history predicts the future.

The Read

One rule. No exceptions.

Test every batch you distribute. Every time. Before you distribute. Not after. Not after a few units have gone out and nothing bad has happened yet. Before.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Three-Minute Gate

Step 1 — Before distributing any batch, test it. Marquis and fentanyl strip, minimum. Both.

Step 2 — The three-minute gate is this: if you do not have three minutes to run the test, you do not have the time to distribute safely. Stop. Wait. Come back when you have three minutes.

Step 3 — The window does not outrank the test. The social pressure does not outrank the test. The line being long does not outrank the test. The source being solid does not outrank the test. Nothing outranks the test.

Step 4 — If you cannot test: do not distribute. This is the only rule in the chapter that has no exceptions or qualifications.

Step 5 — Carry the kits. At every festival, in your own kit, separate from any supply. Marquis. Mecke. Simons. Fentanyl strips. They cost less than the ticket. They cost less than the phone call.

Vibe Check

Testing does not prevent all harm. A fentanyl-negative result does not mean the substance contains exactly what is labeled. Unknown adulterants, dosing errors, and individual tolerance variations can all produce harm in tested substances. The test is necessary. It is not sufficient. What it is: the minimum accountability threshold for anyone distributing anything to anyone.

Mirror Set

Have you ever distributed something without testing it? Have you ever told yourself you would test it “later” in a way that meant you would test it after the window for the test to be useful had closed? The gap between “I’ll test it later” and “I told them it was clean” is a sentence you may be living inside right now.

Refractions

Camp Kitchen Leads: If your camp has a supply that multiple people are accessing from, the camp kitchen lead is the de facto testing authority. Build the testing protocol into the camp’s pre-event briefing. Kits, results, documentation.

First-Time Distributors: The first time you distribute anything to anyone else is the time when the test matters most, because you have no baseline for your source and no experience with the downstream effects of distribution. Test obsessively in year one.

Runs in Every Direction

The Skipped Test gets skipped by the touring vet who’s done this a hundred times, the first-timer who doesn’t own a kit, the camp lead in a hurry before doors, and the distributor who’s never once had a bad batch. The reasons are identical regardless of who’s skipping it: inconvenience, momentum, trust in the source, belief that this time is probably fine. None of those reasons change the test’s function.

Sober Tuesday

The safety inspection that was scheduled for next quarter because the plant was running at capacity and the shutdown for inspection would cost the week’s production. The risk was known. The window was inconvenient. The inspection was postponed. The batch failed in the field. The postponement was the Skipped Test.

Track 04: The Screenshot Drop

(reputation cascade and the no-recall problem)

The Behavior

His name is in a screenshot.

The screenshot is in a group chat of eighty people. Each of those eighty people is in other group chats. The screenshot will be in those group chats within the hour. The content of the screenshot is his name and the information that his batch is involved in a medical emergency. By the time he sees the screenshot, the screenshot is already downstream of the original send. By the time he finishes reading the screenshot, his name is probably in forty more group chats.

He cannot recall the screenshot.

This is the no-recall problem. Information distributed through informal social networks cannot be centrally removed. Every attempt to delete it from one location does not remove it from the locations it has already reached. Every send multiplies the locations it has reached. The cascade is exponential and it is irreversible and the only variables that were available to affect it were the variables that existed before the batch was distributed.

The Screenshot Drop is not a punishment imposed by the scene. It is a function of how information travels in peer networks. It is also, in this specific instance, the correct function. The information “don’t take anything from [name], the batch is involved in a medical emergency” is harm-reduction information of the highest urgency. The people sending the screenshot are doing the right thing. The problem is that they are doing the right thing with his name in it, and his name is there because he did not do the right thing first.

The Read

The Screenshot Drop is not something you can manage after the fact. It can only be prevented by the test that was not run. But it can be partially addressed by the recall play — the set of actions available in the first ten minutes after the information that the batch is bad.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Recall Play

If you have distributed a batch and receive information that the batch is causing harm:

Step 1 — Contact every buyer immediately. Every single one. “Stop. Don’t take anything you got from me. There may be something wrong with the batch.” Text, call, find in person. Every one. Within ten minutes.

Step 2 — Stop distribution immediately. Cap the container. Do not sell or give another unit.

Step 3 — Contact the medical tent or harm-reduction team directly. Before they find you. This is critical. Before they find you. “I think I may have distributed something that is causing harm. I want to help. What do you need from me?” This call, made proactively, before the Rangers arrive at the tent, is the difference between cooperation and apprehension.

Step 4 — Destroy the remaining supply. Dispose of it in a way that cannot be recovered.

Step 5 — Give the medical team everything you know. Your source. The form of the substance. Where it was distributed. When. To whom. All of it. “We’re not cops. We just need to know what was in it” is the sentence you are going to hear. Answer it completely. The girl in the medical tent is more important than your source’s identity.

Vibe Check

The recall play does not undo the harm. There is no counter-drop that undoes the harm. What the recall play does is limit the additional harm — the caps that have not yet been taken, the people who have not yet received the information, the medical team that does not yet know what they are treating.

Mirror Set

Have you ever received information that something you distributed was causing harm and not immediately acted on it? Have you ever waited — for more information, for certainty, for the situation to clarify — while the clock on the recall window was running? The ten minutes is not approximate. The ten minutes is the interval between the batch’s first harm and the point at which the additional harm is preventable.

Refractions

Harm-Reduction Volunteers: When someone contacts you proactively with the recall information, that person deserves protection, not prosecution. Build a culture where the proactive call is possible and rewarded. The call saves lives. The fear of consequences for making the call costs lives.

Runs in Every Direction

The Screenshot Drop runs equally against the guy who bought from a stranger, the woman who bought from her regular, the nonbinary festie who bought from a friend of a friend, and everyone across the distribution spectrum who assumed they’d remember exactly what it looked like. The no-recall problem is a physics problem. It has no social bias.

Sober Tuesday

The product recall issued two weeks after the company received the first complaint report. The ten-day gap between first complaint and public recall is the Skipped Recall Play in default-world manufacturing. People were harmed in those ten days who would not have been harmed if the recall had run immediately.

Track 05: The Forty-a-Cap Reframe

(moral licensing via pricing)

The Behavior

The markup justifies the distribution.

The logic is: I am charging forty dollars, which is a fair price, which reflects the real costs of acquiring and transporting the substance, which means the transaction is a legitimate market exchange, which means I have done what a legitimate market participant does, which means the responsibilities of the transaction have been discharged by the fairness of the price.

The forty-a-cap reframe converts the price into a moral receipt. I charged fairly, therefore I have done right by the people I sold to. The price is the accountability. The price is the test. The price is the answer to the question “did you take this seriously” — because forty dollars is a serious price, a responsible price, a price that says I am not exploiting anyone.

The reframe is a version of the moral licensing problem from Chapter 3: one positive attribute of the transaction — the fair price — licenses the absence of another, more important attribute — the test. The fairness of the price becomes the permission slip for the unfairness of the skip.

The Read

The price is not the test. The price is not harm reduction. The price is not accountability. The price is a number. The accountability is: did you test it, did you disclose its contents accurately, did you provide accurate dosing information, do you have a plan if something goes wrong?

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Full Accountability Checklist

Step 1 — Tested? Kit, result, documentation.

Step 2 — Disclosed contents accurately? Not “molly,” but what specifically, from what source, tested with what result.

Step 3 — Provided dosing information? The weight or count, the expected onset, the expected duration, the known interactions.

Step 4 — Provided harm-reduction information? “If anything feels wrong, go to the medical tent or the harm-reduction tent. Tell them you don’t know what it was.”

Step 5 — Recall plan? Do you have every buyer’s contact information? Can you reach them in ten minutes if you need to?

If all five are yes: the transaction has accountability attached to it. If any one is no: the price is a number and the accountability is missing.

Vibe Check

Fair pricing is genuinely better than price gouging. Harm in a fair-price transaction is genuinely different from harm in an exploitative transaction in terms of intent. Intent matters morally. Intent does not change the harm. The chapter does not say fair pricing is irrelevant. It says fair pricing is not sufficient.

Mirror Set

Have you ever told yourself that the fairness of your price meant you had taken your responsibilities seriously? Have you ever felt the moral legitimacy of not ripping people off as a cover for the moral illegitimacy of not testing the product? Both can happen in the same transaction. The second one is the problem.

Refractions

Harm-Reduction Advocates: When working with distributors on safety protocols, the price-is-accountability reframe is one of the most common blocks to testing adoption. Addressing it directly — “the fair price is good, the test is also required, they are not substitutes for each other” — moves the conversation faster than any other approach.

Runs in Every Direction

The Forty-A-Cap Reframe runs across the guy fronting eight hundred dollars for eighty caps, the femme distributor who prices hers the same way, the touring vet who’s done the math a dozen times, and anyone who’s decided a fair price is the same thing as due diligence. It runs across all genders and all distribution scales. The moral licensing through fair pricing is available to anyone who charges a fair price. It is equally unavailable as accountability.

Sober Tuesday

The gym that says it treats its members fairly — clean equipment, friendly staff — and therefore does not need to fix the emergency exit that has been chained shut for a year. The friendliness is real. The safety accountability is also required. They are not the same thing.

Have You Been the Wook?

Mirror Check At The Tent Flap

This chapter’s mirror is the chapter’s whole argument.

The most relatable Wook in the book is the one who is not trying to hurt anyone. The one who has built a self-image that is largely accurate — generous, connected, knowledgeable, a good person to have in the scene — and who has used that self-image to construct a series of reasoning errors that accumulated into a batch that killed a nineteen-year-old he met at Christmas.

He is not a monster. He is a person who thought he was not a dealer, thought his guy was solid, thought he would test it Sunday, thought forty a cap was fair, and thought none of these thoughts were the same as the thought: I am going to distribute an untested substance to people who trust me and call it friendship.

He didn’t think that thought. He thought a series of other thoughts that pointed in the same direction.

Have you ever distributed something without testing it? Have you ever used your source’s track record as a substitute for the test result? Have you ever marked up a substance and told yourself the markup was doing the accountability work? Have you ever been the person whose name is in the screenshot?

The reckoning is not the chapter’s question. The reckoning is not punitive. The reckoning is: what do you do at the next event? Do you test? Do you change the framing? Do you stop distributing entirely? Do you build the recall plan?

The girl in the Santa hat deserves the answer to be: yes, I test. Every time.

That is the only honor this chapter can offer her.

Tales From the Camp Network — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Devi Raman has been coming to this festival for three years. She is twenty-six. She does not distribute substances. She does take them, carefully, from tested sources, which is a distinction she worked out in her head during her first year in the scene after a night that did not go badly but that could have, easily, if the person she was with had been less careful.

The person she is currently buying from is a guy named Sam. Sam is known in her camp network. Sam has been at this festival for four years. Sam is the person her friend Dani described as “solid, he tests everything, show him your strips and he’ll compare results” — which is the specific quality reference Devi has learned to listen for.

She finds Sam on Thursday evening and runs the protocol she has been running since year two.

She says: “Can I see the test result?”

Sam does not flinch. He has his phone out. He has a photo of the test result, timestamped Thursday afternoon. Marquis, purple-black. Fentanyl strip, two lines — negative. He turns the phone so she can see clearly.

She has her own strips. She tests one unit herself, in front of him. Purple-black. Two lines. She keeps the strip. She photographs it. She buys four caps.

This is the watermelon moment. Not in the emotional register of some other chapters — smaller, more ordinary. Standing at the edge of the water station, phone out, photographing a test strip that has produced the result that means “not today, not this.” The feeling in her chest when the strip develops correctly is not relief in the dramatic sense. It is the small, specific satisfaction of a system working the way systems are supposed to work — of having built the protocol and run it and received the correct output.

She puts the strip photo in a folder on her phone labeled “testing.” The folder has photographs going back two years. Every test she has run. Every result. Every festival.

On Saturday afternoon, her group chat lights up.

The message says what the messages on Saturday afternoon at this festival always say, the message that the scene dreads and that eventually arrives at every major event: a batch is bad, people are in the medical tent, do not take anything from anyone you didn’t vet yourself.

Devi reads the message. She looks at the folder on her phone. She looks at the test results from Thursday.

She texts Dani: “Sam’s stuff tested clean. I have the results. I’ve also stopped taking anything else today until this clears.”

She goes to find the people in her camp who might need information. She is not an authority. She is a person who has strips and results and the specific calm of someone whose protocol has already run.

She finds two people who were thinking about taking something from someone else — a different source, someone they know less well, someone who said “my guy is solid” in roughly those words.

She shows them the Saturday messages. She says: “I’d wait. Not because your guy isn’t, but because we don’t know yet where the bad batch is coming from and until we do, the test is the only answer.”

They wait.

The Saturday messages continue. The bad batch is identified by the Bunk Police booth by early evening. The source is not Sam. The source is someone whose name Devi does not know, whose eighty caps are now somewhere in the festival’s population, and whose testing protocol apparently ran on the same Thursday logic as the man in the good tent who is by now sitting with two Rangers and a Bunk Police volunteer with a kind face and tired eyes.

Devi’s folder has Thursday’s results. Her two campmates did not take the unvetted source’s supply.

The watermelon moment was the strip developing correctly.

The rest was the protocol doing its job.

The Fanny Pack

Front pocket — chapter essentials

Chapter 15. The kit is in this pocket.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer (identity laundering): If you are distributing a substance for profit or access, the responsibilities of distribution apply regardless of what you call it.

The My Guy Is Solid (trust transitivity): Your source’s two-year track record is evidence about your source. It is not evidence about the batch. The test is the evidence about the batch.

The Skipped Test (availability heuristic override): Test every batch before distributing. Every time. Not sometimes. The three-minute gate: if you don’t have three minutes for the test, you don’t have time to distribute safely.

The Screenshot Drop (reputation cascade): The recall play runs in the first ten minutes. Contact every buyer. Stop distribution. Call the medical tent before they find you. Destroy the remaining supply. Give everything you know.

The Forty-A-Cap Reframe (moral licensing via pricing): The price is not the test. The accountability checklist is: tested, disclosed, dosed, harm-reduced, recall plan.

“My guy has been solid for two years” is the sentence that precedes the mass-casualty batch.

The Soundcheck

The Testing Protocol Drill

This is the shortest drill in the book. It takes three minutes. It is also the most important drill in the book.

Get a test kit. If you do not have one: order one before your next event. DanceSafe.org or Bunk Police. It will arrive before the event. This is not optional.

At your next event, before you distribute anything or take anything from a source you have not personally tested:

Step 1: Marquis test. Procedure from Safety Appendix. Document the result. Photo.

Step 2: Fentanyl strip. Procedure from Safety Appendix. Document the result. Photo.

Step 3: If both results are as expected: proceed.

Step 4: If either result is unexpected: do not distribute. Do not take. Bring the sample to the Bunk Police booth or DanceSafe table for additional testing.

Three minutes. Every batch. No exceptions.

The drill is the protocol. The protocol is the minimum. The minimum is what the girl in the Santa hat deserved and did not receive.

The Sunrise Set

Zero slang. Just the true thing.

The batch chapter is the hardest chapter in the book to end gently.

There is a nineteen-year-old who did not come home.

There is a mother in Fremont who drove to a hospital in the middle of the night.

There is a man sitting in a good tent whose hands stopped shaking by Sunday but who will be answering a different question for a very long time: what would have happened if I had spent twenty minutes in the Bunk Police line on Thursday afternoon?

The answer is: we would not be in this chapter.

This is not the chapter that says substances are the enemy. They are not. They are chemistry. Chemistry is what it is, and what it is changes depending on what is in the substance and what you know about what is in the substance and whether you have the tools to find out.

The tools exist. The kits cost forty dollars. The line was twenty minutes long.

The chapter is here to say: twenty minutes of inconvenience is not comparable to any of the other outcomes in evidence.

Test the batch.

Every time.

For the girl in the Santa hat.

For the people who are going to take what you brought to the next one.

For the thirty seconds at sunrise when you get to say: I did everything that could be done, and everything that could be done was done.
