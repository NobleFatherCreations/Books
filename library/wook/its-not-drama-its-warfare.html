The Drop

“The Tank”

The cold open

📍 FESTIE POV

The Gorge Amphitheatre parking lot at four in the afternoon smells like two things: hot asphalt with a top note of sunscreen, and money leaving people faster than their brain can track it.

You get here at peak lot and you feel it immediately — the pressure of all those bodies having an experience, the way the sound from the main stage leaks over the ridgeline like a promise that costs thirty-two dollars in service fees. The Columbia River is down there somewhere, reflecting silver in the kind of light that makes Pacific Northwesters feel chosen by God. The lot is car-camping, RV-camping, shade structures made of PVC and prayer, a guy in a camp chair with a Coors Banquet who has been here since Wednesday and has watched the whole civilization rise up around him with the philosophical detachment of someone who has seen it all before and still finds it disappointing.

His name is Daniel Weiss. He is twenty years old. He has been on tour for eighteen days.

His Subaru — a ’99 Forester with a cracked rear window, a sleeping bag, one duffel, and a bag of trail mix with only raisins left because he ate all the M&Ms first like an idiot — is backed in at Lot D near the water truck. He has $340 to his name. Not in an account. In his wallet. Three hundred and forty actual dollars, the last of what he saved doing dish shifts at the breakfast spot on Valencia before he quit to do this thing his friends called a bad idea and he called a spiritual necessity.

He has been practicing the nod.

Not like a nod nod. The nod. The slow deliberate one that says I have been around, I know what’s good, I move through this world with the easy weight of someone who does not need to ask where the bathrooms are. He practices it in the Subaru window on I-84 somewhere between Hood River and The Dalles. He looks like a twenty-year-old practicing a nod.

He has been saying kind. As in: That’s kind of you, brother. He heard an OG say it and it sounded like a whole life philosophy compressed into four syllables, which is exactly what being twenty years old on your first real tour feels like.

Hopper finds him at 5:47 p.m.

Hopper is forty-one, wearing a bucket hat with a hand-sewn patch of a running deer that has been on that hat for eleven years. He has a gray-streaked beard and the kind of easy gait that comes from walking parking lots for decades. He has three gold rings on one hand and no watch. He smells like sandalwood and nag champa and somebody’s van, and he has the unhurried warmth of a man who has never been in a hurry because he has always been right where he needed to be.

He sees Daniel trying to light a camp stove with a match that has already gotten damp.

“Hold on,” Hopper says. He has a lighter before Daniel can say he’s fine.

He lights the stove. He watches Daniel’s instant rice start to do its thing. He says, “First tour?”

Daniel says yes before deciding whether he wants to.

Hopper smiles. Not a predatory smile. Not even a warm smile, really — more like the smile of someone watching something they recognize from the inside. He says, “Man, I remember that.” And then he says, “You eaten today?” And Daniel says the rice, which is not ready yet. And Hopper says, “Come on. My camp’s got rice and beans. Real ones. You can eat, rest, I’ll show you around later.”

Daniel Weiss, eighteen days into his first Dead summer, $340 in his wallet, a bag of raisin-only trail mix, practicing the nod — he says yes.

That is the first yes.

Somewhere in the back of his head, in the part still narrating his own tour to an audience that does not exist, Daniel thinks: Hopper’s basically my sherpa out here. He will think about that word again in October, in a hallway outside a courtroom in Douglas County, and it will not sound the same.

Hopper’s camp is three rows over and it is the real thing: a shade canopy stretched tight between two tent poles with actual stakes, a propane burner, a blue cooler with ice in it, a Coleman lantern, and four or five people in varying states of horizontal. There is a guy with a mandolin not playing the mandolin. There is a woman doing something with macramé. There is the smell of cumin, actual cumin, not incense pretending to be cumin.

Hopper makes a plate and hands it to Daniel without ceremony, which is the smartest move in the whole manual — ceremony would have put up Daniel’s guard. No ceremony is just Tuesday.

Over the rice and beans they talk. Not about the play. About music. About where Daniel grew up (Cedar Rapids, Iowa, yes the whole state is one big flat thing, yes he knows, thanks). About the Dead, the setlist from the night before, whether Phil’s bass was mixed wrong in the second set. Hopper laughs at Daniel’s joke about I-84. He remembers Cedar Rapids — passed through it in ’07, great diner on the main strip.

The thing about Hopper is: he is not performing warmth. He is warm. That is the whole engine of this. If he were performing, Daniel’s nervous system would catch the edge of it, the effort, the production of the thing. Hopper has been doing this for nineteen years and he has made warmth into muscle memory and he cannot tell the difference anymore either. That is the deepest version of the move — where the operator has run the play so long they have genuinely incorporated it into their personality. The exploitation and the kindness are not separate. He is not pretending to like Daniel. He does like Daniel. He also needs Daniel to hold a tank.

Those two facts coexist without contradiction and that is what makes Hopper difficult to spot and this chapter necessary.

At seven-thirty, during the set break, Hopper says: “Hey, I have a thing. It’s simple. Easy money.”

He explains it flat. No pressure in his voice, which is its own pressure. Just hold the tank during set break. Stand right here, look friendly, twenty an hour, I handle the balloons, you don’t have to do anything. He makes it sound like the easiest logistics problem in the world, which it is, on its face, and Daniel says yes — he says it like he’s been handed a kidney, like someone finally decided he was useful — and they shake hands and Hopper says good man, which lands in Daniel’s chest like a hand on his shoulder.

That is the second yes.

It is the one that matters.

Two guys from the shade canopy get up around eight without being asked — bandanas tied low, the same practiced way, neither of them having said ten words to Daniel all evening — and start running the balloon rig off the second tank. Hopper does not introduce them. They do not introduce themselves. Daniel reads this as them being busy.

Set break hits at 8:14 p.m. Pacific time.

Four thousand bodies spill out of the amphitheatre into the lot. The sound from the stage cuts and for thirty seconds the world sounds like: gravel underfoot, cooler lids, the river somewhere below, voices, voices, voices. Then the economy kicks in. The nitrous setup on the south end does not just have a line — it has a crowd, the kind that has its own gravity, that pulls people by the familiar logic of everyone else is already doing it.

Daniel is holding the tank.

He is standing where Hopper told him to stand. He looks friendly. He is twenty years old and he has twenty dollars an hour and his hands on the valve and he is telling himself this is fine, he is just holding it, Hopper is the one doing the transactions.

At 8:22 p.m. a girl in a white flowy skirt cuts the line.

She is not aggressive about it. She is high and happy and she wants a balloon. She gets one. She gets another one. She is standing up and she is laughing when the third balloon leaves her and her knees go — not a stumble, not a lean — they go, like a light switch. She drops straight down and her skull hits the asphalt with a sound that does not belong in that lot or any lot or anywhere near music, a sound like something that cannot be taken back.

The blood under her ear is dark.

The lot freezes.

One second.

Two.

Then:

Hopper is gone.

The bandana guys are gone.

The cashbox is gone.

The balloon crew at the setup twelve yards over has achieved a collective act of teleportation and their van is already pointing toward the exit. They do not run. Running is how you get chased. They walk with the calm efficiency of people who have executed this particular exit strategy multiple times and the walk itself is a performance of innocence.

Daniel Weiss is still standing with the tank.

He does not move for three full seconds because his brain has not yet told his body what has happened. He is still in the previous reality where he is just holding a tank for twenty an hour look friendly. The blood under the girl’s ear is real and his brain is receiving it but hasn’t decided yet what to do with it and during those three seconds a security guard comes out of the crowd from forty yards north at a dead sprint pointing directly at him yelling something that resolves, as he gets closer, into: don’t move, don’t fucking move.

The tank comes up to his hip.

His clean t-shirt has the name of a band on it.

His actual face is out.

Hannah Reyes. Twenty-three. From Irvine. She went to the medic tent by ambulance at 8:29 p.m. She had a bleed she never woke up from. She died Monday at 9:14 a.m. in a hospital in Wenatchee. Her parents, who drove seven hours from Southern California, were with her. There was a playlist on in the room. Someone decided that was right.

Daniel’s parents drove twenty-three hours from Cedar Rapids.

His mother cried the entire walk through the parking lot. Not at Daniel, at the parking lot — at the asphalt and the heat and the fact that she had driven twenty-three hours to get here and that this was where the drive ended. His father said nothing in the car. Not because he was angry, or not only because he was angry — also because he did not have the words for what he was watching happen to his son, who had driven to a concert in a used Subaru and was now being handed back to him by a public defender in a county he had never been to.

In the hallway outside the courtroom, Daniel does the nod. At his own public defender. Slow and deliberate, I have been around, I know what’s good — eighteen days of practice firing off on schedule at a man holding a folder with his name on it. He catches it half a second after his chin comes back up.

It is the last time he does it.

Hopper was at a Phish run in Keene Valley by Tuesday.

Different kid. Different tank. Same exit.

Here is the thing the chapter has to tell you, and it might be the only thing that matters in all thirty-three chapters:

That kid said yes for the exact same reason you have said yes to things that cost you.

He was new. He was broke. He was alone in a lot where everyone else seemed to already know the choreography. Someone competent and warm and unhurried walked over, fed him, learned his name, and made him feel like he was useful, which is — and write this down in whatever part of your brain is still readable at 3 a.m. — the most reliable access route into a person who has not yet learned to protect themselves.

Not threat. Not lust. Not drugs. Not money.

Belonging.

Hopper did not hack a stranger. He made a friend. The friend happened to have hands and a face and twenty years of not knowing who Hopper was.

The tank was twenty dollars an hour.

The price was not twenty dollars an hour.

That is what this book is for.

The Real F*cking Setlist

What this chapter is actually about

This chapter is about the difference between conflict and predation. Conflict is messy. Conflict can repair. Conflict gets louder when two people slow down and listen. Predation is directional — it has a beneficiary and a cost center, and the cost center is you. Predation does not repair when named. It maneuvers. It upgrades the cover story. It survives because we keep calling it drama, personality, a bad match, a complicated situation, a thing that happened once, a misunderstanding between two people who both have their version. There are not two versions. There is a pattern. There is intent. There is the refusal to change when the pattern is named. Those three together are not drama. Those three together are a setlist, and the headliner has played this festival before.

The Tapers’ Section

Greene · Goffman · Simon

You do not need to become a library goblin to clock a Wook. But a little theory in the fanny pack keeps you from mistaking a pattern for a vibe.

Robert Greene gives us the ugly playbook: power is often built sideways, through favors, access, timing, dependence, and social theater. In festival language, the Wook does not always kick down the tent. Sometimes he becomes the only person who knows where the mallet is.

Erving Goffman gives us the stage: every person is performing a role, managing an impression, using the room as part of the act. In scene language, the Wook is not just talking to you. He is talking to the camp, the green room, the group chat, the volunteer lead, the people watching from the tea tent, and the one person with the car keys. The performance is the weapon.

George Simon gives us the closing argument: some people know exactly enough to manipulate and exactly enough to deny it. His memory only fails when the receipt has his name on it. His version of events is a spreadsheet. Yours is “a lot of energy.” The cognitive sophistication required to execute repeated, targeted exploitation while maintaining plausible deniability is not accidental. It is a skill. And like all skills, it gets better with practice.

That is enough theory. Put it under the cooler.

You are in.

The Wook’s Setlist

The fronts in this chapter

Three fronts in the Invisible War. Three Tracks in this chapter:

The Overt Lot Rat — loud, obvious, grabby, immediate. Takes what is in reach while you are deciding whether to be polite about it.

The Covert Facilitator — helpful, connected, beloved, impossible to question without looking ungrateful. Turns rescue into debt so slowly you only notice when you’re underwater.

The Sleeper Cell — long game, deep roots, trusted until the root system starts choking the camp. Their reputation enters the room before the harmed person does.

Different outfits. Same appetite. All three want leverage.

FIELD SPECIMEN №01

The Lot Rat

Habitat: between the port-a-potties and your trust. Approach vector: grilled cheese, seven dollars, “for the family.” Knows everyone. Belongs to no one.

→ The cheese is real. The family is not.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Overt Lot Rat

(antisocial personality typology)

The Behavior

The Overt Lot Rat does not have a long game because that would require a calendar app and object permanence.

He is the guy selling “clean doses” out of a crushed Altoids tin while refusing to walk twenty yards to the reagent tent. He is the woman who “just needs to crash for one night” and somehow wakes up wearing your hoodie, using your charger, and explaining that your missing cash is “scarcity mindset.” He is the couple at the renegade stage who keeps pushing shots on the one person already blinking at manual speed.

The Overt Lot Rat takes what is visible. Phone on a blanket. Wallet in an open tote. Drunk friend separated from crew. Newcomer who does not know the walk from the bass stage back to family camp is a full mythological journey. Vendor who left the cashbox under the table because “we’re all family here,” which is exactly the sentence raccoons would invent if raccoons had burner profiles and a patchwork vest.

This Wook does not need spiritual language. This Wook has hands.

They crowd your space. They test your no. They ask for your lighter and keep it. They ask for your water and take the whole jug. They ask where you are camped before they ask your name. They get too close at the rail, too familiar in the hammock grove, too interested in who you came with, too casual about your altered friend.

They are not subtle. The scene just keeps trying to be nicer than reality.

The Read

Clock the Ninety-Second Pattern.

A normal messy person can be annoying for ninety seconds. A predator reveals direction in ninety seconds.

Ask yourself: Did they accept the first boundary or negotiate it? Did they move closer after you moved back? Did they ask again after you said no? Did they switch targets when you became inconvenient? Did their friendliness vanish when access vanished?

The Overt Lot Rat is not confused by your boundary. They are offended by the existence of friction. Watch the eyes after the no. The mouth says: All good, family. The eyes are already checking the next tent.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Ninety-Second Follow-Up

Do not debate the raccoon.

Step 1 — Name the first boundary once, clean sentence: “No, you can’t come into our camp.” “No, she’s not leaving with you.” “No, that bag stays here.”

Step 2 — Wait ninety seconds. Not spiritually. Literally. Check the time. Count songs. Watch behavior.

Step 3 — Track the response. Three categories only:

Green: accepts and redirects without punishment.

Yellow: jokes, guilts, hovers, bargains, or keeps the door open.

Red: escalates, insults, touches, follows, recruits others, or moves to a weaker target.

Step 4 — Act on the category.

Green: release it.

Yellow: add distance and witnesses.

Red: activate crew, staff, security, harm reduction, ranger, camp lead, or sober anchor.

Step 5 — Document boringly. Not “predator at bass stage.” Write: “Saturday 1:42 a.m., man in green baja hoodie asked three times to enter camp after no, moved toward intoxicated Maya, left when Ben stood up.” Boring notes survive sunrise. Dramatic notes get eaten by the group chat goblin.

Vibe Check

Not every person with bad social timing is a predator. Some people are high, awkward, concussed by bass, autistic, panicking, lonely, or having the kind of weekend where their nervous system is a raccoon in a trench coat operating a forklift. The test is not “did they annoy me?” The test is: what did they do after clear information?

Mirror Set

Have you ever kept asking after someone gave you a soft no because you wanted the answer to become yes? Have you ever treated “maybe later” like a scavenger hunt? Have you ever used your altered state as a hall pass for grabbing, borrowing, entering, touching, or taking? Run the Ninety-Second Follow-Up on yourself. The rail sees all.

Refractions

First-Timer: The Overt Lot Rat loves your confusion. If someone offers instant family before asking if you have water, slow down.

Vendor: Your booth is not a free charging station, therapy couch, storage unit, or flirtation-based layaway program. You are running a business in the dust.

Camp Lead: If one person keeps “accidentally” crossing kitchen, sleeping, or consent boundaries, do not make the newest femme explain it at the camp meeting.

Touring Artist/DJ: The person hanging around artist parking without a role may be harmless. Or they may be mapping who is alone, altered, flattered, and tired.

Runs in Every Direction

The Overt Lot Rat is not always a dude with a crusty beard and a staff he calls medicine. It can be the hot rave girl who keeps touching people after they freeze. The flow-arts guy who ‘adjusts your form’ in the hoop circle with both hands and calls it teaching. The queer scene celebrity who thinks desirability is consent. The older burner auntie who steals labor with hugs. The couple that hunts together near the afters and calls it play. Predation wears every outfit in the costume bin.

Sober Tuesday

At noon on a Tuesday in default world, this person is the coworker who takes things off your desk while you’re in the bathroom and says they thought you were done with it. They are usually charming in the first two weeks and become a slow tax after that.

Track 02: The Covert Facilitator

(ingratiation grooming)

The Behavior

The Covert Facilitator is where the scene gets stupid, because this one looks useful.

They have the extra wristband. The spare tent. The artist contact. The golf cart ride. The shade cloth. The plug. The green room password. The volunteer lead’s ear. The invite to the “real afters.” The van seat. The generator adapter nobody else packed because nobody else thinks in adapters. They solve problems with one hand and invoice your soul with the other.

At first, it feels like rescue. Your shift runs late — they cover it. Your ride flakes — they “got you.” Your camp forgot stakes — they have extras. Your set time moves and the green room is locked — they have the code. Each rescue is real. The kindness inside it is real. And every single one goes on a tab you didn’t see being opened.

The thing about the Covert Facilitator is that the debt is not financial. Financial debt has numbers. This debt is relational. You owe them your loyalty, your positive word-of-mouth, your silence during difficult moments, your presence at their set, your endorsement to the people they want to reach, your willingness to look the other way when someone else describes an experience that sounds familiar. The payment is always extracted at the worst possible time — when you most need to speak, when you most need to leave, when you most need to be believed.

They do not make you feel trapped. They make you feel grateful. Trapped people run. Grateful people stay.

The Read

A clean favor increases your options. A Wook favor narrows them.

Ask: Can you leave this person’s orbit without social cost? Can you say no to their offer without losing standing? Has their help ever arrived at a moment that also happened to create dependence? Do they remind you — verbally or atmospherically — of what they have done for you?

Watch for the Inventory: not a bill, just the frequent, casual accounting of their generosity. “After everything I set up for your camp.” “I called in a favor for that.” “I was the one who got you that access.” The Covert Facilitator has a spreadsheet where you have memories. That asymmetry is the weapon.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Favor Ledger

Step 1 — Before accepting significant help (a ride, a couch, a wristband, a slot, housing, a connect), ask yourself: what would they need from me that I would find hard to say no to? Not paranoid. Just awake.

Step 2 — Name the terms out loud. “I really appreciate this. I want to make sure we’re both clear this isn’t creating an obligation.” Watch the reaction. A clean helper says “of course.” A Wook says “of course” and goes quiet in a way that means the opposite.

Step 3 — Pay it forward, not back. If someone gives you a clean thing, you give something equal forward — to someone else, if possible. This breaks the bilateral debt loop.

Step 4 — At any point when you feel the pull of I can’t say no because of what they did — that is the tab calling. Name it internally and act in your own interest anyway.

Step 5 — One safe person is not a network. It is a choke point. If one person has become your only access to transportation, housing, work, belonging, or care — audit how that happened and start building parallel access this week. Not this season. This week.

Vibe Check

Some people are just generous. Some communities run on gift culture that is genuinely reciprocal. Not every favor carries a tab. The tell is not the giving. The tell is what happens when you stop receiving — do they stay equally warm? Do they maintain equal investment? Or does the whole thing recalibrate around what you can give back?

Mirror Set

Have you ever reminded someone of a favor while they were in the process of saying no to you? Have you ever leveraged someone’s gratitude to override their hesitation? Have you ever made your help feel contingent on loyalty? The Covert Facilitator is the hardest Wook to see from the inside because the giving feels genuinely good — and it often is genuinely good — and the invoice comes so quietly.

Refractions

Queer or Trans Festie: If your only safe person at an event is the person who booked you or housed you or connected you, their reputation becomes your cage. Build lateral networks before you need them.

BIPOC Festie: In predominantly white festival spaces, the person who “takes you under their wing” first carries particular power. That power is not automatically clean. Watch the terms.

Sober/ND Festie: If someone controls your access to sober space, sensory retreat, or the logistical structure that makes the event safe for your body — your gratitude is real and the leverage is real. They do not have to be malicious. The architecture of dependence is still the architecture of dependence.

Artist/DJ: The booker who helps you break through and never stops reminding you they broke you through has installed themselves as a permanent tax on your career.

Runs in Every Direction

The Covert Facilitator can be your boyfriend with the van, your girlfriend with the guest list, your nonbinary camp lead with the only shower key, your polycule hub with all the invites, your sober mentor with the moral high ground, your love-and-light camp mom who reframes every boundary you set as you ‘not being open,’ your favorite DJ’s manager with a laminated smile. Power is not gendered. Access is access.

Sober Tuesday

At work, this is the coworker who covered your shift twice and now mentions it during every performance review. In your social circle, this is the friend who has bankrolled enough dinners to have standing to say “I think you’re making a mistake” as a full sentence, no follow-up required.

Track 03: The Sleeper Cell

(long-game embedded predation)

The Behavior

The Sleeper Cell is the one that makes everybody tired before the conversation starts.

They have been around forever. They helped build the shade dome in 2014. They know the ranger captain. They were at the first warehouse before the bathrooms had doors. They loaned the generator during the rain year. They introduced half the camp. They have photos with every beloved person and one story about saving someone during a bad trip that has become load-bearing architecture. Their reputation enters the room before the harmed person does. That is the weapon.

The Sleeper Cell does not need to grab your bag. They already helped decide where bags go. They do not need to isolate you loudly. They become the center of gravity — if you pull away, the whole camp tilts. If you speak up, people look at the calendar instead of the conduct. “But they’ve always been solid.” “They were there for me during the rough years.” “They do so much for the community.” “I’ve never seen that side.” No kidding, Moonbeam. That is what a side is.

The Sleeper Cell runs a long con through selective goodness. Generous in public. Strategic in private. Indispensable during crisis. Vague during accountability. Wounded when questioned. Surrounded by people who owe them one from six summers ago. They build a house out of receipts other people remember emotionally. Then they move the walls.

The Read

Watch for reputation shielding.

A good reputation can coexist with accountability. A shielded reputation cannot.

Ask: Do concerns about this person disappear into their history of good deeds? Are harmed people treated like threats to community stability? Does the group ask for impossible proof but accept the Wook’s vibes as evidence? Does everyone know “something happened” but nobody knows what can be said? Are newer people warned privately but not protected publicly? Does the person always survive the fallout cleaner than the people naming harm?

The Sleeper Cell’s tell is not that everyone hates them. The tell is that too many people have a private story and no shared language.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Three-Source Soundboard

Do not try to solo a Sleeper Cell with a kazoo.

Step 1 — Find three sources outside the same social circuit:

Source A: someone from your camp or crew.

Source B: someone from another camp, stage, or city.

Source C: someone default-world adjacent — therapist, organizer, former partner, sober friend, promoter, harm-reduction lead, housemate.

They cannot all be in the same group chat. That is not three sources. That is one group chat wearing three hats.

Step 2 — Ask only behavior questions. Not: “Do you think they’re a predator?” Ask: “Have you seen them retaliate when told no? Have you seen them isolate newer people? Have you seen them control access to rides, substances, housing, gigs, or camp resources? Have concerns been raised before? What happened after concerns were raised?”

Step 3 — Separate story from pattern. One bad breakup is data. Three people describing the same move in different costumes is a setlist.

Step 4 — Build a boring timeline. Dates. Locations. Behaviors. Witnesses. Outcomes. No adjectives if you can help it. “Creepy” loses in committee. “Asked three volunteers under twenty-five to share private sleeping space after late shifts in May, June, and August” survives the meeting.

Step 5 — Move protection before prosecution. You may not have enough to get the Wook removed by Friday. You may have enough to keep them away from first-timers, sleeping areas, intoxicated people, volunteers, green rooms, cashboxes, and people they have already targeted. Community safety does not have to wait for a courtroom-shaped thunderbolt.

Vibe Check

Long-standing community members are not suspicious because they are long-standing. Do not punish people for being useful, beloved, awkward, old-school, or bad at Instagram apologies. The question is not: “Do they have power?” The question is: “What happens when their power is questioned?”

Mirror Set

Have you ever hidden behind your history? Have you ever said, “After all I’ve done for this camp,” when someone named a real harm? Have you ever let your old good deeds stand between you and a current repair? If your reputation cannot survive accountability, it is not a reputation. It is staging.

Refractions

Veteran: Your nostalgia may be someone else’s gag order. The fact that they were kind to you in 2012 does not erase what they did last season.

Builder/Sound Camp Crew: Indispensable people become dangerous when nobody cross-trains their role. If only one person knows the generator, the patch bay, the trailer lock, or the stage plot, you built a throne by accident.

Camp Lead: Private warnings without public protocols create a predator aquarium. Everyone swims around the shark and calls it community.

Sober/Disabled/Neurodivergent Readers: If one embedded person controls your access needs, transportation, medication storage, sensory retreat, or sober support, their reputation can become your cage without them ever intending it.

Runs in Every Direction

The Sleeper Cell can be the beloved elder, the charming femme organizer, the queer house-scene connector, the disabled activist nobody wants to question, the sober leader with a decade chip, the touring bassist, the sound guy, the camp mom, the medicine man, the medicine woman, the medicine person with a headlamp and zero receipts. Identity can explain context. It does not cancel impact.

Sober Tuesday

In default world, this is the longtime employee who helped found the company and whose tenure makes every HR complaint into an institutional crisis. The performance review goes back to 2009. The harm happened in March. These are not the same thing.

Have You Been the Wook?

Mirror Check At The Disco Ball

Everybody wants this book to be about that guy.

The problem is the disco ball. It throws pieces of everyone.

Have you ever made your crisis the camp’s weather? Have you ever turned your favor into debt? Have you ever used “family” when you meant “free labor”? Have you ever gotten someone high and then treated their looseness as consent to your plan? Have you ever kept someone close because they made you feel important, even after they tried to step back? Have you ever let people believe a story because correcting it would cost you status?

Do not collapse into shame. Shame is just ego wearing wet socks.

Tales From the Gorge — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario as the Drop. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist.

Priya Chandrasekhar is twenty years old. She is at the Gorge for the first time. She drove up with her friend Tamsin, who knew someone who knew someone who had a camp spot, which is how twenty-year-olds end up in parking lots in the Columbia River Gorge knowing exactly one person and having the general sense that it will be fine.

It is fine. It is also a lot.

By Friday afternoon she has learned: wear dust-colored clothes because white becomes a spiritual experience by noon; porta-potties in the sun have a very specific atmosphere that no amount of patchouli is going to address; and the nod — the slow deliberate one that says I have been around — is something that takes about three years to land naturally and before that you look like a twenty-year-old practicing a nod.

At 6 p.m. a guy with a bucket hat and three gold rings finds her trying to untangle a camping chair.

He has a lighter and a multi-tool and the unhurried warmth of someone who has helped with camping chairs before. He introduces himself as Remy. He helps with the chair. He offers to show her where the water truck is. He mentions, in passing, that he needs someone to keep an eye on a tank during set break — just hold it, look friendly, he handles the rest, twenty an hour, easy.

Priya says: “Hold on, let me think about that.”

She does not say it meanly. She is not performing caution. She genuinely just — pauses. Her body did something before her brain caught up: a slight hesitation, a cooling in her chest, the same feeling she gets when a car salesman in default world pivots too quickly from friendly to asking for something specific. She recognizes it the way you recognize a word in a language you barely speak — not certain, but adjacent to certain.

She says: “What is the tank?”

He explains: nitrous. Balloons. Lot economy. It’s fine, it’s legal, well, it depends.

She says: “That’s okay, I don’t think I want to do that.”

He says: “No worries.” His warmth does not cool. He does not make her feel stupid. He walks her to the water truck anyway. He introduces her to two people from his camp. They are genuinely lovely. The whole thing is genuinely lovely.

And Priya stays warm back, and drinks the water, and watches set break from thirty yards east of where the nitrous setup is, and she sees the cash moving and the balloons going and the whole economy of it. She sees a girl in a white skirt take three in a row standing up, and she watches a guy in a camp shirt — not Remy, a different guy — step toward the girl with a hand out before her knees do their thing, and she watches the medical response that happens, and she watches the setup get quiet and orderly in a way that has the particular efficiency of people who know exactly when to stop and where to go.

She texts Tamsin: “I’m near the water truck, come here.”

Tamsin comes. They find their camp. They eat food. At the set they are next to each other for the whole second half.

That night Priya tells Tamsin about Remy, about the offer, about the pause. Tamsin says: “Good pause.” Priya says: “Yeah. Good pause.”

The watermelon moment is this: standing at the water truck, feeling the cold of the metal on her palm, watching the lot do its thing, and knowing — not in her brain yet, but in her sternum, in the space below her collarbone — that something in that offer had a weight to it she did not want to carry.

Her body knew.

She listened.

That is the whole lesson. It takes one chapter to explain and about six seconds in real time if you are paying attention.

The Domain Audit Drill

This is the first drill. Do not make it dramatic. Do not light incense. Do not announce to camp that you are “calling your power back” unless you want the group chat to start molting.

Open a boring note on your phone. Title it something no Wook will click. Suggestions: Packing Cubes. Sourdough Starter Notes. Mileage. Tent Repair. Receipts 2026. Hydration Plan.

List six domains. For each one: Who has access? Who has control? What is my backup?

Domain 1 — Transportation. Who has the keys? Can you leave without the person you came with? Do you have gas money, rideshare money, shuttle info, or a default-world contact who knows where you are?

Domain 2 — Shelter. Who knows where you sleep? Can someone remove you from camp? Do you have a backup plan — friend, motel, sober space, staff contact?

Domain 3 — Money. Who owes whom? Who controls the Venmo loop? Who paid for the ticket, booth, gas, room, wristband? Are terms written down?

Domain 4 — Substances/Medicine. Who is supplying? Who is pressuring? Who is testing? Who knows your medications, allergies, limits, or recovery status? Can you say no without losing belonging?

Domain 5 — Communication. Who has your phone? Who knows your passcode? Who controls the group chat narrative? Who has your passwords, socials, or artist page?

Domain 6 — Career/Access. Who controls your gigs, slots, intros, green room access, vendor placement, or stage time? Are they making you more independent or more dependent?

If any domain has one answer for access, control, and backup — one person, one path, no redundancy — that is where a Wook, if one is nearby, will find the lever.

The Sunrise Set

Zero slang. Just the true thing.

Some people use the word family to mean the thing family is supposed to mean: the people who show up when it costs them something, who celebrate your wins without accounting for them, who can be told a hard truth without converting it into a debt.

Some people use the word family to mean: you owe me now.

You are allowed to notice the difference. You are allowed to need real help and also be careful about where you accept it. You are allowed to say thank you and no at the same time.

Help that makes you smaller is not help. Help that makes your world narrower is not generosity. Help that arrives at exactly the moment that creates the most dependence is worth a second look.

The scene at its best runs on real generosity — spontaneous, no receipt, no balance sheet, given because someone needed it and you had it to give. That generosity is what makes this worth showing up for.

When help comes with invisible terms, it is not scene generosity. It is leverage wearing a kind face.

You are allowed to love the magic and protect yourself at the same time. They are not opposites. They are the same move.
