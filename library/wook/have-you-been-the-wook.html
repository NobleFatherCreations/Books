The cold open

📍 FESTIE POV

He is forty-nine years old.

His name is David Reyes and he has been in the scene for fourteen years and he runs the build crew for one of the larger regional camps in the Pacific Northwest and he is the guy younger festies call when they need a couch in default world, the guy who has driven three people to the airport at four in the morning because they needed it, the guy whose camp has a policy about sober support and accountability and who has been, by every external metric the scene applies to these things, one of the good ones.

He is reading this book in a camp chair in his backyard on a Sunday afternoon in September.

He has been reading it since July. Not straight through. In the way you read a book that you know is going to be good and that you are also slightly afraid of, which means you pick it up for two chapters and put it down for a week and then pick it up again and then put it down because a chapter landed somewhere you needed it to not land. He has been reading it the way you eat something that is simultaneously too good and too hot — in careful bites, with time between them for the temperature to become manageable.

He is in Chapter 29 now.

He put the book down twice in Chapter 24. Once in Chapter 21. Once at the very beginning of Chapter 5, which he put down, and then picked up, and then read the full cold open without stopping, and then sat with for a while.

He is in Chapter 29.

He is reading the cold open.

He reads the first paragraph. The camp elder, the couch in default world. He reads the second paragraph. The way the recognition lands. He reads the third paragraph.

He goes very still.

He puts the book down.

There is a name.

He has been telling himself, for two years, that he doesn’t quite remember it.

The name is Mira.

He remembers it.

He has been telling himself he doesn’t quite remember it because “doesn’t quite remember it” is the specific internal formulation that has allowed him to file the situation in the category labeled “things that need revisiting but not today” for two years without revisiting them. The category is a real category. He has visited it occasionally. He has approached the edge of it and determined that the contents are not in a state to be engaged with productively and backed away. He has done this approximately eight times in two years.

He remembers the name.

Mira Sandoval.

She was twenty-three. She is twenty-five now. She joined his camp two and a half years ago as a first-season arrival — energetic, skilled, a wire-wrap artist who produced extraordinary work and who was immediately beloved by the camp, including by him, including in the way that was the beginning of the problem.

He remembers exactly where they sat.

The back of the kitchen dome, after the Saturday dinner cleanup when most of the camp had migrated to the main stage, in the specific low amber light of the camp lanterns that have been at every camp since 2014. He remembers the conversation. He remembers what he said. He remembers what she said. He remembers the moment she stopped speaking — not ran out of words, stopped, the specific quality of a person who has understood something about the conversation that they are going to need time alone with.

He remembers the text she sent three days later, the one he responded to with warmth and care and exactly the right words, the words that were also designed to move the narrative in a direction that protected him.

He remembers the texts that stopped.

He has told himself, for two years, that she drifted. That she found a different camp. That the connection was what it was and she moved on in the way people in the scene move on, season to season, camp to camp, no drama, no incident.

He has not asked anyone in her former social circle what she is doing.

He has not asked because asking would require him to receive an answer, and the answer might contain her name, and her name in someone else’s mouth would require him to respond to it, and responding to it would require him to be in the same room with what happened.

He picks the book back up.

He reads: A beloved camp elder — 49, fourteen years in, runs the build crew for one of the big regional camps, the guy younger festies call when they need a couch in default world.

He puts the book down.

He picks it up.

He reads the next paragraph.

He puts the book down again.

He looks at the backyard — the garden that needs weeding, the camp gear stacked against the fence because the last event ended three weeks ago and he hasn’t fully struck the camp yet, the coffee that is cold now, the September afternoon that is doing the thing September afternoons do in the Pacific Northwest where the light goes golden-horizontal and everything looks like it has been slightly improved by a filter it is not actually running.

He is forty-nine years old.

He has been in the scene for fourteen years.

He has run the accountability camp. He has run the briefing. He has been the person people call.

He also did a thing in a kitchen dome in low amber lantern light two and a half years ago, and he has been telling himself for two years that he doesn’t quite remember the name of the person it was done to, and the name is Mira Sandoval, and the book he is holding has the chapter that tells him what to do next.

He picks the book back up.

He reads the chapter.

He is going to read the rest of this chapter now, and so are you, and the chapter is going to say the things it has to say, and some of them he will recognize from the inside and some you will recognize from the inside, and the chapter’s job is not to be gentle about the recognition.

The recognition is what it is.

The recognition is the point.

Accountability is not the apology letter that you spend six weeks crafting because you need it to land exactly right, by which I mean you need it to make you look like the kind of person who writes good apology letters. Accountability is: you stop doing the thing. You stop doing the thing first. Then you figure out what the damage was. Then you ask the person what they need, not what you need to give them. The Tapers’ Section

AA’s Eighth Step · Restorative Practice · The Behavior-Change Literature

Very brief. The chapter earns its length through content, not theory.

AA’s Eighth Step is: make a list of all persons we had harmed, and become willing to make amends to them all. The scene has been borrowing harm-reduction concepts from recovery culture for twenty years. This one is worth borrowing directly. The list is the start. The willingness is the second thing. The amends — which are different from apologies, which we cover in Track 3 — come after.

Restorative practice in justice contexts prioritizes the needs of the harmed person over the procedural satisfaction of the institution. Translated to the scene: accountability is not primarily about making the person who caused harm feel better about themselves. It is about what the person who was harmed needs. What they need may not include a conversation with the person who harmed them. What they need may be the behavior change and the distance and the acknowledgment to the community. Their terms, not yours.

The behavior-change literature has a consistent finding: self-reported insight without behavior change is not change. The person who can describe their harmful pattern in sophisticated language while continuing to run the pattern is not accountable. They are articulate. The difference between being articulate about your harm and being accountable for it is the behavior change. That is the chapter’s spine.

You are in.

🎫 THE TRACKS

Track 01: The Honest Inventory

(moral inventory — AA-derived, applied scene-wide)

The Behavior

The honest inventory is a list.

Not a confession. Not a public statement. Not a post. A private list, written on paper or in a note titled “camping gear” or “event logistics” or whatever you need it to be titled so that you will write it honestly and not perform it.

The list has four columns.

Column one: the person. Their name. If you cannot bring yourself to write their name, that is information about the column.

Column two: what you did. Specific. Behavioral. Not your interpretation of the event — what you actually did. Not “I was working through my own stuff” or “we had a complicated dynamic” — what you actually did. The move. Which Track from this book it maps to, if you can find it.

Column three: what it cost them. Not what it cost you. What it cost them. Their disruption, their loss, their adjustment, their subsequent experience. You may not know this fully. You can say “I don’t know the full impact” as part of the entry. The not-knowing is also information.

Column four: the status. Nothing done. Acknowledgment made. Behavior changed. Amends attempted. Amends completed. Mira two years ago unfiled under “doesn’t quite remember the name” becomes: Mira Sandoval, amber lantern light, kitchen dome, the thing I said, the text I sent to manage the narrative, the texts she stopped sending, the cost — don’t know the full extent, the two years I didn’t look — status: nothing done.

That is the honest inventory.

The Read

The inventory is honest when it costs you something to read back.

If you can read back your own inventory and feel fine about it, either you are more accountable in practice than most people or you have written the inventory that protects you rather than the one that reflects what happened. The honest inventory is uncomfortable. That is not a design flaw. That is the test.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The List Protocol

Step 1 — Make the list. Private. Not for publication. Not for the person you harmed. For you first.

Step 2 — Four columns: name, what I did, what it cost them, status.

Step 3 — Write one entry per person. No minimum number. No maximum. As many as it takes to be honest.

Step 4 — Read it back once. Note what you feel when you read it. The feeling is data about whether the list is honest.

Step 5 — Keep the list. It is a living document. You will add to it as you remember things and as you do things. The goal is not to complete it. The goal is to maintain it honestly.

Vibe Check

Some people have short lists. Some people have very short lists because they have already done this work and their behavior has been consistently accountable over years. The short list is possible. The short list that contains only things you have already resolved and nothing new is less likely, because most people who have been in the scene for years have a longer list than they have been accounting for.

Mirror Set

The entire Track is a mirror. The mirror set here is the entire Track. You are holding it right now.

Refractions

Scene Veterans: The longer you have been in the scene, the longer the honest inventory is likely to be, because the longer you have been here the more people you have been in relationship with and the more situations have occurred. Tenure is not an exemption from the list. Tenure is the reason the list has content.

Camp Leads and Event Organizers: If you have held a position of power in the scene, the inventory includes the decisions made from that position that cost other people things. The structural harm — the labor distribution, the access control, the exit narrative — goes on the list the same as the interpersonal harm.

Runs in Every Direction

The honest inventory belongs to the camp lead auditing his own six years, the twenty-three-year-old checking whether one bad night was a mistake or the start of a pattern, and the veteran of the scene who has more names on the list than she expected. The format applies to everyone. Nobody’s exempt because their intentions were good.

Sober Tuesday

The performance review, self-assessment version. Most people write the version that supports the raise. The honest version includes the times the work caused harm to colleagues, the times the decisions were wrong, the times the impact was real and the intent was good and the two things are different. The honest inventory is the performance review your actual colleagues would write.

Track 02: The Pattern vs. Mistake Audit

(intent / impact / repetition assessment)

The Behavior

There is a difference between a mistake and a pattern.

A mistake is: you did a harmful thing once, without intent, without repetition, without using the full framework that harm-reduction knowledge provides to avoid it. A mistake is: you trusted a source you shouldn’t have trusted and someone got hurt and you changed your sourcing practice immediately. A mistake is: you said something once that landed wrong and you corrected it the same day.

A pattern is: you did a similar thing across multiple situations, with multiple people, in a way that either recurred despite knowing it caused harm or that you had enough information to recognize was likely to cause harm and continued anyway. A pattern is: the same play run in different contexts across multiple years. A pattern is: the amber lantern light conversation in 2022 and the different version of the same conversation in 2019 that you have also filed under a different “doesn’t quite remember” category.

The distinction matters because mistakes and patterns require different responses. A mistake requires acknowledgment, repair where possible, and changed behavior going forward. A pattern requires all of that plus a more fundamental examination of what drove the repetition — what the pattern was serving, what it was getting you, what would have needed to change for you to stop before you did.

The Read

For each item on the honest inventory: is this a mistake or a pattern?

The test: how many times have I done this, or a version of this, with how many different people?

One time: audit for mistake or pattern based on what you knew and when.

Multiple times with different people: pattern. The conversation is different.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Pattern Map

Step 1 — For any inventory item that could be a pattern: list every instance you can identify. Not just the most recent. All of them.

Step 2 — What did the instances have in common? The situation type, the people targeted, the specific behavior, the social context, the result. What is the shape of the pattern?

Step 3 — What did the pattern produce for you? Not what it cost others — what it gave you. Social capital. Access. Control. Validation. Belonging. Something. The pattern had a function. What was it?

Step 4 — What would you have needed to meet that need differently? Not “I was working through my stuff” — specifically, what resources, relationships, or practices would have allowed you to meet that need without running the pattern?

Step 5 — Which of those resources do you now have? Which do you not? The ones you do not have are the ones to build before the pattern recurs.

Vibe Check

Most people have made genuine mistakes and do not need to treat themselves as patterns. The pattern audit is for situations where the honest inventory reveals repetition across multiple people over multiple years. The pattern audit is not a torture device. It is the analysis that precedes the change.

Mirror Set

Have you ever said “I made a mistake” about something that, examined honestly, was the third instance of the same move? “Mistake” as a category protects you from the pattern analysis. The pattern analysis is where the change becomes possible.

Refractions

Long-Tenured Scene Professionals: Fourteen years in the scene with twelve-lever persuasion running for some portion of that time is a long pattern. The pattern map will be long. The pattern map is worth making.

Sober Mentors and Camp Leads with Authority: When the pattern runs through positions of trust and authority, the pattern’s impact is amplified by the power differential. The map needs to include the power differential as a variable.

Runs in Every Direction

The distinction applies the same way to the camp lead running his third audit, the first-season vendor checking a single bad night, the touring artist counting how many times a version of the same excuse has come up, and anyone running the audit regardless of their identity. The analysis is the same. The inventory is the same.

Sober Tuesday

The “I was distracted that day” explanation that covers the third time the same mistake occurred in the same meeting context with the same person in the same situation. One distracted day is a mistake. Three is a pattern the explanation is protecting.

Track 03: The Apology That Actually Means It

(restorative apology architecture)

The Behavior

Most apologies are for the apologizer.

This is not a cynical statement. It is a structural observation. The conventional apology — the public statement, the letter, the DM, the camp-meeting speech — is typically constructed to produce an outcome for the person giving it: relief from guilt, improved reputation, a sense of closure, the social signal of having done the right thing. These outcomes may accompany a genuine apology. They should not be the primary driver of it.

An apology that actually means it has a different structure.

It does not require a response from the person who was harmed.

It does not ask for forgiveness.

It does not explain the circumstances in a way that positions the harmful behavior as understandable.

It does not time itself for the apologizer’s convenience.

It does not put the person who was harmed in the position of managing the apologizer’s feelings about having apologized.

It says: “I did [specific thing]. The impact was [what I understand the impact to have been, with the acknowledgment that I may not have the full picture]. I am sorry. I am not asking for anything from you. [Optional: here is what I have changed in my behavior.]”

That is the apology that actually means it.

The architecture is the point. The architecture is what protects the apology from becoming another extraction — the extraction of forgiveness, the extraction of absolution, the extraction of a response that makes the apologizer feel better about what they did.

The Read

Before any apology: ask “who is this for?”

If the honest answer is “for me” — that is where to start. Not by suppressing the apology. By figuring out what you need in order to offer it in a way that is genuinely for the person you harmed.

The apology the person who was harmed might need from you is not the same as the apology that would make you feel better. Adjust.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Apology Architecture

Step 1 — Ask: does the person want contact from you? If you do not know: do not make contact. The apology that arrives without the recipient’s consent is another instance of prioritizing your needs over theirs.

Step 2 — If contact is appropriate: use the formula. “I did [specific behavior]. I understand the impact was [specific impact, with acknowledgment of incomplete knowledge]. I am sorry. I am not asking for a response.”

Step 3 — Do not explain. The explanation is for you. If you include the explanation, the apology becomes a negotiation about whether your circumstances mitigated the harm. They did not. The harm occurred.

Step 4 — Do not ask for forgiveness. Forgiveness is the harmed person’s to give or not give at their own pace and on their own terms. Asking for it is asking them to do emotional labor for you.

Step 5 — Send it and release it. The apology is not the beginning of a conversation unless the other person initiates one. The apology is the thing you needed to say. You have said it. You do not wait for a grade.

Vibe Check

Some people do not want a direct apology. For some people, the best thing you can do is change your behavior and stay out of their life. The question “does the person want contact from you” is not a formality. It is the most important question in the whole Track. Answer it before you proceed.

Mirror Set

Have you ever apologized primarily to feel better about yourself? Have you ever structured an apology so that the most prominent content was an explanation of your circumstances? Have you ever timed an apology for a moment that was convenient for you and not for the person you harmed? All of these are apologies for the apologizer. The apology that actually means it is for the person you harmed. Start there.

Refractions

Online and Scene-Platform Apologies: A public apology on Instagram or in the camp group chat is an apology to the audience, not to the person harmed. It may be appropriate. It is not a substitute for the direct apology and it carries its own set of problems, including the pressure it places on the harmed person to respond publicly in a context they did not choose.

Power-Differential Situations: When the apology comes from a person who still holds power over the person they harmed — same camp, same scene, same circuit — the apology can function as coercion regardless of intent. Make sure the person has the genuine freedom to not respond before you send it.

Runs in Every Direction

The architecture applies whether it’s the camp lead apologizing to someone he outranks, the veteran apologizing to a first-timer, the vendor apologizing to a customer, or anyone adjusting for a power differential, a platform, and whether contact is even appropriate. The specific delivery changes. The architecture is the same.

Sober Tuesday

The family group text apology. “Sorry if what I said came across wrong.” This is not an apology. This is a sentence about a misunderstanding that does not exist. The apology that actually means it says: “I did [specific thing]. I am sorry.” That sentence. In the family group text. Without the explanation or the forgiveness request.

Track 04: The Restitution Question

(amends in the action sense, not the word sense)

The Behavior

The apology is a word.

Amends is an action.

AA’s ninth step is not: apologize to everyone you harmed. It is: make direct amends wherever possible except when to do so would injure them or others. The amends is the action that repairs the damage where repair is possible. The words are the beginning. The action is the accountability.

In scene contexts, the Restitution Question is: “What do I need to do that is different from what I have been doing?”

Not “what do I need to say.” What do I need to do.

Sometimes the action is: stop attending the same events as the person you harmed so they do not have to manage your presence while they are trying to heal.

Sometimes the action is: distribute credit and money and recognition that was owed and withheld.

Sometimes the action is: tell the truth to the camp network that was told a managed version of the story.

Sometimes the action is: cross-train the function you have been holding hostage so the camp can operate without you.

Sometimes the action is: stop distributing the untested batch.

Sometimes the action is: don’t go to the next festival for a season.

Sometimes the action is not dramatic. Sometimes it is: update the terms of the labor distribution so that the skim stops running. Sometimes it is: stop applying the three-asks pattern to people you find in the circuit. Sometimes it is: test the batch.

The restitution is specific. The restitution is actionable. The restitution is the changed behavior made concrete.

The Read

For each inventory item: what is the action that constitutes genuine amends, as distinct from the words that constitute the apology?

If the action and the words are identical — if the only amends you can offer is the apology — that is acceptable when the harm was a single instance with no ongoing dimensions. For pattern harm, there is usually a behavioral dimension to the amends that goes beyond the apology.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Action Inventory

Step 1 — For each item on the honest inventory: what is the action equivalent of the apology?

Step 2 — Is the action in your power? There are some amends you cannot make — you cannot undo what happened, you cannot restore what was taken, you cannot remove the Encore from someone’s head. The action inventory is about what is in your power. Those things.

Step 3 — Is the action what the harmed person needs, or what you need to give? These may not be the same. Ask if you can.

Step 4 — Do the action before announcing the action. The announcement of the action is for you. The action is for them.

Step 5 — The ongoing action is the changed behavior. Every event you attend without running the pattern is the amends. The amends is not complete in one gesture. It is the continued changed behavior over time.

Vibe Check

Some situations have no action available except the changed behavior going forward. The harm was done, the person is gone, the direct amends is not appropriate or not possible. In those cases: the changed behavior is the restitution, and it is available, and it is the thing the chapter is asking for.

Mirror Set

Have you ever offered an apology while continuing the behavior the apology was about? Have you ever described yourself as accountable while the pattern was still running? The apology without the changed behavior is a speech, not accountability.

Refractions

Camp Leads with Structural Harm: The restitution for structural harm — the labor skim, the access hierarchy, the soft exile pattern — is structural. The word-level apology does not repair the structural harm. The changed structure repairs the structural harm. The new labor distribution. The restored access. The transparent budget.

Touring Professionals: The restitution for vendor row harm, for the LLC Trapdoor, for the unpaid labor — is the money. The words are not the money. The check is the money. Send the check.

Runs in Every Direction

The Restitution Question asks the same thing of the camp lead who took credit for someone’s idea, the vendor who underpaid a helper, the veteran who let a bad pattern slide for years, and anyone facing a different kind of harm. What action, not what words. The question applies universally.

Sober Tuesday

The colleague whose idea you presented without credit. The apology is “I should have credited you.” The amends is crediting them now, publicly, in the context where the presentation was made. The words are the apology. The public credit is the amends.

Track 05: The Quieter Wook Test

(behavior modification vs. concealment)

The Behavior

Some people who cause harm get quieter.

They don’t stop causing harm. They get quieter about it. More sophisticated. More calibrated to the information the community now has. The forty-five-minute warm conversation gets compressed to twenty-three minutes because someone read the chapter about three-asks. The mason jar gets replaced with something subtler. The Aged-Out Disappearance gets managed with more care. The Sister Word gets used less frequently because the Sister Word is now in a book.

The Quieter Wook has absorbed the knowledge and applied it to concealment rather than to change.

This is the chapter’s hardest Track because it requires internal honesty about a question that concealment specifically avoids: “Am I actually changing, or am I just better at hiding?”

The concealment operates by producing the external markers of accountability — the quieter approach, the modified vocabulary, the absence of the most recognizable patterns — without producing the internal change that the external markers are supposed to represent. The Quieter Wook looks accountable from outside. The Quieter Wook knows what they are.

The Read

The test is internal. External observers cannot run this test on you. Only you can.

The test question: “If I were in an identical situation to the one on my inventory, in which the social cost calculation was the same and the information available to the community was the same, would I do it again?”

If the honest answer is “probably not, because the community would notice” — that is concealment. The behavior change is driven by social risk, not by actual change.

If the honest answer is “no, because I understand what it costs and I don’t want to cost someone that” — that is change.

The test is the difference between those two answers.

Counter-Drop — Run This: → The Closed-Door Audit

Step 1 — The closed-door audit is run in the specific mental space of: nobody will know, no social cost attached, same person, same situation, same access, same context, same opportunity.

Step 2 — In that space: would you do it again? The inventory item. The pattern.

Step 3 — If the answer is “probably yes, but I know better now so I wouldn’t” — you are describing self-regulation under social pressure. That is useful. It is not the same as change.

Step 4 — Self-regulation under social pressure is a starting point, not a destination. The destination is: the thing is genuinely unappealing because you understand its cost. How do you get there? Therapy, accountability communities, continued practice of naming the pattern and choosing differently when it would be easy to run it.

Step 5 — The test recurs. Run it quarterly. The answer may change over time. The change in the answer is the evidence of actual change.

Vibe Check

Some people pass the Quieter Wook Test and are genuinely accountable. Genuine change is possible. The test is not designed to produce a predetermined negative answer. It is designed to distinguish the two outcomes accurately. If your honest answer to the closed-door audit is “no, genuinely, not because of social risk but because I understand the cost” — that is the answer. That is what the chapter is asking for.

Mirror Set

Have you ever described yourself as having changed while privately knowing the change was calibration rather than transformation? Have you ever used the vocabulary of accountability to manage your reputation while your behavior continued? The Quieter Wook has the best vocabulary for describing their own accountability. The vocabulary is not the same as the accountability.

Refractions

People in accountability communities: The people who have done the most formal accountability work are the most skilled at the vocabulary. The vocabulary fluency is not the test. The closed-door audit is the test.

Scene Figures with Significant Social Capital: When your social capital makes the social risk of discovery high, the Quieter Wook move is especially likely because the cost of concealment is low and the cost of discovery would be high. The closed-door audit is the only test that is not subject to that social risk calculation.

Runs in Every Direction

The Test is available to the camp lead who’s learned the vocabulary of accountability, the veteran who’s stopped saying the specific things he got called out for, the vendor who’s cleaned up his language but not his behavior, and anyone of any identity, tenure, or position. The vocabulary is equally available to everyone. The closed-door audit is the only test that matters.

Sober Tuesday

The manager who attended the DEI training, learned the vocabulary, stopped saying the specific things they got feedback about, and continued the structural behavior that the specific things were surface expressions of. The training worked on the surface. The surface is not the structure. The structure requires the closed-door audit.
