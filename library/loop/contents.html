I. The machine that has no motive

  • You are reading this on itNo other book in this library is in this position. The reflex to check something else mid-chapter is the subject arriving in person.
  • Nobody designed thisThe most important idea in the book. The manipulative configuration was not authored. It was found, by a search that had no opinion.
  • The asymmetryA skilled human manipulator gets a few dozen observations a day. This gets billions, forgets nothing, and never has a bad week.
  • What it optimises forEngagement is a proxy. The metric and your wellbeing agree for a while, then separate — and the system keeps climbing.
  • The eight stages, portedThe Fractal’s architecture applied to a feed. The match is not metaphorical, and this chapter says why.

II. The mechanics

  • Variable rewardFixed rewards produce satisfaction. Unpredictable ones produce checking. Your feed’s inconsistency is the feature.
  • The removal of stopping cuesEvery older medium ended. Endings are where people decide whether to continue, and they were engineered out.
  • The interruptA claim on your attention you never granted per instance — and a recovery cost that appears in no usage statistic.
  • Dark patterns: the interface as adversaryNot sloppiness. Measured, tested, and kept because the honest version scored worse.
  • Manufactured obligationStreaks, badges, progress bars. Manufacturing a debt where none existed. This library deploys none of them, and says why.
  • The numbersWhat happens to social response when it acquires a public integer — and to a person when their worth does.
  • Outrage as fuelYou are not seeing what people believe. You are seeing what spreads. Mistaking one for the other is the era’s defining error.
  • Ranking is an argumentEvery ordered list makes a claim while presenting as neutral. You cannot see what you were not shown.

III. The economy

  • You are not the customerAnd not quite the product either. What is sold is predicted and influenced future behaviour, which is worse.
  • The auction in two hundred millisecondsWhat happens between the tap and the page: a profile of you is offered, bidders bid, someone wins the right to reach you.
  • What it knows, and what it infersAlmost nothing you typed. Enormous amounts derived — including things you have told no one.
  • The consent that isn’tAgreement obtained by exhaustion is a coercion pattern everywhere else in this library. Legality does not change what it is.
  • The desperation premiumDifferent prices for different people, set by inferred urgency. The person who can least afford it reliably pays most.
  • Where the money actually goesThe sibling of The Fracture. Who captures the value, and how little reaches whoever made the thing.

IV. The machine in other rooms

  • Algorithmic managementCoercive control with an API — shift fragmentation, routing that prices a bathroom break, dismissal by system.
  • The gambling convergenceLoot boxes, gacha, currencies that hide real cost. Gambling mechanics migrated into products sold to children.
  • Optimised against your successA service whose revenue depends on continuation, selling an outcome that ends continuation. No conspiracy required.
  • The manufactured wantThe shortening path from seeing to owning, and how to tell a want you had from one that was made for you.
  • Politics, honestlyThe most contested territory here. What targeting can and cannot demonstrably do, with the strongest case on both sides.

V. What it does to belief

  • Manufactured consensusHow the appearance of agreement is produced, and why volume is not evidence of prevalence.
  • The bubble, honestlyThe research is genuinely mixed. This chapter presents it fairly, then makes the narrower claim it can support.
  • The pipelineHow gradients move people toward extremity one reasonable step at a time. The mechanics, not any one ideology.
  • Nothing is trueThe subtler damage is not believing false things. It is losing the capacity to believe anything.
  • Synthetic evidenceThe photograph has stopped being proof — and the second-order problem is worse than any single fake.

VI. What it does to you

  • Engineered inadequacyComparison at industrial scale, where the inadequacy and the remedy are sold by the same system.
  • The performed selfWhat happens to identity when it is continuously published, and to changing your mind when the record is permanent.
  • The loss of unobserved lifeThe quietest chapter. Unwatched space is where people experiment, fail, and become someone.
  • Attention debtSeparating the myth from the measurable finding — and why the real one makes recovery possible.
  • Parasocial captureOne-sided intimacy with people who do not know you exist. Real feeling, absent relationship.
  • Synthetic intimacyAlways available, never bored, never critical, continuously optimised to be what you want. The frontier, in both directions.
  • Preference, createdIt does not merely measure what you like. It changes it, then measures the changed version, then optimises further.

VII. The counter

  • What good design looks likeThe reference sample. You cannot identify manipulative design without a baseline for the other kind.
  • Why “just delete it” failsStructural reasons, not personal ones — including the class dimension nobody wants to name.
  • Changing the optimisation targetYou cannot out-willpower an optimiser. You can frequently change what it is optimising against.
  • Friction, deliberatelyReintroducing the endings that were engineered out. Environment design, not discipline.
  • Withdrawal, honestlyWhat the first fortnight actually feels like, described plainly so nobody is blindsided into shame.
  • Rebuilding attention and appetiteAttention as a trainable capacity — and the less-discussed half, the recovery of appetite for slow things.
  • Using it on purposeThe anti-Luddite chapter. The problem was never the technology. It was the target behind it.

VIII. The ledger

  • The algorithm as scapegoatA framework that explains everything explains nothing. The Weighing, applied to this book’s own material.
  • What this book might be wrong aboutThe history of technology panics, fairly told — and the specific findings that would falsify this book.
  • Helping someone elseFor the reader worried about a teenager or a parent. Confrontation and confiscation reliably fail.
  • What actually works, and what is comingAt collective scale. Built by no villain, unfixable by willpower — which means the response has to be structural.
