Controls Your relationship with your own chemistry

Captured The body’s right to decide what enters it

The Narrative — from the inside

The body has a pharmacy inside it. That is the thing the industry needed you to forget. Your body produces its own painkillers — endorphins so potent that their name is literally a contraction of "endogenous morphine." Your body produces its own anti-anxiety medication — GABA, serotonin, oxytocin — molecules that regulate fear, stabilize mood, and generate the felt sense of safety without a prescription, without a co-pay, without a side effect profile printed in font so small it requires a magnifying glass to read. Your body produces its own anti-inflammatory compounds. Its own immune modulators. Its own sleep chemicals. Its own stimulants. Its own relaxants. The body is, in fact, the most sophisticated pharmaceutical manufacturing facility on earth — a system that has been developing, testing, refining, and deploying chemical interventions for three hundred million years of vertebrate evolution, across every conceivable environmental challenge, with a safety record that no pharmaceutical company in existence can match. And the body's pharmacy is responsive. It doesn't just produce chemicals on a fixed schedule. It produces them in response to signals — the signal of movement, the signal of rest, the signal of sunlight, the signal of human touch, the signal of laughter, the signal of tears, the signal of food that the body recognizes as food, the signal of sleep that is deep enough and long enough, the signal of safety, the signal of belonging. Each signal triggers a cascade. Each cascade produces the molecules the body needs at the moment the body needs them, in the dosage the body can metabolize, at a rate the body can sustain. The system was not designed by a committee. It was not approved by a regulatory agency. It was not tested in a twelve-week trial with a sample size of four hundred. It was tested across the entirety of evolutionary time. In every environment the species has ever encountered. And it passed. You are here because it passed. Every ancestor who contributed to your genetic line survived because their body's pharmacy worked. And your body carries that pharmacy. Right now. Producing the chemicals you need. If you give it the signals it was designed to receive. But you don't give it the signals. Not because you don't want to. Because the civilization made the signals expensive, inaccessible, or invisible. The signal of movement — the body needs to move, but the job requires sitting. The signal of rest — the body needs to sleep, but the economy requires productivity. The signal of sunlight — the body needs light, but the office has no windows. The signal of human touch — the body needs contact, but the culture has made touch suspect. The signal of whole food — the body needs nourishment, but the food system provides product. The signal of safety — the body needs to feel safe, but the economy runs on precarity. The signal of belonging — the body needs community, but the infrastructure produced isolation. Each signal that the civilization blocked is a signal the body's pharmacy needed to function. And.

Stage 1: Idealize

Targeting

01 · Love Bombing

Warmth delivered faster than trust can be earned.

Individual. The pharmaceutical sales representative arrives at the doctor's office with warmth, expertise, and gifts — samples that allow the doctor to give the patient something immediately, studies that support the product, lunch for the office, branded pens and notepads that keep the product name in the doctor's visual field all day — and the warmth is professional and personable and persistent, and the representative visits regularly, remembers the doctor's name, the nurse's birthday, the office manager's preferences — and the relationship that develops feels like partnership and functions as influence, because the doctor who prescribes the representative's product is the doctor the representative will continue to visit, and the visiting is the reward, and the not-visiting is the punishment, and neither is ever stated explicitly.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry love-bombs the medical profession — funding medical schools, sponsoring conferences, endowing professorships, providing research grants — and the generosity creates a relational environment in which the industry is not an external commercial entity but an integrated partner in the medical enterprise, and the integration makes questioning the partner feel like questioning the enterprise itself.

Civilizational. The pharmaceutical industry is idealized as the vanguard of human health — "we're developing the cures," "we're fighting disease," "we're saving lives" — and the idealizing wraps a commercial industry in the sacred cloak of healing, which means the consumer who purchases the product is not buying a commodity but participating in a mission, and the mission makes the price feel secondary.

02 · Weaponized Generosity

A gift you can’t refuse, recorded on a ledger you never signed.

Individual. The free sample is the most effective form of weaponized generosity in this sector — a two-week supply of a medication that the patient tries, that works, that creates a preference, and that now requires a prescription that costs $300 per month, and the preference was created by the gift, and the gift cost the company pennies.

Institutional. The industry provides free continuing medical education (CME) to doctors — education that the doctors need to maintain their licenses and that is provided by the companies whose products the education features, and the education is the generosity and the education is also the marketing, and the marketing is more effective because it arrives disguised as learning.

Civilizational. The pharmaceutical industry's development of vaccines and life-saving medications is genuine generosity that is also the shield behind which the industry's extractive practices are protected — "we developed the COVID vaccine in record speed" invoked every time the industry is criticized for drug pricing, and the invocation converts the industry's genuine contribution into the deniability for the industry's ongoing extraction.

Stage 2: Hook

Entrapment through investment

03 · Future Faking

A future vivid enough to keep you waiting, designed never to arrive.

Individual. "We're developing a cure" is the pharmaceutical industry's most durable future fake — the promise of a cure deployed to sustain the patient's investment in the management while the cure is perpetually five to ten years away, because a cure is a one-time purchase and management is a recurring revenue stream.

Institutional. "The pipeline is full of promising therapies" is the industry's institutional future fake — spoken at every investor meeting, printed in every annual report, sustaining the stock price that drives the executive compensation that drives the decision-making — and the pipeline may be full and the promise is the thing that keeps the investment flowing.

Civilizational. "Precision medicine will transform healthcare" is the civilizational future fake — the promise that genomic analysis will enable individually tailored pharmaceuticals that will be more effective and have fewer side effects, deployed to sustain faith in the pharmaceutical model while the current model continues to produce blockbuster drugs designed for the largest possible market rather than for the individual patient.

04 · Hoovering

The pull back in — nostalgia, guilt, and manufactured urgency about what you’ll lose.

Individual. The patient who stopped taking the medication receives the refill reminder — from the pharmacy, from the insurance company, from the health app — and the reminder is positioned as helpful and the helpfulness is the hoover, because the patient who stopped may have stopped for a reason, and the reminder overrides the reason with the routine.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry re-engages lapsed prescribers through updated studies, new indications, reformulated products — "we've addressed the concerns, the new version is better" — and the re-engagement pulls the prescriber back into the product relationship.

Civilizational. Every health scare — every pandemic, every outbreak, every new disease that enters the public consciousness — hoovers the population back into the pharmaceutical relationship through fear, urgency, and the promise that the industry has or will have the product that will protect them.

Stage 3: Devalue

Transfer of authority over your worth

05 · Devaluation

The praise that hooked you, slowly converted into the criticism that keeps you striving.

Individual. The patient's own capacity for healing is devalued — "you can't manage this with lifestyle changes alone," "this is a chemical problem that requires a chemical solution," "your brain isn't producing enough serotonin" — and the devaluation of the body's pharmacy ensures that the patient does not try the intervention that would render the pharmaceutical unnecessary.

Institutional. Alternative and complementary medicine is systematically devalued — herbal medicine dismissed as folklore, acupuncture dismissed as placebo, nutritional intervention dismissed as insufficient, movement therapy dismissed as supplementary — and the devaluation ensures that the pharmaceutical intervention remains the first-line treatment rather than the last resort.

Civilizational. The body itself is devalued — positioned as a machine that malfunctions rather than a living system that responds to conditions — and the mechanical framing ensures that the solution is always a part to be replaced rather than a condition to be changed.

06 · Gaslighting

Making you doubt your own memory until the system becomes your only source of truth.

Individual. The patient who says "I don't think this medication is working" is told "give it more time," "we need to adjust the dosage," "sometimes it takes weeks to take effect" — and the statements may be clinically accurate and the statements also function as gaslighting when the medication genuinely isn't working, because the patient's experience of the medication is overridden by the prescriber's faith in the product, and the faith was installed by the industry that produces the product.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry marketed the "chemical imbalance" theory of depression for decades — a theory that was never conclusively proven by the scientific literature but that was repeated so consistently in advertising, in medical education, and in public health messaging that it became common knowledge — and the theory was not a scientific conclusion, it was a marketing narrative that positioned the pharmaceutical product as the correction of a biological error rather than the management of a human experience, and the narrative manufactured the demand for the product the narrative described.

Civilizational. The population is told "modern medicine has added decades to human lifespan" — a true statement that obscures the fact that the increase is overwhelmingly due to public health measures (sanitation, clean water, vaccination) rather than pharmaceutical intervention, and that the pharmaceutical industry's contribution to longevity is real but significantly smaller than the industry's marketing suggests, and the gap between the contribution and the claim is the gaslighting.

07 · Double Bind

Every available choice routed to the same punishment.

Individual. The patient faces a foundational double bind — take the medication and accept the side effects (weight gain, sexual dysfunction, cognitive blunting, dependency), or don't take the medication and face the condition the medication manages (depression, anxiety, pain, sleeplessness) — and the bind is presented as though these are the only two options, while the third option (addressing the conditions that produced the symptoms) is rarely presented because the third option is not a pharmaceutical product.

Institutional. Doctors face a structural double bind — prescribe and manage the side effects, or don't prescribe and risk the patient's deterioration and the malpractice liability that accompanies it — and the bind is resolved in favor of prescribing because the liability risk of not prescribing exceeds the liability risk of prescribing, and the asymmetry was created by a legal and medical culture that the pharmaceutical industry shaped.

Civilizational. The population faces a civilizational double bind — accept pharmaceutical intervention as the primary model of healthcare and live with the dependency, the side effects, and the cost, or reject pharmaceutical intervention and be labeled "anti-science" and "a danger to public health" — and the bind offers no position from which a citizen can engage critically with the pharmaceutical model without being classified as either a consumer or a denier.

Stage 4: Confuse

Destruction of your inner compass

08 · Intermittent Reinforcement

Reward and punishment on an unpredictable schedule — the chemistry of addiction.

Individual. The medication works and then it doesn't — effective for months, then less effective, requiring dosage adjustment, then effective again at the higher dose, then requiring augmentation with a second medication — and the unpredictability of the response keeps the patient in the pharmaceutical relationship because the last period of effectiveness proves that effectiveness is possible.

Institutional. FDA approvals arrive unpredictably — the drug that was expected to be approved is delayed, the drug that was not expected is fast-tracked — and the unpredictability keeps the industry in a state of competitive frenzy that produces more products rather than better ones.

Civilizational. Pharmaceutical breakthroughs arrive intermittently — the cancer drug that extends life by four months, the gene therapy that cures a rare disease, the vaccine that prevents a pandemic — and each breakthrough is amplified into evidence that the model works while the chronic, ongoing, unremarkable management of millions of patients on medications that do not cure anything generates no headlines and enormous revenue.

09 · Moving the Goalposts

A finish line that moves the instant you reach it, because it doesn’t exist.

Individual. The threshold for treatment keeps lowering — the cholesterol number that was acceptable last decade is a treatment target this decade, the blood pressure that was normal last year requires medication this year, the blood sugar level that was monitored last month is now pre-diabetic — and each threshold reduction expands the market by converting healthy people into patients.

Institutional. The definition of disease itself expands — "pre-hypertension," "pre-diabetes," "sub-clinical depression" — each "pre-" and "sub-" category a new market created by the lowering of a threshold that was set by a committee with ties to the company that sells the treatment.

Civilizational. The goalpost of "health" has moved from the absence of disease to the optimization of biomarkers — the total cholesterol below 200, the LDL below 100, the A1C below 5.7, the blood pressure below 120/80 — and the optimization creates a permanent patient class, because optimization has no end point, only a moving target that ensures the population is always one number away from needing the next prescription.

10 · Strategic Ambiguity

Language engineered to mean whatever the listener needs it to mean.

Individual. The drug advertisement says "may help reduce symptoms" — a phrase that is legally precise and clinically empty, because "may" means it might not, "help" means it won't do it alone, "reduce" means it won't eliminate, and "symptoms" means it won't address the cause — and the phrase sounds like a promise while committing to nothing.

Institutional. Clinical trial results are reported with strategic ambiguity — "statistically significant improvement" may mean a two-point change on a hundred-point scale, a difference that is mathematically meaningful and clinically irrelevant, and the ambiguity of "significant" allows the result to sound more impressive than it is.

Civilizational. "Evidence-based medicine" has become strategically ambiguous — a phrase that should mean "medicine that is supported by rigorous evidence" and that increasingly means "medicine that has been studied by the companies that sell it and published in journals that are funded by the companies that sell it" — and the ambiguity allows the phrase to confer authority without requiring independence.

11 · Projection

The system’s own behavior, pinned to your chest.

Individual. The industry that produces the dependency projects the cause onto the patient — "addictive personality," "medication non-compliance," "the patient wasn't taking it as prescribed" — the language of patient responsibility deployed to shield the product from the accountability the product's effects demand.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry that funds the research, designs the studies, controls the data, and influences the publication projects the responsibility for the evidence onto the academic institutions that are dependent on the industry's funding — "the science supports our product," said by the entity that funded the science.

Civilizational. The industry that priced insulin at $300 per vial — a product that costs $3 to manufacture, that was discovered a century ago, and that people die without — projects the pricing onto "the cost of innovation," as though the innovation justifies a markup of ten thousand percent on a molecule that has been off-patent for decades.

12 · DARVO

Deny it happened, attack the one who named it, swap victim and offender.

Individual. The patient who reports a side effect is told they are experiencing "nocebo effect" — the suggestion that the side effect is caused by the patient's expectation rather than by the product, and the suggestion reverses the causation so that the patient is the source of the symptom rather than the drug.

Institutional. Scientists who publish findings critical of pharmaceutical products are accused of "cherry-picking data," "having an agenda," or "undermining public trust in medicine" — the researchers who are trying to protect the public are reframed as the threat to the public, and the company whose product caused the harm is reframed as the defender of scientific integrity.

Civilizational. Communities that resist pharmaceutical intervention — that question vaccine safety, that challenge medication mandates, that advocate for informed consent — are labeled "anti-science" and "anti-vaxxer" regardless of the specificity or validity of their concern — and the labeling ensures that any questioning of any pharmaceutical product is conflated with the rejection of all medicine, and the conflation protects the industry from the targeted, specific, legitimate criticism that individual products and practices may deserve.

13 · Normalization

Harm raised in increments too small to trigger alarm, until it’s the background hum.

Individual. The first prescription felt significant — a threshold crossed, a line between the body managing itself and the body requiring chemical assistance — and by the fifth prescription the significance was gone, and the normalization of pharmaceutical dependence is the industry's most important desensitization: a population in which taking daily medication is as unremarkable as brushing teeth is a population that will not question whether the medication is still necessary.

Institutional. Polypharmacy — the practice of prescribing multiple medications simultaneously — has been normalized to the point where the average American over sixty-five takes five or more prescription medications daily, and the interactions between the medications are often unknown because the drugs were not tested in combination, and the normalization means the risk is accepted as routine.

Civilizational. The normalization of pharmaceutical intervention in childhood is the sector's most consequential desensitization — millions of children on stimulants for ADHD, antidepressants for anxiety, antipsychotics for behavioral issues — and the normalization of medicating developing brains with chemicals whose long-term effects are not fully understood has been so complete that the parent who questions the prescription is the parent who must explain their position, not the system that prescribed it.

Stage 5: Isolate

Elimination of comparison

14 · Isolation

Cutting the lines to everyone who could tell you this isn’t normal.

Individual. The patient is isolated from the information they need to make an informed decision — the clinical trial data is proprietary, the side effect incidence is reported in language that obscures its significance, the alternative treatments are not presented by the prescriber because the prescriber was trained in a curriculum that did not include them — and the isolation from information is the system's most effective mechanism, because an informed patient might choose differently, and a different choice would reduce the revenue.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry isolates academic research from independent evaluation — funding studies, controlling the data, publishing favorable results and burying unfavorable ones (publication bias), and providing the peer reviewers who evaluate the studies it funds — and the isolation of the research from independent scrutiny ensures that the evidence base the medical profession relies on is the evidence base the industry produced.

Civilizational. The population is isolated from its own pharmacological heritage — the botanical medicine, the herbal traditions, the indigenous pharmacopoeia that was developed over millennia and that has been systematically marginalized by a regulatory framework that recognizes only the patentable molecule, not the plant it was derived from, and the marginalization is the isolation.

15 · Triangulation

A third party inserted to carry the message the system wanted carried.

Individual. The industry triangulates between the patient and the doctor — the patient sees the commercial and asks the doctor for the product, the doctor who was already being detailed by the representative prescribes it, and the triangulation ensures that the demand appears to originate from the patient rather than from the industry that created the demand through the advertising.

Institutional. The industry triangulates between countries — "if you regulate prices here, we'll invest in research there" — the threat of capital flight used to prevent the price regulation that every other developed nation has implemented and that the United States has not, because the pharmaceutical lobby is the largest in Washington.

Civilizational. The industry triangulates between the population and the disease — "without us, the disease wins" — and the triangulation positions the industry as the essential intermediary between the human and the threat, without which the human is defenseless, and the positioning ensures that the industry's pricing power, its lobbying influence, and its marketing practices cannot be challenged without appearing to side with the disease.

Stage 6: Extract

Harvesting

16 · Flying Monkeys

Recruiting other people to enforce the system while it keeps clean hands.

Individual. Your doctor is the pharmaceutical industry's most trusted flying monkey — a person of genuine authority and genuine care whose prescribing behavior was shaped by an education that was funded by the industry, by continuing education that was provided by the industry, by clinical guidelines that were influenced by the industry, and by a professional culture that was saturated by the industry — and the doctor's sincerity is what makes the influence effective.

Institutional. Patient advocacy organizations function as the industry's flying monkeys — groups that genuinely represent patients' interests and that are funded by the companies that sell the treatments the groups advocate for, and the funding creates an alignment between the patients' desire for treatment and the company's desire for market expansion.

Civilizational. The media functions as the industry's civilizational flying monkey — reporting on drug approvals as breakthroughs, covering pharmaceutical research as science journalism, and presenting the industry's narrative as the public health narrative, because the advertising revenue the industry provides to the media creates a financial relationship that discourages critical coverage.

17 · Smear Campaign

Poisoning your reputation in advance, so your testimony arrives pre-discredited.

Individual. The patient who questions the medication is labeled "non-compliant" — a clinical term that sounds like a diagnosis and that converts the patient's autonomous decision-making into a treatment failure that is attributed to the patient rather than to the product.

Institutional. Scientists whose research produces findings unfavorable to pharmaceutical products are subjected to industry-funded counter-research, professional marginalization, and in some documented cases, legal threats — and the smearing ensures that the scientific conversation stays within the boundaries the industry has defined.

Civilizational. Any movement that questions pharmaceutical practices — the vaccine safety movement, the anti-overmedication movement, the informed consent movement — is smeared as "anti-science" without distinction between the legitimate concerns and the fringe claims, and the smearing protects the entire industry by bundling all criticism into a single category that can be dismissed.

18 · Silent Treatment

Silence weaponized — the refusal to engage, dressed as discipline.

Individual. The pharmaceutical company that was asked about the side effect does not respond — the call center loops, the inquiry is "escalated," the response never arrives, and the patient who experienced the harm is left with the harm and the silence.

Institutional. Requests for clinical trial data — the raw data, not the published summary — are met with proprietary claims, legal challenges, and years of delay, and the stonewalling ensures that the independent analysis that might reveal what the published analysis concealed cannot be conducted in a timeframe that is relevant to the patients currently taking the medication.

Civilizational. The silence around the pharmaceutical industry's influence on medical education, clinical guidelines, regulatory agencies, and public health policy is the sector's most consequential stonewalling — an influence that is documented, quantified, and published in academic literature and that generates no sustained public response because the literature is inaccessible and the industry's public relations is excellent.

19 · Manufactured Consent

A vote whose outcome was decided before the question was asked.

Individual. You "consent" to the medication by accepting the prescription — the consent manufactured by the authority of the prescriber, the urgency of the diagnosis, and the absence of presented alternatives.

Institutional. The FDA approval process manufactures the consent of the medical profession — the drug was reviewed, the drug was approved, therefore the drug is safe and effective — while the review process is funded by the industry (user fees now constitute the majority of the FDA's drug review budget), the reviewers have financial ties to the industry, and the post-market surveillance that would catch long-term effects is inadequate.

Civilizational. The public's consent for the pharmaceutical model of healthcare was manufactured through the systematic marginalization of every alternative — the regulation of supplements into a category with minimal oversight, the defunding of research into non-pharmaceutical interventions, and the cultural narrative that positions the pill as the default response to any form of suffering.

20 · Trauma Bonding

Pain and relief alternating until the source of the wound becomes the only cure.

Individual. The patient is bonded to the medication through the cycle of symptom and relief — the symptom produces the suffering, the pill produces the relief, the relief produces the gratitude, the gratitude produces the dependency, and the dependency produces the bond — and the bond is maintained by the fear of what would happen without the pill, and the fear may be justified and the fear is also the bond.

Institutional. The medical profession is bonded to the pharmaceutical industry through decades of financial interdependence — the funding, the research support, the educational sponsorship, the conference underwriting — and the bond keeps the profession inside a relationship that the profession knows is compromised but cannot exit without losing the resources the relationship provides.

Civilizational. The population is bonded to the pharmaceutical model through the cycle of crisis and product — the disease terrifies, the drug promises, the drug delivers partial relief, the partial relief sustains the faith, and the faith sustains the model even when the model's overall performance does not justify the faith.

21 · Learned Helplessness

Punishing every attempt to act until the body stops trying and calls it peace.

Individual. The patient who has been on medication for years has learned that they cannot manage their own chemistry — "I can't function without my medication," "my brain doesn't work without it," "I've tried to stop and I can't" — and the learned dependence on the chemical is the learned helplessness, and the helplessness was produced by a treatment that replaced the body's function rather than restoring it.

Institutional. The medical profession has learned that it cannot practice without pharmaceutical support — "what else would I prescribe?" "the patient expects a prescription," "there's nothing else I can offer" — and the helplessness is the product of a training that did not include the alternatives.

Civilizational. "There's no cure, only management" is the pharmaceutical industry's civilizational learned helplessness — spoken about chronic conditions that generate recurring revenue, conditions that in some cases could be resolved through the restoration of the signals the body needs but that the system has defined as permanent because permanent conditions require permanent medication and permanent medication is the business model.

22 · Benevolent Control

Restriction, surveillance, and command — all framed as care you’d be ungrateful to refuse.

Individual. The doctor who says "I'm prescribing this for your own good" is exercising clinical judgment that may be entirely correct and that also transfers the sovereignty over the patient's internal chemistry from the patient to the prescriber — and the transfer is the control, dressed in the language of care.

Institutional. The FDA controls what the population is allowed to ingest — a genuine public health function that is also a gatekeeping function that determines which substances are legal and which are not, and the determination is influenced by the industry that profits from the legal substances and that has no financial interest in the substances that cannot be patented.

Civilizational. The pharmaceutical industry positions itself as the guardian of human health — "we are developing the treatments that save lives," "without us, people would suffer and die" — and the positioning makes the industry feel like a benevolent protector rather than a commercial entity, and the feeling prevents the scrutiny that the entity's pricing, lobbying, and marketing practices deserve.

23 · Infantilization

Keeping you perpetually ‘not ready’ so your own discernment is never trusted.

Individual. The patient is treated as incapable of understanding their own medication — "you wouldn't understand the mechanism," "trust the science," "just take it as prescribed" — and the infantilization ensures that the patient does not ask the questions that would reveal the information the industry prefers they not have.

Institutional. The population is excluded from pharmaceutical policy through technical complexity — clinical trial design, biostatistics, pharmacokinetics, regulatory science — fields that are genuinely complex and that the complexity is also the barrier that prevents public participation in decisions about what chemicals the population is encouraged to put in its body.

Civilizational. The human body is infantilized — treated as a system that cannot manage its own health without pharmaceutical intervention, that requires chemical maintenance the way a machine requires mechanical maintenance — and the framing denies the body's capacity for self-regulation, self-repair, and self-medication, capacities that are documented, measurable, and systematically underfunded because they cannot be patented.

24 · Identity Erosion

Replacing who you were with a system-approved version of who you should be.

Individual. The diagnosis becomes the identity — "I am diabetic," "I am bipolar," "I am a chronic pain patient" — and the identity is organized around the condition that requires the medication, which means the identity requires the medication, which means stopping the medication threatens the identity.

Institutional. The doctor's identity has been eroded from healer to prescriber — the physician who was trained to understand the whole person now functions as the node through which the pharmaceutical product reaches the patient, and the prescribing is the primary tool and the primary identity.

Civilizational. The human identity as a self-healing organism has been eroded by a pharmaceutical culture that positions the body as fundamentally insufficient — requiring chemical supplementation to function, chemical correction to feel, chemical maintenance to survive — and the erosion is the industry's most important product: a population that does not believe in its own body.

25 · Spiritual Bypassing

Money and debt repurposed as a leash that makes leaving unaffordable.

Individual. "The medication will take care of it" bypasses the deeper inquiry — what the symptom is communicating, what the body needs, what conditions produced the suffering — and the bypass is efficient and the efficiency is the appeal, because the inquiry takes time and the pill takes thirty seconds and the thirty seconds feels like a solution and the solution prevents the inquiry.

Institutional. "We follow the science" is the pharmaceutical industry's spiritual bypass — a phrase that sounds like rigor and that shields the industry from the question of whether the science it follows was the science it funded, designed, and selectively published.

Civilizational. "Better living through chemistry" is the civilizational spiritual bypass that underwrites the pharmaceutical model — the belief that chemistry can solve what connection, conditions, and community are supposed to provide, and the belief is powerful because the chemistry does provide partial relief, and the partial relief is the bypass that prevents the demand for the complete change.

26 · Financial Control

Spiritual or therapeutic language used to bypass your pain and dodge accountability.

Individual. Drug pricing in the United States is a form of financial control so extreme that it constitutes a survival threat — insulin at $300 per vial for a person who will die without it, cancer drugs at $10,000 per month for a person who has no alternative, and the pricing is set by the manufacturer with no negotiation required because the United States is the only developed nation that does not negotiate drug prices at the national level, a policy maintained by the lobbying of the pharmaceutical industry.

Institutional. The pharmaceutical industry's financial control of the medical profession — through funding, through education, through research, through guidelines — ensures that the profession that prescribes the products is the profession the industry has invested in, and the investment is the control.

Civilizational. The pharmaceutical industry is the most profitable industry in the world — profit margins that exceed those of oil, finance, and technology — and the profitability is maintained by a pricing structure that charges the population the maximum the population can bear for products the population cannot refuse.

Stage 7: Discard

Disposal

27 · Manufactured Crisis

A permanent emergency — too urgent for you to stop, think, and ask if it’s true.

Individual. "If you don't take this medication, your condition will worsen" is the prescriber's manufactured urgency — sometimes accurate, sometimes the installation of fear that overrides the deliberation the decision deserves.

Institutional. "The antibiotic resistance crisis" and "the pandemic preparedness crisis" are manufactured policy crises that justify emergency authorizations, expedited approvals, and reduced regulatory scrutiny — the crises are real and the response to the crises serves the industry's expansion.

Civilizational. Disease awareness campaigns — "do you have these symptoms? you may have [condition]" — manufacture the perception of illness in a healthy population, expanding the market by converting wellness into worry and worry into diagnosis and diagnosis into prescription.

28 · Discard

Released the moment maintaining you costs more than you produce.

Individual. The patient whose insurance changes, whose medication is removed from the formulary, or whose condition no longer responds to the treatment is discharged from the pharmaceutical relationship — the prescription not renewed, the refill not authorized, the patient left to manage the withdrawal and the return of the symptoms without the support the system provided while the system was being paid.

Institutional. Drugs are withdrawn from the market when the liability exceeds the revenue — Vioxx, Bextra, Darvocet — each one a product that was approved, marketed, prescribed, and consumed before the harm exceeded the profit, and the withdrawal is the discard.

Civilizational. Populations that cannot pay are discarded — the developing nations that cannot afford the patented medication, the uninsured Americans who cannot afford the co-pay, the elderly who must choose between food and prescriptions — and the discard is the market functioning as designed, which is to serve the population that can pay and ignore the population that cannot.

Stage 8: Replace

Proof

29 · Replacement

Your seat filled before it cools — proof the warmth was for the position, not for you.

Individual. One medication replaces another — the antidepressant that stopped working replaced by the antidepressant that hasn't been tried yet, the statin replaced by the newer statin, the painkiller replaced by the stronger painkiller — and each replacement requires a new prescription, a new adjustment period, a new set of side effects, and a new revenue cycle.

Institutional. One class of drugs replaces another across decades — the tricyclics replaced by the SSRIs, the SSRIs supplemented by the SNRIs, the SNRIs supplemented by the atypicals — and each replacement is presented as an advance while the outcomes for the population have not meaningfully improved.

Civilizational. One pharmaceutical paradigm replaces another — the era of antibiotics replaced by the era of chronic disease management, the era of blockbuster drugs replaced by the era of biologics and specialty drugs — and each paradigm generates new revenue from new products for new conditions that were previously managed by the body's own systems. And finally plausible deniability.

30 · Plausible Deniability

The harm structured so it can never be proven intentional. ‘It was God’s will.’

Individual.
