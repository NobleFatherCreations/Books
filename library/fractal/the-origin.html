BEFORE THE PATTERN

There was a before.

This is the first and most important claim of this chapter, because the pattern has existed for so long that it has colonized the imagination. The cycle feels permanent. It feels like human nature. It feels like the inevitable product of what humans are — selfish, competitive, hierarchical, extractive. The pattern has been running for so long that questioning the pattern sounds like questioning gravity. "This is just how things are." "This is just how people are." "This has always been the way." It has not always been the way.

There was a time — a long time, a time that lasted hundreds of thousands of years longer than the time that has followed it — when the cycle was not the organizing principle of human life. The evidence is archaeological, anthropological, and indigenous. The evidence is in the bones, in the settlements, in the tools, in the burial sites, in the oral histories of the peoples who remember what the rest of the civilization has forgotten.

For the vast majority of human history — roughly 290,000 of the 300,000 years that anatomically modern humans have existed — humans lived in small, mobile, kin-based groups. These groups were not utopias. They had conflict. They had violence. They had interpersonal manipulation. The narcissistic personality did not begin with agriculture. The capacity for domination, deception, and extraction exists in the human brain and has existed since the brain evolved.

But the capacity for a thing and the civilization built around a thing are different.

A person can be manipulative. That is individual psychology.

A civilization organized around manipulation — where every institution, every structure, every law, every economy, every cultural assumption is built on the logic of the cycle — that is something else entirely. That is not human nature expressing itself. That is human nature captured by a pattern and the pattern scaled to the size of the world.

The question is: when did the scaling begin? When did the cycle go from a thing that happened between people to the thing that organized everything?

The answer is traceable. And the answer begins in the ground.

THE FIRST SURPLUS

Roughly ten thousand years ago, in the Fertile Crescent — modern-day Iraq, Syria, Turkey, and surrounding regions — humans began to do something they had never done before. They planted seeds deliberately. They domesticated animals. They settled.

This is called the agricultural revolution. It is taught in schools as the beginning of civilization. The story is told as progress — the moment the human rose from the primitive and began to build. The story is incomplete.

What agriculture produced — before the city, before the temple, before the army, before the empire — was surplus. More food than could be eaten in the moment. Grain that could be stored. Animals that could be kept. Calories that existed beyond the immediate need.

Surplus seems like abundance. Surplus seems like safety. And in its earliest form, surplus may have been both. The group that could store grain through the winter was less vulnerable to famine. The group that could keep animals had protein on demand. The surplus was a buffer between the human and the scarcity that had shaped the species for three hundred millennia.

But surplus introduced something that had not existed in the mobile, kin-based group. Something the human nervous system was not designed for. Something that would become the seed of the entire fractal.

Surplus introduced the possibility of accumulation beyond need.

In the mobile group, you could not accumulate beyond what you could carry. There was a physical limit to ownership. The body was the container. When the group moved, the possessions moved with the body, and the body could only carry so much. This physical limitation was also a social limitation — extreme accumulation was structurally impossible. And without extreme accumulation, extreme hierarchy was structurally impossible. You could not be dramatically richer than your neighbor because you could not store dramatically more than your neighbor. The body set the limit. The limit set the social structure.

Agriculture removed the limit.

The granary could hold more than the body. The pen could hold more animals than the family needed. The field could produce more food than the household consumed. And the excess — the amount beyond what was needed — could be held by someone. Controlled by someone. Distributed by someone.

The person who controlled the surplus controlled the group's survival.

That is the first power differential at scale. Not the strongest person in the group — strength had always existed. Not the wisest person — wisdom had always existed. But the person who stood between the group and its food supply. The person who controlled access to the thing the group needed to survive.

The first gatekeeper.

The pattern did not arrive in a single moment. It arrived in the structural possibility created by the surplus. The surplus created the gatekeeper. The gatekeeper created the hierarchy. The hierarchy created the institution. And the institution created the cycle.

Not because the gatekeeper was a narcissist. But because the structure — the power differential between the person who controls the survival resource and the people who depend on the survival resource — IS the condition the cycle requires to operate. The cycle does not need a narcissist. It needs a power differential and an unmet need on the other side of it. Agriculture created both.

THE FIRST CAPTURE

What happened next happened gradually. Across centuries. Across dozens of generations. Not as a conspiracy. As an emergence.

The surplus needed to be stored. The storage needed to be protected. The protection required labor. The labor required organization. The organization required hierarchy. The hierarchy required justification.

And the justification reached for the oldest, deepest, most powerful need the human carried: the need for the sacred.

The temple was among the first permanent structures in the earliest agricultural settlements. Not the house. Not the market. The temple. The archaeological evidence from Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey — dated to approximately 9,500 BCE, predating agriculture in the region — suggests that the impulse toward the sacred may have preceded and even motivated the transition to settlement. The human did not settle and then build a temple. The human may have built a temple and then settled around it.

This matters because it reveals the sequence: the sacred came first. The need for the sacred was not produced by civilization. The need for the sacred was already there — ancient, pre-verbal, wired into the species. The temples of Göbekli Tepe were built by hunter-gatherers who had not yet discovered agriculture. The sacred was the original impulse. The purest frequency.

And the sacred was the first need to be captured.

The temple that began as the expression of the sacred became the institution of the sacred. The open-air ceremony became the enclosed ritual. The communal experience became the mediated experience. The direct relationship between the human and the sacred became the indirect relationship — mediated by the priest, the temple, the ritual, the hierarchy that formed around the surplus and claimed the sacred as its justification.

"The grain is stored in the temple because the gods require it."

That sentence — or its ancient equivalent — is the first institutional capture of a human need. The surplus is in the temple. The priest guards the temple. The priest speaks for the gods. The gods require the grain. Therefore: obeying the priest is obeying the gods. And obeying the gods is the condition for receiving the grain.

The sacred was the gateway. The first need captured. The need that, once captured, provided the justification for the capture of every subsequent need.

Because once the sacred was institutionalized — once the relationship between the human and the divine was mediated by a structure that also controlled the surplus — the template existed. The proof of concept was complete. A human need could be captured by an institution that positioned itself between the human and the need, and the institution could extract compliance, labor, resources, and obedience in exchange for access to the thing the human already carried. The cycle did not need to be invented after that. It only needed to be replicated. And replication is what patterns do.

THE FIRST TECHNOLOGIES OF CONTROL

The pattern needed infrastructure to scale beyond the village. Four technologies provided it.

WRITING

Circa 3,400 BCE. Mesopotamia. The first written records are not poems. They are not prayers. They are not stories.

They are inventories.

Lists of grain. Lists of cattle. Lists of laborers. Lists of debts. The first use of the written word was accounting — the recording of who owns what, who owes what, and who controls what. Writing was the technology that allowed the surplus to be tracked beyond the memory of any individual. The ledger replaced the oral agreement. The record replaced the relationship.

This is not an incidental detail. This is the origin of plausible deniability at civilizational scale. Before writing, the agreement was relational — two people, face to face, in a community where the agreement was witnessed and remembered by the community. The agreement could not be abstracted. It could not be separated from the bodies that made it.

abstracted. It could not be separated from the bodies that made it.

Writing separated the agreement from the body. The contract could now exist independently of the people who made it. The terms could be set by one party and enforced on another across time and distance. The person who controlled the record controlled the agreement. And the record could say whatever the record-keeper needed it to say.

The first technology of control was not the weapon. It was the ledger. The ability to record the extraction — to make it official, to make it permanent, to make it enforceable beyond the relationship — was the infrastructure the cycle needed to survive the death of any individual gatekeeper. The gatekeeper could die. The record survived. The institution survived. The cycle survived.

Writing gave the cycle immortality.

CURRENCY

Circa 3,000 BCE. The abstraction of value.

Before currency, exchange was relational. You gave me grain. I gave you labor. The exchange was specific, contextual, embedded in the relationship between two people and two needs. The exchange could not be accumulated beyond the need — you could not store a thousand personal favors. You could not hoard ten years of grain-for-labor exchanges.

Currency changed the physics of extraction.

The coin — the shell, the bead, the metal disc, the abstract token — allowed value to be separated from the thing it represented. The grain could now be converted into a token. The labor could be converted into a token. The token could be accumulated without limit. Stored without decay. Transported without weight. Concentrated without the physical constraints that the surplus itself imposed.

Currency is the technology that gave the cycle its most powerful technique: financial control. Before currency, financial control was limited to the physical control of the surplus — the granary, the herd, the storehouse. After currency, financial control was unlimited. The token could represent anything. The debt could grow without boundary. The extraction could be abstracted from the body entirely — you no longer had to physically take the grain. You could take the number.

The abstraction of value into currency is the moment the cycle gained access to infinite extraction. Because the physical world has limits — the granary fills, the field depletes, the body tires. But the number has no limit. The debt can grow forever. The interest can compound forever. The extraction can continue long after the physical body has nothing left to give — because the extraction is no longer physical. It is numerical. And numbers do not stop.

Currency gave the cycle infinity.

CODIFIED LAW

Circa 1,754 BCE. The Code of Hammurabi. Babylon.

The first comprehensive legal code did not emerge from the people's desire for justice. It emerged from the ruler's need to standardize the extraction. The code established property rights — which formalized the surplus-holder's claim. It established debt law — which formalized the extraction of labor from the debtor. It established penalties for theft — which criminalized the redistribution of the surplus. It established class distinctions — which formalized the hierarchy the surplus had created. The law did not create justice. The law created the institutional framework for the cycle's operation. The law gave the cycle legitimacy. Before codified law, the gatekeeper's control of the surplus was enforced through personal authority and communal pressure. After codified law, the gatekeeper's control was enforced through the state. The violence was no longer personal. It was systemic. The punishment for challenging the surplus-holder was no longer the surplus-holder's anger. It was the state's machinery.

Codified law is the technology that gave the cycle its DARVO capability at civilizational scale. The person who challenges the extraction is now the criminal. The person who hoards the extraction is now the protected. The law reversed the moral order — the taker became the owner and the person who objects became the thief. The reversal was codified. Written in stone. Enforced by the sword. The law gave the cycle legitimacy.

ORGANIZED MILITARY

Circa 2,500 BCE. The first standing armies. Sumer, Egypt, Akkad.

The surplus created the possibility of feeding people who did not grow food. For the first time in human history, a significant portion of the population could be dedicated to a task other than survival — and the first task the surplus funded was violence.

The standing army is the technology that gave the cycle its enforcement mechanism. Before the army, the cycle could only operate within the range of the gatekeeper's personal influence — the village, the settlement, the city. After the army, the cycle could operate across geography. The surplus could be extracted from the neighboring city. The neighboring region. The neighboring continent.

The army is the projection technique at civilizational scale. The civilization that produces the extraction accuses the other civilization of the threat that justifies the extraction. "They are dangerous. They are barbaric. They threaten our way of life." The army is deployed. The extraction follows. And the extracted population is told that the extraction is protection.

The military gave the cycle range.

THE SCALING

With these four technologies — writing, currency, law, and military — the cycle had everything it needed to scale from the village to the empire.

The first empires — Sumer, Akkad, Egypt, Babylon, Assyria — were the first civilizational-scale expressions of the cycle. Each one followed the same eight stages: Idealize the territory. The land is rich. The people are in need of civilization. The gods have ordained the expansion.

Hook the population through dependency. Control the grain supply. Control the water. Control the trade routes. Make the survival of the population dependent on the continuation of the empire. Devalue the conquered. They are not fully human. Their gods are false. Their customs are primitive. Their bodies are labor. Their land is resource.

Confuse the resistance. Impose the language. Impose the religion. Impose the history. Replace the conquered population's story with the empire's story until the conquered cannot remember their own.

Isolate the communities. Divide the conquered along tribal, ethnic, linguistic, or religious lines. Prevent the formation of a unified resistance. Triangulate. The conquered fight each other instead of the empire.

Extract everything. The labor. The resources. The tribute. The taxes. The grain. The gold. The bodies for the army. The children for the priesthood. The land for the expansion.

Discard the depleted. When the region is exhausted — the mines empty, the soil dead, the population reduced — move on. Withdraw the army. Withdraw the administration. Leave the ruins. The abandoned city is the empire's discard.

Replace with the next territory. The cycle does not mourn. It scouts. The next frontier. The next conquest. The next supply source. The empire grows not because it wants to but because it must — the cycle requires fresh supply the way fire requires fresh fuel. The extraction depletes the source. The depletion requires replacement. The replacement requires expansion. The expansion IS the cycle at civilizational scale.

This is not ancient history. This is the template.

Every empire that followed — Persian, Greek, Roman, Ottoman, Mongol, Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch, British, French, American — followed the same eight stages. The technology changed. The geography changed. The vocabulary changed. The cycle did not.

THE PATTERN BECOMES INVISIBLE

Somewhere in the scaling — sometime between the first temple and the present moment — the pattern achieved its most important victory.

It became invisible.

Not hidden. Invisible. There is a difference. Hidden implies something that is deliberately concealed — something that someone is keeping from you. Invisible implies something that is so pervasive, so total, so foundational that it cannot be seen. Not because you are not looking. Because it is the thing you are looking with.

The fish cannot see the water.

The pattern became the water.

This happened through a specific mechanism — the same mechanism documented as technique number 13 in every sector of this book: normalization.

The normalization occurred across three dimensions:

THE NORMALIZATION OF HIERARCHY

The pattern required hierarchy to operate — the power differential between the controller and the controlled. In the original kin-based group, hierarchy was fluid, contextual, and limited by the body's carrying capacity. After agriculture, hierarchy became fixed, structural, and unlimited. But the hierarchy needed to feel natural. So the pattern normalized it.

Through religion: "God placed the king on the throne. The hierarchy is divine. To challenge the hierarchy is to challenge God." The sacred need — the first captured need — was deployed to justify the structure the capture had created. The priest who controlled the temple endorsed the king who controlled the army who protected the priest who controlled the temple. The circle was selfreinforcing. And the reinforcement was framed as cosmology rather than politics. Through philosophy: "Some men are born to rule and some are born to serve." Aristotle wrote this. The philosopher who is credited with the foundations of Western thought explicitly argued that hierarchy is natural — that the master-slave relationship is the organic order of the world. The normalization was intellectual. Rationalized. Given the authority of reason itself.

Through economics: "The market is the natural expression of human exchange. Competition is natural. The cream rises to the top." The normalization of economic hierarchy — the idea that the rich are rich because they are better and the poor are poor because they are less — was installed so deeply that it persists today as a cultural assumption that most people do not even recognize as an assumption. It feels like observation. It is normalization.

Through biology: "Survival of the fittest." Darwin's evolutionary principle — which describes adaptation to environment — was co-opted by social Darwinism to argue that human hierarchy is the natural product of biological competition. The strong dominate the weak. The domination is nature's design. To challenge the hierarchy is to challenge nature itself.

Each normalization built on the previous one. Divine right normalized the political hierarchy. Philosophy normalized the intellectual justification. Economics normalized the material hierarchy. Biology normalized the biological justification. By the time all four normalizations were installed, the hierarchy was so thoroughly justified — from every possible angle — that it was no longer perceived as a structure that was built. It was perceived as a feature of reality itself.

The pattern did not hide the hierarchy. The pattern made the hierarchy feel like gravity.

THE NORMALIZATION OF EXTRACTION

The pattern required extraction to sustain itself. The extraction needed to feel inevitable. So the pattern normalized it.

Through labor: "Work is virtuous. Idleness is sin. You earn your keep. Nothing is free." The extraction of labor — the conversion of the human body's energy into the system's product — was reframed as the human's moral obligation. The person who works is good. The person who rests is suspect. The extraction is not extraction. It is virtue.

Through taxation: "You owe the state a portion of what you earn." The extraction of wealth was formalized, legalized, and moralized. The state provides services — roads, defense, governance — and the provision justifies the extraction. The tax is not a technique. It is a social contract. Except that the contract was never negotiated by the people who pay it.

Through debt: "You must borrow to build." The extraction of future labor — the mortgage, the student loan, the credit card — was normalized as the path to prosperity. The debt is not a leash. It is an opportunity. The extraction of thirty years of future labor for a house is not financial control. It is the American Dream.

Through consumption: "You deserve this. You worked for this. Treat yourself." The extraction of wealth through consumption was normalized as reward. The system that extracts your labor six days a week encourages you to return the wage on the seventh. The extraction is a circle — labor in, wage out, consumption back in. The circle is so normalized that opting out of the consumption feels like deprivation rather than liberation.

Each normalization built on the previous one. By the time the labor was moralized, the taxation formalized, the debt normalized, and the consumption ritualized, the extraction was invisible. It was not perceived as extraction. It was perceived as life. "This is just how things work." That sentence is the normalization's signature. The cycle's flag planted in the mind of the population. Claiming the territory of the imagination. Making the alternative unimaginable.

The final and most complete normalization was the normalization of the cycle's own pattern — the eight stages perceived not as a cycle but as the natural rhythm of human experience.

The idealization at the beginning of relationships, institutions, and eras — perceived not as a technique but as genuine hope. "Things always start well."

The devaluation that follows — perceived not as a technique but as reality. "The honeymoon phase doesn't last."

The extraction that follows — perceived not as a technique but as the cost of living. "Nothing is free."

The discard that follows — perceived not as a technique but as the natural end. "Everything has its season."

The replacement that follows — perceived not as a technique but as progress. "Out with the old, in with the new."

The eight stages — documented, named, identified as the narcissistic abuse cycle in the clinical literature — are so normalized at the civilizational level that they are perceived as the natural rhythm of life. The cycle feels like the seasons. Feels like the inevitable arc of all things. Feels like maturity. Feels like realism. Feels like the way the world works.

It is not the way the world works.

It is the way the pattern works. And the pattern has run so long, and scaled so completely, and normalized so thoroughly, that the difference between the pattern and the world has disappeared from view.

Until now.

THE ACCELERATION

The pattern scaled slowly for eight thousand years. Then it accelerated.

Colonialism was the cycle exported. The European empires — Spain, Portugal, Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium — ran the eight stages on entire continents simultaneously. The idealization of the "new world." The hook of trade and treaty. The devaluation of the indigenous — "savages," "heathens," "primitives." The confusion of imposed language, imposed religion, imposed history. The isolation of communities through borders that cut across kinship lines. The extraction of land, labor, resources, and bodies. The discard of depleted regions. The replacement of indigenous populations with settler populations.

The colonial period is the cycle at continental scale. And the colonial period is not history. The borders drawn by the colonizers are still the borders. The languages imposed by the colonizers are still the official languages. The religions installed by the colonizers are still the dominant faiths. The economic structures built by the colonizers — designed to extract from the colony and deliver to the empire — are still the economic structures. The International Monetary Fund and the World Bank operate on the same logic as the colonial trading company: idealize the developing nation, hook it with loans, devalue its sovereignty through structural adjustment, confuse through economic jargon, isolate it from regional solidarity, extract the resources, discard the depleted economy, and move to the next emerging market.

Colonialism did not end. Colonialism changed its vocabulary.

THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION (1760–1840)

The industrial revolution did not invent the extraction. It mechanized it.

Before industry, the extraction was limited by the capacity of the human body and the animal body. The mine could only go as deep as the human could dig. The field could only produce as much as the human could harvest. The extraction was limited by biology.

The machine removed the biological limit. The extraction could now operate at a pace and scale that the body alone could never achieve. The factory could run twenty-four hours. The mine could go deeper. The field could be harvested faster. The body was no longer the unit of extraction. The machine was.

But the body was still needed. Not to perform the extraction — the machine performed the extraction. The body was needed to operate the machine. The human was reduced from the source of labor to the operator of the thing that replaced the source of labor. The devaluation was literal — the human body went from being irreplaceable (the only thing that could do the work) to being interchangeable (one of many things that could operate the machine).

The industrial revolution is the moment the cycle learned to extract faster than the body could replenish. Before industry, the extraction was limited by the body's recovery time — the farmer could not harvest faster than the field could regrow. After industry, the extraction had no recovery constraint. The machine could strip the resource faster than the resource could regenerate. The factory could consume the worker faster than the worker could rest.

The acceleration was exponential. And the exponential extraction is the reason the ecological crisis documented in the epilogue exists — the earth's body is being extracted from at a rate that exceeds the earth's capacity to heal. The industrial revolution is the moment the cycle outpaced the planet.

THE INFORMATION AGE (1950–present)

The information age did not invent the confusion. It industrialized it.

Before mass media, the cycle's confusion stage was limited by the reach of the human voice and the printed page. Gaslighting required proximity — the person who gaslit you had to be in the room. Propaganda required distribution — the pamphlet had to be carried by hand.

Radio, then television, then the internet, then social media, then the algorithm removed every constraint on the confusion stage. The gaslighting could now reach every home simultaneously. The normalization could operate at the speed of broadcast. The manufactured consent could be produced in real time, adjusted in real time, and targeted to the individual in real time. The algorithm is the most significant acceleration in the history of the cycle. The algorithm does what no human narcissist can do — it learns what works on you specifically and delivers it without rest, without error, without fatigue, without conscience. The algorithm runs intermittent reinforcement on billions of nervous systems simultaneously. The algorithm isolates each user inside a personalized reality. The algorithm extracts attention, data, and behavioral prediction and sells it to the highest bidder. The algorithm is the cycle automated. The cycle no longer needs a human operator. The code runs it. At scale. At speed. Without sleep.

The information age is the moment the cycle became self-operating. Before the algorithm, the cycle required a human in each position — a priest in the temple, a teacher in the school, a doctor in the office, a boss in the corner office. After the algorithm, the cycle can operate without a human in any position. The code is the priest. The code is the teacher. The code is the doctor. The code is the boss. The code is every technique in every stage running simultaneously on every device in every pocket in every country on earth.

The acceleration is no longer exponential. It is autonomous. The cycle is running itself.

It has no center. There is no person running the pattern. No cabal. No conspiracy. No committee. This is what makes the pattern so difficult to confront — there is no one to confront. THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION

There is a difference between saying "people are corrupt" and saying "the pattern corrupts structures."

If people are the problem, the solution is better people. Replace the corrupt leader with a virtuous one. Elect the honest politician. Hire the compassionate doctor. Appoint the faithful priest. The solution is personnel.

If the pattern is the problem, the solution is not better people inside the same structures. The solution is structures that the pattern cannot enter. Structures that do not create the conditions the pattern requires — the power differential, the gatekeeping, the mediation between the human and the need.

This is why reform fails. Not because the reformers are insincere. Because the reform replaces the person inside the pattern without replacing the pattern inside the institution. The new leader enters the old structure and the old structure runs the new leader through the same cycle. The compassionate doctor enters the billing system and the billing system extracts through the compassionate doctor's hands. The faithful priest enters the hierarchy and the hierarchy love bombs through the faithful priest's warmth.

The pattern does not need bad people. It needs structures. And the structures are intact. This is also why individual healing — while necessary — is insufficient. The person who heals from the narcissistic relationship and then enters the healthcare system encounters the same cycle in a different building. The person who breaks the generational chain and then sends their child to school encounters the same cycle in a different institution. The healing of the individual without the restructuring of the institution produces a healed person inside an unchanged machine. The person is better. The machine is the same. And the machine will run its cycle on the better person with the same efficiency it ran on the wounded one.

Both are necessary. The internal work and the structural work. The mirror and the building. The frequency and the architecture.

Neither alone is sufficient.

THE QUESTION OF INTENT

This chapter has described the pattern as if it has purpose — it feeds, it defends itself, it replicates, it adapts. But does it have intent?

The honest answer is: it does not need intent to operate.

A virus does not intend to infect. It infects because the conditions are met — the receptor exists, the entry mechanism works, the host's defenses are insufficient. The virus has no mind. The virus has structure. And the structure does what structures do.

The pattern is similar. The cycle does not intend to extract. It extracts because the conditions are met — the power differential exists, the need exists, the mediation exists, the normalization is in place. The pattern has no mind. The pattern has structure. And the structure does what structures do.

This means that the question "who is doing this?" — while natural, while the first question every survivor asks — is the wrong question at the civilizational level. At the relational level, the question has an answer — your ex was doing it, your parent was doing it, the specific human with the specific personality disorder was doing it. At the institutional level, the question begins to dissolve — the doctor is not doing it, the billing system is doing it through the doctor. At the civilizational level, the question is fully dissolved — no one is doing it. The structure is doing it. The pattern is doing it. The pattern that has been replicating for ten thousand years, through every available structure, adapting to every era, defending itself against every attempt at recognition, feeding on every body that carries a need.

This does not absolve individuals who consciously exploit the pattern. The pharmaceutical executive who sets the price of insulin at 300 dollars knows what they are doing. The politician who manufactures the crisis knows what they are doing. The abusive partner who gaslights knows what they are doing. Individual conscious exploitation exists — it is Level 4 on the intent spectrum documented in Chapter 31.

But the pattern's existence does not depend on those individuals. Remove every conscious exploiter from every institution tomorrow and the pattern continues. Because the pattern is in the structure. Not in the person. The person is the vehicle. The structure is the road. And the road was paved ten thousand years ago.
