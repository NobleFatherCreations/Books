2.1 · Hook

Future Faking

Promises of a future that secure present compliance with no guarantee of delivery.

Also called: Paper Deferral

Individual

Your manager keeps saying "next album, you'll headline, I promise" — and every cycle the promise moves while you keep signing the extensions that pay him now.

Institutional

The deal memo talks about tour support, marketing pushes and wide releases "once we see traction" — a future that lives in the pitch and never appears in the binding pages.

Civilizational

"Pay your dues now and the break comes later" is a promise with no date attached — and it secures years of present compliance.

2.2 · Hook

Identity Hooking

Binding your self-worth to their system so leaving feels like losing yourself.

Also called: Identity Installation; Social Identity Threat

Individual

Your producer tells you "without my sound, you're just another singer" — tying who you are as an artist to staying in his studio.

Institutional

The label's brand deck defines your persona, your look and your backstory — so leaving the deal feels like losing the version of yourself the public knows.

Civilizational

The belief that an artist is their career, with no self outside the stage, makes leaving a bad deal feel like ceasing to exist.

2.3 · Hook

Premature Exclusivity

Pushing for commitment before evaluation is possible.

Individual

Days after you meet, a manager asks you to stop talking to anyone else and sign an exclusive agreement "so I can fight for you properly" — commitment before you can compare.

Institutional

The development program's enrollment packet requires exclusive representation before the artist has seen what the program actually delivers.

Civilizational

"If you shop it around, you'll lose them" teaches artists that loyalty means committing before they can evaluate anything.

"The offer's gone if this isn't signed by tonight — you don't need a lawyer for this, it's standard" — said at the after-party, a pen in your hand and the label head waiting.

Institutional

Civilizational

2.6 · Hook

Manufactured Scarcity

"This is your only chance" framing to trigger fear of loss.

Also called: FOMO Lock; Manufactured Oversubscription

Institutional

Showcase organisers advertise "limited slots" and "final spots for industry night" — scarcity that sells the fee as a narrow way in.

Civilizational

The belief that each person gets a single shot turns every offer into the last one — and people rarely negotiate the last one.

2.31 · Hook

Door-in-the-Face

A large request you are expected to refuse comes first, so the smaller request that follows feels like a fair compromise.

Individual

The tour manager asks you to play every night for two months with no days off; when you refuse, he asks for "just" three back-to-back weeks — and that now feels like a fair compromise.

Institutional

The label opens with a draft asking for everything, every right and every territory, forever — so the slightly smaller version that follows looks generous by comparison.

Civilizational

The industry habit of starting every ask at the extreme trains artists to feel lucky when it shrinks — the smaller demand never gets judged on its own terms.

Seen in these patterns The Big Ask

2.32 · Hook

Anchoring

The first number, price or standard you hear becomes the reference point, so everything after it is judged against it.

Individual

The first thing the producer says is "most people pay double for a session with me" — and every rate after that sounds like a discount, whatever it really is.

Institutional

The publisher sends its "standard" split before any talk begins — the first figure in the room becomes the reference point, and every counteroffer is judged by how far it strays from it.

Civilizational

The business tells and retells the worst deals as normal — so the first terms any new artist hears set what "fair" feels like for the rest of their career.

Seen in these patterns The Normalization Engine Perspective Poisoning The Big Ask

2.33 · Hook

Bait and Switch

An attractive offer gets your commitment, and what you actually receive is worse, changed or missing once leaving has become costly.

Also called: Lowball Technique; Terms Modification

Individual

The manager promised the support slot, the studio time and the producer you wanted — but once you have signed and left your old team, the slot goes to someone else and the studio is his friend's basement.

Institutional

The label sells a "partnership" with marketing and creative control, then after signing the budget shrinks and the approvals multiply — the offer that won you is gone just when leaving has become costly.

Civilizational

The industry treats the pitch and the deal as two different things — so finding the promise changed after you committed is shrugged off as how everyone learns.

Seen in these patterns The Platform Lock-In

2.36 · Hook

Agent of Influence Operations

A credible voice promotes another party's interests without disclosing the connection, so you take their view as independent.

Individual

A veteran artist you admire tells you which manager to sign with as friendly advice — and you only learn much later that he takes a cut of everyone he sends there.

Institutional

Industry panels and "independent" reviewers recommend a distributor's services without saying they are paid by it — so you take their view as neutral expertise.

Civilizational

The business runs on word of mouth that nobody discloses — so a trusted voice with a hidden stake passes as honest guidance, and no one thinks to ask who benefits.

2.38 · Hook

Mystical Manipulation

Experiences presented to you as spontaneous, divine or fated were in fact arranged in advance.

Individual

Institutional

Civilizational

The lore of the chance encounter that changes everything makes artists read arranged moments as signs — so a scripted break feels like proof you were meant to sign.

2.41 · Hook

Parasocial Manipulation Engineering

A one-way sense of closeness with someone you follow is cultivated and then used to sell to you or move you.

Individual

A star you have followed for years answers your message, calls you "fam" in a voice note — and a week later asks you to buy into his course and push it to your friends.

Institutional

The label's fan team scripts "personal" posts, late-night lives and replies that feel one-to-one — closeness built at scale and then aimed at ticket drops, bundles and merch.

Civilizational

The business treats the feeling that you know a performer as fair game — so a one-way bond is mined for money and no one calls it anything but fandom.
