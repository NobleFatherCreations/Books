The scene

You're a songwriter, twenty-seven, and the song is yours. You know it is yours the way you know the layout of your own bedroom: you wrote it there, on a borrowed keyboard, at two in the morning, with the duvet around your shoulders and your phone propped on a stack of paperbacks recording a voice memo you still have. Two verses, a chorus that arrived whole. You sent the demo to a publisher who liked it, and the publisher played it to a label, and the label played it to a producer who "tightened it up," and now it is on the radio. You heard it in a supermarket last Thursday and had to stand very still by the oranges.

You are not stupid about this business. You signed a split sheet in the session room, the day of the producer's tightening. It had your name on it. You remember thinking, I should check the percentages, and then somebody brought coffee.

A friend sends you a screenshot. It is the registration record for the song, from the database that performing rights organizations use. You open it on your phone, sitting on the edge of the bed. There are five names under writers. Yours is fourth. Under it, a name you have never heard, and a name you have only seen in a producer's credits. The shares are in a column on the right, and the one next to your name is smaller than the one next to the producer's.

You take a screenshot of the screenshot, because your hands need something to do.

You write to your publisher. You are polite. You ask who the other writers are, when they were added, and why. A reply comes in two days from a junior administrator. It is warm. It says that registrations are handled by the label's metadata team, and that the publisher simply submits what it is given. It adds, kindly, that there may be a mapping issue.

You write to the label's metadata team. You get an automatic reply and a ticket number. After eight days, a second reply: the label sends data to the performing rights organization based on the split information supplied by the publisher and the producer's representatives. If there is an error, it would be corrected at the source.

You write to the rights organization. They answer within the week. They are very helpful, and they say that they register works as submitted by the publisher or the writers and cannot alter shares without written agreement from all parties. You ask who submitted the five-writer version. They say they are unable to share that information.

You write to the publisher again. A different person answers. She is new, and she has no record of the earlier conversation. She asks you to resubmit your question through the writer portal. You do. You get a ticket number.

Over the next seven months, you build a folder on your laptop called Credit. It has a hundred and twelve emails in it. In March, you are invited to a "stakeholder alignment call" with the publisher, the label and the producer's management. Everyone is lovely. A shared document is circulated with a column headed "Proposed Resolution." You are asked to comment by Friday. Your comments are acknowledged. Nothing in the document changes.

In June, you get a letter from the publisher's legal department. It thanks you for your engagement. It confirms that "the matter has been reviewed with all relevant parties on multiple occasions," lists the dates of the emails, the call and the portal tickets, and states that the publisher has "acted responsively throughout." It reminds you, in the final paragraph, that discussions about the matter are confidential.

You count the tickets. Eleven. You read the letter at your desk, in the bedroom with the keyboard. The song is on the radio in the other room.

The thing that is hardest to say aloud is that everyone you have spoken to was kind, and every one of them did their job, and the job, in every case, was to send you to someone else.

That's The Accountability Black Hole.

What it is

The Accountability Black Hole is the sequence where a question about credit, ownership or money enters an organization or a network of organizations and never exits with an answer, using Diffusion of Responsibility, Plausible Deniability, Process Exhaustion, HR Shielding, Confidentiality as Weapon, Pseudo-Consultation and Paper-Trail Traps to convert a writer's trust that someone, somewhere, owns the problem into the loss of a clear point of responsibility to direct the concern toward. It may benefit any party whose interest is served by the record staying as it is, because each participant performs a function without producing a result. It exploits the sector's real fragmentation across publishers, labels, distributors, rights organizations and producers' representatives, and it is hard to detect because every individual response is courteous and procedurally correct. The writer may lose years of effort, the credit and share of a song, income attached to it, and the belief that a clear question has a clear owner. Whether this reflects design, drift or the genuine complexity of fragmented rights would require additional evidence.

The tactics, in order

The component tactics, in order:

  1. Diffusion of Responsibility — The publisher says the label's metadata team handles registrations; the label says it sends what the publisher and the producer's representatives supply; the rights organization says it registers what it is given. This functions by distributing a single decision across so many parties that none can be asked to own it. Concern rises when every party can describe the others' roles accurately and none can say who submitted the five-writer version.
  1. Plausible Deniability — "There may be a mapping issue." "If there is an error, it would be corrected at the source." This functions by offering an innocent explanation that cannot be tested, so that no one has to say whether the credits were changed deliberately or by accident. Concern rises when an innocent explanation is offered repeatedly and no one examines whether it fits the facts.
  1. Process Exhaustion — Tickets, portals, resubmissions, new contacts who have no record of the old ones, a folder of a hundred and twelve emails. This functions by turning a question into a long administrative effort, so that the cost of pursuing it rises faster than any expected result. Concern rises when the same question has to be restarted with a new person or a new channel more than once.
  1. HR Shielding — The letter from the publisher's legal department describes the matter as "reviewed with all relevant parties on multiple occasions," placing the organization's procedural record between the writer and any person. This functions by moving the dispute out of the hands of people who could resolve it and into the hands of people whose role is to protect the organization. Concern rises when the response to a credit question changes author from a person who knows the song to a function that manages risk. Awaiting expert review
  1. Confidentiality as Weapon — The rights organization cannot say who submitted the registration; the closing paragraph of the letter reminds the writer that the matter is confidential. This functions by turning privacy, which may be legitimate, into a reason the writer cannot find out what happened or talk to anyone who might know. Concern rises when confidentiality is cited to prevent the writer from learning facts about their own work, or from seeking advice.
  1. Pseudo-Consultation — The "stakeholder alignment call" and the shared document with a "Proposed Resolution" column, in which the writer's comments are acknowledged and nothing changes. This functions by creating the appearance of participation without any commitment to change the outcome. Concern rises when the writer is invited to comment but cannot point to a single change that resulted.
  1. Paper-Trail Traps — The June letter that lists the emails, the call and the eleven tickets as proof of responsiveness. This functions by repurposing the writer's own persistence as evidence that the organization acted properly, so the complaint becomes the documentation of its own handling. Concern rises when a record of the writer's effort is presented as a record of the organization's diligence.

Why this order

Diffusion has to come first, because the other tactics need there to be nowhere for the question to land. In a fragmented rights chain, this is not a manufactured condition. It is the starting condition. The question "who did this?" arrives in a system with several plausible answers and no obligation to choose one, and that is what makes the pattern so difficult to distinguish from ordinary industry plumbing.

Plausible Deniability follows because it gives each hand an innocent reading of its own role. A "mapping issue" cannot be wrong, because it does not claim anything. Process Exhaustion then converts that innocence into time. Each redirect is individually reasonable, and the sum is a year.

HR Shielding is the point at which the writer's question stops being a creative or financial matter and becomes a risk to be managed. The change of author is subtle: the first replies came from people who knew the catalog, and the last comes from a legal department that knows the file. Confidentiality as Weapon seals that move. It is difficult to object to privacy, which is why it works.

Pseudo-Consultation supplies legitimacy. A call with all the parties, a shared document and a deadline for comments are what a fair process looks like from the outside. They are also what an exhausted writer may accept as an ending. The final layer, Paper-Trail Traps, is where the pattern becomes self-sustaining: every email, ticket and call the writer generated to get an answer is now evidence that the organization answered.

The conversion point is the letter. It turns a seven-month effort into a record of responsiveness, and it turns the writer's persistence into a reason for closing the matter. What weakens the sequence: asking the single question that forces a name, "who submitted this registration, and on what date?"; collecting the split sheet and the demo timestamp early, before the process starts; separating the factual question about the record from the commercial question about the share; and finding out whether the registration can be challenged by the writers directly, which depends on the rules of the relevant body. Varies by place

One pattern, three altitudes

Individual

The writer may become a person with a very good filing system and a very small sense of progress. Over time, they may find that they remember the ticket numbers better than the melody.

They may begin to rehearse the same explanation to each new contact, and to hear in their own voice a flatness that was not there at the start. They may second-guess the original session, wondering whether they really did sign what they remember signing. They may feel shame at having been the person who did not check the percentages, though almost nobody does.

They may also feel unable to enjoy the song, or to stop listening for it. The person may withdraw from other collaborations out of caution, or begin to view every future split sheet as a threat, which can be an entirely reasonable response and also a real cost to the work.

Institutional

Rights in a single song may be split across writers, producers, publishers, administrators, labels and a performing rights organization, with registration data passed between them and corrected only at the source Common pattern. Metadata errors are common and often simple mistakes, and a corrected record can take time to propagate Common pattern.

Ticket-based support systems can confirm that a request was logged without confirming that a decision was made Composite. Where no one role is accountable for resolving a conflict between a writer's split sheet and a registered record, the conflict can circulate indefinitely Composite. Confidentiality terms in agreements and in organizational policy may restrict what a body can share about who submitted data Awaiting expert review.

The honest reading in most cases is that the rights chain is genuinely fragmented, that registrations often are wrong for innocent reasons, and that every person who replied may have been sincerely trying to help. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the five-writer record was submitted with a split sheet that differs from the one the writer signed, whether any party ever offered the submission date, and whether similar disputes have ended in the same loop.

Civilizational

The myth is that the business side is boring, technical and best left to the people who understand it, and that a creative person who looks too closely at it is being petty. Around it sits the belief that a hit song belongs to everyone who touched it, and that generosity about credit is the mark of a professional.

There is a second, older story: that a writer should be grateful that anyone recorded the song at all. In a sector where thousands of demos are sent for every one that is released, the writer who asks about shares can feel like someone who has been given a gift and is counting the wrapping.

The value being exploited is trust that a system with so many competent people in it must have someone responsible somewhere. Nothing here is an argument against the many careful administrators who keep metadata correct.

The public story is a song that went out into the world and is loved. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a writer who is not sure whether the story is partly theirs.

What you might notice

  1. A registration record lists writers or shares that differ from the signed split sheet.
  2. Each party identifies a different party as the source of the registration.
  3. No party can say who submitted the version in question, or when.
  4. Replies explain what might have gone wrong in general terms without checking this record.
  5. The writer is asked to resubmit the same question through a new portal or channel.
  6. New contacts have no record of earlier conversations.
  7. Responses change author from catalog or creative staff to legal or compliance staff.
  8. Confidentiality is cited as a reason information about the writer's own work cannot be shared.
  9. A group call or shared document invites comments, but the record does not change.
  10. A closing letter lists the writer's own communications as proof of the organization's responsiveness.
  11. Corrections are promised "at the source" with no date and no named person.

Spot it

SPOT IT — The Name Test

Ask: "Can anyone tell me who submitted this registration, and on what date?"

Concern rises if: every party names another party and none names a person.

SPOT IT — The Restart Test

Ask: "How many times have I had to explain this from the beginning?"

Concern rises if: new contacts routinely have no record of earlier conversations.

SPOT IT — The Change Test

Ask: "After the call or the shared document, what actually changed in the record?"

Concern rises if: nothing did, and the invitation to comment was the only event.

SPOT IT — The Letter Test

Ask: "Is my own effort being presented as proof of the organization's diligence?"

Concern rises if: the letter lists your emails and tickets as evidence that the matter was handled.

SPOT IT — The Silence Test

Ask: "Is confidentiality being used to protect other parties, or to stop me learning about my own work?"

Concern rises if: it is cited to withhold facts about your own song or to discourage advice.

Before you read too much into it

Metadata errors are common and often innocent. Credit can be disputed in good faith, a producer can legitimately contribute to a song, and rights chains really are fragmented. A closing letter that lists prior contacts may be an honest attempt to record what was done.

The discriminator is whether any party ever names a decision. Genuine confusion eventually produces a name, a date or a corrected record. The pattern produces courtesy without a single point at which anyone says, "this was submitted by X, on this date, from this document."

Other benign lookalikes:

  • A genuine mapping error. Data fields do get crossed between systems.
  • A real collaboration. Others may have contributed meaningfully to the song.
  • Legitimate privacy. A body may be restricted in what it can share about other parties.
  • Slow propagation. A corrected record may take time to appear everywhere.
  • A sincere alignment call. Gathering the parties can be a real step toward resolution.

Concern strengthens when: no party will say who submitted the version in question; the writer is asked to resubmit the same question through a new channel more than once; and the final letter lists the writer's own efforts as proof of responsiveness.

What to keep

  • The signed split sheet, the date it was signed, who was present, and any earlier or later drafts.
  • The original demo or voice memo with its timestamp, and any messages that show authorship before the session.
  • Screenshots of the registration record showing every writer and share, with the date you captured it.
  • A log of every inquiry: date, recipient, channel, ticket number, reply and the name of the person who replied.
  • The shared document from any "stakeholder" call, with versions before and after your comments.
  • The closing letter, and a note of exactly which of your communications it lists.
  • Publishing agreement clauses on credit, registration and administration, and any confidentiality language.
  • Emails showing who recommended the producer's changes and who communicated the final splits.

Plus the Universal Evidence list in the Sector Standards.

What breaks the chain

  • Ask the single question that forces a name. "Please confirm who submitted the registration listing five writers, on what date, and from which split document."
  • Send the same written request to all parties at once. "I am sending the same request to the publisher, the label and the registering body so that each can confirm its own role."
  • Compare the signed split sheet to the record. A one-page table of names and shares, with the page and date of each source, is far harder to deflect than a story.
  • Keep an inquiry log. Date, recipient, ticket, reply, name. It turns an experience into a sequence.
  • Ask what the process is and when it ends. "What is the process for correcting a registration, and by what date will I receive a decision?"
  • Respond to the closing letter in writing. "I note that the letter lists my communications. I have not received an answer to the question of who submitted the registration."
  • Seek independent advice. An entertainment attorney with no tie to the publisher or label, a songwriter advocacy group, or a musicians' union, particularly before the matter is treated as closed or any release is considered. Check the rules and time limits of the registering body. Varies by place
  • Ask the body about its own dispute channel. Many have a formal route for credit disputes, which differs from the general support desk. Awaiting expert review
  • Do not accept a confidentiality term as part of a correction without advice. A quiet fix that bars you from discussing the matter is a moment for counsel.

When to get help, and where

LOW → MODERATE

The record differs from the signed split sheet; two or more parties each name another as responsible; you have restarted the same question more than once; no party offers the submission date.

MODERATE → HIGH

A closing letter lists your efforts as proof of responsiveness; confidentiality is cited against your questions; income from the song is being paid on a share you did not agree to; the approximate time limits of any dispute process are approaching. Appropriate outside support may include an entertainment attorney, a royalty auditor or forensic music accountant, a musicians' union or songwriter advocacy group, the formal dispute channel of the registering body, and the arbitration or mediation route in your publishing agreement Varies by place.

HIGH → URGENT

Any threat, intimidation or blackmail connected to the credit; evidence of altered or forged documents, which may indicate fraud; pressure to sign away your share under a deadline; or signs of crisis in yourself. Prioritize safety, contact emergency services or a crisis line as relevant, tell a trusted person, and do not confront the party involved or sign anything alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

Words you could use

  • "Could you confirm who submitted the registration that lists five writers, and on what date?"
  • "I'm sending the same question to each party so that everyone is working from the same request."
  • "My signed split sheet shows [names and shares]. The registration shows something different. Can you help me reconcile them?"
  • "What is the process for correcting a registration, who owns it, and by when will I have a decision?"
  • "I'd like to understand what changed in the record as a result of the call."
  • "Thank you for the summary of our contacts. It doesn't yet answer the question of who submitted the registration."
  • "If there is a confidentiality concern, could you tell me what can be shared about my own work?"
  • "I'm going to take advice before I treat this matter as closed."
If eleven tickets all prove that someone answered, which of them proves that someone decided?
