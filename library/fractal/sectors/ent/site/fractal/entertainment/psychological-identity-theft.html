The scene

You're a thirty-three-year-old acoustic folk performer, and you have just signed with a label after eleven years of playing rooms of forty people. You know your audience by their faces. You know the songs by the places you wrote them: a parked car outside your father's care home, a borrowed kitchen in a winter of no work. You play a worn guitar with a scar on the shoulder where a strap pin gave way in a town you no longer remember.

The first meeting is warm. Two executives and a young marketing lead sit in a glass room with coffee and tell you what they heard. "It's the authenticity," one says. "People are starved for someone who is simply real." The marketing lead says your lyric about the car park made her cry on a train. You go home and tell your sister it is the first time anyone in the business has seen you the way you see yourself.

The second meeting has a slide. It says "Broadening the Lane." The same executive, equally warm, says there is a lot of room between where you are and where the audience is, and the label would like to explore a bridge. Nothing is being taken from you. You are being extended.

At the third meeting a stylist is present. She is friendly and quick, and she has a mood board, and on it there is a woman who is clearly meant to be you. Her hair is a shade lighter. Her sleeves are looser, her boots higher, her smile brighter than yours has ever been. "She's you with the volume up," the stylist says. Everyone laughs, so you laugh.

At the fourth meeting there is a document. It is called the Brand Deck and it has a section titled Persona Brief. You are given a printed copy. The first page lists what the brand is: Warm. Sunlit. Resilient. Fearless. Underneath, in a smaller line, is a list of what the brand is not: Heavy. Introspective. Difficult. Your song about the car park has been moved to an appendix marked "tone: reference only."

You read your own name beside the word "Persona." You ask what happens to the songs that do not fit. The marketing lead, who still seems kind, says, "We'd love for you to see this as a way to grow, not to lose anything." The stylist has already begun pinning a fabric swatch to your sleeve.

Over the following months the changes arrive like weather. Your hair is cut, then lightened. The songs for the record come in from co-writers whose names you do not know, and they are bright and well made. At a call someone refers to you in the third person while you are on the line, "she's still finding the brand," and the room discusses how to "manage her" through the autumn run.

You find you have stopped playing the car-park song in your own kitchen. When you try, it feels like reading someone else's diary. In a photo shoot you hear yourself say, without being asked, "Sorry, I'm not usually this heavy." The photographer smiles. The persona brief says the word. You said it first.

One evening, in a hotel mirror, you watch a bright, easy woman pull a cardigan over her shoulders, and you realize you cannot recall which of the four meetings it was in which the person you were quietly stopped being the one in the room.

That's Psychological Identity Theft.

What it is

Psychological Identity Theft is the sequence where a person's self-concept is first fused to another party's flattering view of them, then gradually dissolved, then replaced with installed labels and judgments until the replacement feels as though it was always theirs, using Identity Hooking, Identity Erosion, Labeling as Identity Warfare, Shame Installation, Dehumanization and Internalized Conditioning to convert the need for a stable, self-defined identity into the loss of one's original sense of self. In the entertainment and music industry it can concentrate in artist development, persona and brand briefs, styling, image provisions and "positioning" work around a newly signed artist. It may benefit whoever owns the output and the brand. It is hard to detect because each change is small, framed as growth and often accompanied by real expertise. What can be lost is the ability to recognize oneself in one's own work.

The tactics, in order

The component tactics, in order:

  1. Identity Hooking — The label names the artist's real qualities back to them in rich detail: "it's the authenticity," "the car park lyric made me cry." This functions by fusing the artist's sense of being understood to a specific audience's view of them, so that disappointing that view later feels like losing oneself. Concern rises when the praise is unusually precise about the person's inner life before any terms, brand work or creative plan are discussed.
  1. Identity Erosion — Hair, clothes, sound, co-writers and the songs the artist plays at home change a step at a time, always with an offer to "extend" rather than replace. This functions by dissolving the old self in pieces too small to refuse individually. Concern rises when every change runs in the same direction, away from what the artist originally brought, and nobody can say where it stops.
  1. Labeling as Identity Warfare — The brand deck sorts qualities into approved and unapproved: Warm, Sunlit and Resilient on one side; Heavy, Introspective and Difficult on the other. This functions by turning descriptions of how a person can be marketed into verdicts about who they are. Concern rises when the labels are applied to the person rather than to a product plan, and when the unapproved words describe things the artist is known and loved for.
  1. Shame Installation — The original self comes to feel deficient: the car-park song is "reference only," and the artist apologizes for being "heavy." This functions by teaching the artist that their natural way of being is an embarrassment to be managed. Concern rises when apologies begin to precede the artist's own work and when the artist stops playing their own songs in private.
  1. Dehumanization — The artist is discussed as a managed thing: "she's still finding the brand," "we need to manage her through the autumn." This functions by reducing the person to an object of planning whose reactions are variables, not views. Concern rises when decisions about the artist are discussed in their presence in the third person, or when the artist is referred to as "product" or "asset" in ways that remove their agency.
  1. Internalized Conditioning — The brand's frame becomes the artist's own: they correct themselves before anyone does and describe their old songs in the brief's vocabulary. This functions by relocating enforcement inside the person, so that the label no longer needs to press for the persona to hold. Concern rises when the artist uses the document's words about themselves without noticing.

Why this order

Identity Hooking has to come first because nothing can be replaced until it has been seen. The first meeting is a gift: someone describes you accurately, and that accuracy earns trust. It also creates a debt of recognition. If the people who understood you ask for a change, refusing feels like betraying the one audience that saw you.

Identity Erosion can then proceed without alarm. Because the label has already said it loves the real you, each adjustment can be offered as help in reaching more people, not as a replacement. The pace matters. A single instruction to become someone else would be refused; four meetings of "expansion" are not.

Labeling as Identity Warfare converts the changes into a standard. Once the Brand Deck lists approved and unapproved qualities, the artist no longer faces a styling choice; they face a judgment about their character. The labels also give the process a document, which is what turns a series of conversations into something that feels settled.

Shame Installation makes the labels sting. Without it, a persona can be resisted as a costume. With it, the artist begins to experience the original self as the problem. This is where independent evaluation weakens: the thing they would have used to judge the process, their own taste, has been marked as a flaw.

Dehumanization raises the exit cost. A person who is discussed as a managed thing learns that their objections are inputs, and the room has stopped waiting for their view. It also provides plausible deniability, because the language is the ordinary shorthand of planning meetings and rarely meant cruelly.

Internalized Conditioning closes the loop. The key conversion point is the apology in the photo shoot: the moment the artist starts doing the label's work. After it, the pattern no longer needs the label in the room. What weakens the sequence is a written statement of what the artist will and will not change, outside advice before the brief is accepted, and a trusted friend who knew the artist before the deal and can say what they see.

One pattern, three altitudes

Individual

The person may begin to describe their own history in the brand's vocabulary and feel a stranger's discomfort when they play older songs. Over time, they may stop writing the material they used to write, in case it counts as "heavy."

Institutional

Artist development, brand strategy and styling are standard parts of how recorded music is positioned Common pattern. Persona briefs, brand decks and image or approval provisions can turn those functions into written standards for how an artist must look, sound and be described Composite. Where an agreement gives the label approval over image and the brief sorts personal qualities into approved and unapproved, the artist's character can become a compliance matter Awaiting expert review. Where co-writers, stylists and producers are selected by the label, the creative input available to the artist may narrow without any single decision to narrow it Composite. When the result underperforms, the artist is often the most visible name attached to a plan that others wrote Common pattern.

The honest reading is that many artists want and benefit from development, that styling can be a creative partnership, and that a label investing money has real interests in how its project is presented. Intent would require additional evidence: for example, whether the artist's stated non-negotiables were recorded and honored, whether changes were requested or consented to, and whether similar rewrites recur across the label's roster.

Civilizational

The myth that hides the pattern is that the true artist is a raw material and the industry is the refinery. People say "it's a business," "you're a brand now," and "nobody sees the real you anyway," and they say them with a kind of weary realism that sounds like wisdom.

The public story is a performer who glows, and who seems to have arrived fully formed. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is an artist who can no longer sing the song that made someone want to sign them.

What you might notice

  1. Praise in early meetings is unusually specific about the artist's inner life or biography before terms are discussed.
  2. Changes to appearance, sound or songwriting are requested in a series, each framed as an extension.
  3. A written brand deck or persona brief lists approved and unapproved personal qualities.
  4. Songs central to the artist's identity are relegated to "reference" or removed from planned releases.
  5. Co-writers, stylists and producers are assigned by the label, without a meaningful artist veto.
  6. The artist is discussed in the third person, or as a product or asset, in their presence.
  7. Requests for creative input are redirected to "the team."
  8. The artist begins to apologize for their own style, or to describe their old work in the brief's terms.

Spot it

SPOT IT — The Mirror Test

Ask: "Does the person in the marketing materials look, sound and speak like the person who walked in on the first day?"

Concern rises if: the changes began after signing, nobody can say who asked for them, and you did not.

SPOT IT — The Word Test

Ask: "Are the labels in the brief describing a plan for presenting me, or describing me?"

Concern rises if: the unapproved list contains the qualities that first drew the label to you.

SPOT IT — The Third-Person Test

Ask: "When decisions about my work are discussed, am I addressed or am I discussed?"

Concern rises if: the room speaks about you rather than to you, and moves on after you object.

SPOT IT — The Apology Test

Ask: "Have I started apologizing for my own songs or style before anyone has objected?"

Concern rises if: you hear yourself using the brief's words about your own work.

Before you read too much into it

Artist development is a real discipline, and a good stylist or producer can help a performer find a stage version of themselves that still feels honest. Lookalikes specific to this combo include:

  • Collaborative reinvention. An artist who wanted a new look or sound and led the change.
  • Honest brand work. A deck that describes how the label will present the artist, built with the artist, reviewed by their advisor and open to revision.
  • Reasonable approval rights. A defined, narrow approval over released materials, clearly explained and negotiated.

Concern strengthens when: the labels in the brief describe the person rather than the plan; the artist's own songs and past are treated as liabilities; and the artist's objections are recorded as attitude problems, not as input.

What to keep

  • The Brand Deck, Persona Brief, mood boards and any versions, with dates and the meeting at which each was shown.
  • Notes of each meeting: who was present, what was requested, what was said about authenticity, and what you answered.
  • Early emails and messages that praised your existing work, kept beside the later ones that asked you to change it.
  • The image and approval provisions of your recording or management agreement, with the drafts that preceded them.
  • Records of co-writer, stylist and producer assignments, including who chose them and whether you were offered a say.
  • Your own list of non-negotiables, dated, and any response from the label to it.

Plus the Universal Evidence list in the Sector Standards.

What breaks the chain

  • Write your non-negotiables before the next meeting. A short list in your own words, dated, of what you will and will not change. Share it, and ask for a written reply.
  • Ask for the document, in full. "Could I have a copy of the Brand Deck and Persona Brief, and the date each version was shown?"
  • Get independent advice on image and approval provisions. An entertainment attorney with no tie to the label can read them before you are asked to accept the brief. Varies by place

When to get help, and where

LOW → MODERATE

A brief that lists personal qualities as approved or unapproved; your own songs moved to "reference only"; co-writers and stylists assigned without your say; you begin apologizing for your own style.

MODERATE → HIGH

The image and approval provisions are cited to override your objection; release of your own material is blocked; you are told that declining the persona risks being shelved, dropped or labeled difficult; terms for what you may say about the brief appear in an NDA or non-disparagement clause. Relevant routes may include an entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, an artist advocacy or legal-aid clinic, and the mediation or arbitration process named in the agreement Varies by place.

HIGH → URGENT

Signs of a mental-health crisis, self-harm thoughts, a relapse or an inability to eat or sleep, or any threat attached to your refusal: contact a doctor, a crisis line or emergency services, and tell a trusted person where you are. Do not sign anything, delete anything or negotiate alone, and do not confront the people involved.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

Words you could use

  • "Before I respond to the Persona Brief, could I have a copy and a week to read it?"
  • "I'd like to keep my own songs in the plan. Can we write down which ones, and where?"
  • "I'm happy to talk about how the project is presented. I'd rather not discuss who I am."
  • "Could we note my non-negotiables in the agreement, in writing?"
  • "I'd like to be addressed in these meetings, not discussed. Could we direct the questions to me?"
  • "Who made the choice of co-writers, and could I have a say?"
If the person in the photographs walked into the room where you first played your songs, would anyone there know her?
