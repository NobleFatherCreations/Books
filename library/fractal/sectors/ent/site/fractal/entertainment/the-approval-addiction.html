The scene

You're a twenty-six-year-old conservatory violinist, and for fourteen months you have been the strings arranger in a celebrated mentor's studio, the room with the acoustic panels the color of oatmeal and a wall of framed records you stopped noticing around month three.

You got here the way people like you get anywhere: one email, one audition, one afternoon in which a famous musician listened to your arrangement of a verse you had written for a quartet and said, "Where have you been hiding?" You went home and sat on the floor of your apartment. You had spent your whole training being told you were good, which is different from being told you were seen.

For the first months, he saw you constantly. He would stop a session to say, to the whole room, "That. That is what I mean. Listen to what she did with the second violins." Engineers looked over. A pop vocalist you had grown up listening to said you were the reason the bridge worked. At the end of one Thursday he drove you to the train station himself and said you reminded him of himself at your age, and that he did not say that often.

Then the Tuesdays started. You hand in a string chart he praised on Monday and he reads it in silence and says, "I thought you had more than this." You say it is the same approach as yesterday. He says, "Yesterday I was being generous." The room laughs a little, kindly, the way rooms do. You laugh too. You write it down, because writing things down is what you do.

You can't predict it. A chart he calls "sublime" on Wednesday is "student work" on Friday. An idea you were told to cut is credited back to you in a meeting with an executive, in the same voice. You begin to spend your mornings listening for the weather in his greeting. A warm "good morning" and you can breathe; a flat one and your hands go cold before you've taken off your coat.

At some point you stop asking whether the work is good. You ask whether he will like it.

The documents arrive in the same week. The first is the annotated session notes. After every session he writes in the margins of the shared file, and the file is shown to the label contact and the vocalist's manager. They are scrawled, quick, funny. Beside your bar sixteen: "Lovely instinct. Wrong instrument for this brain." Beside a harmonic choice: "Conservatory reflex." Beside nothing at all, at the bottom: "Gifted but fragile. Needs a firm hand." You read that line on a train and feel your face go hot in front of strangers.

The second document is a conversation, not a page. In the kitchenette, over the espresso machine he is proud of, he says that when you're ready he will write you a reference letter that "will open every door in this city." He says it softly, a kind of gift. He says he does not do that for many people. He says a letter like that depends on knowing someone's character, and that he is still learning yours.

You nod. You do not ask when. You do not ask what ready means. You notice that you are already planning your behavior to deserve it.

That night you open a blank page to draft a message to a former classmate who has offered you a recording project outside the studio. You write "I'd love to" and delete it, and write "Let me check with" and stop, with your thumb above the screen, because it is suddenly very clear that you are asking a man's permission to be asked. And you cannot remember, with real effort, what you thought of your own playing before you met him.

That's The Approval Addiction.

What it is

The Approval Addiction is the sequence where praise and criticism from a powerful, admired figure are alternated until a person's sense of worth depends on that figure's opinion, using Conditional Love, Intermittent Reinforcement, Pedestal Ploy, Negging, Shame Installation and Identity Hooking to convert the need for approval from an intermittently warm authority into the loss of an independent basis for self-worth. It benefits whoever relies on the person's effort, availability and loyalty, and it exploits real features of the sector: mentorship that does open doors, session notes that are normal, reference letters that matter. It is hard to detect because genuine mentors also praise, criticize and gatekeep. Intent would require additional evidence beyond the pattern.

The tactics, in order

The component tactics, in order:

  1. Conditional Love — Warmth, attention and belonging arrive when the work pleases the mentor and withdraw when it does not. This functions by teaching that closeness is earned by each delivery rather than given by the relationship. Concern rises when the warmth tracks the mentor's mood or needs more than the quality of the work.
  1. Intermittent Reinforcement — "Sublime" on Wednesday, "student work" on Friday, with no stable standard in between. This functions by making rewards unpredictable, which tends to make people watch the source of reward more closely and work harder to earn it. Concern rises when identical work gets opposite reactions and the person cannot name what changed.
  1. Pedestal Ploy — Public praise in front of engineers and executives, "that is what I mean," a drive to the station. This functions by raising the person high enough that the fall is costly, and by creating a debt of gratitude that is hard to question. Concern rises when praise is public and showy but never converts into pay, credit or written commitments.
  1. Negging — Backhanded lines delivered lightly: "Wrong instrument for this brain," "conservatory reflex." This functions by planting doubt under cover of humor, while the person is expected to laugh along. Concern rises when the barbs target the very skills that make the person valuable, and objecting is treated as lacking humor.
  1. Shame Installation — "Gifted but fragile. Needs a firm hand," written where others will read it. This functions by attaching a flaw to the person's identity, rather than to a piece of work, and making it visible to people who matter. Concern rises when comments describe the person rather than the music, and the notes circulate beyond those who need them.
  1. Identity Hooking — "You remind me of myself," "I'm still learning your character," the letter held as a prize. This functions by fusing the person's self-image with the mentor's endorsement, so that leaving feels like losing who they are. Concern rises when the person cannot picture their career without this one figure's approval.

Why this order

Conditional Love goes first because everything else needs a relationship in which warmth already matters. If the mentor were simply unkind, the target would leave. A first phase of genuine attention builds the reservoir that later withdrawal will draw down.

Intermittent Reinforcement comes next because it turns that warmth into something to chase. Steady praise is taken for granted and steady criticism is escaped; praise that arrives unpredictably keeps attention locked on the source. Pedestal Ploy then raises the stakes of the fall, since the more public the elevation, the more expensive any loss of standing becomes, and it plants gratitude that makes later questions feel ungrateful.

Negging is what weakens independent evaluation. A small barb, delivered with a laugh, is easy to dismiss in the moment and hard to argue with later. It trains the person to doubt their own ear before they doubt his. Shame Installation raises the exit cost, because a label like "fragile," written in notes seen by the people who hire you, makes leaving look risky. Identity Hooking closes the loop and supplies the plausible deniability: a mentor can always say he was invested in the person's growth.

The key conversion point is the first time the person asks "will he like it?" before "is it good?" After that, the mentor no longer needs to apply the pattern; the person applies it to themselves. What weakens the sequence: independent feedback from trusted peers, written standards for the work, and a reference or credit arrangement that does not depend on one person's mood.

One pattern, three altitudes

Individual

The target may begin to scan for the mentor's mood before they begin work, and to feel physical relief or dread at a greeting. Over time, the person may stop trusting their own judgment about their playing, and defer to remembered phrases from the notes.

They may start to turn down outside projects, or to ask permission to accept them. They may feel gratitude and shame at once, and find it hard to explain to friends why a place where they were so praised feels so small. They may also come to believe that the reference letter is the measure of their worth, so that deadlines and delays in it feel like verdicts.

Institutional

Mentorship in studios and writing rooms often runs on informal arrangements: shared notes files, verbal promises about credits or letters, and gatekeeping over introductions to labels and managers Common pattern. A shared notes file that is read by third parties can turn private feedback into reputation Composite.

Where an arranger's pay, credit and future references all depend on one senior figure, there may be no neutral place to ask what the standard is, and no written record of what was promised Composite. Genuine mentors also write blunt notes and withhold references until they know someone well, so the same documents can describe either good or harmful practice. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the standards stayed consistent, whether credit and pay followed the praise, and whether others describe the same cycle.

Civilizational

The myth is that great art comes from great teachers who are hard on you, and that the harshest critic is the one who believes in you most. Around it sits the belief that talent needs to be broken to be refined, and that gratitude for being chosen is a debt that never closes.

The value being exploited is a good one: the wish to learn from someone extraordinary. The public story is the apprentice who became a star because a master saw something in them. It can be true. It is also the story told about the apprentices who stayed, and rarely about the ones who stopped playing.

What you might notice

  1. Reactions to near-identical work vary widely without a stated reason.
  2. Praise is delivered publicly and is not followed by pay, credit or a written commitment.
  3. Humorous barbs target the skill the person was hired for.
  4. Notes describe the person's character ("fragile," "reflex") rather than the music.
  5. Session notes are shared with third parties who do not need them.
  6. A reference letter is promised, but timing and criteria are never stated.
  7. Warmth in greetings, access or invitations changes after disagreement.
  8. The person is discouraged from outside projects, or must ask first.
  9. The mentor says "I don't do this for many people" in connection with a favor.
  10. Peers describe a similar warm-then-cold cycle.

Spot it

SPOT IT — The Same-Chart Test

Ask: "If I handed in the same work on another day, would the answer be the same?"

Concern rises if: it plainly would not, and no one can say what changed.

SPOT IT — The Outside Ear Test

Ask: "What would a trusted peer outside this room say about this piece?"

Concern rises if: you feel unable to ask anyone else, or you assume they would be wrong.

SPOT IT — The Letter Test

Ask: "Do I know what the reference requires, and by when?"

Concern rises if: the criteria are never stated and the letter is raised whenever you hesitate.

SPOT IT — The Margin Test

Ask: "Do the notes describe my music, or me, and who else reads them?"

Concern rises if: the notes label your character and circulate to people who hire you.

Before you read too much into it

Real mentors are demanding, uneven, funny and blunt. Honest harsh feedback, a high bar and a refusal to write a reference before knowing someone well are all ordinary. A mentor who explains standards, is consistent between similar pieces, welcomes outside work and gives credit and pay on schedule is not running this pattern.

  • Honest quality variation. Some charts are weaker, and a mentor's reaction may honestly differ.
  • Direct feedback culture. Some studios are blunt with everyone, and the bluntness is not aimed.
  • Cautious references. A mentor may reasonably wait to write a letter.
  • Your own sensitivity to critique. Early-career feedback can feel larger than it is.

Concern strengthens when: the standards are different for the same work; barbs are aimed at the person, not the work; the mentor's warmth and the letter are tied to compliance.

What to keep

  • The annotated session notes and the version history of the shared notes file, showing who could see which comments and when.
  • Dated copies of the charts or arrangements and the reactions each received, for comparing reactions to similar work.
  • Any written or messaged reference to the promised reference letter, including timing, criteria and conditions.
  • Messages praising the work, kept beside messages criticizing similar work.
  • Records of credits, pay and invoices for your arranging work, and any discussion of credits.
  • Messages in which outside projects were discussed, approved, discouraged or declined.
  • A dated private journal of the cycle, noting what changed between reactions.
  • Names of peers or engineers who witnessed public praise or put-downs.

Plus the Universal Evidence list in the Sector Standards.

What breaks the chain

  • Ask for the standard in writing. "What would make this chart a yes?" A real standard can be stated.
  • Keep your own record of which pieces got which reactions, with dates.
  • Seek outside ears. A teacher, a peer, a section player you trust outside the studio. Consistent feedback from them is a benchmark the mentor's mood is not.
  • Ask for notes to be limited to the people who need them, in a calm, practical tone.
  • Ask about the reference directly, once: "What would you need to see, and roughly when?" Then make a list of other references.
  • Keep credit and pay separate from praise. Ask for credit and fees in writing before the session.
  • Maintain at least one outside project or relationship so that your sense of your work does not run through one person.
  • Avoid public confrontation or retaliatory leaks of the notes. Preserve and consult first.

When to get help, and where

LOW → MODERATE

Reactions vary for the same work; barbs about your character appear in notes; the letter is mentioned whenever you hesitate; you stop outside projects or ask permission for them.

MODERATE → HIGH

The letter, credit or work are made explicitly conditional on compliance; you are told your career depends on staying; notes with character labels are circulated to people who hire you; others describe the same pattern; credit or pay for your arranging is withheld. Relevant routes may include an independent entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, and, where credits are disputed, a performing rights or collective management body Varies by place.

HIGH → URGENT

Threats to your reputation or livelihood if you leave; stalking or unwanted contact; any sexual pressure or exploitation; withholding of belongings, documents or access; talk of self-harm or signs of a mental-health crisis. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront the mentor or sign anything alone.

Words you could use

  • "Can you tell me what would make this chart a yes? I'd like to aim at it."
  • "I got a very different reaction to a similar approach last week. Can we talk about what you're hearing?"
  • "I'd like to keep the notes between us and the people who need them for the session."
  • "I'm grateful for the offer of a reference. What would you need to see, and roughly when?"
  • "I have an outside project and I'd like to take it. I'll make sure my studio commitments are covered."
  • "Can we confirm my credit and fee for this arrangement in writing before we start?"
If every kind word and every cruel one disappeared tomorrow, what would you say about your own playing?
