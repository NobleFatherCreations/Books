The scene

You're a twenty-two-year-old singer making your debut album, and tonight you are standing in a vocal booth that smells faintly of old coffee and warm electronics, wearing headphones that are a little too big, on take one hundred and forty of the lead vocal.

You are not complaining. You want to be clear about that, to yourself, because you have had this conversation in your head a hundred and forty times. The producer is gifted. People have told you so since the first day, and the first day was wonderful: he listened to your demo with his eyes closed and said, "There's something real here. I want to find all of it." Nobody had ever said that. You would have walked through a wall.

The first week, you did eleven takes of the opening verse and they felt like play. He would lean on the talkback, grinning, and say, "That one's alive. Give me one more with a little less polish." You loved it. You were being taken seriously.

By the second week the count had a sound. "Take twenty-six." "Take thirty." There is a session log taped to the console, a tidy grid with a column for take numbers and a column for notes, and you can see it through the glass. He writes on it in capital letters. You have started to read the log before you read his face.

The notes change. At first they say things like "warm," "close," "nearly." Then "almost." Then "again." Then, one day, nothing in the note column at all, just the number, and you realize that the empty box has become the worst thing it could say.

"The reference vocal is sitting at an eighty," he says one afternoon, tapping a laptop. "Yours is a seventy-one. Let's get you there." You do not know what the numbers mean. You do not ask. He plays you the other singer's track, the one the label loves, and you hear that her vowels are smooth as river stone and yours are, well, yours.

You do another take. It is better. He says, "That's good for you." You carry the phrase into the bathroom and look at it in the mirror.

By take eighty the goalposts have started to move. A take that was the one on Tuesday is, by Thursday, "a good scratch." He uses the word "just": "Just clean up the breath in bar nine." "Just a bit more truth." You are not sure how to give more truth to a breath. You try. Your voice begins to have a gravel in it that you hide with tea.

When you stop, he is kind. "Go home. Sleep. We'll get it tomorrow." And you go home and lie on the bed and replay the takes in your head, finding, in each one, the flaw. On the walk to the studio the next morning you realize you have started apologizing to the walls.

Tonight, take one hundred and forty. You hear your own voice in the headphones and it sounds like a stranger's. The grid on the console, through the glass, shows a column of numbers, and below it a clean white line at the bottom where the next number will go. You find that you are not afraid of singing badly. You are afraid of the white line, because you have begun to believe the number is who you are, and that you are only as good as the count you have left.

Through the talkback the producer says, softly, "That was beautiful." Your whole body drops with relief. Then he says, "One more."

You nod before the sentence is finished.

That's The Perfectionism Trap.

What it is

The Perfectionism Trap is the sequence where a standard is set so high and so unstable that the target can never arrive, using Impossible Standards, Conditional Worth, Comparative Devaluation, Moving Goalposts, Shame Cycling, Internalized Critic, and Performance Addiction to convert the drive to meet a self-set high standard into permanent inadequacy. It benefits whoever profits from more output, more takes, more compliance, or a more malleable artist. It exploits the real ambition of a young performer and the genuine value of craft. It is hard to detect because rigor, high standards and "one more take" are also what excellent producers do, and the person often experiences the pressure as their own. What may be lost is the sense that rest, a good-enough take or a finished song can ever count. Intent would require additional evidence, and real creative rigor must be told apart from this pattern.

The tactics, in order

The component tactics, in order:

  1. Impossible Standards — A "reference vocal" and an unspecified "something real." This functions by setting a target that cannot be reached or measured, so effort never produces arrival. Concern rises when the standard is undefined, cannot be demonstrated, or is described as just beyond the current attempt.
  1. Conditional Worth — "That was beautiful. One more." Praise tied to the next take, regard tied to the count. This functions by tying the person's value to the latest performance, so approval has to be earned again and again. Concern rises when warmth fades after weak takes and returns after strong ones.
  1. Comparative Devaluation — The other singer's smooth vowels, the seventy-one against the eighty. This functions by measuring the person against an idealized other, so their difference is read as deficit. Concern rises when comparisons are made to people whose conditions you cannot see, and when scores appear without a stated method.
  1. Moving Goalposts — Tuesday's keeper becomes Thursday's scratch. This functions by changing the criteria after the work is done, so success cannot be locked in. Concern rises when previously approved work is later called inadequate with no new information, and when the criteria cannot be written down.
  1. Shame Cycling — The grid with the empty box, the "good for you," then the kindness at the end of the night. This functions by alternating disappointment and reassurance, so relief and hurt both bind the person to the next attempt. Concern rises when the person feels worse after reassurance, and when kindness arrives mainly after discomfort.
  1. Internalized Critic — Apologizing to the walls and finding the flaw in every replay. This functions by relocating the evaluator inside the person, so the pressure continues when no one is in the room. Concern rises when you criticize your own work more harshly than anyone in the room does.
  1. Performance Addiction — Hearing "one more" and nodding before the sentence ends. This functions by making striving feel like the only way to be safe or worthy, so stopping feels like falling behind. Concern rises when rest is experienced as failure and the count feels like a measure of the self.

Why this order

Impossible Standards has to come first because the trap needs a goal that cannot be reached and cannot be disproven. If the target were told, "we need a take that does X, in this range," they could meet it and leave. The vagueness is the architecture. It is also the most defensible tactic, since high standards sound like care.

Conditional Worth follows because the standard needs an emotional price. Once approval is tied to the next take, every attempt is also a test of whether the person is still welcome. Comparative Devaluation prepares the person to accept that the problem is them: a smooth reference vocal does not prove the target's voice is wrong, but beside a number it begins to look that way. Independent evaluation weakens here, since the target is now measuring themselves against a rule they did not choose.

Moving Goalposts raises exit cost: any approved work can be retroactively revoked, so stopping always risks being told the work was not done. Shame Cycling is what makes the loop self-sustaining, because relief alternates with hurt and the person cannot tell which is the pattern. The Internalized Critic supplies plausible deniability: by now nobody needs to criticize; the artist does it, and the room can honestly say it only asked for one more.

Performance Addiction closes the loop. The conversion point is the moment the person says "I'll do another" without being asked. What would weaken the sequence: a written target with a defined end, a take limit agreed before the session, an outside listener with no stake, and rest treated as part of the schedule, not as a reward.

One pattern, three altitudes

Individual

The person may begin to hear every take as a list of faults. Over time, they may sing more cautiously, with less of the quality that got them noticed. They may lose the ability to say when a take is good, relying on the room to decide. They may feel relief when a session ends and dread before the next, and may confuse the dread with commitment. They may apologize to people who have done nothing, struggle to rest on days off, and feel that a finished song is a risky thing. Physical strain, including to the voice or sleep, belongs with a medical professional or a voice specialist to assess, and this material does not diagnose anything. They may also quietly decide that their discomfort is the price of being serious.

Institutional

Recording is an expensive, schedule-bound process, and take counts, reference tracks and scoring are common tools for managing it Common pattern. A session log that records only the count can come to function as a measure of the performer, not of the process Composite. Debut projects often involve strong label and producer expectations, and a performer with no leverage may have little say in how many takes are enough Common pattern. Whether an album budget, an option, or recoupment is influenced by studio time is a matter of the agreement Varies by place. The honest reading in most cases is that producers genuinely chase quality, that comping many takes is normal, and that perfectionism is often shared. Intent would require additional evidence, such as moving criteria, a score with no method, and a pattern across several artists.

Civilizational

The myth is that great art demands suffering and that genius is a thousand tries. "Real artists suffer for their art" and "pay your dues" are the cultural versions of "one more take," and they are partly true: craft takes repetition, and many great records were made the hard way. The value being exploited is the artist's own seriousness. The language that makes it sound noble is "pushing you," "finding all of it," "I believe in you." The public story is a luminous record and a producer who got the best out of her. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a young singer who now believes that the worth of her voice is the number of tries she has left.

What you might notice

  1. Takes in the dozens or hundreds with no stated target or end point.
  2. Notes in the session log that shrink to a number or an empty box.
  3. Reference scores or comparisons with no explanation of the method.
  4. Comparisons to other artists whose conditions are unknown.
  5. Approved takes later reclassified as "scratch" with no new information.
  6. Praise followed immediately by a request to continue.
  7. The performer apologizing, over-explaining or asking permission to rest.
  8. The performer criticizing takes more harshly than the room does.
  9. Breaks and sleep treated as optional or as a reward.
  10. The performer unable to say when a take is good without checking the room.

Spot it

SPOT IT — The Target Test

Ask: "Can anyone say, in a sentence, what a finished take sounds like?"

Concern rises if: "No, 'I'll know it when I hear it.'"

SPOT IT — The Keeper Test

Ask: "Has a take that was approved ever been un-approved without new information?"

Concern rises if: "Yes, more than once."

SPOT IT — The Warmth Test

Ask: "When does the room feel warmest, and what did I just do?"

Concern rises if: "After a take that pleased them, and cold after one that did not."

SPOT IT — The Rest Test

Ask: "How do I feel on a day off?"

Concern rises if: "Like I'm falling behind."

Before you read too much into it

Demanding producers and perfectionist artists are not the pattern. Recording many takes to comp a performance is normal craft, high standards can be an honest form of care, and a young singer may genuinely want more takes and feel energized by them. Honest rigor has a stated target, an agreed end, criteria that stay put, and a performer who can stop. A producer who says "we're done for today, this is the take" is not running a trap.

Concern strengthens when: (1) approved work is repeatedly revoked without new reasons; (2) warmth and approval track the next take; (3) the standard can never be specified or the end never agreed; (4) the performer cannot rest without feeling they are losing.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

What to keep

  • The session log, with take counts and notes for each day, photographed or copied as it stood.
  • Booking confirmations and invoices showing hours and days of studio time.
  • Messages or notes in which the standard, reference or score was described.
  • Any comparison made to another artist, with the date and who said it.
  • Notes of changes in the verdict on a take that was earlier approved.
  • Records of rest, sleep, vocal strain or health concerns raised, and how they were answered.
  • Your own voice memos of the demo and early takes, with timestamps, for comparison.

Plus the Universal Evidence list in the Sector Standards.

What breaks the chain

  • Agree on a target before the session. "What does done sound like, and how many takes before we pick from what we have?" Put it in writing.
  • Ask for the method behind any score. "What does seventy-one measure?" A number with no method is an opinion.
  • Lock the keeper. At the end of each day, ask which takes are approved, and ask for that in a short message.
  • Get an outside ear. A trusted musician, a vocal coach or an independent engineer with no stake can tell you where you actually are.
  • Keep your early demos. They are a baseline for your own voice and an evidence trail.
  • Put rest on the schedule. Breaks, sleep and a day off are part of the work, not a reward for it.
  • Name the count. Say to yourself, "The number is a measure of the session, not of me."
  • Protect your voice. If you notice strain, pain or hoarseness, stop and see a medical or voice professional.
  • Review the agreement. Ask a lawyer with no tie to the label what the album schedule, budget and delivery terms actually require.

When to get help, and where

LOW → MODERATE

Takes run past an agreed or sensible number with no stated target, approved work is repeatedly revoked, or you dread sessions and find rest uncomfortable.

MODERATE → HIGH

Studio time, delivery or recoupment is being tied to the take count; you are pressured to continue despite voice strain; the standard shifts after you meet it; or other performers describe the same experience. Relevant routes may include an entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild, a voice or medical professional, and an artist advocacy clinic Varies by place.

HIGH → URGENT

You are in distress, thinking of self-harm, using substances to cope, or being pressured, threatened or isolated in connection with your work; or you are physically unwell or injured. Prioritize safety: contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and seek medical and mental-health professionals. Do not confront anyone and do not sign or deliver anything while in crisis.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

Words you could use

  • "Before we start, can we agree what the target for this part is, and when we stop?"
  • "Could we mark which takes are approved at the end of each day?"
  • "What does that score measure, and how was it taken?"
  • "I'd like to hear the keeper from Tuesday again before we change it."
  • "I need a break and some water. Let's come back in twenty minutes."
  • "I'd like a second opinion on where this vocal is, from someone outside the room."
  • "My voice is feeling strained. I'd like to stop for today and see someone."
If the number of takes were hidden from you, what would you say the vocal was worth, and why do you trust the number more than your own ear?
