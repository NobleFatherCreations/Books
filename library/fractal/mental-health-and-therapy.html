The Narrative — from the inside

You went because you were hurting. That is the thing that must be said first and held throughout — whatever follows, whatever the analysis reveals, whatever the pattern exposes — you went because you were hurting. And the hurt was real. The anxiety that made the mornings feel like walking into a wall. The depression that sat on your chest like a weight that had no name. The relationship that was eating you alive. The childhood that was still running your adulthood like a program you couldn't find in the task manager. The thing that happened to you that you told no one about. The thing you did that you told no one about. The sleep that wouldn't come. The sleep that wouldn't stop. The thoughts that spiraled. The feelings that flatlined. The body that was screaming something you couldn't translate. You went because you were hurting and the hurt was real and the going was brave and nothing — not a single word that follows — diminishes the courage it took to sit in that chair for the first time and say to a stranger: something is wrong and I need help. That sentence — "something is wrong and I need help" — is one of the most vulnerable sentences a human being can speak. It requires the admission that the self is not functioning. That the internal architecture is compromised. That the thing you've been managing — performing around, compensating for, numbing against, pretending isn't there — has finally exceeded your capacity to manage it alone. The sentence is a surrender. And the surrender is sacred. Because the person who says "I need help" is the person who has stopped performing wellness and started telling the truth about its absence. And truth-telling about internal experience is the original frequency of this sector — the human capacity to know that something is wrong inside and to seek the presence of another human who can sit with the wrongness without flinching. That capacity is ancient. It is older than psychology. Older than psychiatry. Older than any therapeutic modality with a name and a certification and a billing code. In every human community that has ever existed, there was someone who held this role — the person you went to when the inside didn't work. The shaman. The elder. The healer. The confessor. The wise woman. The friend who could listen without fixing. And the relationship between the hurting person and the helper was characterized by certain qualities that were not accidental: the helper knew the hurting person. They shared a community. They shared a context. They shared a language. They shared a worldview. The helping happened inside a relationship that existed before the crisis and would exist after it. The helping was not a transaction. It was a dimension of the bond. And the bond held the helping the way a riverbed holds the river — not as a container that restricts but as a shape that gives the flow somewhere to go. And then the helping became a profession. And the profession became an industry. And the industry became a sector. And the sector generates over two hundred and eighty billion dollars.

Stage 1: Idealize

Targeting

01 · Love Bombing

Warmth delivered faster than trust can be earned.

Individual. The first therapy session is designed to produce attachment — the warm office, the comfortable chair, the undivided attention, the empathic reflection, the sense of being truly heard for the first time, the validation that says "your feelings make sense" and "you're not crazy" and "what happened to you matters" — and the validation is real and the validation is also the onboarding, because a client who feels seen in the first session is a client who will return for the second, and the return is the hook. The therapist is not performing the love bombing consciously — the therapist is doing what they were trained to do, which is create a safe relational environment — but the system that trained them designed the training to produce attachment that generates retention, and the retention generates revenue.

Institutional. The mental health industry positions itself as the guardian of the inner world — "your mental health matters," "it's okay to not be okay," "therapy is self-care" — and the language wraps the industry in the sacred cloak of emotional wellbeing, which means engaging with the industry feels like an act of self-love rather than a consumer transaction, and the feeling is the hook.

Civilizational. The destigmatization of mental health — a genuine and important cultural achievement — has been captured by the industry as a marketing strategy, because a population that is not ashamed to seek therapy is a population that will seek therapy, and a population that seeks therapy is a market, and the market has grown every year since the destigmatization began, and the growth is celebrated as progress while the question of whether more therapy equals better mental health remains unanswered.

02 · Weaponized Generosity

A gift you can’t refuse, recorded on a ledger you never signed.

Individual. The sliding scale, the free first session, the pro bono slot — genuine acts of generosity that are also client acquisition strategies, because the client who receives the generosity feels gratitude, and the gratitude creates loyalty, and the loyalty sustains the therapeutic relationship past the point where the client might otherwise evaluate whether the relationship is still serving them.

Institutional. Employee assistance programs (EAPs) offer free sessions — typically three to six — and the offering is positioned as employer generosity while functioning as a screening mechanism that identifies which employees need ongoing treatment and channels them into the insurance-funded therapy system, and the generosity is the onramp to the billing.

Civilizational. The cultural message that "therapy is a gift you give yourself" is weaponized generosity at civilizational scale — the framing of a commercial transaction as an act of self-love, which means the person who cannot afford therapy is the person who cannot afford to love themselves, and the inability to afford becomes a character flaw rather than an economic condition.

Stage 2: Hook

Entrapment through investment

03 · Future Faking

A future vivid enough to keep you waiting, designed never to arrive.

Individual. "We're going to get to the root of this" is spoken in the first session and may remain the therapeutic goal for years — the root always a little deeper, the breakthrough always a little further, the resolution always one more insight away — and the proximity of the resolution sustains the engagement, and the engagement sustains the billing.

Institutional. "We're developing new treatments," "the science is advancing," "better interventions are coming" — each one a future fake that sustains the population's faith in the system while the current outcomes do not justify the faith.

Civilizational. "We're going to solve the mental health crisis" is spoken at every policy hearing, every awareness campaign, every industry conference — the crisis that has worsened every decade despite increasing investment, and the promise is renewed every year with the same sincerity and the same failure to examine whether the model that hasn't solved the crisis is the model that can.

04 · Hoovering

The pull back in — nostalgia, guilt, and manufactured urgency about what you’ll lose.

Individual. The therapist contacts the client who stopped coming — "I noticed you haven't scheduled, I want you to know the door is open" — and the contact may be genuinely caring and the contact is also a re-engagement attempt, because a client who stops attending is revenue that stopped flowing.

Institutional. Mental health awareness campaigns re-engage the population during designated months — Mental Health Awareness Month, Suicide Prevention Month, World Mental Health Day — each one activating the population's awareness of its suffering and channeling the awareness toward the professional services the industry provides.

Civilizational. Every mass tragedy, every celebrity mental health disclosure, every viral social media post about depression or anxiety hoovers the population back into the therapeutic frame — the crisis activates the awareness, the awareness activates the seeking, the seeking activates the billing.

Stage 3: Devalue

Transfer of authority over your worth

05 · Devaluation

The praise that hooked you, slowly converted into the criticism that keeps you striving.

Individual. The client's own coping mechanisms — the strategies they developed to survive before therapy, the self-knowledge they accumulated through lived experience, the resilience they demonstrated by making it to the chair — are subtly devalued in favor of the therapist's framework. "Those coping mechanisms served you then but they're not serving you now" — a sentence that may be clinically accurate and that also performs the devaluation of the client's pre-therapeutic self, positioning the self that existed before professional intervention as a less functional version that requires upgrading.

Institutional. Peer support, community care, friendship, and family are devalued as inadequate substitutes for professional treatment — "you need to talk to a professional," "your friends can't help you with this," "this requires clinical intervention" — and the devaluation ensures that the community-based care that served the species for two hundred thousand years is positioned as inferior to the professional service that has existed for roughly a century.

Civilizational. The entire population's emotional competence is devalued by a culture that has pathologized normal human experience — grief is a disorder, anxiety before a test is a disorder, the child who moves too much has a disorder, the adult who can't focus in an open-plan office has a disorder — and the pathologizing ensures that the population perceives itself as sick, because a population that perceives itself as sick is a population that seeks treatment, and the seeking is the market.

06 · Gaslighting

Making you doubt your own memory until the system becomes your only source of truth.

Individual. The client who says "I think this therapy isn't working" is told "resistance is part of the process" — and the statement may be clinically valid, resistance is a real therapeutic phenomenon — but the statement also functions as gaslighting when it is applied to a client whose therapy genuinely isn't working, because the client's accurate assessment of the treatment's ineffectiveness is reframed as a symptom of the condition the treatment is supposed to be addressing, and the reframing makes it impossible for the client to evaluate the treatment's effectiveness because every negative evaluation is automatically classified as the pathology.

Institutional. The mental health industry that has presided over a worsening mental health crisis — rising rates of depression, anxiety, suicide, and substance abuse despite unprecedented access to therapy and medication — responds to the crisis by prescribing more of what it has already been providing, and the prescription of more of the same in the face of worsening outcomes is institutional gaslighting, because the institution is telling the population that the solution to the crisis is the thing the institution sells, while the crisis has worsened during the period when the thing the institution sells has been more available than ever.

Civilizational. "You just need to talk to someone" gaslights the person whose suffering is caused by systemic conditions — the poverty, the housing insecurity, the exploitative job, the food desert, the environmental toxicity — by positioning the solution as internal and individual when the cause is external and structural, and the gaslighting is the conversion of a political problem into a therapeutic one, which depoliticizes the suffering and ensures that the person who needs a union organizer gets a therapist instead.

07 · Double Bind

Every available choice routed to the same punishment.

Individual. The client faces a foundational double bind — trust the therapist and risk losing sovereignty over your own inner world, or maintain sovereignty and risk being labeled "resistant" or "avoidant" or "not ready for the work" — and the bind ensures that the only acceptable therapeutic posture is openness, and openness in the context of a power imbalance is vulnerability, and vulnerability in the context of a revenue relationship is exposure.

Institutional. The therapist faces a structural double bind — prioritize the client's healing and risk shorter treatment duration (less revenue for the practice), or prioritize treatment duration and risk complicity in the management of a condition that could be resolved — and most therapists resolve the bind in favor of the client, but the system that employs them does not incentivize that resolution.

Civilizational. The population faces a civilizational double bind with mental health — seek treatment and enter a system that may create dependency while pathologizing your experience, or don't seek treatment and navigate the suffering alone in a civilization that dismantled every other form of communal care — and the bind is maintained by a culture that offers no third option between professional treatment and untreated suffering.

Stage 4: Confuse

Destruction of your inner compass

08 · Intermittent Reinforcement

Reward and punishment on an unpredictable schedule — the chemistry of addiction.

Individual. The therapeutic breakthrough arrives unpredictably — weeks of sessions that feel like treading water followed by a single moment of insight that changes everything followed by weeks of treading water again — and the unpredictability produces the same attachment that intermittent reinforcement produces in any relationship: the client cannot predict when the breakthrough will come, so they keep attending, because the last breakthrough proves that breakthroughs are possible.

Institutional. Insurance authorization arrives unpredictably — the sessions approved for six weeks, then denied, then appealed, then partially approved — and the unpredictability creates anxiety for both the therapist and the client, and the anxiety binds both parties to the system that controls the authorization.

Civilizational. Mental health research produces intermittent breakthroughs — a new medication, a new modality, a new understanding — each one amplified by media into a revolution that fades into incrementalism, and the cycle of breakthrough-and-disappointment sustains the population's investment in the research without producing the consistent improvement the investment promises.

09 · Moving the Goalposts

A finish line that moves the instant you reach it, because it doesn’t exist.

Individual. The therapeutic goals shift as the therapy progresses — the initial goal of managing the anxiety becomes the goal of understanding the anxiety becomes the goal of exploring the childhood roots of the anxiety becomes the goal of reparenting the inner child becomes the goal of integrating the shadow — and each shift extends the treatment, and the extension is justified by the depth of the work, and the depth has no bottom, which means the treatment has no end, which means the revenue has no ceiling.

Institutional. The criteria for "evidence-based treatment" shift with sufficient regularity to keep the industry in perpetual motion — yesterday's gold standard is today's outdated modality, and the practitioner who was trained in the previous standard must be retrained in the current one, and the retraining generates revenue for the training institutes, and the client receives a new approach that may or may not be more effective than the one it replaced.

Civilizational. The definition of mental health itself has expanded — from the absence of severe impairment to the presence of optimal functioning, from "not being sick" to "thriving" — and the expansion is philosophically admirable and commercially brilliant, because a standard of thriving is a standard that almost no one meets, which means almost everyone is a potential client.

10 · Strategic Ambiguity

Language engineered to mean whatever the listener needs it to mean.

Individual. The therapist says "we'll work at your pace" — a sentence that sounds like client-centered care and that also means the therapy will last as long as the therapist determines your pace requires, and the determination is made by the person whose income depends on the continuation.

Institutional. Terms like "evidence-based," "trauma-informed," and "holistic" have become so ubiquitous in the mental health industry that they have lost specific meaning — every practice claims them, few define them, and the ambiguity allows any approach to market itself as rigorous without demonstrating rigor.

Civilizational. "Mental health" itself has become strategically ambiguous — a phrase that encompasses everything from schizophrenia to feeling stressed before a presentation, from suicidal ideation to dissatisfaction with your job — and the ambiguity ensures that the term generates universal relevance without requiring the specificity that would reveal the vast difference between conditions that genuinely require clinical intervention and experiences that require a walk, a friend, a nap, or a different job.

11 · Projection

The system’s own behavior, pinned to your chest.

Individual. The therapist who has not done their own work may project their unresolved material onto the client — the therapist with attachment wounds may diagnose attachment disorders where they don't exist, the therapist with control needs may pathologize the client's autonomy, the therapist with unprocessed grief may redirect the session toward losses that echo their own.

Institutional. The mental health industry that has failed to address the mental health crisis projects the failure onto the population — "people aren't seeking help early enough," "there's still too much stigma," "the public doesn't understand mental health" — while the industry's own treatment model may be contributing to the crisis through over-diagnosis, over-medication, and the conversion of normal human struggle into treatable pathology.

Civilizational. A civilization that produces mental illness through its economic, social, and environmental conditions projects the cause onto the individual brain — "you have a chemical imbalance," "your brain isn't working properly," "this is a biological condition" — and the biological framing, while partially true for some conditions, also functions as a projection that protects the civilization from the acknowledgment that the conditions it creates are the primary cause of the suffering it then sells the treatment for.

12 · DARVO

Deny it happened, attack the one who named it, swap victim and offender.

Individual. The client who says "I don't think this therapy is helping" may be told they are "sabotaging their own progress" — the client who raised a legitimate concern about the treatment's effectiveness is reframed as the person who is undermining the treatment, and the therapist who failed to help effectively is protected by the clinical language that converts the client's feedback into the client's pathology.

Institutional. Therapists who critique the industry — who question the DSM's expansion, the pharmaceutical entanglement, the over-pathologization of normal experience, the chronic management model — are labeled as "anti-science," "against treatment," or "harmful to people who need help" — and the critics who are trying to improve the system are reframed as the people who are damaging it.

Civilizational. Communities that develop their own mental health practices — peer support networks, indigenous healing traditions, community-based crisis response — are told they are "practicing without a license," "putting people at risk," and "undermining evidence-based care" — and the communities that are trying to reclaim the commons of human care are characterized as threats to the professional monopoly that replaced the commons.

13 · Normalization

Harm raised in increments too small to trigger alarm, until it’s the background hum.

Individual. The first time you took a pill for your feelings it felt significant — a threshold crossed, a line between managing your own internal experience and outsourcing that management to a chemical — and by the fifth prescription the significance was gone, and the normalization of pharmaceutical intervention in the experience of being human is the mental health sector's most intimate desensitization.

Institutional. The expansion of the DSM has normalized the pathologization of ordinary experience — the child who daydreams has ADHD, the adult who worries has GAD, the person who grieves has PGD, the teenager who is moody has a mood disorder — and the normalization ensures that the population perceives its natural emotional range as a series of conditions that require professional management.

Civilizational. The normalization of being "in therapy" is the sector's defining achievement — the conversion of a clinical intervention into a lifestyle identity, the therapist as essential as the gym membership, the session as routine as the haircut — and the normalization is celebrated as destigmatization while also functioning as market expansion, because a population that normalizes therapy is a population that consumes therapy, and the consumption is the revenue.

Stage 5: Isolate

Elimination of comparison

14 · Isolation

Cutting the lines to everyone who could tell you this isn’t normal.

Individual. The therapeutic frame itself is an isolation mechanism — the confidentiality that prevents you from discussing what happens in session with the people in your life, the boundary that separates the therapeutic relationship from all other relationships, the fifty-minute container that holds the most intimate disclosure you've ever made and then closes until next week — and the isolation is called "the frame" and the frame is clinically justified and the frame also ensures that the therapist becomes the only person who holds the fullness of your story, and the singularity of the holding is the dependency.

Institutional. The specialization of mental health care isolates the client from integrated treatment — the therapist for the trauma, the psychiatrist for the medication, the primary care physician for the body, each one treating a dimension of the same person in isolation from the others, communicating through referral letters and progress notes, and the person who is a whole being is fragmented by a system that was designed around specialties rather than around the human.

Civilizational. The professionalization of emotional support has isolated the population from its own capacity for care — the friend who says "I'm not qualified to help with this, you should see a professional" is exercising appropriate caution and also performing the isolation that the professionalization produced, because a civilization that tells its members they are not equipped to support each other emotionally is a civilization that has privatized the commons of human connection and replaced it with a billable service.

15 · Triangulation

A third party inserted to carry the message the system wanted carried.

Individual. The therapist may unintentionally triangulate between the client and the client's relationships — "it sounds like your partner is exhibiting narcissistic traits," "your mother seems to have an anxious attachment style," "your boss appears to be gaslighting you" — and the clinical language, applied to people who are not in the room and cannot respond, creates a framework in which the client's perception is validated by professional authority while the other parties are diagnosed in absentia, and the triangulation positions the therapist as the only person who truly understands the client's experience.

Institutional. Different therapeutic modalities triangulate against each other — "CBT is evidence-based but doesn't address root causes," "psychodynamic therapy is deep but takes too long," "EMDR works for trauma but not for personality disorders" — and the triangulation keeps the client cycling between approaches, each one promising what the last one didn't deliver.

Civilizational. The mental health industry triangulates between the individual and the system — "your depression is a chemical imbalance" positions the problem inside the brain rather than inside the economy that produced the conditions that produced the depression — and the triangulation ensures that the individual seeks chemical intervention rather than systemic change.

Stage 6: Extract

Harvesting

16 · Flying Monkeys

Recruiting other people to enforce the system while it keeps clean hands.

Individual. Friends and family enforce the therapeutic frame — "have you talked to your therapist about this?" said in response to a person sharing their pain, redirecting the sharing away from the relationship and toward the professional, and the redirection is caring and the caring reinforces the system's monopoly on emotional support.

Institutional. Social media influencers function as the mental health industry's flying monkeys — "therapy is the best investment you'll ever make," "everyone should be in therapy," "you can't heal what you won't reveal" — each one a genuine expression of personal experience that also functions as marketing for the industry, and the marketing is more effective because it comes from a person rather than a brand.

Civilizational. The education system functions as the mental health industry's referral pipeline — the teacher who refers the disruptive child for assessment, the school counselor who recommends therapy, the school psychologist who conducts the evaluation that produces the diagnosis that produces the referral that produces the billing — and the referral is often appropriate and the pipeline is always flowing.

17 · Smear Campaign

Poisoning your reputation in advance, so your testimony arrives pre-discredited.

Individual. The client who leaves therapy is subtly pathologized — "they weren't ready for the work," "they're in avoidance," "premature termination" — clinical language that converts the client's autonomous decision to leave into a symptom of the condition the therapy was supposed to treat, which means leaving therapy is itself evidence that you need therapy.

Institutional. Critics of the mental health industry — those who question the DSM's expansion, the pharmaceutical influence, the chronic management model, or the over-pathologization of normal experience — are smeared as "anti-mental-health," "promoting stigma," or "denying people treatment" — and the smearing conflates criticism of the industry with opposition to helping people, which makes the criticism feel dangerous.

Civilizational. Alternative approaches to mental health — indigenous healing, somatic practices, community-based care, psychedelic therapy, spiritual practice — are smeared as "unproven," "dangerous," or "pseudoscience" — while the evidence base for many conventional treatments is itself remarkably thin, and the smearing protects the conventional industry's market share by making alternatives seem irresponsible.

18 · Silent Treatment

Silence weaponized — the refusal to engage, dressed as discipline.

Individual. The therapist who disagrees with the client's decision to reduce sessions, to stop medication, or to end treatment may withdraw warmth — not overtly, but subtly, the way any relational partner communicates disapproval through tone, pace, and attention — and the withdrawal activates the client's attachment system and produces the compliance the withdrawal was designed to generate.

Institutional. Requests for transparency about treatment outcomes, success rates, and long-term follow-up data are met with institutional opacity — "outcomes are complex," "we respect client confidentiality," "every case is unique" — and the opacity prevents the evaluation that would reveal whether the investment the population is making in professional mental health care is producing the results the investment was supposed to produce.

Civilizational. The silence around therapy's limitations is the sector's most consequential stonewalling — the studies that show modest effect sizes for many common interventions, the research that questions the chemical imbalance theory, the data that suggests the therapeutic relationship matters more than the technique, the evidence that community connection may be more therapeutic than professional treatment — all of it available in the literature and absent from the public conversation, because the conversation that includes these findings would be a conversation that challenges the industry's fundamental value proposition.

19 · Manufactured Consent

A vote whose outcome was decided before the question was asked.

Individual. You "consent" to the diagnosis by participating in the assessment — the questionnaire that asks how often you feel sad, the intake form that collects your history, the clinical interview that evaluates your symptoms — and the participation is recorded as consent for the label that follows, even though the client rarely understands that the label will follow them, that the label affects future treatment, insurance, and even custody proceedings, and that the label was assigned through a process the client was not fully informed about.

Institutional. The DSM's diagnostic categories manufacture the consent of the profession — the book is treated as authoritative, its categories as scientific, its expansion as progress — and the profession's acceptance of the categories is the consent, despite the documented influence of pharmaceutical funding on the committees that wrote them.

Civilizational. The public's consent for the medicalization of human emotion was manufactured through a decades-long marketing campaign — "depression is a chemical imbalance," repeated in advertisements, doctor's offices, and public health messaging until it became common knowledge — and the message was originated by pharmaceutical marketing departments, not by the scientific literature, and the literature has never fully supported the claim, and the claim manufactured the consent for the medication that the claim was designed to sell.

20 · Trauma Bonding

Pain and relief alternating until the source of the wound becomes the only cure.

Individual. The therapeutic relationship can become a trauma bond — the client who shares their deepest wounds with the therapist develops an attachment that is maintained not by the therapy's effectiveness but by the vulnerability of the disclosure, and the vulnerability creates a dependency that feels like connection, and the connection feels like healing, and the feeling is genuine and the bond is also the mechanism that keeps the client returning.

Institutional. Therapists are bonded to the profession through the shared experience of holding other people's pain — the secondary trauma, the compassion fatigue, the vicarious exposure to the worst of human experience — and the bond keeps the therapist inside a system that underpays and overworks them because the work itself has become their identity, and leaving the work feels like abandoning the people.

Civilizational. The population is bonded to the mental health industry through the cycle of crisis and relief — the suffering activates the seeking, the seeking produces the temporary relief, the relief fades, the suffering returns, the seeking resumes — and the cycle bonds the population to the industry the way any pain-relief cycle produces attachment: not through the quality of the relief but through the reliability of the cycle.

21 · Learned Helplessness

Punishing every attempt to act until the body stops trying and calls it peace.

Individual. The client who has been in therapy for years without resolution has learned that they cannot manage their own inner world — "I can't do this without my therapist," "I don't trust my own judgment," "I need to process this in session before I can act" — and the learned dependence on the professional replaces the client's own capacity for self-knowing, which was the capacity that was there before the therapy began and that the therapy was supposed to strengthen.

Institutional. The mental health profession has learned that it cannot address the systemic causes of mental illness — "we treat individuals, not systems," "we can't do anything about housing policy," "poverty is outside our scope" — and the scope limitation is the helplessness, because the profession that is closest to the suffering is the profession that has defined itself as unable to address the causes of the suffering.

Civilizational. "Mental health is a personal responsibility" is the civilizational learned helplessness that protects the system — the belief that the individual must manage their own mental health through personal choices (therapy, medication, self-care) while the civilization continues to produce the conditions (poverty, isolation, exploitation, environmental toxicity) that generate the mental illness — and the helplessness is installed so deep that the person who is sick because the system made them sick seeks treatment from the system rather than demanding the system change.

22 · Benevolent Control

Restriction, surveillance, and command — all framed as care you’d be ungrateful to refuse.

Individual. The therapist who says "I think you should consider medication" is exercising clinical judgment that may be entirely appropriate and that also transfers the management of the client's internal experience from the client to a chemical, and the transfer is the control, dressed in the language of care.

Institutional. Insurance companies control access to mental health care — determining how many sessions are covered, which diagnoses qualify, which modalities are approved, which therapists are in-network — and the control is framed as resource management while functioning as treatment management, because the entity that decides what care you receive is the entity that profits when you receive less care.

Civilizational. The mental health industry positions itself as the necessary guardian of the population's inner world — "you shouldn't try to process this alone," "you need professional support," "self-help is not enough" — and the positioning converts the human capacity for self-knowing, for communal care, for intuitive wisdom about one's own experience into insufficient practices that require professional supplementation, and the supplementation is the control.

23 · Infantilization

Keeping you perpetually ‘not ready’ so your own discernment is never trusted.

Individual. The therapeutic frame can infantilize the client — the expert who interprets your experience, the professional who holds the knowledge about your own inner world, the authority who determines what your dreams mean, what your anxiety signifies, what your depression is communicating — and the expertise is real and the expertise also positions the client as the person who does not understand their own experience, who needs the professional to decode what the body and mind are saying.

Institutional. The diagnostic system infantilizes the population — the classification of normal human experiences as disorders communicates to the population that they cannot trust their own emotional responses, that the sadness they feel requires a professional assessment to determine whether it is clinical sadness or acceptable sadness, and the need for professional adjudication of one's own feelings is the infantilization.

Civilizational. The mental health industry infantilizes the species — positioning the human being as a creature that cannot navigate its own emotional terrain without professional guidance, that cannot process its own grief without clinical support, that cannot manage its own anxiety without pharmaceutical assistance — and the infantilization is performed on a species that successfully navigated its emotional terrain for two hundred thousand years without a single DSM code.

24 · Identity Erosion

Replacing who you were with a system-approved version of who you should be.

Individual. The diagnosis becomes the identity — "I am bipolar," "I am ADHD," "I am borderline," "I am anxious" — and the label replaces the person the way every label in every sector replaces the person: the student becomes the GPA, the worker becomes the title, the patient becomes the diagnosis, and the human underneath the label disappears into the classification.

Institutional. The therapeutic identity replaces the communal identity — "I'm in therapy" becomes a defining characteristic, the therapeutic vocabulary becomes the language through which all experience is filtered ("I was triggered," "I need to set boundaries," "that's my attachment style"), and the vocabulary that was meant to illuminate the inner world becomes the lens through which the entire world is perceived, and the lens is the therapy's lens, not the person's.

Civilizational. The language of therapy has eroded the language of ordinary human experience — every preference is a "boundary," every reaction is a "trigger," every difficult person is a "narcissist," every rupture is "trauma," every discomfort is a "mental health issue" — and the erosion converts the complexity of human life into a clinical vocabulary that sounds like understanding and functions as a reduction, because the vocabulary was designed to describe pathology and is now being used to describe existence.

25 · Spiritual Bypassing

Money and debt repurposed as a leash that makes leaving unaffordable.

Individual. "I'm doing the work" is the therapeutic spiritual bypass — a sentence that sounds like accountability and that can function as a substitute for actual change, because the "work" is defined as the attending of sessions and the processing of material and the gaining of insight rather than the changing of behavior in the world outside the session, and the insight that doesn't change behavior is the bypass.

Institutional. "Mental health is health" is the industry's spiritual bypass — a true statement used to prevent the distinction between conditions that are genuinely medical and experiences that are genuinely human, because the equation of all mental suffering with medical illness depoliticizes the suffering and individualizes the cause.

Civilizational. "Self-care is radical" is the civilizational spiritual bypass that converts political impotence into personal practice — the person who cannot change the system that is making them sick is told that the sickness can be managed through individual practice (therapy, meditation, exercise, journaling), and the practice may genuinely help and the helping is the bypass, because the person who is managing their response to the system is the person who is not demanding that the system change.

26 · Financial Control

Spiritual or therapeutic language used to bypass your pain and dodge accountability.

Individual. The cost of therapy is a class barrier — $150 to $350 per session, weekly, for months or years — and the cost ensures that the population that is most in need of support (low-income, uninsured, marginalized) is the population least able to access it, and the population that can access it is the population whose suffering is often less severe but more funded.

Institutional.
