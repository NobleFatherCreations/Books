Family court, honestly

Does family court recognise coercive control?

The short answer

Family courts vary a lot in how well they recognise coercive control, as opposed to physical violence. Some have made real progress; others still push for contact with both parents and miss non-physical abuse. Get advice from a lawyer who knows coercive control, and present a pattern with dates.

Information, not legal, medical or therapeutic advice. If you are in danger, contact your local emergency services. Full notice.

Somebody has probably told you the judge is bound to see straight through them. Somebody else has told you family courts never believe victims. Both people meant well, and neither is reliably right. What helps is an honest picture, so you can plan for the court you’ll stand in.

Does family court see abuse that isn’t physical?

More than it used to, and less than it should. Many courts still focus mostly on physical violence, so coercive control without bruises can be missed. Some places have made real changes:

  • England and Wales: a major government review in 2020 found that family courts often minimised domestic abuse, put too much weight on contact with both parents, and didn’t hear victims well. Reforms followed, including the Domestic Abuse Act 2021.
  • Canada: the Divorce Act now lists coercive and controlling behaviour as family violence that courts must consider.
  • United States: several states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, have written coercive control into their family or protective order laws.
  • Australia: family law changed in 2023 to put children’s safety first.

Practice still varies by country, by court and by judge. The Decoder’s pages on Children as Leverage and Legal Abuse describe what courts often fail to see.

Neither “the court will understand” nor “the court never believes victims” is safe to assume. Find out how your court works.

Who will I meet?

Depending on where you are:

  • A judge or magistrates, who make the decisions.
  • A child’s representative: a guardian, a children’s lawyer or an independent children’s lawyer, who speaks for the child’s interests.
  • A family court adviser or evaluator, who interviews parents and children and writes a report. In England and Wales this is often Cafcass; in the US, a custody evaluator; in Australia, a family consultant.
  • A mediator, who tries to help parents agree.

Each of them is trying to work out what’s best for the children. A calm, clear account of the pattern, with the effect on the children, helps them most.

Should I go to mediation?

Not necessarily. Mediation assumes two people can negotiate fairly. Where there’s been control, that often isn’t true.

  • England and Wales: you usually have to attend a mediation meeting before applying to court, but domestic abuse is an exemption.
  • United States: many states let you opt out of mandatory mediation where there’s been domestic violence.
  • Australia: family dispute resolution is usually required, but there are exemptions for family violence.

If you do mediate, ask for separate rooms (“shuttle mediation”), and say you don’t feel safe negotiating face to face.

Say: “There’s a history of domestic abuse and I don’t feel able to negotiate safely in the same room. I’d like an exemption, or shuttle mediation.”

How do I present a pattern rather than incidents?

Courts hear a lot of competing claims. What stands out is a calm summary backed by records:

  • Start with the pattern in one or two sentences, numbers first. “Since we separated in May 2023, there have been 23 late or missed handovers, 140 messages outside the agreed app, and five applications, four of them dismissed.”
  • Then the effect on the children. Courts decide for children, so tie each point to them. “Our daughter now has nightmares before handovers.”
  • Then the evidence, organised by type.
  • Leave out labels. Describe behaviour, not diagnoses. See Is my ex a narcissist?.

Notice what that summary doesn’t do. It doesn’t call anyone a name or guess at motives. It gives a judge nothing to mistake for bitterness, because it’s only dates and counts. What to document and how shows how to build the timeline behind it.

What if they use the case to pressure me?

Expect messages that wave the court around. A calm reply on the record is worth more than a clever one.

They say: “Keep this up and I’ll tell the judge you’re keeping the kids from me.” What it’s doing: Using the court as a threat, and the children as the lever, so you back down before anyone neutral looks at it. See Legal / Administrative Threats. A reply you could send: “I want the children to spend time with you when it’s safe for them. I’m happy for the court to look at the arrangements. Please keep messages to the children’s plans.”

Every line of that reply is something you’d be glad for a judge to read. It supports their relationship with the children, welcomes the court, and sets one boundary.

What about “parental alienation”?

You may be accused of turning the children against the other parent, especially if a child is reluctant to see them. This is one of the most disputed areas in family law.

What’s agreed is that a parent can wrongly turn a child against the other parent, and that this harms children.

What’s disputed is how often it happens, how it should be judged, and whether alienation claims are being used to discredit parents who raise abuse. Research in the US found that when mothers alleged abuse and fathers then claimed alienation, courts often disbelieved the abuse.

If you’re accused:

  • stay calm and keep the focus on the children
  • show that you support the children’s relationship with the other parent wherever it’s safe
  • explain the child’s reluctance with facts, not your interpretation
  • get a lawyer who knows this area well

See also When your child is pulled into it and When they say you’re the abuser.

How often do narcissists win in court?

People ask this a lot. There’s no reliable figure, and calling someone a narcissist won’t help you in court. What’s true is that charming, confident people can do well in short hearings.

Calm, organised parents with clear records still do better than anxious ones with long lists of grievances. You can’t control how they come across. You can control how prepared you are.

How are decisions about children made?

Family courts decide based on the child’s welfare or “best interests.” The details differ by country, but courts usually consider:

  • the child’s needs, and their wishes depending on age and understanding
  • any harm the child has suffered or is at risk of
  • each parent’s ability to meet the child’s needs
  • the effect of any change

Domestic abuse is meant to be central to that assessment. In England and Wales, courts follow guidance called Practice Direction 12J when abuse is raised. In many US states, a finding of domestic violence affects custody decisions, sometimes with a presumption against custody for the abusive parent. Ask your lawyer or advocate how this works where you live.

Reports and evaluators

Before deciding, courts often ask a professional to report.

  • England and Wales: a Cafcass officer may write a “section 7” report after speaking to both parents and, often, the children.
  • United States: courts may appoint a custody evaluator or a guardian ad litem.
  • Elsewhere: family consultants or social workers play a similar role.

When you speak to them, be calm and factual, bring your record, and keep the focus on the children’s experience. Say clearly what you’re worried about and why.

Protections in court

You can ask for protections if you’re frightened of the other person:

  • separate waiting rooms and different arrival times
  • a screen in the courtroom, or giving evidence by video
  • in England and Wales, people accused of abuse can’t cross-examine the person they’re accused of abusing in person. The court appoints a lawyer to ask the questions instead.

Ask before the hearing. You don’t need to be sure you’ll need them.

Contact centres and supervised contact

If there are concerns about a parent’s behaviour, courts may order time with the children to be supervised, or to take place at a contact centre. In the UK, accredited centres are listed by the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search “supervised visitation center [your area].” Handovers through a centre can also keep parents apart.

If the decision goes against you

  • Ask your lawyer to explain the reasons, in writing if possible.
  • Appeals usually need permission and have short time limits, often 21 days in England and Wales. Get advice quickly.
  • If circumstances change later, like new incidents, you may be able to apply to change the order.
  • Look after yourself first. A bad decision can feel like being abused all over again. Call a helpline that night, not a lawyer at midnight.

What can I control?

  • How well documented the pattern is.
  • How calm and child-focused you are.
  • Whether you have a lawyer who understands coercive control. See Legal help with little or no money.
  • Whether you ask for protections at court. See Your day in court.
  • Whether you meet every deadline.

What can I share about the case?

Be careful before you post, publish or pass on anything from family court. The rules are strict, and breaking them can be serious.

  • England and Wales: cases about children are private. Publishing information from them, or sharing court documents with people outside a permitted list, can be contempt of court. You can usually share information with your lawyer, a McKenzie friend, a doctor or therapist, and a domestic abuse adviser, for advice and support. Posting about the case on social media, even without names, can break the rules. Identifying the children is never allowed.
  • United States: rules vary by state. Family court records are sometimes sealed, and judges can make orders limiting what parents say publicly. Ask your lawyer before you post anything.
  • Elsewhere: assume family court matters are private until a lawyer tells you otherwise.

You can still talk about your own experience of abuse in general terms with friends and support services. If you want to speak out publicly, get legal advice first.

Checking what’s driving your case

An honest check, just for you. This is about what’s in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.

A court case can turn a hard situation into a contest. Every so often, ask yourself:

  • Is what I’m asking for about my children’s safety and needs, or about winning?
  • Am I opposing something because it’s harmful, or because it came from them?
  • What would my children say they need, if I asked in a way that didn’t put them in the middle?
  • Could I explain each request to a fair stranger in one calm sentence?

If one of these stings, talk it through with your lawyer or advocate before the next step. Courts notice which parent keeps the children at the centre. So do children, later.

What helped in Carla’s case

This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.

Carla went into family court expecting not to be believed. She brought a two-page timeline with dates and references to evidence: police logs, school records, messages. She asked for a separate waiting room and a screen.

When the Cafcass officer interviewed her, she spoke about her daughter’s experience: the nightmares, the fear before handovers. She didn’t call her ex names. The court ordered contact at a supervised centre for six months, with a review. It wasn’t everything she hoped for. It was safe, and it was based on what had happened.

Write your two sentences

Open your record and write the pattern in two sentences at the very top: since when, what kind of thing, and how many times. Numbers, not adjectives. That’s the opening of every conversation you’ll have with the court.
