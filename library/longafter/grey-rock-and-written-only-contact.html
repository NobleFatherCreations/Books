Grey rock and written-only contact

What is the grey rock method, and does it work?

The short answer

The grey rock method means being as dull as a grey rock when you have to deal with an abusive ex: short, flat, factual replies that give them nothing to react to. It can work well in writing and at handovers. It can also make some people escalate, so use it with a safety plan.

Information, not legal, medical or therapeutic advice. If you are in danger, contact your local emergency services. Full notice.

You can’t go no contact. So you want the next best thing: a way to deal with them that gives them nothing to work with.

What is the grey rock method?

The grey rock method is a way of dealing with someone who feeds on your reactions by making yourself as dull as a grey rock. You keep every reply short, flat, polite and factual. You share no news, feelings or opinions, and you don’t argue or explain, so there’s nothing for them to grab hold of.

In practice:

  • short answers: “Yes.” “No.” “Noted.” “5pm Friday.”
  • a flat, polite tone
  • nothing personal: no news, opinions or feelings
  • no arguing, defending or explaining
  • no questions that open a conversation

You’re aiming for the conversational sparkle of a parking receipt. The idea is that when pressure gets no reaction, they get bored and move their attention elsewhere.

When should I not use grey rock?

Read this part first. Grey rock can make some people try harder to get a reaction. If the person has been violent, has threatened you, or escalates when ignored, grey rock alone isn’t a safety plan.

Don’t rely on grey rock:

  • in person with someone who may become violent
  • when you’re living together. See Still under the same roof.
  • when a sudden change in your behaviour could tip them off that you’re planning to leave
  • where the flatness could look like coldness to a court, without context

If any of these apply, plan with a domestic abuse service and use A safety plan for after you’ve left.

Does grey rock work?

There’s very little research on grey rock itself. It comes from survivors and the people who support them. Many people find it cuts conflict and protects their peace, especially alongside written-only contact. Some find the other person escalates at first, then gives up. Some find it doesn’t help at all. Watch what happens and adjust.

The Decoder’s page on Stonewalling describes refusing to engage as a control tactic. Grey rock works the other way round. You answer what’s needed and nothing more. It’s never a way to punish someone with silence.

How do I grey rock someone?

In writing, it looks like this:

They send: “I can’t believe you’d do this to the kids. You’ve always been selfish. Are you even going to answer about Saturday?”

Say: “Saturday pickup is 10am at school as agreed.”

They send: “I heard you’ve got a new boyfriend. Hope he knows what he’s getting.”

No reply needed.

At a handover:

They say: “So you’re just going to ignore me now?”

Say: “Here’s her bag. Her medicine’s in the front pocket. Bye.”

Yes, the brilliant comeback you rehearsed in the shower stays in the shower. It was very good. It’s still not going in the message.

They say: “Wow, you’ve turned into a robot. The kids must love having a parent with no feelings.” What it’s doing: Poking for a reaction, so your reaction becomes the story they tell. See Baiting. A reply you could send: “I’m happy to talk about the children’s arrangements. Pickup is 10am Saturday at school.”

What is written-only contact?

Written-only contact means all communication happens in writing: a co-parenting app, email or text. Phone calls and doorstep conversations are off the table.

Why it helps:

  • A record. Everything is dated and kept. See What to document and how.
  • Time. You can read, wait, and reply when you’re calm.
  • Less pressure. It’s harder to be talked round in writing.
  • Better behaviour. People often write more carefully when they know a judge could read it.

Courts often support written-only arrangements in high-conflict cases, especially through co-parenting apps. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.

How do I set it up?

Say: “From now on I’ll communicate in writing, by email or through the app. I’ll reply there to anything about the children. I won’t be discussing things by phone or at handovers.”

Then hold to it. If they call, let it go to voicemail and reply in writing if a reply is needed. More replies are in Words you can use.

If they tell anyone you’ve “cut them off,” your messages say otherwise: every question about the children answered, in writing, on time.

Five grey rock exchanges

They send: “You’ve ruined everything. The kids hate being with you.”

Reply: “Pickup is at 5 on Friday.”

They send: “Who was that man at your house?”

Reply: No reply needed. If there’s a question about the children in the message, answer only that.

They send: “I’ve been thinking about us. Can we talk properly?”

Reply: “I’ll keep our messages to the children’s arrangements.”

They send: “Fine, I’ll take them to my mum’s this weekend instead. You can’t stop me.”

Reply: “The order says they’re with me this weekend. Pickup is at 10 on Saturday.”

They send: “You never answer me. What kind of parent are you?”

Reply: “I answer messages about the children. Is there something about them you need?”

Each reply is short, polite and about the arrangement. There’s nothing in it to argue with.

Grey rock in person

At handovers and school events:

  • Keep your face neutral and your voice even.
  • Say less than feels natural: “Hi. Here’s the bag. Bye.”
  • Let comments about your looks, your new partner or your home slide past.
  • Stand where you can leave easily, and have somewhere to go afterwards.
  • If they start an argument, say: “I’m not going to discuss this here. Send me a message on the app and I’ll reply there.” Then leave.

Grey rock with the children watching

Children notice tension even when nothing is said, and grey rock can look cold to them. Soften it for their sake: warm with the children, neutral with the other parent.

Say (to your child, cheerfully): “Have a great weekend. I’ll see you Sunday.”

If your child asks why you don’t talk to their other parent, keep it simple: “Grown-ups sometimes talk less after they separate. We both love you.”

Is grey rock the same as ignoring them?

Grey rock means answering what needs answering, briefly, without emotion. You still answer messages about the children, meet deadlines and turn up to handovers. Courts expect parents to communicate about their children. Grey rock lets you do that without getting pulled in.

How do I keep grey rock from wearing me down?

Being a grey rock is tiring. You’re holding back everything you’d love to say. Say it somewhere else: to a friend, a therapist, a journal. It’s a tool for this one relationship. Everywhere else, you get to be your whole, unboring self.

The Sovereign Divine Feminine covers grey rock in The Sovereign Playbook, and the same “respond, don’t react” rule in post-separation control.

The questions to ask before you reply

Open your phone’s notes, make a note called “Before I reply” and type these three lines:

  • Does this message ask something about the children, safety or money with a deadline?
  • If yes, what’s the shortest polite answer?
  • If no, can I leave it unanswered?

Read it before every reply. It takes ten seconds and stops most arguments before they start.
