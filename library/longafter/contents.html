The Long After

I. The thing nobody warned you about

  • Leaving was the middleCulture treats it as the ending. For a lot of people, it’s closer to the midpoint.
  • Why it often gets worse firstWell documented, structurally explainable, and not evidence you did it wrong.
  • What you’re allowed to feel about someone you leftMissing them, relief, grief, tenderness — often at once. None of it means the leaving was a mistake.
  • The grief nobody will let you haveMourning what you hoped for is real grief, even when everyone else only sees the harm.
  • What this book is notNot therapy, not a timeline, not a verdict on when you’re “over it.”

II. Post-separation abuse

  • The shape it takes when they can’t reach you directlyRestricted contact doesn’t end coercion. It finds indirect channels, and they look different enough to be missed.
  • Children as the channelThe single most common and effective channel. Protecting them matters more than winning any exchange.
  • Money as the channelDelayed payments and manufactured disputes function as ongoing leverage.
  • The narrative campaignA sustained effort to control what others believe — and it works partly because you don’t fight it.
  • Your family and mutual friendsSome people get recruited into it, wittingly or not. That doesn’t mean they think you’re lying.
  • Surveillance and technologyShared accounts, old devices, tracking apps — a practical audit most people never think to run.
  • The new partnerA recognised pattern, not proof something is uniquely wrong with your situation.
  • HooveringThey come back, often exactly when you’ve become stable. It has a name, and naming it reduces its pull.
  • When it stopsFor some people it does, eventually. For others it takes years. Both are honest answers.

III. The systems

  • Systems abuseCourts and legal process can become the continuation, not the remedy. Naming it helps before it’s resolved.
  • What to document and howThe same instrument as everywhere in this library, and it matters more here because this unfolds across years.
  • Solicitors, legal aid, and no moneyFree and low-cost help exists specifically for this. Most people don’t know to look.
  • Family court, honestlyEnormous variance in whether coercive control is recognised, not just physical violence.
  • Police and protective ordersStill available, still sometimes necessary, with real and honestly stated limits.
  • Housing and benefitsThe practical floor specific to a post-separation situation, not the immediate-crisis version.
  • Where to find the right bodyWhich specific body, in your country, can actually compel an answer.

IV. The body and the mind

  • What your nervous system is doingHypervigilance and an exaggerated startle response don’t switch off just because the danger has technically ended.
  • SleepOne of the most common, least discussed effects. Knowing why reduces the fear that something else is wrong.
  • The anniversary effectDates, songs, places bring it back with force, for longer than expected. That’s documented, not failure.
  • MemoryPatchy and non-linear is expected. It doesn’t undermine what you do remember.
  • Anger, arriving lateOften the last feeling to surface, sometimes years later. Not a sign of instability.
  • Finding a therapist who understands thisNot all therapy is suited to this specifically. Knowing what to ask improves the odds.
  • When it’s more than aftermathSome of this can tip into something needing urgent help. Here is how to tell, and where to go.

V. The people

  • The friends who didn’t believe youCommon, painful, and understandable for ordinary human reasons that don’t excuse it.
  • The friends who did, and are tiredThey’re human too. Sustaining that relationship takes some awareness of what it costs them.
  • Telling someone newA right level and a right time. Both oversharing early and permanent silence carry real costs.
  • Your children, and what to sayAge-appropriate honesty that serves them — not a way of getting them to understand your side.
  • Your parentsThey may have been part of the pattern that made you vulnerable to begin with. This chapter holds that honestly.
  • Making new people from scratchA practical, learnable skill, not something that either happens naturally or doesn’t.

VI. Rebuilding

  • Money from zeroUnglamorous on purpose. Excitement about “starting over” does you a disservice.
  • Work againExplaining a gap or a reduced-capacity period is common and navigable.
  • Your own tastes, rediscoveredWhat you actually like, found again. Allowed to be light, even fun.
  • Trust as a practiceA graduated, evidence-based practice, not a switch — applied here to learning to trust anyone new.
  • Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re notNo universal timeline. Both rushing to prove you’re fine and waiting for a mythical readiness are real pressures.
  • The version of you from beforeSome of that person comes back. Some doesn’t. A third, new version is what’s actually available.
  • What “recovered” actually meansNot the absence of difficult feelings. The restoration of choice and capacity.

VII. The ledger

  • If you go backMany people who eventually leave for good go back at least once first. Common, not a moral failure.
  • What this book might have wrongPublished rather than buried.
  • Helping someone in year oneWritten to be forwarded. What actually helps, and what feels helpful but isn’t.
  • The long afterNot a straight line, and that’s fine. What’s actually available, years out.
