If you're not safe right now

Where do I go for domestic abuse help today?

The short answer

If you are in danger now, call your emergency number. For domestic abuse help that isn’t an emergency, a domestic abuse helpline can talk you through what to do next. They are free and confidential, and many are open all hours. You don’t need to be sure it’s serious enough to call.

If someone is hurting you, threatening you, or outside your door, call your emergency number now. Nothing in this book matters more than that call.

You do not need to be sure it’s “bad enough.” Call handlers would much rather hear from you early.

Emergency numbers

  • United States and Canada: 911
  • United Kingdom: 999. If you can’t speak, call 999 from a mobile, then press 55 when the recording asks. This tells the operator you need help. It doesn’t share your location, so say where you are if you can. It doesn’t work from a landline.
  • Australia: 000
  • Ireland: 112 or 999
  • Anywhere else: search “[your country] emergency number” now, and save it in your phone under a plain name.

Domestic abuse helplines

These lines are free and confidential. They can help you plan, find a refuge or shelter, and explain your options where you live.

  • United States: National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.
  • United Kingdom: National Domestic Abuse Helpline (England), 0808 2000 247. Scotland: 0800 027 1234. Wales: Live Fear Free, 0808 80 10 800. Men: Men’s Advice Line, 0808 801 0327. LGBT+ people: Galop, 0800 999 5428. Honour-based abuse and forced marriage: Karma Nirvana, 0800 5999 247.
  • Canada: find your nearest shelter at sheltersafe.ca, or call 211 to be connected to local services.
  • Australia: 1800RESPECT, 1800 737 732.
  • Ireland: Women’s Aid, 1800 341 900. Men’s Aid, 01 554 3811.

Anywhere else, search: “[your country] domestic abuse helpline.” The national charity will come up near the top.

If you’re thinking about harming yourself

Call a crisis line now. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans are on 116 123. In Australia, Lifeline is 13 11 14. Then read When it’s more than aftermath, which was written for this moment.

Leaving was the middle

Why is it still so hard after I left?

The short answer

After leaving an abusive relationship, many people find that the hardest part isn’t over. The control often moves to new channels, your body stays on alert, and the people around you expect relief. None of that means leaving was a mistake.

You left. In every story you’ve ever heard, that was the ending. The door closes, the music changes, and the healing starts.

For a lot of people, it doesn’t go like that. Leaving turns out to be the middle.

Why does it still feel this hard?

Three things usually happen at once after leaving an abusive relationship.

The control changes shape. The person you left loses most of their reach in a single day. Often they look for it somewhere else: through the children, through money, through what they tell your friends, through the courts. It looks different from what happened at home, so it can take a while to recognise it as the same thing. What post-separation abuse is names it, and The shape it takes when they can’t reach you directly shows how to spot it.

Your body hasn’t caught up. For months or years, staying alert kept you safe. Your nervous system learned that lesson well. It doesn’t unlearn it because you changed address. The jolt when your phone buzzes, the sleepless nights, the startle at a raised voice: What your nervous system is doing explains why these hang on.

The people around you expected a different story. They expected relief, maybe some sadness, then a steady climb to fine. When you miss the person, or fall apart months later, or go quiet, they don’t know what to do with it. Sometimes neither do you.

If leaving didn’t feel like the ending you were promised, you didn’t do it wrong. The promise was incomplete.

Was leaving a mistake, then?

No. When someone who relied on control loses it, things often get harder for a while. That difficulty says nothing about whether you should have stayed. For many people the first months are the roughest stretch, and Why it often gets worse first explains what drives that and how to stay safe through it.

Leaving was a real achievement, and it still wasn’t the end. Both of those are true.

  • People and rebuilding: friends, new people, money, work, home, trust, dating, and who you are now.

Anyone who has left, at any distance in time. A partner, a husband or wife, a girlfriend or boyfriend, a parent or a family that ran your life. What post-separation abuse is

What is post-separation abuse, and what does it look like?

The short answer

Post-separation abuse is when someone keeps trying to control, frighten or punish a former partner after the relationship ends. It often runs through children, money, the courts, technology and other people. It is a recognised form of domestic abuse, and in some places parts of it are crimes.

You left, and the control didn’t stop. It went somewhere else. Post-separation abuse is the name for that.

What is post-separation abuse?

Post-separation abuse is a pattern of behaviour by a former partner that keeps control over you after the relationship is over. The aim, conscious or not, is the same as before: to keep you reachable, unsettled and answering to them.

Researchers describe it as coercive control carried on by other means. When the person you left can no longer walk into your kitchen, they use the roads still open to them. The shape it takes when they can’t reach you directly maps those roads.

They may not want you back. They want you reachable.

What does post-separation abuse look like?

Here are the common forms. Each has its own chapter.

  • Through the children. Arguments at every handover. Questions to the children about your life. Running you down to them. Last-minute changes to the schedule. See Children as the channel and the Decoder’s page on children used as leverage.
  • Through money. Child support paid late or not at all. Debts left in your name. A house sale dragged out for years. See Money as the channel and financial control.
  • Through the courts. Filing after filing. Delays. Applications that drain your time and savings. This is often called litigation abuse or legal abuse. See Systems abuse and litigation abuse and the Decoder’s legal abuse.
  • Through the story. Telling friends, family, your employer or the school that you’re unstable, dangerous or lying. See The narrative campaign and smear campaigns.
  • Through other people. Messages passed through your sister. Mutual friends who start “checking in.” See Your family and mutual friends.
  • Through technology. Tracking apps, shared accounts, a smart doorbell they still control. See Surveillance and technology.
  • By turning up. Following, watching, appearing where you are. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.
  • By coming back. The warm message on your birthday, the apology, the emergency. See Hoovering.

Is post-separation abuse domestic violence?

Yes. Post-separation abuse is domestic abuse that continues after the relationship has ended. Most definitions of domestic abuse include former partners, and many include psychological, emotional and economic abuse as well as physical violence.

In England and Wales, for example, the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 defines domestic abuse to include controlling or coercive behaviour and economic abuse between people who are, or have been, partners.

Is post-separation abuse a crime?

Some of it is, depending on where you live.

Threats, assault, stalking, harassment, criminal damage and breaking a court order are crimes almost everywhere. A pattern of controlling behaviour is a crime in a growing number of places:

  • England and Wales: controlling or coercive behaviour became a crime in 2015. Since 2023 the law also covers former partners who no longer live together.
  • Scotland: the Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018 covers partners and ex-partners.
  • Ireland: coercive control became a crime under the Domestic Violence Act 2018.
  • New South Wales, Australia: coercive control in current and former relationships became a crime in July 2024.
  • United States: there is no federal coercive control crime. Several states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, now allow courts to consider coercive control in protective order or custody decisions.
  • Canada: the Divorce Act has included coercive and controlling behaviour in its definition of family violence since 2021.

Laws change. Search “[your country or state] coercive control law” for the current position, and see Police and reporting.

How is this different from a high-conflict divorce?

Plenty of separations are hard. People argue about money and children, say things they regret, and grieve. That is conflict, and it usually runs both ways and eases with time.

Post-separation abuse is one-directional and purposeful. One person keeps creating problems, and the problems keep landing at the moments that hurt you most. Calling it a “high-conflict divorce” can hide that, because it makes two people sound equally responsible. Abuse, or a hard breakup? gives a fair test.

How common is post-separation abuse?

There’s no single reliable figure. Studies use different definitions and groups of people. What research consistently finds is that separation is a time of raised risk, and that many people who experienced coercive control report it continuing after they left. Why it often gets worse first covers the risk.

Does post-separation abuse ever end?

For many people it fades or stops. For some it continues for years in a smaller form. When it stops is honest about the range and what tends to help.

  • Decide how much contact you’ll have, and keep it in writing: No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.

Why it often gets worse first

Is leaving an abusive relationship the most dangerous time?

The short answer

Leaving an abusive relationship can be the most dangerous time, especially with a very controlling partner. Losing control is what drives the escalation. So plan for safety now; staying doesn’t make you safer.

You expected things to calm down. Instead the messages doubled, the threats got sharper, or the court papers arrived. Many people find the first months after leaving are the hardest of all.

Read this as a reason to plan, and never as a reason to go back. If you’re in danger now, go to If you’re not safe right now.

Is leaving an abusive relationship the most dangerous time?

For many people, yes. Leaving an abusive relationship is linked with a higher risk of serious harm, especially in the weeks and months straight after, and especially when the partner was very controlling.

A large US study of women killed by partners found that separating from a highly controlling partner after living together was one of the strongest risk factors. Other studies of separated women report the same pattern: harassment and threats often rise around the time of leaving.

This doesn’t mean harm is likely for you. Most people who leave are not seriously hurt. It means this period deserves a plan.

Why does it get worse after leaving?

Control was the point of the relationship for the person you left. When you go, they lose most of it at once. Some people respond by trying harder to get it back. The more of their sense of themselves depended on controlling you, the harder they push.

You may notice:

  • more messages, calls and contact through other people
  • threats, sometimes about the children, money or your reputation
  • “emergencies” that need your attention right now
  • turning up at your work, home or the school gate
  • court applications that arrive in a cluster
  • a sudden switch to charm and apology, which is covered in Hoovering

The Decoder’s page on intimidation describes the threats and shows of force that often come with this.

When is the risk usually highest?

Risk tends to rise around moments when the person you left feels their control slipping further:

  • the first weeks and months after you leave
  • when they learn where you live
  • court dates and decisions about the children
  • when you start a new relationship, or they hear about one
  • anniversaries, birthdays and holidays
  • when you stop replying

Knowing these moments in advance lets you plan around them. A safety plan for after you’ve left covers how.

What signs mean I should get help quickly?

Some signs carry more weight than others. Threats to kill you or themselves, past strangling or choking, access to a weapon, stalking, and violence that’s getting worse all call for help from a domestic abuse service or the police now, not later. Warning signs to take seriously lists them and explains what to do.

Does this mean I left badly?

No. The escalation comes from their loss of control, not from anything you did wrong. There’s no way of leaving that guarantees a calm response from someone who relied on controlling you.

Knowing this can’t make it easier, but it can take away one weight: the idea that you caused it.

What helps right now?

  1. Make a plan. Use A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  2. Keep a record. Write down each incident the same day. What to document and how shows a four-line format.
  3. Talk to a domestic abuse service. They know the local options, from refuges to protective orders. Find help has numbers.
  4. Narrow the contact. Fewer channels means fewer openings. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.

The shape it takes when they can't reach you directly

Why is my ex still controlling me after the breakup?

The short answer

Post-separation control is what happens when one way of reaching you closes and the person you left finds another. It can look very different from what happened at home, which is why it’s easy to miss. The test is what it does, not what it looks like.

You blocked the number. The messages stopped. Then your mother called to say they’d been round “just to talk.” Then the school forwarded an email. Then a letter arrived from a solicitor.

One door closed. Others opened.

Why is my ex still controlling me?

Control in a coercive relationship was never about one method. It was about keeping influence over you. When the obvious route closes, the need behind it doesn’t go away. It finds the next route.

This isn’t always a cold plan. Sometimes it is. Often it’s a habit, a sense of being owed, or a refusal to accept that you get to decide. Either way, the effect on you is the same.

“Just go no contact” is sometimes complete protection. Sometimes it closes one door while several others stay open.

Why is it so easy to miss?

Because it looks nothing like the old arguments. A custody application doesn’t feel like being shouted at. A late payment doesn’t feel like being watched. Even professionals meant to help can treat each incident as a separate, ordinary dispute.

The pattern only shows when you line the incidents up. That’s one reason a record matters so much: What to document and how.

How can I tell control from ordinary friction?

Ask one question of each incident: does it work to keep control over me, or to unsettle me, whatever it looks like?

A late payment during a real job loss is a problem. A late payment that always lands the week of your exams, every time you set a boundary, is a message.

Not every hard moment after a separation is abuse. Separations involve real disagreements. Abuse, or a hard breakup? helps you sort one from the other honestly, which also helps you be believed.

What do I do once I see the pattern?

  • Name it, privately. Seeing the shape takes some of its power away.
  • Close the channels you can. Some you can shut completely; others, like contact about children, you can narrow to writing. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • Write it down. Dates and facts, kept somewhere safe.
  • Respond less. Each reply teaches them which channel still works. See Messages you don’t have to answer.

Is my ex a narcissist?

What does a narcissist do after a breakup, and does the label matter?

The short answer

People search “narcissist after breakup” because the word names a pattern they lived through. Only a clinician can diagnose narcissistic personality disorder, and you don’t need a diagnosis to protect yourself. What matters is the behaviour, and the behaviour after a breakup is predictable.

If you’ve typed “narcissist” into a search bar at 3am, you’re in very large company. The word has become the shorthand for a certain kind of person: charming, then cruel; sorry, then cruel again; never wrong.

It’s a useful word. It’s also a limited one.

Is my ex a narcissist?

Maybe. Maybe not. Narcissistic personality disorder is a diagnosis that a qualified clinician makes after assessing someone. Most people who behave badly after a breakup will never be assessed, and many controlling people wouldn’t meet the full criteria.

What you can know is what they did, how often, and what it did to you. That is enough.

You don’t need to know what’s wrong with them to protect yourself from what they do.

Why does the word help so much?

Because it names something. Before you had the word, you may have thought each awful moment was a one-off, or your fault. The word told you it was a pattern, that other people have seen it, and that it has a shape. That recognition matters.

What are the limits of the label?

  • Courts and services work with behaviour, not labels. “He’s a narcissist” will get you less traction than “he has sent 140 messages since the order, including 12 threats.” See What to document and how.
  • It can make the person sound more powerful than they are. Online content about narcissists can make them seem all-knowing. They aren’t.
  • It can keep your attention on them. Months of reading about narcissism can become another way your life revolves around them.

What does a narcissist do after a breakup?

Whatever the diagnosis, the patterns people describe after a breakup with a controlling partner are consistent. Here they are in plain language, with where to read more.

They go silent, or they block you. The silent treatment is a punishment and a test: will you chase? See Messages you don’t have to answer.

They tell a story about you. You’re unstable, you cheated, you’re keeping the children from them. See The narrative campaign and the Decoder’s page on smear campaigns.

They turn it around. You’re the abuser, you’re the one who hurt them. This is often called DARVO: deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. See the Decoder’s DARVO and When they say you’re the abuser.

They move on fast. A new partner appears within weeks, sometimes days. It can feel like proof you were nothing. It usually means they needed someone, and you weren’t available any more. See If they left you.

They come back. The warm message, the apology, the emergency, usually when you’ve just started to feel steady. See Hoovering and the Decoder’s hoovering.

Will a narcissist come back after a breakup?

Often, yes. A return after silence is so common it has a name. The timing tends to follow your recovery, not their growth. Hoovering covers the forms it takes and how to answer, or not.

Why did they block me?

Blocking can be a way of having the last word, of punishing you, or of making you chase. It can also simply mean they’ve moved their attention. Whatever the reason, a block does one useful thing: it closes a channel. You don’t have to reopen it.

Can a narcissist change?

People can change, but deep change in long-standing patterns is slow and rare. It shows up as consistent behaviour over a long time, not as promises. If you’re wondering whether someone has changed, look for accountability without pressure on you. Trust as a practice offers a careful way to judge.

So what should I call it?

Call it what helps you. In court, with a lawyer or with a domestic abuse service, describe behaviour. In your own head, use the word that makes it make sense. The Weighing, another book in this library, covers how to judge people fairly when you’re not sure.

Abuse, or a hard breakup?

How do I tell a normal breakup from a narcissist breakup, or from abuse?

The short answer

The difference between a normal breakup and a narcissist breakup, or any abusive one, is pattern and purpose. Ordinary conflict runs both ways and eases with time. Abuse runs one way, keeps landing where it hurts most, and serves to keep control.

Every separation hurts. People argue over money and children, say things they’d take back, and grieve. So how do you know when it’s more than that?

This chapter gives you a fair test. Being fair matters for your own peace of mind, and it matters when you need other people, including a court, to believe you.

What does a normal breakup look like?

Normal breakups can be ugly. They often include:

  • arguments about money, the house, the children and who gets what
  • anger, blame and bad days on both sides
  • one person grieving more, or longer, than the other
  • slow, clumsy agreements that eventually stick
  • contact that gets less heated over months

What marks an ordinary breakup is that the conflict goes both ways and runs down. Both people are hurt. Both, at times, behave badly. Over time it settles.

What does a narcissist breakup, or an abusive one, look like?

Whatever word you use for the person, an abusive breakup has a different shape:

  • One direction. The problems keep coming from one side.
  • Timing. Trouble arrives when you’re doing well, when you set a limit, or when you won’t answer.
  • Purpose. Each incident leaves you more drained, more afraid or more tied to them.
  • No settling. Agreements get broken or reopened. Calm stretches end the moment you relax.
  • Fear. You plan your life around their reactions.

Ordinary conflict is about the issue. Control is about you.

The three-question test

For any incident, ask:

  1. Pattern: has this, or something like it, happened before? How often?
  2. Purpose: what did it achieve for them? Contact, control, punishment, an audience?
  3. Effect: what did it do to you? Did it change what you did, where you went, what you said?

One late payment is a late payment. Late payments that always arrive in the week of your job interview, after every boundary you set, for two years, are a pattern with a purpose. Write the answers down. Over time, the record will show you which you’re dealing with. See What to document and how.

What if it’s both?

Often it is. A real disagreement about schooling can sit alongside a pattern of control. You can take the disagreement seriously and still name the control.

Be careful of a word that gets used a lot here: “high-conflict.” It makes two people sound equally responsible. Where one person is driving it, the Decoder calls that framing a false equivalence. You can say plainly: “There are real disagreements, and there is also a pattern of control coming from one side.”

Am I the abuser?

Many people who were controlled ask this. Years of being told that everything was your fault can leave you checking yourself for blame. The Decoder describes how that self-doubt gets planted in self-gaslighting induction.

Some honest questions:

  • Were you afraid of them, or were they afraid of you?
  • Did you change your life to avoid their reactions, or did they change theirs to avoid yours?
  • When you shouted or snapped, was it after months of provocation? (This is sometimes called reactive abuse. See When they say you’re the abuser.)
  • Do you want to control what they do now, or do you want them to leave you alone?

If you read those and feel relief, that tells you something. If you read them and recognise yourself in the other side, that’s worth facing too. The Weighing has a careful chapter, Am I the one doing it?, and The Repair is written for people who want to stop harming someone.

Why does getting this right matter?

Because it protects you. Professionals see a lot of separations where both people claim to be the victim. A calm account of a pattern, with dates and details, stands out from a list of grievances. It also keeps your own thinking clear on the days when they’re charming and you start to doubt yourself.

  • If it’s mostly ordinary conflict: No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid still helps you keep it calm.
  • If you’re unsure: start a record and look again in a month.

If your story doesn't look like the usual one

Does post-separation abuse happen to men, LGBTQ+ people, and people leaving their families?

The short answer

Post-separation abuse against men is real, and so is abuse after same-sex relationships, abuse by parents and families, and abuse that uses immigration status or disability. Most of this book applies to you. This chapter covers what’s different and where to find help made for your situation.

A lot of writing about leaving abuse assumes one story: a woman leaving a man she lived with. That story is common. It isn’t the only one.

If the examples in this book don’t look like your life, the patterns probably still do. This chapter covers what’s different.

Does post-separation abuse happen to men?

Men are abused by partners too, by women and by men. In England and Wales, official estimates suggest around one in three adults who experience domestic abuse in a year are men.

Men often meet extra barriers after leaving:

  • being disbelieved, or laughed at
  • being treated as the likely aggressor by default
  • fewer services, and fewer refuges that take men
  • a story from the ex that he’s the dangerous one, which people find easy to believe

The patterns in this book apply in full. The Silence, another book in this library, is written for men and covers recognition and help in depth. In the UK, the Men’s Advice Line is on 0808 801 0327. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline serves everyone.

What about after a same-sex or queer relationship?

Rates of abuse in LGBTQ+ relationships are similar to, or higher than, rates in straight relationships. Some tactics are particular to these relationships:

  • threatening to out you to family, work or your community
  • using a small community against you, where everyone knows both of you
  • telling services it was “mutual,” and services that expect a man and a woman and miss what happened
  • for trans people, misgendering, attacks on transition, or threats about documents and healthcare

The Decoder’s page on identity-based isolation covers tactics that use who you are against you. LGBTQ+ domestic abuse services exist in many places; in the UK, Galop runs a helpline on 0800 999 5428. Elsewhere, search “LGBTQ domestic abuse support [your area].”

Leaving a controlling parent or family

Many people reading this left a family, not a partner. A parent who controlled money, friends, faith or marriage. Siblings who report back.

The patterns are close cousins: guilt, contact through relatives, a story about you told at every gathering, and returns dressed as “we just miss you.” Read Your family and mutual friends and Your parents.

Honour-based abuse and forced marriage

If your family or community controls you in the name of honour, or is pressuring you to marry, the danger after leaving can come from several people at once. Specialist services understand this. In the UK, Karma Nirvana runs a helpline on 0800 5999 247, and the Forced Marriage Unit gives advice, including for people at risk of being taken abroad. Elsewhere, search “forced marriage help [your country].”

When immigration status is used against you

“Leave and you’ll be deported” is a common threat. It is often untrue, and many countries have protections for people whose status depended on an abusive partner:

  • United States: the Violence Against Women Act lets some survivors apply for status without the abuser’s help (a self-petition), and there are U visas for victims of certain crimes.
  • United Kingdom: the Migrant Victims of Domestic Abuse Concession gives some people temporary access to benefits while they apply to stay.

Get advice from an immigration lawyer or a domestic abuse service before acting. The Decoder’s page on status threats describes how this pressure works.

Abuse in teenage relationships is real abuse, and you deserve help made for you. In the US, love is respect offers support by phone (1-866-331-9474) or text (LOVEIS to 22522). In the UK, Childline is on 0800 1111. Elsewhere, search “teen dating abuse help [your country].” If you’re in danger now, see If you’re not safe right now.

The first 30 days

What do I do in the first month after leaving an abusive relationship?

The short answer

This is an after leaving an abusive relationship checklist for the first month: safety first, then your phone and accounts, documents, money, children, and your record. Do what you can. Nothing on this list has to be done in a single day.

The first weeks after leaving are a blur. This after leaving an abusive relationship checklist is for that blur. You’re tired, you may be in a new place, and everything feels urgent. This list puts things in a sensible order. Tick off what applies. Skip what doesn’t. Come back to it.

Keep the list somewhere the person you left can’t see it.

What do I do in the first three days?

  • Save emergency and helpline numbers under plain names. See If you’re not safe right now.
  • Tell one or two trusted people where you are and agree a code word that means “call for help.”
  • Make a basic safety plan: routes, routines, who collects the children. A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  • If there’s any risk of violence, talk to a domestic abuse service or the police about a protective order. Protective orders.

Days 1 to 7: your phone and accounts

Start with your email. It’s the key that resets everything else.

  • Change your email password. Turn on two-step login. Store the backup codes on paper.
  • Then change passwords for banking, social media, cloud storage, shopping and streaming.
  • Check which devices are logged in to each account, and log out the ones you don’t recognise.
  • Turn off location sharing with them on your phone, in apps and on social media.
  • Check for tracking: Surveillance and technology.

One warning: sudden changes can alert someone who is watching. If you’re worried about how they’ll react, plan the order with a domestic abuse service. The Decoder’s page on account control explains how shared accounts become a lead.

Days 1 to 14: documents

Gather originals, or photograph them if you can’t take them yet:

  • passports, ID, birth certificates (yours and the children’s)
  • marriage, divorce, custody or protective orders
  • bank statements, pay slips, tax records, pension details
  • tenancy or mortgage papers, car documents
  • medical records and prescriptions
  • any evidence of abuse you already have

Keep one copy somewhere safe away from home and one encrypted copy online in an account only you can open.

Days 1 to 30: money

  • Open a bank account in your name only, at a bank you didn’t share.
  • Move your pay to it, and change any direct debits that matter.
  • Get your credit report (free in most countries) and look for accounts you didn’t open. See Money as the channel.
  • List what you owe and what’s in joint names. Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork covers the longer job.
  • Ask about emergency funds and benefits. See Housing and benefits.

Days 1 to 30: children

  • Tell the school or nursery who may and may not collect your children. Give them a copy of any court order.
  • Decide how contact with the other parent will happen, ideally in writing. See Co-parenting or parallel parenting?.
  • Give the children simple, honest reassurance. See Your children, and what to say.

Days 1 to 30: start your record

Start a record now, even if nothing has happened yet. Date, what happened, who saw it, what it changed. The record has a template. A month of plain notes is worth more later than a long account written from memory.

Days 7 to 30: people and paperwork

  • Change your address with your doctor, bank and employer. If your address must stay hidden, see Your address, your records, your mail.
  • Update emergency contacts at work, at school and on medical records.
  • Check who is named as beneficiary on any life insurance or pension.
  • Ask your employer about leave or safety support. See Work again.

And look after yourself

Eat something. Sleep when you can. Expect your body to feel wired and wrecked at once; What your nervous system is doing explains why. If you find yourself in crisis, go to When it’s more than aftermath.

If they left you

What happens after a narcissist breaks up with you?

The short answer

After a narcissist breaks up with you, or any controlling partner does, it can feel like being thrown away. The fast replacement, the cold silence and the sudden return are common patterns. Being left doesn’t mean you deserved it, and it doesn’t mean it’s over.

You didn’t leave. They did. Maybe it was sudden. Maybe there was someone new within days. Maybe they told everyone it was your fault.

Most writing about abuse assumes you were the one who walked out. If you were the one left, the pain has a different shape, and this chapter is about that shape.

Why does being left hurt this much?

Because control often ends with a discard. After months or years of being told you were the problem, being dropped can feel like the final verdict. It isn’t. It’s often the last move in a pattern you were inside.

The Decoder’s page on blame and discard describes it: you’re made responsible for everything, then dropped for it.

Being left by someone who hurt you is not proof that you were the problem.

What happens after a narcissist breaks up with you?

The patterns people describe are consistent, whether or not the person would ever be diagnosed with anything.

They replace you fast. A new partner appears almost at once. Often the new relationship looks perfect online. This usually says more about their need for an audience than about you. The Decoder calls the comparison game triangulation.

They go cold. You might be blocked, ignored or spoken to like a stranger. The silence can feel unbearable, especially if they used to be your whole world.

They rewrite the story. You were unstable, you pushed them away, you were the abusive one. See The narrative campaign.

They come back. Weeks or months later: a message, a “how are you,” an apology, a crisis. Often just as you’ve started to feel better. See Hoovering.

Why did my ex move on so fast?

A very fast move often means the next person was already lined up, or that they can’t stand being alone. It rarely means you were easy to replace. A new relationship built that quickly is usually built on the same patterns, and the shine tends to wear off.

It’s very hard not to compare. Try not to watch. Checking their profile is about why that urge is so strong and how to ease it.

Is it still abuse if they ended it?

Yes, if the pattern continues. Many people who were left find the control keeps going: through the children, money, shared friends, or by turning up again when it suits them. Everything in What post-separation abuse is applies.

What about the grief?

Being left brings grief, sometimes mixed with relief you’re ashamed of, sometimes mixed with longing you’re ashamed of too. You may grieve the person, the future you planned, the version of them you loved. All of that is allowed. Read The grief nobody will let you have and The trauma bond, which explains why you can miss someone who treated you badly.

What do I do if they come back?

Decide now, while you’re steady, what you’ll do. Most people find that not replying, or one short closing reply, works better than a long conversation. Hoovering has wording for both. If you’ve already replied, you haven’t failed: the same chapter covers that too.

What helps right now?

  • Stop the feed. Mute or block their accounts and the new partner’s.
  • Tell one person the real story. Being left often comes with being misrepresented. One person who knows the truth is a lifeline.
  • Keep a record if anything continues after the break: messages, contact through others, problems with shared things. See What to document and how.
  • Read Leaving was the middle. It’s written for people who left, but almost all of it applies to being left.

Still under the same roof

How do I cope with being separated but living together?

The short answer

Being separated but living together with someone controlling is one of the hardest places to be. You can’t avoid them, and the risk can rise once they know you’re leaving. Plan quietly, keep a record, protect your phone and money, and get advice before you announce anything.

Being separated but living together is a common trap. You’ve decided it’s over, or they have. But the lease runs to spring, or the house hasn’t sold, or you can’t afford to move yet. So you share a kitchen with someone you’re trying to get away from.

If you’re in danger in the home now, go to If you’re not safe right now.

Is it safe to stay while separated?

For some people, staying for a while is manageable. For others it’s dangerous. The weeks around a separation are a time of raised risk, especially if the person has been violent, threatened you, or controlled you closely. Why it often gets worse first and Warning signs to take seriously can help you judge.

If any warning sign applies, talk to a domestic abuse service before you tell the person you’re leaving. In many places a court can order an abusive partner out of a shared home; ask about an occupation order, exclusion order or protective order.

How do I cope day to day?

  • Keep plans quiet. Don’t announce dates or details until you’re ready and safe.
  • Keep conversations short and practical. Bills, children, logistics. Avoid debates about the relationship.
  • Move important talks to writing. Text or email gives you a record and keeps things calmer. See Grey rock and written-only contact.
  • Have a room with a lock, if you can, and a charged phone in it.
  • Keep a go bag at a friend’s house or work: documents, cash, medicine, chargers.
  • Expect pressure. Controlling people often tighten their grip when they sense you leaving: checking where you go, keeping track of your time, limiting the car or money. The Decoder describes these in time control and mobility control.

What should I protect first?

Your phone. Assume a shared home means shared access. Change passwords on a device they haven’t touched, and check for tracking. See Surveillance and technology.

Your money. Open an account in your name, at a different bank, with statements sent somewhere safe. Move what you can of your own income.

Your documents. Copy or remove passports, birth certificates and financial records now. Things go missing during separations.

Your record. Write down incidents the same day and store the notes away from the house. See What to document and how.

What about the children?

Keep their routines steady. Don’t argue in front of them, and don’t ask them to take sides or keep secrets. If they ask what’s happening, give them a short, true answer: “Mum and Dad are going to live in different houses. You haven’t done anything wrong.” Your children, and what to say has more.

If you plan to move with the children, get legal advice first. Moving children without agreement can count against you in some places. See Moving, passports and travel with children.

What if it’s my home, or our home?

The rules on who can stay, and how a shared home is sold or transferred, depend on whose name is on it and where you live. Get legal advice before you leave a home you own or rent jointly, because leaving can affect your claim in some places. Legal help with little or no money lists free and low-cost options.

When it’s time to go

Leave at a time they don’t expect, with your documents, money, phone and medicine. If you need help removing belongings safely, many police services will attend while you collect them; ask locally. Then start The first 30 days.

A safety plan for after you've left

How do I stay safe after leaving an abusive relationship?

The short answer

A safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship covers your home, your routines, work, school, handovers, your phone and car, and who to call. It is most useful in the first months and around court dates. Write it on paper and keep it where they can’t find it.

Most safety plans are written for the day you leave. A safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship is different. This one is for the months after, when you’re living your life and the person you left knows more about that life than you’d like.

If you’re in danger right now, go to If you’re not safe right now first.

Keep this plan somewhere they can’t reach: in your head, with a friend, or on paper hidden away from home. Don’t leave it on a shared device.

Why do I need a safety plan after leaving?

Because separation is a time of raised risk, and because the person you left may know your routines, your workplace, your children’s school and your friends. Why it often gets worse first explains the risk. A plan doesn’t mean you expect the worst. It means you’ve thought about it once, calmly, so you don’t have to think about it in a panic.

The safety plan worksheet puts all of this on one page.

Home

  • Change the locks if they ever had a key. Ask your landlord, or a domestic abuse service, which may help with the cost.
  • Add a door chain, a peephole or a video doorbell that only you control.
  • Light the outside of your home at night.
  • Keep your phone charged and near you at night.
  • Plan an exit from each room you spend time in, and a place to go.
  • Tell a neighbour you trust what the person and their car look like, and ask them to call the police if they see them.
  • If they turn up, don’t open the door. Call the police if they won’t leave or if you feel threatened, and write it down afterwards.
  • When the practical side is done, A home that feels like yours covers making it feel safe.

Routines and routes

People who want to find you watch for patterns.

  • Vary when you leave home and which way you go, especially for the first months.
  • Change your gym, supermarket or coffee shop if they knew you went there.
  • Park in well-lit places near entrances.
  • Know where the nearest police station and busy public places are on your usual routes.

Work

  • Tell someone you trust at work: a manager, HR or security.
  • Give reception or security a photo of the person and ask them not to let them in or confirm you’re there.
  • Ask to have your calls screened and your name removed from public staff lists.
  • Ask about changing your hours or work location for a while.
  • In many places employers must take reasonable steps to keep staff safe, and some countries give paid leave for domestic abuse. See Work again.

School and childcare

  • Tell the school, nursery or childcare who may collect your children and who may not.
  • Give them a copy of any court order, with a photo of the person if collection is restricted.
  • Agree a password that anyone collecting your child must give.
  • Ask the school not to give out your new address or phone number.

Handovers

If you have to hand children over, plan each exchange: a public place with cameras, a school handover, a trusted third person, or a supervised contact centre. Don’t hand over at your home if you can avoid it. Handovers covers this in detail.

Your phone, accounts and car

  • Change passwords from a device they never touched, starting with your email.
  • Turn off location sharing, and check for tracking apps and trackers. Surveillance and technology has the steps.
  • Check your car for trackers, especially if they had access to it.

People

  • Choose two people you trust and tell them the plan.
  • Agree a code word or emoji that means “call the police” or “come now.”
  • Ask one person to check on you on court days and other high-risk days.

What to keep ready

Some people keep a small bag at a friend’s house or at work, even after they’ve left:

  • copies of documents and court orders
  • some cash and a bank card
  • medicines and spare glasses
  • phone charger, spare keys
  • a written list of important numbers, in case your phone is taken

When is the risk usually higher?

Plan ahead for:

  • court hearings and decisions
  • your new relationship becoming known
  • birthdays, anniversaries and holidays
  • the day they’re served with papers
  • the day you stop replying
  • any time they’ve just been refused something

On those days, tell someone where you’ll be, avoid being alone at places they know, and keep your phone charged.

If they break a court order

Write down what happened, when and where. Keep messages. Report the breach to the police. A pattern of recorded breaches is what usually gets a stronger response. See Protective orders.

If anything on this list feels dangerous to do

Some steps, like changing every password at once, can alert someone who is watching. If you’re worried about how they’ll react, plan the order with a domestic abuse service. Warning signs to take seriously helps you judge how careful to be.

Warning signs to take seriously

What are the domestic violence risk factors I shouldn’t ignore?

The short answer

Some domestic violence risk factors are linked to serious harm: past strangling or choking, threats to kill, access to weapons, stalking, violence that’s getting worse, and separation itself. If any apply to you, talk to a domestic abuse service or the police now. If none apply, that doesn’t prove you’re safe.

This chapter is short and plain on purpose. It lists the signs that services treat as most serious. It isn’t a score, and it can’t tell you you’re safe. Only you, with help, can judge that.

If you’re in danger now, call your emergency number. If you’re not safe right now has the numbers.

Which warning signs matter most?

Research on serious and fatal domestic violence has found these risk factors again and again:

  • Past strangling or choking. Even once. Even if it left no mark.
  • Threats to kill you, the children, or themselves.
  • Access to a gun or other weapon, or threats with one.
  • Violence that is getting more frequent or more severe.
  • Stalking: following, watching, turning up, tracking.
  • Extreme jealousy or control, and saying things like “if I can’t have you, no one can.”
  • Separation, especially recent, especially from someone very controlling.
  • Violence during pregnancy. See Pregnant, or with a new baby.
  • Forced sex.
  • Threats of suicide used to control you. The Decoder describes this in emotional blackmail through self-harm threats.
  • Unemployment, heavy drinking or drug use alongside the above.
  • Breaking court orders.
  • Threats to hurt or kill a pet. See Pets and the things left behind.

The Decoder’s page on intimidation describes the threats and shows of force that often sit around these signs.

Why does strangulation matter so much?

Because it is one of the strongest warning signs there is. A study of women attacked by partners found that a past attempt to strangle them was linked to a many-times-higher risk of later attempted or completed murder.

Strangling can also cause injuries you can’t see: brain injury from lack of oxygen, damage to the throat and neck, strokes days or weeks later. If you were ever strangled or choked, even long ago, tell a doctor. Signs to mention include a changed voice, trouble swallowing, headaches, memory problems or dizziness. It is also evidence: tell the police or your lawyer that it happened. Getting sick after leaving covers what to tell a doctor.

What should I do if some of these apply?

  1. Talk to a domestic abuse service today. They use risk checklists like these every day and can help you plan. Find help.
  2. Tell the police, especially about threats, weapons or strangling.
  3. Ask about a protective order. See Protective orders.
  4. Make or update your safety plan. See A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  5. Tell someone you trust what’s happening, so you’re not holding it alone.

When you call, use plain words that get attention: “He strangled me last year.” “She has said she’ll kill me.” “He has a gun.” “He’s been following me.” Professionals are trained to act on those sentences.

What if none of these apply?

That’s good news, but it isn’t a guarantee. These lists come from patterns across many cases. They can’t see your situation the way you can. If you’re frightened, trust that. Fear is information.

What if they’re threatening to harm themselves?

Threats of suicide can be real distress, a way of controlling you, or both. You are not responsible for keeping them alive, and you don’t have to go back to prevent it. If the threat sounds immediate, call emergency services and tell them. Then step back. If thoughts of harming yourself are coming up for you too, go to When it’s more than aftermath.

Surveillance and technology

Is my ex tracking me, and how do I check?

The short answer

If you’re asking “is my ex tracking me,” check four places: shared accounts, location sharing, hidden apps on your phone, and trackers on your car or belongings. Do it from a device they never touched, and in a calm order, because sudden changes can alert someone who is watching.

They seem to know where you’ve been. They mention a conversation you had with a friend. They turn up at the café you go to on Saturdays. You start to wonder if you’re imagining it.

You may not be. Tracking after a separation is common, and it’s usually done with ordinary tools: shared accounts, location sharing, and cheap trackers.

Is my ex tracking me?

Signs that someone may be tracking you:

  • they know where you are, or were, without being told
  • they know what you said in private messages or emails
  • your phone’s battery drains fast or it runs hot when idle
  • settings change that you didn’t change
  • you get alerts that an unknown tracker is moving with you
  • your accounts show logins from devices you don’t recognise

None of these proves tracking on its own. Several together are worth checking.

The Decoder describes this as monitoring and surveillance, with a separate page on location tracking.

How do I check safely?

Before you start: if you think they’re watching closely, sudden changes can tell them you’ve noticed. Think about the order, and if there’s any risk of violence, plan it with a domestic abuse service first.

Work from a device they have never had access to: a new phone, a friend’s, a library computer.

1. Shared accounts. These are the most common route in. The Decoder covers this in account control.

  • Start with your email. Change the password, turn on two-step login, and save the backup codes on paper.
  • Then cloud storage (Apple ID or iCloud, Google), social media, banking, shopping, streaming and any shared calendars.
  • In each, look for “devices” or “where you’re logged in” and remove anything you don’t recognise.
  • Remove them as a “family” member, trusted contact or recovery contact.

2. Location sharing.

  • On iPhone: Settings, your name, Find My. Check who can see your location, and check Family Sharing.
  • On Android: Google Maps, Location sharing; and Settings, Google, Location.
  • Check apps like Life360, Snapchat (Snap Map) and fitness apps.
  • Check the car’s own app if it has one.

3. Your phone. Stalkerware is software hidden on a phone to report what you do.

  • Look for apps you don’t recognise, especially ones with access to location, messages or the microphone.
  • Check whether the phone allows apps from outside the official store when you didn’t set that up.
  • Run a reputable security app that detects stalkerware.
  • If you find something, think before deleting it. Removing it alerts the person and destroys evidence. A domestic abuse service or the police can help you decide.
  • The cleanest fix is a new phone with a new account that they never had access to.

4. Trackers on your car and things.

  • Small Bluetooth trackers can be hidden in cars, bags, coats or children’s belongings.
  • iPhones warn you about an unknown AirTag travelling with you. Android phones can scan for unknown trackers in Settings, under Safety and emergency.
  • Check the car: under bumpers, inside wheel arches, under seats, in the boot and glovebox, and around the OBD port under the dashboard.
  • Check gifts and toys the children bring back from visits.

5. Home devices. Smart speakers, cameras, doorbells and thermostats they set up may still report to their account. Reset them to factory settings, or remove them.

6. Social media.

  • Make accounts private and review who follows you.
  • Turn off location tags, and ask friends not to tag you or post where you are.
  • Check old posts that show your routines.

What should I do if I find something?

Photograph it where you found it before you touch it. Write down the date and place, using the format in What to document and how. Then talk to the police or a domestic abuse service. Tracking someone without consent is a crime in many places, often as stalking or harassment. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

Do I have to stay suspicious forever?

No. Do the check once, properly, and fold what you learn into your safety plan. Repeat it after handovers or visits if devices go back and forth, or if something new makes you wonder. Then let it go. The aim is to close the doors, not to live on guard.

Stalking and harassment after a breakup

What counts as stalking after a breakup, and what can I do?

The short answer

Stalking after a breakup is a pattern of unwanted contact or attention that makes you afraid or distressed: following, watching, turning up, repeated messages, contact through others, or online harassment. It is a crime in many places. Keep a record of every incident and report it.

It starts small. A message after you asked them to stop. A car that looks like theirs outside your work. Your friend says they asked where you live now.

One of these is a worry. A pattern of them is stalking.

If you feel in danger now, call your emergency number. If you’re not safe right now has the numbers.

What counts as stalking after a breakup?

Stalking is a pattern of unwanted behaviour aimed at you that causes fear, alarm or distress. Common forms:

  • following you, or waiting where you’ll be
  • turning up at your home, work, gym or children’s school
  • driving past, or parking outside
  • repeated calls, messages or emails after you’ve asked them to stop
  • new numbers or accounts after you block them
  • contacting your friends, family or employer to find out about you
  • sending gifts or leaving things for you
  • tracking your phone or car
  • posting about you online, or making fake accounts to watch or contact you

The Decoder describes the watching in monitoring and surveillance and the threats in intimidation.

How is stalking different from hoovering?

Hoovering is an attempt to pull you back, usually with charm, apology or an emergency. Stalking is about watching and pursuing. It may not want you back at all. The two can overlap, and both can be part of post-separation abuse. If the contact makes you afraid, or follows you after you’ve said no, treat it as stalking. Hoovering covers the pull-back.

Is stalking a crime?

In many places, yes:

  • United States: stalking is a crime in every state, and some forms are federal crimes.
  • England and Wales: stalking and harassment are offences under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, and courts can make stalking protection orders.
  • Scotland: stalking is an offence under the Criminal Justice and Licensing (Scotland) Act 2010.
  • Ireland: stalking became a separate offence on 1 November 2023, with civil stalking orders added later.
  • Canada: criminal harassment is an offence under the Criminal Code.
  • Australia: every state and territory has a stalking offence.

Search “[your country or state] stalking law” for the details.

How do I document stalking?

Stalking is a pattern, so the record is what proves it. For each incident, write down:

  • the date and time
  • what happened, in plain words
  • where you were
  • who saw it
  • how it affected you (for example, you left work early, or changed your route)

Keep screenshots of messages and calls, with the date and the number or account. Save voicemails. Keep gifts or notes in a bag without handling them more than you need to. What to document and how has a four-line format.

Should I report it?

If you feel unsafe, yes. Report early, even if each incident seems small. Police respond more strongly to a recorded pattern than to a single event. Ask for a crime reference number every time. See Police and reporting.

You can also ask about a protective or stalking order, which makes contact itself a breach. See Protective orders.

What helps day to day?

  • Don’t engage. Replies, even angry ones, teach them that the channel works.
  • Block and keep a record at the same time: screenshot first, then block.
  • Tell your workplace, school and neighbours, and give them a photo. If you share a workplace or a small town, see When you share a town, a workplace or a community.
  • Vary your routes and routines for a while.
  • Check your phone and car for tracking: Surveillance and technology.
  • Lean on a stalking helpline if one exists where you live. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline is run by the Suzy Lamplugh Trust.

Why does stalking matter so much for safety?

Stalking by a former partner is one of the warning signs services take most seriously. Warning signs to take seriously explains why. If it’s escalating, update your safety plan.

Intimate images and online threats

My ex is threatening to post pictures of me. What can I do?

The short answer

If your ex is threatening to post pictures or videos of you, you are not powerless. Sharing intimate images without consent, and in many places threatening to, is a crime. Free tools can block images from being posted on major platforms, and helplines can get images taken down.

They have photos or videos of you from the relationship. Now they’re threatening to send them to your family, your boss, or post them online. Or they already have.

This is abuse. It is not your fault for having trusted them. And there is more you can do than you might think.

If you feel in danger, or you’re thinking about harming yourself because of this, go to If you’re not safe right now or When it’s more than aftermath first.

Is it illegal for my ex to post pictures of me?

In many places, yes. Sharing intimate images without consent, sometimes called image-based abuse or “revenge porn,” is a crime in:

  • United States: almost every state, and since 2025 a federal law, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, which also requires platforms to remove reported images quickly.
  • England and Wales: sharing intimate images without consent is an offence, and so is threatening to share them.
  • Scotland: an offence under the Abusive Behaviour and Sexual Harm (Scotland) Act 2016, including threats.
  • Ireland: an offence under the Harassment, Harmful Communications and Related Offences Act 2020, known as Coco’s Law.
  • Canada: an offence under the Criminal Code.
  • Australia: offences in every state, plus a national removal scheme run by the eSafety Commissioner.

Fake images made with editing or AI tools are covered by some of these laws too.

What do I do right now?

  1. Don’t pay, don’t send more images, and don’t negotiate. Giving in rarely ends it.
  2. Save the evidence. Screenshot the threats, with dates, usernames and links. Save links to anywhere an image has been posted. Do this before blocking.
  3. Block images before they spread. StopNCII.org lets adults create a digital fingerprint of an image on their own device. The image never leaves your phone. Partner platforms then block matching images. For images taken when you were under 18, use Take It Down, run by the US National Center for Missing and Exploited Children.
  4. Report to the platform. Every major platform has a reporting route for intimate images shared without consent. In the US, platforms must now remove reported images within 48 hours; if one doesn’t, you can report it to the Federal Trade Commission at TakeItDown.ftc.gov.
  5. Get help with removal. In the UK, the Revenge Porn Helpline helps adults get images removed. In Australia, report to the eSafety Commissioner. In the US, the Cyber Civil Rights Initiative runs a crisis line.
  6. Report to the police, with your evidence.

What if they’re threatening to tell people things, not share images?

Threats to “expose” you, to post your address, or to contact your employer are also abuse. Posting someone’s private details is often called doxxing; the Decoder describes it in doxxing. Using your name or reputation against you is covered in reputation hijacking and in The narrative campaign. Many of these threats are crimes too, as harassment or malicious communications. Keep the evidence and report it.

What about fake accounts and online harassment?

Report fake accounts to the platform as impersonation. Keep a record of every account and post. A pattern of online harassment can be stalking. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

How do I cope with this?

Image-based abuse can bring intense shame and fear. Both are the intended effect. The shame belongs to the person who shared or threatened. Tell one person you trust. If the images have been seen by people you know, you don’t owe anyone an explanation beyond “someone shared private images of me without my consent, and it’s being dealt with.”

Your address, your records, your mail

How do I keep my address from my ex? Is there an address confidentiality program?

The short answer

An address confidentiality program gives you a substitute address to use on public records, so an abusive ex can’t find you through them. Most US states run one, and other countries have similar protections. You can also hide your address on voter rolls, at school and with your doctor, and remove it from people-search websites.

You moved. Now you want to keep it that way. But your address is in more places than you think: voter rolls, court papers, school records, delivery apps, and websites that collect public data.

What is an address confidentiality program?

In most US states, an address confidentiality program gives survivors of domestic abuse, stalking or sexual assault a substitute address. You use it for state and local government records, including voter registration, and your mail is forwarded to your real address. Search “[your state] address confidentiality program.” A domestic abuse service can usually help you apply.

Other places have their own protections:

  • United Kingdom: you can register to vote anonymously if your safety is at risk. You’ll need a court order or a letter from a qualifying professional.
  • Australia: you can apply to be a silent elector, so your address doesn’t appear on the public roll.
  • Canada and Ireland: ask your electoral office about keeping your address off public lists, and ask a domestic abuse service what exists locally.

The Decoder’s page on location tracking shows how a location gets found and used.

Where else does my address appear?

Work through these, starting with the most public:

  • Voter rolls: see above.
  • Court papers. Ask the court or your lawyer whether your address can be withheld from the other side. Many courts allow this where there’s a risk.
  • School and childcare. Ask them not to release your address or phone number to the other parent, and give them any court order.
  • Doctor, dentist and hospital. Ask them to flag your record so your details aren’t shared.
  • Your employer and bank. Ask them to keep your address private and to verify anyone who calls about you.
  • Deliveries. Check shared shopping and food delivery accounts; old addresses and new orders can show up there.
  • Social media. Remove location tags, check photos that show your street or front door, and ask friends not to post your address or check you in.

How do I remove my address from people-search sites?

In the US especially, data broker websites list addresses, phone numbers and relatives. Most have an opt-out form. You can search your own name to see what comes up, then opt out site by site, or use a paid removal service. Some address confidentiality programs give extra protection here. In the UK and the EU, you can ask companies to delete your data under data protection law.

What about my mail?

  • Set up mail forwarding from any old shared address, so letters about you don’t land with them.
  • Think about a PO box or a mailbox service for your public address.
  • Change your address with banks, pensions, insurers and government agencies directly, rather than relying on forwarding.

Should I change my name?

Some people change their name for safety. It can help, but it doesn’t erase records that link the old and new names, and the process often creates public records of its own. In some places courts can seal a name change where there’s a safety risk. Get advice from a domestic abuse service or a lawyer first.

What if they find me anyway?

It isn’t your fault, and it doesn’t mean you did this wrong. Update your safety plan, tell the police if there’s any threat, and look at how they might have found you: shared contacts, the children’s belongings, tracking. See Surveillance and technology. If they turned up, write it down and see Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

Children as the channel

How do abusive exes use children after separation?

The short answer

In post-separation abuse, child custody and contact are the most common channels for control. It shows up at handovers, in questions put to children, in running you down to them, and in games with the schedule. Your children’s wellbeing matters more than winning any single exchange.

If you have children with the person you left, they are probably the one channel you can’t close. You’ll see each other at handovers. You’ll need to agree on schools and doctors. And the children carry messages, gifts and moods between two homes.

That makes child custody and contact the most common route for post-separation abuse to continue. It also makes this the chapter that needs the most care, because the wrong response can hurt the children themselves.

If you’re worried about a child’s safety right now, contact the police or your local child protection service. If you’re in danger yourself, go to If you’re not safe right now.

How do abusive exes use children after separation?

These are the patterns parents and researchers describe most often.

At handovers. Long conversations at the door. Questions, accusations, comments in front of the children. Arriving late, early, or not at all.

Through questioning. Asking the children where you went, who visited, what you said, whether you have a new partner. Children become reporters without knowing it.

Through running you down. Telling the children you broke up the family, that you’re unstable, that you don’t love them, or that they’d be happier with the other parent. Sometimes it’s said outright; sometimes it’s a sigh or a look.

Through the schedule. Last-minute changes that wreck your plans. Rigid insistence on every minute when it suits them, and ignoring the plan when it doesn’t. Holidays and birthdays used as bargaining chips. See Holidays and the first year’s calendar.

Through gifts and promises. Big presents, special trips, a pet, or promises that the children can live with them full time, often timed around court.

Through medical, school and activity decisions. Refusing consent for treatment or a passport. Contacting teachers and doctors with stories about you. Turning up at your parents’ evening or your child’s match.

Through the courts. Applications to change arrangements, often repeated. See Systems abuse and litigation abuse and Divorce and separation proceedings.

The Decoder describes the core moves: children used as leverage, triangulation when a child is put between you, and parentification when a child is made to carry an adult’s feelings.

The aim of the tactic is to provoke you. The children are watching what you do next.

Is this post-separation abuse, or just a hard co-parent?

Many co-parents are difficult. They’re late, disorganised, or bad at communicating. That’s frustrating, not abusive. The difference is pattern and purpose. Ask whether the difficulty keeps landing where it hurts you most, and whether it continues when it costs the children. Abuse, or a hard breakup? has a fair test.

What does this do to children?

Research on coercive control finds that children are affected directly. They are never only bystanders. Children can become anxious, withdrawn, angry or clingy. Some feel they have to take care of a parent. Some start repeating things that are plainly someone else’s words. How children react, and what helps them covers the signs by age and what helps.

What should I do?

Protect the children first, and the argument last. Matching hostility, or using the children to carry messages or gather information in return, harms them in the same way, whoever starts it.

Make handovers short and dull. Hello, the bag, goodbye. If handovers are where things go wrong, change how they happen: school handovers, a public place, a third person, or a contact centre. See Handovers.

Move communication to writing. A parenting app or email gives you a record and keeps things calmer. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.

Think about parallel parenting. When cooperation isn’t possible, each parent runs their own home with minimal contact. See Co-parenting or parallel parenting?.

Keep a record. Late handovers, cancelled visits, things the children report, messages. Write down what the child said in their own words, without asking leading questions. See What to document and how.

Don’t interrogate the children. Questions like “What did Daddy say about me?” put them in the middle. Let them talk; listen; write it down later.

Get advice before court. Family courts vary widely in how they see coercive control. See Family court, honestly.

What do I say when my child repeats something cruel about me?

Stay calm. Don’t attack the other parent. Correct the fact gently, and tell the child it’s not their job to sort out grown-up problems:

Say: “I can see that’s confusing. That isn’t true, and you don’t need to worry about it. Grown-ups sort out grown-up things. I love you, and that won’t change.”

When should I involve professionals?

If a child talks about being hurt, frightened, or asked to keep secrets, or if their behaviour changes sharply, speak to your child’s doctor or school, and get legal advice. If you believe a child is at immediate risk, contact the police or child protection services.

Money as the channel

What is post-separation financial abuse, and what can I do about it?

The short answer

Post-separation financial abuse uses money to keep control after a break-up: child support paid late or not at all, debts run up in your name, assets hidden, a house sale dragged out, or your work disrupted. Keep a record, check your credit, and use the enforcement routes that exist.

You’ve left, but you still share a mortgage. Or a car loan. Or a child. And every month, money becomes the place where the fight continues.

What is post-separation financial abuse?

It is using money to control, punish or exhaust a former partner. Many laws now recognise it. In England and Wales, economic abuse is part of the legal definition of domestic abuse, and it covers former partners.

Common forms:

  • Child support paid late, short, or only after repeated chasing, especially when you need it most.
  • Debts in your name that you didn’t agree to, known as coerced debt. Loans, credit cards, phone contracts, utility bills.
  • Joint accounts and loans emptied, or used to run up new debt you’re both liable for.
  • Hidden income or assets, especially before a financial settlement. See Divorce and separation proceedings.
  • A shared home used as a hostage: refusing to sell, refusing to buy you out, letting the mortgage fall behind.
  • Disrupting your work: calling your employer, turning up there, or making handovers clash with your shifts.
  • Tax and benefits sabotage: false claims, refusing to sign forms, reporting you to the authorities.
  • Expensive litigation that drains your savings. See Systems abuse and litigation abuse.

The Decoder describes the patterns: financial control, financial enmeshment when your money is tangled with theirs, and financial dependence by design.

The money matters. The timing tells you what it’s for.

How do I tell financial abuse from an ordinary money dispute?

Real disputes over money happen in calm separations too: what the house is worth, who keeps the pension, what’s fair. The pattern worth naming is when money trouble keeps arriving at the moments it hurts most, and keeps going after agreements are made.

What should I write down?

  • Each payment due and each payment made: date, amount, and how late.
  • Messages about money, kept as screenshots and exports.
  • Statements for any joint accounts.
  • Any debt you didn’t agree to: the lender, the date, the amount.

See What to document and how for a simple format.

My ex won’t pay child support. What can I do?

Most countries have a government child support service that can calculate payments and collect them, taking money from wages or bank accounts if needed:

  • United States: each state has a child support enforcement agency.
  • United Kingdom: the Child Maintenance Service, including a “collect and pay” option where it collects the money for you.
  • Australia: Services Australia’s Child Support.
  • Canada: each province runs a maintenance enforcement program.
  • Ireland: maintenance is set by the District Court, which can order deductions from earnings.

Using the official service also means you don’t have to deal with them directly about money.

What about coerced debt?

Get your credit report from each credit agency. It’s free in most countries. Look for accounts you didn’t open or agree to. You can usually dispute them with the lender and the credit agency, and in some places a police report or a domestic abuse service’s letter helps. In the UK, Surviving Economic Abuse has guidance. In the US, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how to dispute errors.

How do I protect myself now?

  • Open an account in your name only, at a bank you didn’t share.
  • Remove yourself from joint accounts where you can, or ask the bank to require both signatures.
  • Change passwords and security answers on every financial account.
  • Ask lenders to note that you’re a victim of economic abuse; many banks have specialist teams.
  • Get advice before you stop paying anything in joint names, because you may both be liable.

The narrative campaign

My ex is spreading lies about me. How do I handle a smear campaign after a breakup?

The short answer

A smear campaign after a breakup is a sustained effort to control what other people believe about you: that you’re unstable, cruel, unfaithful or dangerous. It often works because it sounds calm and reasonable. Most of the time the best response is steady behaviour, a few short corrections to people who matter, and a record.

A friend goes quiet. Your mother-in-law stops answering. Someone at the school gate looks at you differently. Then you hear it: the story going round about you.

What is a smear campaign after a breakup?

It’s a sustained effort to shape what other people think of you and of the separation. Common lines:

  • you’re mentally unstable, or “not coping”
  • you were the abusive one
  • you cheated, or left for someone else
  • you’re keeping the children from them
  • you’re after money
  • they’re “worried about you”

It’s often delivered calmly, with sadness and concern. That makes it more believable to people who weren’t inside the relationship. The Decoder describes the tactic in smear campaigns and the larger aim, owning the official version of events, in narrative monopoly.

They often tell the story before you do. That gives them a head start, not the truth.

Why does it work so well?

Because you often don’t fight it. You assume the truth is obvious, or you’re too tired, or you don’t want to drag people in. Meanwhile, the people hearing the story only have one version. And the story-teller is often good at seeming reasonable.

Should I fight back publicly?

Usually not. A public back-and-forth reads to outsiders as “they’re both difficult,” which tends to help whoever is more comfortable with conflict. It also hands the other person new material: an angry post, a sharp message, a screenshot they can share.

Not responding isn’t losing. Most people who matter will come to see a fuller picture through how you behave over time.

When is a correction worth it?

Some audiences matter enough for one calm, short correction:

  • your children’s school or nursery
  • your employer, if they’ve been contacted
  • professionals involved in your case: social workers, a child’s representative, a mediator
  • close family and friends who you want to keep

Keep it brief and factual, without attacking the other person:

Say: “You may have heard some things about me from [name]. We’ve separated, and there’s a court process. I’d rather not go into it, but I wanted you to hear from me that I’m fine, the children are well, and I’m happy to answer anything that affects you.”

For professionals, stick to facts and documents: “These are the messages I’ve received since the order.” The Decoder’s reputation hijacking page covers how your name gets used against you.

How do I keep evidence of a smear campaign?

  • Screenshot posts, messages and comments, with dates and names.
  • Write down what people tell you, who said it, and when, in their words.
  • Keep any written contact with your employer, the school or professionals.

This matters if the story turns into a false report to the police, social services or your employer. See When they say you’re the abuser and What to document and how.

Is it defamation?

Possibly, but defamation cases are expensive, slow and hard to win, and they keep you tied to the other person. Harassment law may fit better where there’s a pattern. Get legal advice before threatening any action.

How do I live with it?

  • Tell one or two people the whole truth, so you aren’t carrying it alone.
  • Let some people go. Those who believe the story without asking you may not be the people you need now. Your family and mutual friends and The friends who didn’t believe you cover this.
  • Keep living well, visibly and quietly. Over time, that is the strongest answer.

Your family and mutual friends

Why are people taking my ex’s side? What are flying monkeys?

The short answer

“Flying monkeys” is the popular name for people who carry an abusive ex’s messages, pressure or stories to you, knowingly or not. Most mean no harm: they’re loyal, misinformed or avoiding conflict. You get to decide, one person at a time, how much contact to keep.

Your sister says your ex “seems so sad.” An old friend messages to say you should “just talk.” Your former in-laws ask the children about your new home.

The person you left may not be contacting you directly. Other people are doing it for them.

What are flying monkeys?

“Flying monkeys” comes from The Wizard of Oz, where the witch sends winged monkeys to do her work. People now use it for friends, relatives or colleagues who end up acting for an abusive person: passing on messages, gathering information, applying pressure, or repeating the story.

The Decoder calls this proxy recruitment.

It’s a vivid word. Use it in your head if it helps. Most of the people involved don’t think of themselves that way, and treating them as enemies can cost you people who might come round.

Why do people take my ex’s side?

Usually for ordinary reasons:

  • Familiarity. They knew your ex first, or longer, or in a different setting where they were charming.
  • One version. They’ve only heard one side, often a calm, sad, convincing one. See The narrative campaign.
  • Avoiding conflict. Staying “neutral” feels kinder. In practice it often means accepting whichever version arrived first.
  • Disbelief. It’s hard to believe someone you like could do what you’ve described.
  • Self-interest. A shared business, a family peace, a social circle they don’t want to lose.

Silence from people you love often means they haven’t engaged with the question at all. That hurts, and it’s different from deciding you’re lying.

The Decoder’s page on silent coalitions describes how groups go quiet around abuse.

How do I close the third-party channel?

  • Ask directly: “Please don’t pass on messages from [name], and please don’t share anything about me or the children with them.”
  • Give less: share less news with people who you know report back.
  • Don’t send replies through others. If something must be said, say it in writing through your usual channel.
  • Record it: contact through others can break a protective order. Write down who passed on what, and when. See What to document and how.

What do I say to someone who’s been pulled in?

Keep it short, and don’t ask them to choose:

Say: “I know you care about both of us. I’m not going to discuss [name] with you, and I’d ask you not to pass anything on. I’d still like to see you.”

If they keep pushing:

Say: “I’ve asked you not to bring this up. If it keeps happening, I’ll need some distance for a while.”

Do I have to cut everyone off?

No. You decide person by person. Some relationships are worth a calm conversation. Some will repair with time and your steady behaviour. Some have become too costly for now. There’s no rule that says you must keep everyone, or lose everyone who didn’t immediately take your side.

If it’s your own family who has sided with your ex, Your parents covers that particular pain. If you share a workplace, a church or a small town, see When you share a town, a workplace or a community. For friends who didn’t believe you, see The friends who didn’t believe you.

The new partner

My ex is contacting my new partner. What do I do?

The short answer

If your ex is contacting your new partner, it’s a common pattern, not a sign your new relationship is doomed. It usually comes as concern or “warnings.” Tell your partner about the history early, agree how you’ll both respond, and keep a record. The same applies, in reverse, if your ex’s new partner contacts you.

You’ve met someone. It’s early, or it’s serious. Then your ex finds out, and your new partner gets a message: “I think you should know some things about who you’re with.”

Why is my ex contacting my new partner?

A new relationship can feel to the person you left like the final loss of their hold on you. Contact with your partner is a way to get some of that back: to unsettle the relationship, to recruit an audience, or to remind you they’re still there.

It often looks like:

  • a “friendly warning” about your past, your mental health or your parenting
  • the same story they’ve told everyone else, now for your partner. See The narrative campaign.
  • sudden charm towards your partner, or attempts to befriend them
  • more contact with you, or more trouble over the children, just as the relationship becomes public

The Decoder calls the comparison game triangulation. Stories told to turn people against you are covered in smear campaigns.

The new relationship can survive this. What you’re seeing is the old pattern noticing you’ve moved on.

What do I tell my new partner?

Early, calm honesty helps most. Your partner doesn’t need every detail. They need enough to recognise what’s happening if it comes:

Say: “My ex didn’t take the break-up well, and they sometimes contact people in my life with stories about me. If they contact you, I’d rather you hear it from me first. You don’t have to reply.”

Telling someone new covers how much to share and when.

How should we both respond?

  • Don’t reply, or reply once and stop: “Please don’t contact me again.”
  • Screenshot first, then block.
  • Don’t argue the story. A partner defending you to your ex gives your ex the reaction they want.
  • Keep a record of the contact. If there’s a protective order, contact with your partner may breach it. See What to document and how.
  • Watch for escalation. A new relationship is one of the moments when risk can rise. Update your safety plan.

What if my ex’s new partner contacts me?

Sometimes the message comes the other way. Your ex’s new partner reaches out, angry, or worried, or asking “was it that bad?”

You don’t have to answer. If you do, keep it short and safe:

Say: “I’m not going to discuss our relationship. I hope you’re okay. If you ever need help, domestic abuse services are free and confidential.”

Don’t send a long warning. It rarely lands. It usually gets shown to your ex, and it can be used against you. If you believe someone is in danger, you can tell the police; some countries have disclosure schemes that let a new partner ask about past abuse. See Police and reporting.

What if the new relationship struggles under the pressure?

That’s not your fault, and it doesn’t mean you shouldn’t date. Some relationships hold under strain and some don’t. A partner who meets this with calm and curiosity is showing you something good about them. Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not covers the wider picture.

Hoovering

Why is my ex contacting me again, and what is hoovering?

The short answer

Hoovering is when an ex who hurt you tries to pull you back after a break-up, often just as you’ve started to feel steady. It can come as an apology, an emergency, nostalgia, a gift or a crisis. The name matters because a pattern you can see has less pull. You don’t owe a reply.

It’s been weeks of silence. You’ve started sleeping again. Then your phone lights up.

“Hey. I was just thinking about you. I hope you’re doing okay.”

Or: “I’ve been in therapy. I finally understand what I did.” Or: “I found your grandmother’s ring. When can you come and get it?” Or: “I’m in hospital. Please, I just need to hear your voice.”

This has a name.

What is hoovering?

Hoovering is an attempt to pull a former partner back into contact after a break-up or a period of silence. The name comes from the vacuum cleaner: it sucks you back in.

It isn’t always an attempt to restart the relationship. Often the goal is access: to see if you’ll still respond, to reopen a channel, to get a reaction. The Decoder’s page on hoovering gives the short definition. This chapter is about what to do when it happens after you’ve left.

A return that feels uniquely meaningful, uniquely well-timed and uniquely sincere is often one of the most common shapes there is.

What does hoovering look like?

These are the forms people describe most often. Most arrive as a text, a voicemail, a letter, or a message through someone else.

The apology. “I’m so sorry. I’ve changed. I’m getting help.” Often detailed and moving. Often arriving without any change you can see.

The nostalgia. “Remember that summer in Cornwall?” A photo, a song, an in-joke. It goes straight past your thinking and into your chest.

The emergency. An illness, an accident, a death in the family, a breakdown. Some are real. Many evaporate once you respond.

The practical excuse. “I found some of your things.” “There’s post for you.” “We need to talk about the car.” Logistics as a door.

The special date. Your birthday, their birthday, an anniversary, a holiday. Warmth arrives exactly when you’re most open to it. Holidays and the first year’s calendar helps you plan for those days.

The third person. A mutual friend, their mother, your child: “They miss you so much.” See Your family and mutual friends.

The future. “Let’s start again. We’ll move, get married, have the baby.” The Decoder calls this future faking.

The charm offensive. Flowers, gifts, attention, compliments, the person you first fell for. The Decoder calls this love bombing.

The threat dressed as a plea. “If you don’t answer, I don’t know what I’ll do.” This can be real distress, a control tactic, or both. See below.

Why do they come back when I’m finally doing well?

The timing is rarely an accident. Hoovering tends to arrive when you become visibly stable: a new job, a new partner, a happy photo, a quiet stretch with no replies. To someone used to influencing you, your stability can feel like the last of their hold slipping away. That triggers an attempt to get it back.

It also works because of how the relationship trained you. Warmth that comes and goes unpredictably creates a stronger pull than steady warmth. Psychologists call this intermittent reinforcement, and the Decoder describes it as push-pull. One warm message after months of nothing can feel enormous. The trauma bond explains why.

Will a narcissist come back after a breakup?

Often, yes. Whatever label fits the person, returning after silence is one of the most common patterns after a break-up with a controlling partner. Some come back once. Some come back for years, every time you seem to be doing well.

How long does hoovering last?

There’s no fixed answer. For some people it stops after a few attempts that go unanswered. For others it returns on special dates or whenever life changes. What tends to make it fade is consistent silence, because each reply, even an angry one, tells them the channel still works. When it stops covers the range honestly.

Should I reply?

You don’t owe a reply. You don’t owe closure, an explanation, or one last conversation to someone who harmed you.

There are two workable choices:

No reply. For most people this is the cleanest option. Read the message, record it if you’re keeping a record, and don’t answer. Not replying costs you nothing lasting, whatever it feels like in the moment.

One closing reply, then silence. Some people need to say one thing to feel finished. If that’s you, keep it short and final, and don’t respond to whatever comes back:

Say: “I’ve read your message. I’m not going to reopen contact. Please don’t contact me again.”

If you share children, you may need to keep one channel open. Answer only what’s about the children, and ignore the rest:

Say: “I’ll keep our messages to the children’s arrangements. Pickup on Friday is at 5pm at school as agreed.”

More replies are in Words you can use. For deciding what needs an answer at all, see Messages you don’t have to answer.

What if the emergency is real?

Sometimes it is. You can respond to a real emergency without reopening the relationship. If someone says they’re going to harm themselves, call emergency services and give them the details. That is the right help, and it doesn’t have to come from you.

If the same “emergency” keeps happening, or disappears once you reply, that tells you what it was.

I already replied. Have I ruined everything?

No. Nearly everyone replies at some point. It doesn’t erase your progress. Notice what happened afterwards: did things change, or did the old pattern come straight back? Then decide what you’ll do next time, and write that decision down while you’re calm.

If you’ve gone further than replying, and you’re back together or thinking of it, If you go back is written without judgment.

How do I tell hoovering from someone who has changed?

People can change. This book doesn’t claim every returning ex is manipulating you. Real change tends to look like this:

  • Time. It shows over many months, not in one message.
  • Consistency. The same behaviour on good days and bad days, when you’re watching and when you’re not.
  • Accountability without conditions. They name what they did without blaming you or asking for anything in return.
  • No pressure. They respect a “no” and a “not now.”
  • Change that doesn’t depend on your return. They’re getting help because they need it, not as a bargaining chip.

Hoovering usually fails those tests. It arrives tied to your stability, asks for something fast, and turns cold or angry when you don’t respond. If you’re unsure, Trust as a practice offers a slow, careful way to judge.

What if I’m tempted?

That’s normal. The pull tells you how strong the bond was. It tells you nothing about whether to go back. Some things that help:

  • Write down three things that happened that you never want to happen again, and read them when the message arrives.
  • Tell one trusted person before you reply to anything.
  • Delay. Wait 24 hours before any reply. The urge usually fades.
  • Read The trauma bond and Checking their profile.

Should I keep the messages?

Yes, if there’s any chance you’ll need them: for a protective order, a custody case, or a stalking report. Screenshot them with dates, then mute or block. See What to document and how. If hoovering turns into following or turning up, see Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

When they say you're the abuser

What is reactive abuse, and what do I do about false allegations?

The short answer

Reactive abuse is when someone provokes you for months until you snap, then holds up your reaction as proof that you’re the abuser. False reports to the police, child services or your employer can follow. Stay calm, keep a record, get legal advice early, and describe the whole pattern, including your own reaction, honestly.

You shouted. Maybe you swore, or threw something, or pushed them away. It was the worst moment of a very long year. Now it’s the centre of their story: you’re violent, you’re unstable, you’re the abuser.

Or you did nothing at all, and a police officer, a social worker or your employer calls to say someone has made a complaint about you.

What is reactive abuse?

“Reactive abuse” is a term people use for a pattern: one person provokes the other, often over a long time, until the other reacts. The reaction is then recorded, filmed or reported, and used as proof that the provoked person is the abuser.

It isn’t a legal or clinical term. And a reaction is still a reaction: it isn’t automatically excused. But it matters to describe the whole pattern, because a single moment pulled out of context can make the target look like the danger.

Why would my ex say I’m the abuser?

Because it works. Accusing the other person first is a common move in post-separation abuse:

  • DARVO: deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. The person who did harm denies it, attacks your credibility, and claims to be the victim. See the Decoder’s DARVO.
  • Getting in first. A complaint made early can shape how professionals see everything that follows. The Decoder describes this in preemptive accusation.
  • False reports to the police, child services, your employer, a licensing body or your landlord. See the Decoder’s false reports.
  • Counter-applications for a protective order, often filed after you filed yours.

Research on DARVO has found it can make observers believe the victim less and the person accused more. That’s why calm facts matter so much.

The goal of an accusation like this is often to make you defend yourself, so that you stop describing what they did.

What do I do if I’ve been falsely reported?

  1. Stay calm with the professional. Police officers, social workers and HR staff deal with competing accounts every day. Calm answers with dates and details help you.
  2. Don’t contact your ex about it. Anything you send can be used.
  3. Get legal advice before a formal interview, especially with the police. You usually have the right to a lawyer. Legal help with little or no money lists low-cost options.
  4. Gather your record: messages, dates, witnesses, any earlier reports you made. See What to document and how.
  5. Ask exactly what is alleged, so you can answer it.
  6. Keep your answers to facts: dates, what happened, who was there.

What if I did react?

Be honest about it. Denying something that’s on video or in a message will cost you more than admitting it. Then describe the context, factually:

Say: “On 3 March I shouted at him and threw my phone. I’m not proud of it. It happened after eight months of messages like these, which I can show you, and after he followed me to work that morning.”

Context isn’t an excuse, and a professional may still have concerns. But it gives them the whole picture instead of one frame of it.

If you’re not sure whether your reactions crossed a line, read the “Am I the abuser?” section in Abuse, or a hard breakup?.

What if social services contact me about my children?

Cooperate, stay calm, and focus on the children. Explain the separation and any pattern of control, with your record. Ask what the concern is and what they need from you. Get legal advice if it may lead to court. Child services are there to protect children, and a parent who is calm, cooperative and focused on the children’s needs is what they want to see.

What if they apply for a protective order against me?

Don’t ignore it. Read it carefully, get legal advice, and go to any hearing. Bring your record. Don’t contact them, even to sort it out, because that could break a temporary order. See Protective orders and Your day in court.

How do I cope with being called the abuser?

Being accused of the thing that was done to you can feel like losing your mind. Hold on to your record, and to one or two people who know the truth. A therapist who understands coercive control can help too: see Finding a therapist who understands this.

When it stops

Does post-separation abuse ever end?

The short answer

Does post-separation abuse ever end? For many people it does, often fading as the person who caused it finds a new focus or meets consistent limits and consequences. For others it carries on in a smaller form for years. Both are honest answers, and you don’t have to wait for it to end to start living fully.

“How long will this go on?” may be the question people ask most. This chapter gives the honest answer, and the honest answer is a range.

Does post-separation abuse ever end?

For many people, yes. It fades or stops. For others it continues in a smaller form for years, often flaring around court cases, holidays or changes in your life. Researchers and services see both outcomes, and nobody can tell you in advance which one you’ll get.

No list in this chapter is a formula. These are patterns seen across many situations, not promises about yours.

How long does post-separation abuse last?

There’s no reliable figure. Where there are no shared children and no shared property, it often fades sooner, because there are fewer channels. Where there are children, contact may continue until they’re adults, and so may the openings for control. That doesn’t mean it has to stay as bad as it is now.

What tends to go with it stopping?

  • Their attention moves. A new relationship, a new job, a move, or a new target. It’s not fair that this is often what does it, but it’s common.
  • Distance. Moving further away, changing schools, fewer shared places.
  • Consistent limits. Contact kept to writing, kept short, with no exceptions. This removes the occasional reward that keeps most of these patterns going.
  • Consequences. A protective order that’s enforced, a court that recognises the pattern, a judge who restricts repeat applications.
  • Less to fight over. A final financial settlement, a clear parenting plan, a house that’s finally sold.

What keeps it going?

The most common thing is intermittent contact: long silence, then a reply; firm limits, then an exception. Each exception teaches the person that pressure still works sometimes, which is a stronger lesson than “it always works.” The Decoder explains this in push-pull.

This isn’t about blaming you for replying. Everyone does sometimes. It’s about knowing what gives the pattern its fuel, so you can starve it when you’re able.

When does post-separation abuse stop for good?

Sometimes you only know looking back: one day you realise it’s been months since anything happened. Sometimes it returns once more after a long gap. Many people describe a shift from constant pressure to occasional flare-ups, and then to quiet.

What do I do while it’s still going on?

  • Keep your record. See What to document and how.
  • Keep limits steady. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • Use the systems that create consequences: Protective orders and Systems abuse and litigation abuse.
  • Build the rest of your life anyway. Waiting for it to end before you live fully has its own cost. The rebuilding chapters, starting with Money from zero, don’t need it to be over first.

What if it never fully stops?

Then the goal shifts from ending it to shrinking it: fewer channels, shorter contact, less of your attention. Many people who share children with a controlling ex describe getting to a place where it’s still there, but it no longer runs their week. That’s a real outcome, and it’s worth working towards. The long after describes what life can look like years out.

No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid

Should I go no contact with my ex, and what if I can’t?

The short answer

No contact with an ex means no messages, calls, meetings or news passed through others. It’s often the fastest way to let things settle. When children, a court order or a shared workplace make that impossible, low contact keeps it to the minimum, in writing, on your terms.

Every book and forum says the same thing: go no contact. For some people that’s the best advice there is. For others it’s impossible. Most people end up somewhere in between.

What does no contact with an ex mean?

No contact means you don’t communicate with them at all:

  • no calls, messages or emails, in either direction
  • no meetings, “just to talk”
  • no checking their social media
  • no news passed through friends or family
  • no replies when they reach out

It’s a decision about your behaviour, not theirs. They may keep trying. No contact means you don’t answer.

Why does no contact work?

Because every reply keeps the channel open, and silence closes it. It also gives your nervous system a break from constant alert, and gives the trauma bond room to loosen. See The trauma bond.

No contact is a rule you keep, especially on the days you don’t want to. Waiting to feel ready for it rarely works.

When is no contact not possible?

  • You share children. Courts usually expect parents to communicate about the children.
  • There’s a court case. You may have to exchange documents or attend hearings.
  • You share a workplace, a business, a home or a community.
  • There are financial ties that take time to untangle.

In these cases, aim for low contact.

What is low contact?

Low contact means the least contact the situation needs, on terms that protect you:

  • One channel only. A co-parenting app or one email address. Block the rest. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.
  • Writing, not calls or visits. Writing gives you time to think and a record. See Grey rock and written-only contact.
  • Business only. The children’s logistics, the house sale, the court process. Nothing about the relationship, your new life or your feelings.
  • Short replies, at a time you choose. See Messages you don’t have to answer.
  • Third parties where helpful. A lawyer, a mediator, a supervised contact centre.

The Decoder’s page on controlled communication shows how the other side can use contact rules against you. Low contact turns that around: you set the channel.

How do I go no contact with kids involved?

You can’t go fully no contact with a co-parent, but you can get close:

  • use a co-parenting app for everything
  • set handovers at school, or through a third person
  • keep messages to dates, times and the children’s needs
  • don’t respond to anything else

Many people call this parallel parenting. Co-parenting or parallel parenting? explains how it works and how to ask a court for it.

How do I tell them?

You don’t have to announce it. If you want to say something once:

Say: “I won’t be in contact from now on. If there’s something about the children, please use the app. I won’t reply to anything else.”

Then stop. Don’t explain, defend or repeat it.

What about blocking?

Blocking is fine. If you might need evidence later, screenshot messages before blocking, or use a filter that sends their messages to a folder you don’t read every day. See What to document and how.

If they get round a block with new numbers or accounts, that can be harassment or stalking. Keep a record and see Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

What if I break no contact?

Most people do at least once. It doesn’t mean you’ve failed or have to start from zero. Notice what happened when you made contact, and decide what you’ll do next time.

Grey rock and written-only contact

What is the grey rock method, and does it work?

The short answer

The grey rock method means being as dull as a grey rock when you have to deal with an abusive ex: short, flat, factual replies that give them nothing to react to. It can work well in writing and at handovers. It can also make some people escalate, so use it with a safety plan.

You can’t go no contact. So you look for the next best thing: a way to deal with them that gives them nothing.

What is the grey rock method?

The grey rock method means making yourself uninteresting to someone who feeds on reaction. Like a grey rock on a path, you’re there, but there’s nothing to pick up.

In practice:

  • short answers: “Yes.” “No.” “Noted.” “5pm Friday.”
  • a flat, polite tone, without emotion
  • no personal news, opinions or feelings
  • no arguing, defending or explaining
  • no questions that open a conversation

The idea is that if pressure gets no reaction, the person gets bored and moves their attention elsewhere.

When should I not use grey rock?

Read this part first. Grey rock can make some people try harder to get a reaction. If the person has been violent, has threatened you, or escalates when ignored, grey rock alone is not a safety plan.

Avoid relying on grey rock:

  • in person with someone who may become violent
  • when you’re living together. See Still under the same roof.
  • when a sudden change in your behaviour could alert them that you’re planning to leave
  • where the flatness could look like coldness to a court, without context

If any of these apply, plan with a domestic abuse service and use A safety plan for after you’ve left.

Does grey rock work?

There’s very little research on grey rock itself. It comes from survivors and the people who support them. Many people find it reduces conflict and protects their peace, especially combined with written-only contact. Some find the other person escalates at first, then gives up. Some find it doesn’t help at all. Watch what happens and adjust.

The Decoder’s page on stonewalling describes refusing to engage as a control tactic. Grey rock is different. Its purpose is to answer what’s needed and nothing more, never to punish someone with silence.

How do I grey rock someone?

In writing, it looks like this:

They send: “I can’t believe you’d do this to the kids. You’ve always been selfish. Are you even going to answer about Saturday?”

Say: “Saturday pickup is 10am at school as agreed.”

They send: “I heard you’ve got a new boyfriend. Hope he knows what he’s getting.”

No reply needed.

At a handover:

They say: “So you’re just going to ignore me now?”

Say: “Here’s her bag. Her medicine’s in the front pocket. Bye.”

What is written-only contact?

Written-only contact means all communication happens in writing: a co-parenting app, email or text. No phone calls, no conversations at the door.

Why it helps:

  • A record. Everything is dated and kept. See What to document and how.
  • Time. You can read, wait, and reply when you’re calm.
  • Less pressure. It’s harder to be talked round in writing.
  • Better behaviour. People often write more carefully when they know it could be read by a judge.

Courts often support written-only arrangements in high-conflict cases, especially through co-parenting apps. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.

How do I set it up?

Say: “From now on I’ll only communicate in writing, by email or through the app. I won’t answer calls or discuss things at handovers.”

Then hold to it. If they call, let it go to voicemail and reply in writing if a reply is needed. More replies are in Words you can use.

How do I keep grey rock from wearing me down?

Being a grey rock is tiring. You’re holding back things you want to say. Say them somewhere else: to a friend, a therapist, a journal. And remember it’s a tool for this one relationship, not a new personality.

Words you can use

What phrases can I use when co-parenting with a narcissist or an abusive ex?

The short answer

These are phrases to use when co-parenting with a narcissist or any controlling ex: short replies to accusations, schedule games, guilt, emergencies, questions about your life and threats of court. Keep every reply brief, factual, polite and final. Then stop.

You know what you want to say. You also know it would start a war. This chapter gives you replies you can copy, adjust and send.

How should I reply to an abusive ex?

Bill Eddy, a lawyer and therapist who works with high-conflict cases, created the BIFF method. A good reply is:

  • Brief: a few sentences at most.
  • Informative: facts only.
  • Friendly: polite, even if you don’t feel it.
  • Firm: it closes the topic and doesn’t invite more.

Before you send anything, read it as if a judge will see it. Take out anything defensive, sarcastic or emotional. Then wait an hour if you can.

The aim isn’t to win the exchange. The aim is to end it.

Phrases to use when co-parenting with a narcissist

These are grouped by the message you’ve received. Swap in your details.

When they accuse you of something untrue

Say: “I don’t agree with that. Pickup is still 5pm Friday.”

When they rant, insult or bring up the past

Say: “I’ll respond to anything about the children’s arrangements.”

Or don’t reply at all if nothing needs an answer.

When they change the schedule at the last minute

Say: “The agreed time is 5pm. If you can’t make that, let me know by Thursday and I’ll keep the children with me this weekend.”

When they demand an answer now

Say: “I’ve received your message. I’ll reply by Wednesday.”

When they ask about your new partner, your home or your life

Say: “That’s private. Is there anything about the children you need?”

When they try guilt The Decoder calls this guilt-tripping.

Say: “I understand you’re unhappy with the arrangement. It stays as agreed.”

When they threaten: “Do this or else” The Decoder calls this emotional blackmail.

Say: “I won’t be changing the arrangement. If you want to change it, please go through the court or the mediator.”

Keep any threat. If it’s about your safety, see Warning signs to take seriously.

When they threaten court

Say: “That’s your choice. I’ll follow the current order.”

When they announce an “emergency”

Say: “If it’s an emergency, please call 999 [or your local number]. If it’s about the children, I’m happy to discuss it here.”

When they want to “just talk”

Say: “I’ll only communicate in writing about the children.”

When they apologise or say they’ve changed

Say: “Thank you. I’ll keep our contact to the children’s arrangements.”

See Hoovering for more on this one.

When they send messages through the children

Say (to your ex, in writing): “Please message me directly about arrangements, not through the children.”

Say (to your child): “You don’t need to pass on messages. That’s a grown-up job. Thanks for telling me.”

When they contact your family or friends

Say (to the friend): “Please don’t pass on messages. If it matters, they can contact me in writing.”

What should I never put in writing?

  • insults, swearing or sarcasm, however deserved
  • threats, including “or I’ll call my lawyer,” unless you mean to act
  • details about your new home, routine or relationships
  • anything you’d be embarrassed for a judge to read
  • long explanations of your feelings

What if they reply with more?

You don’t have to answer a reply to your reply. If the follow-up doesn’t need an answer, let it sit. Messages you don’t have to answer helps you decide.

Can I keep these somewhere?

The pocket card has the most useful replies on one page. Keep it somewhere private. Write your own too: you know their patterns better than anyone.

Messages you don't have to answer

Should I respond to my ex? Which messages need a reply?

The short answer

Should you respond to your ex? Only when a message needs an answer: a court deadline, the children’s arrangements, a legal or financial matter. Insults, guilt, nostalgia, questions about your life and “we need to talk” don’t. Waiting, then replying briefly or not at all, protects you.

Your phone buzzes. Your stomach drops. You read it three times and start drafting a reply in your head.

Stop. Not every message needs an answer.

Should I respond to my ex?

Sort the message first. Ask: does this need an answer to keep me, my children or my legal position safe?

Messages that usually need an answer:

  • arrangements for the children: times, places, health, school
  • a court deadline, a document, or a question from a lawyer or the court
  • a financial matter with a deadline: the house sale, a joint bill
  • a real emergency involving the children

Messages that usually don’t:

  • insults, accusations, rants
  • guilt: “after everything I did for you”
  • nostalgia, love, apologies, “I miss you”
  • questions about your new partner, home or life
  • “we need to talk,” “call me,” “why are you ignoring me”
  • threats that are meant to provoke a reaction (keep them; report them if they’re about safety)

The Decoder’s page on false dilemmas describes messages that offer only two bad options: “Either you answer me tonight or I’ll tell the kids why.” You don’t have to pick either one.

Urgent for them isn’t urgent for you.

How quickly do I have to reply?

Unless there’s a real deadline, you choose. Waiting a few hours, or a day, does three things: it lets the first rush of fear pass, it lets you write calmly, and it teaches them that pressure doesn’t speed you up. The Decoder covers the pressure itself in time control.

If you’re worried a slow reply could look bad in court, reply to the parts that matter within a reasonable time, and ignore the rest.

How do I answer only part of a message?

Pull out the one thing that needs a reply, and answer just that:

They send: “You’re a terrible mother and everyone knows it. Also I’m taking them to my mum’s on Sunday so I’ll drop them back at 7 not 5. Don’t bother arguing.”

Say: “Return time is 5pm as agreed. If you’d like to change it, please ask by Friday.”

  • Set times to check messages: once in the morning and once in the evening, for example.

What to document and how

How do I document post-separation abuse so it helps in court?

The short answer

To document post-separation abuse, write a short record of each incident the same day: when, what happened, who saw it, and what it changed. Keep messages, photos and reports alongside it, stored where your ex can’t reach. A plain record kept over months is often what makes a court, the police or a service see the pattern.

Post-separation abuse often happens slowly, across months or years. One late handover means nothing to a judge. Forty of them, each written down the same day, tell a story.

A record also protects you from your own memory. Stress makes memory patchy, and patchy memory can be used against you. See Memory, and doubting yourself.

How do I document post-separation abuse?

Use four lines for each incident. That’s a complete record. It doesn’t need to be longer.

  1. When. The date, and the time if you have it.
  2. What happened. The act, in plain words. Not “he was difficult at handover” but “arrived 40 minutes late, and said in front of the children that I had made them wait.”
  3. Who saw it. A name, or “nobody.” Both are worth writing.
  4. What it changed. The missed appointment, the money not paid, the child upset that evening, the route you changed.

Written the same day, four lines is worth more than a detailed account written six months later. That’s the whole reason to do it while it’s boring.

The record has a template you can copy.

Write what you saw and heard, not what you think it meant. “She said she’d make sure I never see the kids” is evidence. “She’s trying to destroy me” is a conclusion.

What should I document?

  • Handovers: time agreed, time they arrived, what was said, who was there.
  • Missed or late payments: due date, date paid, amount.
  • Messages and calls: keep them all, including the ugly ones.
  • Contact that breaks an agreement or order, however small.
  • Contact through other people: who passed on what, and when.
  • Things your children say, in their words, without prompting them. See When your child is pulled into it.
  • Turning up, following, watching. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.
  • Reports you make: police reference numbers, the officer’s name, the date.
  • Effects on you: time off work, doctor visits, therapy, costs.

How do I keep messages as evidence?

  • Screenshot with context: the date, the time, and the sender’s name or number in the shot.
  • Export whole conversations where you can. Most messaging apps have an export option. A whole thread is harder to dismiss than a single cropped image.
  • Keep the originals. Don’t delete them from your phone, even after screenshotting.
  • Save voicemails as audio files.
  • Print the most important ones, and date the printouts.

Be aware that messages can be faked or edited, and you may be accused of it. Keeping originals and full exports protects you. The Decoder’s page on false reports covers what happens when the other side fabricates claims.

Can I record calls or conversations?

It depends on where you live. In some places you can record a conversation you’re part of without telling the other person. In others, everyone must agree. Recording without the right consent can be a crime, and it can count against you in court. Search “[your country or state] recording conversation consent law,” or ask a lawyer first.

How do I turn a record into a timeline?

When you need it for a lawyer, a court or the police:

  1. List incidents in date order: one line each.
  2. Group them by type: handovers, money, messages, children.
  3. Mark the ones with evidence attached.
  4. Add a short summary at the top: what the pattern is, over what period, with a count. “Since the order on 4 June, 63 messages outside the agreed app, 11 late handovers, 4 missed payments.”

That summary is often what a busy professional reads first.

Where should I keep it?

Somewhere entirely separate from any device or account the person you left ever touched:

  • a notebook kept at work, or with a friend
  • an email account that only you can open, with two-step login
  • a document in a cloud account they never had access to
  • a dedicated app, if a domestic abuse service recommends one

Check Surveillance and technology first, so your record isn’t being read.

How do I stop this taking over my life?

A record is a habit, not a project. A few minutes when something happens is enough. If you find yourself writing for hours every evening, or rereading it to feel angry, step back. You’re documenting what happened to you, not running an investigation into them.

Systems abuse and litigation abuse

What is litigation abuse, and how do I prove it in family court?

The short answer

Litigation abuse is using the legal system to keep control over a former partner: repeated filings, delays, false complaints and endless applications that drain your time, money and peace. Courts can limit it. You prove it by showing the pattern, with dates, outcomes and costs.

The court was supposed to settle things. Instead it became the place where the fight continues. Every few months there’s a new application, a new allegation, a new hearing. Each one costs you money, time off work and sleep.

What is litigation abuse?

Litigation abuse is when someone uses the courts to harass, control or wear down another person. People also call it legal abuse, abusive litigation or paper abuse. When it spreads to other agencies too, it’s sometimes called systems abuse.

It can look like:

  • repeated applications about the same issue, often with little basis
  • applications timed around holidays, exams, your new job or your health
  • delay: missing deadlines, asking for adjournments, refusing to share documents
  • false or exaggerated allegations to police, child services or the court
  • complaints to your employer, landlord, regulator or licensing body
  • refusing to settle, so everything goes to a full hearing
  • representing themselves so they can question you directly, where that’s allowed

The Decoder covers this as legal abuse, administrative escalation when it spreads to other agencies, and legal and administrative threats.

If the legal system itself feels like part of what’s happening to you, you may be right.

Is litigation abuse coercive control?

It can be. Many researchers and services see litigation abuse as one of the main ways coercive control continues after separation. Some laws now name it: in England and Wales, the official guidance on controlling or coercive behaviour lists using legal proceedings as a form of it. The criminal law rarely punishes it directly, but family courts can take it into account.

How do I prove litigation abuse in family court?

By showing the pattern, plainly:

  • A timeline of every application, complaint and report: date, what was claimed, the outcome.
  • The outcomes: how many were dismissed, withdrawn or found to have no basis.
  • The costs: legal fees, time off work, travel.
  • The timing: what was happening in your life when each one landed.
  • The effect on the children, if any: missed school, distress around hearings.

Present it calmly and with numbers: “Since the final order in March 2024, there have been seven applications. Six were dismissed. All seven were filed within two weeks of a school holiday.” What to document and how shows how to build this.

Can a court stop repeated applications?

Yes, in many places:

  • England and Wales: family courts can make a barring order under section 91(14) of the Children Act 1989, so a parent needs permission before making new applications. The Domestic Abuse Act 2021 made clear these orders can be used to protect victims of abuse. Civil courts can also make civil restraint orders.
  • United States: many states let courts declare someone a vexatious litigant, so they need permission to file. Some states, including Georgia and Washington, have laws aimed at abusive litigation in family cases.
  • Australia: family courts can restrict proceedings considered vexatious.
  • Elsewhere: search “[your country] vexatious litigant family court.”

Ask your lawyer whether it’s worth applying for one.

What helps while it’s happening?

  • A lawyer who has seen this before, and who treats each filing as part of a pattern rather than a fresh crisis. See Legal help with little or no money.
  • Respond to every deadline. Missing one can hand them a win.
  • Don’t match them. Counter-applications can make you both look high-conflict.
  • Pace yourself. This is often long. Plan your energy as if it will be.
  • Ask about protections in court, so you don’t have to face them alone. See Your day in court.

Legal help with little or no money

Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?

The short answer

Free legal help for domestic violence exists in most countries: legal aid, domestic abuse legal services, law school clinics, free advice lines and court help desks. Many people who qualify never ask. Call one, say you’ve experienced domestic abuse, and ask what they can offer.

The cost of a lawyer stops many people from asking for protections they’re entitled to. You may have more options than you think, and none of them are well advertised.

Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?

Check each of these, one at a time.

Legal aid. Government-funded legal help for people on lower incomes. Many countries give priority or extra eligibility to people who have experienced domestic abuse.

  • England and Wales: legal aid for family cases is usually available if you pass the means test and can show evidence of domestic abuse. Evidence can be a protective order, a police report, or a letter from a doctor or a domestic abuse service.
  • Scotland: apply through the Scottish Legal Aid Board.
  • United States: legal aid organisations in every state help with protective orders and family law. LawHelp.org lists them by state.
  • Canada: each province has a legal aid plan, and many prioritise family violence cases.
  • Australia: Legal Aid commissions in each state, community legal centres, and specialist family violence legal services.
  • Ireland: the Legal Aid Board provides family law services.

Domestic abuse legal services. Many domestic abuse organisations have their own lawyers or advocates, or refer to lawyers who specialise in abuse.

Law school clinics. Supervised students give free help, often with family law and protective orders.

Pro bono schemes. Some law firms take on cases for free. Ask your local bar association or law society.

Court help desks and self-help centres. Many courts have free help with forms and procedures, and some domestic abuse services provide court support workers.

Free advice lines. In the UK, Rights of Women runs a family law advice line for women. Many countries have similar lines.

When you call, say “I’ve experienced domestic abuse.” It can open doors that a general enquiry doesn’t.

What do I say on the first call?

Say: “I’ve left an abusive relationship and I need help with [a protective order / custody / divorce / a court date on (date)]. My income is about [amount]. What can you offer, and what do I need to bring?”

Have ready:

  • any court papers and deadlines
  • your income and benefits details
  • any evidence of abuse you already have, such as police reference numbers or your record. See What to document and how.

What if I don’t qualify for free help?

  • Limited-scope help: some lawyers will do one part of a case, like a single hearing or checking your forms, for a fixed fee.
  • A one-off consultation: often cheaper, and enough to understand your options.
  • Representing yourself, with support from a court help desk or a domestic abuse advocate.

What if I have to represent myself?

Many people do. Some things help:

  • Read the court’s guide for people without lawyers. Most courts publish one.
  • Keep everything in a folder, in date order.
  • Ask for protections if you’re afraid of the other side. In England and Wales, an abuser can’t cross-examine you in person in family court. See Your day in court.
  • Bring a supporter. Many courts allow a McKenzie friend or support person.

How do I find the right lawyer?

Look for someone with experience of domestic abuse and coercive control, not just family law. Ask:

Say: “How much of your work involves domestic abuse or coercive control? How would you approach repeated applications from the other side?”

Family court, honestly

Does family court recognise coercive control?

The short answer

Family courts vary a lot in how well they recognise coercive control, as opposed to physical violence. Some have made real progress; others still push for contact with both parents and miss non-physical abuse. Get advice from a lawyer who knows coercive control, and present a pattern with dates.

You may have heard that family court will see through them. You may have heard that it never does. Neither is reliably true. This chapter gives you the honest picture so you can plan for the court you will stand in.

Does family court recognise coercive control?

More than it used to, and less than it should.

Some places have made real changes. In England and Wales, a major government review in 2020 found that family courts often minimised domestic abuse, put too much weight on contact with both parents, and didn’t hear victims well. Reforms followed, including the Domestic Abuse Act 2021. In Canada, the Divorce Act now lists coercive and controlling behaviour as family violence that courts must consider. Several US states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, have written coercive control into their family or protective order laws. Australia’s family law changed in 2023 to put children’s safety first.

But practice varies by country, by court and by judge. Many courts still focus mostly on physical violence, and patterns of control without violence can be missed. The Decoder’s page on children used as leverage and legal abuse describe what courts often fail to see.

Neither “the court will understand” nor “the court never believes victims” is safe to assume. Find out how your court works.

Who will I meet?

Depending on where you are:

  • A judge or magistrates, who make the decisions.
  • A child’s representative: a guardian, a children’s lawyer or an independent children’s lawyer, who speaks for the child’s interests.
  • A family court adviser or evaluator, who interviews parents and children and writes a report. In England and Wales this is often Cafcass; in the US, a custody evaluator; in Australia, a family consultant.
  • A mediator, who tries to help parents agree.

Each of them is trying to work out what’s best for the children. A calm, clear account of the pattern, with the effect on the children, helps them most.

Should I go to mediation?

Not necessarily. Mediation assumes two people can negotiate fairly. Where there’s coercive control, that often isn’t true.

  • England and Wales: you usually have to attend a mediation meeting before applying to court, but domestic abuse is an exemption.
  • United States: many states let you opt out of mandatory mediation where there’s been domestic violence.
  • Australia: family dispute resolution is usually required, but there are exemptions for family violence.

If you do mediate, ask for separate rooms (“shuttle mediation”), and say you don’t feel safe negotiating directly.

Say: “There’s a history of domestic abuse and I don’t feel able to negotiate safely in the same room. I’d like an exemption, or shuttle mediation.”

How do I present a pattern rather than incidents?

Courts hear a lot of competing claims. What stands out is a calm summary backed by records:

  • Start with the pattern in one or two sentences. “Since we separated in May 2023, my ex has used the children’s handovers and the courts to keep control. There have been 23 late or missed handovers, 140 messages outside the agreed app, and five applications, four dismissed.”
  • Then the effect on the children. Courts decide for children, so tie each point to them.
  • Then the evidence, organised by type.
  • Avoid labels. Describe behaviour, not diagnoses. See Is my ex a narcissist?.

What to document and how shows how to build the timeline.

What about “parental alienation”?

You may be accused of turning the children against the other parent, especially if a child is reluctant to see them. This is one of the most disputed areas in family law.

What’s agreed: a parent can wrongly turn a child against the other parent, and that harms children. What’s disputed: how often it happens, how it should be judged, and whether claims of alienation are being used to discredit parents who raise abuse. Research in the US has found that when mothers alleged abuse and fathers then claimed alienation, courts often disbelieved the abuse.

If you’re accused:

  • stay calm and focus on the children
  • show that you support the children’s relationship with the other parent wherever it’s safe
  • explain the child’s reluctance with facts, not your interpretation
  • get a lawyer who knows this area well

See also When your child is pulled into it and When they say you’re the abuser.

How often do narcissists win in court?

People ask this a lot. There’s no reliable figure, and the word “narcissist” won’t be used in court. What’s true is that charming, confident people can do well in short hearings, and that calm, organised parents with clear records do better than anxious ones with long lists of grievances. You can’t control how they come across. You can control how prepared you are.

What can I control?

  • How well documented the pattern is.
  • How calm and child-focused you are.
  • Whether you have a lawyer who understands coercive control. See Legal help with little or no money.
  • Whether you ask for protections at court. See Your day in court.
  • Whether you meet every deadline.

Your day in court

What should I expect at a family court hearing with an abusive ex?

The short answer

What to expect at a family court hearing: a waiting area, often a short hearing, and a judge who has read some of the papers. Prepare a folder, ask in advance for protections like a separate waiting room or a screen, bring a supporter, and plan how you’ll arrive and leave safely.

The date is set. You’ve been dreading it for weeks, and you want to know what to expect at a family court hearing. This chapter walks you through the day, so at least the practical parts hold no surprises.

What should I do before the hearing?

Ask for protections early. Contact the court, your lawyer, or a court support worker at least a week before, and ask what’s available:

  • a separate waiting room
  • a separate entrance, or arriving and leaving at different times
  • a screen so you can’t see the other person, or giving evidence by video link
  • in England and Wales, victims of domestic abuse are automatically eligible for these measures in family court, and the other person can’t cross-examine you in person

Say: “There’s a history of domestic abuse in this case and I’m frightened of the other party. Which protections can I have at the hearing, and how do I request them?”

Prepare a folder.

  • every court document, in date order
  • your timeline summary on top. See What to document and how.
  • copies of key evidence, flagged
  • a list of the points you want to make, in order
  • a notebook and pen

Plan the day. How you’ll get there, where you’ll park, who’s coming with you, and how you’ll leave without meeting them.

Bring a supporter. A friend, a family member, or a domestic abuse court advocate. Ask the court in advance whether they can come into the hearing.

Wear something comfortable that you’d wear to a work meeting.

The court day checklist puts this on one page.

What happens on the day?

  • Arrive early. Tell security or the usher you’ve asked for a separate waiting room.
  • You may wait a long time. Bring water, a snack, something to read, and your charger.
  • Lawyers may negotiate outside the room. You don’t have to agree to anything you’re not sure about. Ask for time.
  • The hearing itself may be short, sometimes 20 or 30 minutes. The judge has usually read some of the papers.

The Decoder’s page on intimidation describes looks, gestures and comments meant to unsettle you. If anything happens at court, tell your lawyer or the usher straight away, and write it down afterwards.

How do I speak in the hearing?

  • Speak slowly, and address the judge as the court tells you to.
  • Answer the question asked, and stop.
  • Stick to facts, dates and the effect on the children.
  • If you don’t understand a question, say so.
  • If you freeze or cry, that’s all right. Ask for a moment, take a breath, look at your notes.
  • Don’t respond to anything the other side says directly to you. Speak only to the judge.

You don’t have to be perfect. You have to be clear, calm enough, and focused on the children.

What if they’re representing themselves?

That can mean they’re allowed to speak a lot, or try to question you. In England and Wales, they can’t cross-examine you in person if there’s evidence of domestic abuse; the court appoints a lawyer to ask their questions instead. Elsewhere, ask the court in advance what protections apply.

What happens after?

  • Get a copy of any order before you leave, or ask when it will arrive.
  • Read it carefully. Check dates, times and conditions.
  • Give copies to the school or anyone else who needs them.
  • Plan for the next few days. Court dates are among the times when risk can rise. See A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  • Look after yourself. Most people feel wrecked after a hearing, whatever the result. Plan something gentle.

Divorce and separation proceedings

What should I know about divorcing a narcissist or an abusive spouse?

The short answer

Divorcing a narcissist or any controlling spouse often takes longer and costs more than an ordinary divorce, because delay, hidden money and conflict can be tactics. Get advice from a lawyer who understands abuse, gather financial records early, ask for exemptions from mediation if needed, and expect it to take time.

A divorce is hard. A divorce from someone who wants to keep control can feel endless. This chapter covers what tends to be different, and what helps.

What is different about divorcing a narcissist?

Whatever word fits the person, controlling spouses often:

  • delay: missing deadlines, refusing to sign, asking for adjournments
  • hide money: moving it, undervaluing a business, “forgetting” accounts
  • fight everything: refusing reasonable offers so every issue needs a hearing
  • use the children as bargaining chips in the financial case. See Children as the channel.
  • play for sympathy: presenting as the calm, wronged party

The Decoder’s pages on financial control and time control describe the moves.

Delay is often a tactic. Plan as if it will be.

How does divorce work, broadly?

The details differ by country and state, but the main stages are similar:

  1. Starting the divorce. Many places now allow no-fault divorce, so you don’t have to prove what they did. England and Wales introduced no-fault divorce in 2022.
  2. Arrangements for the children. Agreed between you, through mediation, or decided by a court.
  3. The financial settlement. Both sides disclose their finances; then the money, property and pensions are divided by agreement or by a court.
  4. The final order, which ends the marriage legally.

Separating from an unmarried partner usually has no divorce stage, but property and children may still need court orders.

What about hidden money?

Both sides usually have to give full financial disclosure, under oath or with a statement of truth. Gather what you can early:

  • bank, savings and credit card statements
  • pay slips, tax returns, business accounts
  • pension statements
  • mortgage, property and loan documents
  • photos of anything valuable in the home

If you think money is hidden, tell your lawyer. Courts can order more disclosure, and some can penalise someone who hides assets. A forensic accountant may help in larger cases.

Do I have to go to mediation?

Not always. Where there’s domestic abuse, many places offer exemptions, and mediation may not be safe. See the mediation section of Family court, honestly.

How do I keep costs down?

  • Use free and low-cost legal help where you can: Legal help with little or no money.
  • Prepare well, so your lawyer’s time goes further: organised documents, a clear timeline, a list of questions.
  • Don’t use your lawyer for emotional support. A friend, a domestic abuse advocate or a therapist costs less.
  • Ask whether parts of the case can be settled separately.
  • If they keep filing, see Systems abuse and litigation abuse.

How long will it take?

Longer than an ordinary divorce, usually. Some take months; some take years. You can’t control their delays. You can control your own deadlines, your records and your energy.

What about the house, the car and the accounts?

The divorce settles who gets what. You’ll still need to change names, remove yourself from accounts and update beneficiaries. See Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork.

Protective orders

How does a protective order work, and is it worth getting?

The short answer

A protective order is a court order that tells someone to stay away from you and stop contacting you, and breaking it can lead to arrest. How a protective order works depends on where you live, but most places offer emergency, temporary and longer-term orders. It’s a real tool, not a guarantee, so pair it with a safety plan.

If the person you left keeps contacting you, turning up or threatening you, a protective order can put a legal line around you. Crossing it becomes a matter for the police.

If you’re in danger right now, call your emergency number. If you’re not safe right now has the numbers.

How does a protective order work?

A protective order is a court order that can:

  • ban contact, including messages and contact through other people
  • order them to stay a set distance from you, your home, your work or your children’s school
  • order them out of a shared home
  • in some places, deal with temporary custody, firearms, or a shared pet

If they break it, the police can act. In many places a breach is a crime in itself.

What types are there?

Most places have three levels:

  1. Emergency orders. Issued fast, sometimes by police or a judge on the same day, often without the other person present. They last days.
  2. Temporary or interim orders. Last until a full hearing, usually weeks.
  3. Longer-term or final orders. Made after a hearing where both sides can be heard. They often last months or years, and can usually be renewed.

How do I apply?

  • Through a court: most courts have forms and help desks for protective orders. Domestic abuse services can help you fill them in, and in many places there’s no fee.
  • Through the police: in some places the police can apply for you, or issue an emergency order themselves.
  • Through a lawyer, often free for protective orders. See Legal help with little or no money.

What do I write in the application?

  • The most recent incident, in plain words, with the date.
  • The pattern: earlier incidents, with dates. Your record does this work. See What to document and how.
  • Any threats, weapons, strangling or stalking. These carry weight. See Warning signs to take seriously.
  • What you’re afraid will happen.
  • What you’re asking the court to order.

Stick to what happened. Leave out labels and long histories that don’t relate to your safety.

What happens at the hearing?

Usually short. The judge reads your application, may ask you questions, and hears from the other person if they attend. Bring your evidence and a supporter. Ask for protections such as a separate waiting room. See Your day in court.

The other person may deny everything, or apply for an order against you. Stay calm. See When they say you’re the abuser.

How long does a protective order last?

It depends on the type and the place. Emergency orders last days, temporary orders weeks, and longer orders anywhere from months to several years. Note the end date, and diary a reminder a month before so you can ask for a renewal if you still need it.

Do protective orders work?

They help many people. They also have limits.

What an order can do: create a legal basis for the police to act; make a formal record; deter many people, especially those who care about consequences.

What it can’t do: physically stop someone determined to break it. Police responses also vary by place and by officer.

A protective order is a real tool, not a shield. Plan for the gap between a breach and the response.

What do I do if they break it?

  1. If you’re in danger, call your emergency number and say there’s an order.
  2. Write down what happened, when and where, and who saw it.
  3. Keep messages, call logs and photos.
  4. Report every breach, however small, and ask for a reference number each time.

A pattern of recorded breaches is often what produces stronger action. The Decoder’s page on intimidation describes the borderline behaviour people use to test an order.

Where should I keep the order?

Keep a copy on you, and give copies to your children’s school, your workplace and anyone else who might need to call the police. Photograph it on your phone.

Police and reporting

Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?

The short answer

Yes, you can report post-separation abuse to the police. Threats, stalking, harassment, criminal damage, breaking an order and, in some places, coercive control are crimes. Report early, describe the pattern with dates, and ask for a reference number every time.

Many people wait until something dramatic happens before calling the police. But much of post-separation abuse is made of smaller things, and some of those smaller things are crimes.

If you’re in danger now, call your emergency number.

Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?

Yes. You can report:

  • threats, including threats sent by message
  • assault, of any kind
  • stalking and harassment. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.
  • criminal damage to your home, car or belongings
  • breaking a protective order. See Protective orders.
  • sharing or threatening to share intimate images. See Intimate images and online threats.
  • in some places, controlling or coercive behaviour itself, including after separation. See What post-separation abuse is.

What do I say so they hear the pattern?

Officers often see one incident at a time. Help them see the whole picture:

Say: “I’m reporting [today’s incident]. It’s part of a pattern since we separated in [month and year]. I have a record of [number] incidents, including [threats / turning up / messages after the order]. I’d like this recorded as domestic abuse.”

Bring or offer your record and evidence. See What to document and how.

What should I ask for?

  • A reference or incident number. Every time.
  • The officer’s name and badge number.
  • What happens next, and when you’ll hear.
  • A referral to a domestic abuse service, if you don’t already have one.
  • Whether a risk assessment will be done. Many police services use one for domestic abuse.

What is Clare’s Law?

Some places let you ask the police whether someone has a history of abuse. In England and Wales this is the Domestic Violence Disclosure Scheme, known as Clare’s Law. Scotland and parts of Australia have similar schemes. It can help if you’re worried about a new partner, or about someone your ex is now seeing.

What if they make a false report about me?

Stay calm, get legal advice before an interview, and bring your record. The Decoder’s page on false reports describes the tactic, and When they say you’re the abuser covers what to do.

What if the police response is poor?

It happens. If you were dismissed, not believed, or the report wasn’t recorded:

  • ask to speak to a supervisor, or to a specialist domestic abuse officer
  • ask a domestic abuse service to advocate for you
  • make a formal complaint. Most police forces have a complaints process, and many countries have an independent police complaints body.

Keep reporting. Each report adds to the record, even when nothing seems to happen.

Do I have to report?

No. Some people don’t feel safe involving the police, or have had bad experiences with them. That’s your decision. You can still keep a record, get a protective order through a court, and get support from a domestic abuse service.

Housing and benefits

Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?

The short answer

Housing help after domestic abuse can include refuge, emergency housing, priority for social housing, the right to end a lease early, and help to stay safely in your own home. Benefits and child support can provide a floor. A local domestic abuse service usually knows exactly what’s available where you live.

Money and housing are often the biggest reasons people feel stuck. This chapter covers help for the months after you leave, as well as the first days.

Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?

The options usually include:

  • Refuge or shelter: safe, confidential housing, usually short term, often with support workers.
  • Emergency housing through your local council or housing authority.
  • Priority for social housing. In England, people made homeless by domestic abuse automatically have priority need for housing help.
  • Help to stay in your own home safely: better locks, alarms and other changes, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.
  • Transitional housing: longer stays with support, in some places.

Your local domestic abuse service is the best first call. They know what’s free near you, and how long the waits are.

Can I end my lease early?

In many places, yes:

  • England and Wales: options depend on your tenancy. Councils and housing associations often have domestic abuse policies, and joint tenancies can sometimes be transferred into your name.
  • Australia: most states let victims end a tenancy early with evidence of family violence.
  • Canada: several provinces, including Ontario and British Columbia, let survivors end a tenancy early with notice.

  • Emergency funds from domestic abuse charities.
  • Help with legal costs. See Legal help with little or no money.

What do I need to change after leaving an abusive relationship?

The short answer

The first month is about safety. After that comes the slow job of untangling your life from theirs. It’s boring, and it matters: a forgotten joint account or an old insurance form can keep you tied to them for years.

What should I change after leaving an abusive relationship?

Co-parenting or parallel parenting?

Can you co-parent with a narcissist, or is parallel parenting better?

The short answer

Co-parenting with a narcissist or any controlling ex often fails, because co-parenting needs trust and good-faith talk. Parallel parenting is the alternative: each parent runs their own home, contact between you is minimal and written, and the plan spells out the details so there’s little to argue about.

Every parenting guide says the same thing: work together for the children. That’s good advice when both parents act in good faith. When one parent uses every conversation to control or punish, “working together” can become another channel for abuse.

Can you co-parent with a narcissist?

Usually not in the way the guides mean. Co-parenting assumes you can talk, compromise, swap weekends and make decisions together. With a controlling ex, those conversations become openings: for criticism, for pressure, for using the children. The Decoder calls this children used as leverage.

That doesn’t mean your children lose. It means you need a different model.

What is parallel parenting?

Parallel parenting is a way of raising children in two homes with as little contact between the parents as possible. Psychologists who work with high-conflict families developed it. In practice:

  • Each home has its own rules. You don’t try to agree on bedtimes, screens or food. You run your home; they run theirs.
  • Contact is minimal and written. One channel, usually a co-parenting app or email, used for logistics only.
  • The plan is detailed. Exchange times and places, holidays, who decides what, how to handle illness and school events. The more the plan settles in advance, the less there is to fight about.
  • Decisions are split. One parent may handle schooling and the other medical appointments, or decisions follow a set process.
  • Handovers are neutral. At school, in a public place, or through a third person. See Handovers.

Parallel parenting takes the fight out of your children’s lives.

Is parallel parenting bad for kids?

Research on children and divorce consistently finds that ongoing conflict between parents harms children. Parallel parenting reduces that conflict. Children adapt well to two homes with different rules, as long as each home is warm and predictable. What hurts them is being in the middle.

Can parallel parenting be court ordered?

In many places courts can order arrangements that work like parallel parenting: communication only through an app, exchanges at school or a contact centre, and decision-making split between parents.   1. Move all contact to one written channel. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.

  3. Stop discussing anything that isn’t logistics. The Decoder’s page on controlled communication shows how talk gets used; parallel parenting removes the talk.
  4. Let go of their home. You can’t control what happens there, unless it’s unsafe. Focus on making yours steady.
  5. Ask a court to formalise it if they won’t stick to it.

What do I say to my children?

Keep it simple: “In Dad’s house there are Dad’s rules, and in my house there are my rules. Both houses love you.” See Your children, and what to say.

Handovers

How do I make custody exchanges safe with an abusive ex?

The short answer

A safe custody exchange keeps contact between you and your ex to a minimum. Options include handovers at school, in a busy public place with cameras, through a trusted third person, or at a supervised contact centre. Keep exchanges short, and write down anything that goes wrong.

Handovers are where many co-parents meet. They’re also where much post-separation abuse happens: the long conversation at the door, the comments in front of the children, the “accidental” lateness.

How do I make custody exchanges safe?

Choose the option that gives the least contact you can arrange.

School or nursery handovers. One parent drops off in the morning; the other collects in the afternoon. You never meet. This is often the simplest option for school-age children.

A busy public place. A supermarket car park, a café, a library. Choose somewhere with cameras and other people. Some police stations have designated exchange areas or cameras in their car parks.

A trusted third person. A grandparent, friend or relative collects and returns the children. Choose someone calm, who won’t carry messages.

A supervised exchange or contact centre. Staff manage the handover so parents arrive and leave at different times and never meet. In the UK, search the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search “supervised exchange” plus your area, or the Supervised Visitation Network. Elsewhere, ask your court or a domestic abuse service.

Avoid handovers at your home if you can. If you must, hand over at the door, not inside.

A handover should take a minute. Hello, the bag, the medicine, goodbye.

What should the handover look like?

  • Arrive on time, and leave promptly.
  • Keep the children’s bags packed and ready.
  • Pass on only what’s needed: medicine, a school letter.
  • Don’t discuss anything else. If they start, say “I’ll reply in writing” and leave.
  • Keep your voice calm, for the children’s sake.

The Decoder describes the looks, comments and shows of force that often happen at handovers in intimidation, and the use of children as messengers in children used as leverage.

What do I say when they start something at the door?

Say: “I’m not discussing this now. Message me on the app.”

Then turn to the children: “Have a lovely time. See you Sunday.” And go.

What should I write down?

  • the agreed time and the time they arrived
  • anything said in front of the children
  • the children’s state when they returned
  • any change to arrangements made at the door

See What to document and how.

What if my child doesn’t want to go?

Children resist handovers for many reasons: tiredness, missing you, not wanting to stop playing, or something more serious. Stay calm and neutral. Don’t promise they can stay. Listen afterwards, without leading questions. If the resistance is strong or ongoing, read When your child is pulled into it and get advice before refusing contact, because refusing without a court order can count against you in some places.

What if handovers become dangerous?

Change the arrangement now: use a contact centre, involve the police, or ask the court to vary the order. Update your safety plan. Handovers and court dates are among the times when risk can rise.

Your children, and what to say

How do I explain the divorce, or the abuse, to my child?

The short answer

How to explain divorce to a child after abuse: give simple, true facts that fit their age, tell them it isn’t their fault and that they’re loved, and keep adult details out. Don’t use them as messengers, spies or confidants. Answer the questions they ask, not the ones you’re afraid of.

Children notice everything. Saying nothing leaves them to fill the gaps, often with the idea that it’s their fault. Saying too much burdens them with adult problems. The goal is the honest middle.

How do I explain divorce to a child?

Three messages matter at every age:

  1. This is a grown-up decision. “Mum and Dad decided to live in different houses.”
  2. It isn’t your fault. Say it plainly, and say it more than once.
  3. You are loved, and you’ll be looked after. Tell them what will stay the same: their school, their friends, their pets, their bedtime story.

Then fit the rest to their age.

What do I say to young children (under 5)?

Short, concrete, repeated. Young children need to know what happens to them, not why.

Say: “Daddy is going to live in a different house. You’ll sleep at my house on school nights and see Daddy at the weekend. We both love you. It’s not because of anything you did.”

Keep routines steady. Expect clinginess, tantrums or sleep changes. See How children react, and what helps them.

What do I say to children aged 6 to 11?

They’ll ask why. Give a true answer without blaming:

Say: “Mum and I weren’t able to keep our home calm and safe. Grown-ups sometimes can’t fix that together, so we decided to live apart. That’s our job to sort out, not yours.”

If they saw or heard abuse, you can name it without detail:

Say: “You saw some scary things happen. That wasn’t okay, and it wasn’t your fault. I’m keeping us safe now.”

What do I say to teenagers?

Teenagers often know more than you think. Be honest, answer their questions, and respect that they may have their own view of the other parent:

Say: “You’ve probably noticed things weren’t okay. I’m not going to ask you to take sides, and I won’t badmouth your dad. If you want to talk about anything you saw or felt, I’m here.”

Don’t make them your confidant. Their job is to be a teenager, not your support.

What should I never do?

  • Don’t use them as messengers. “Tell your mother she’s late with the money.”
  • Don’t ask them to report back. “Who was at Dad’s house?”
  • Don’t share adult details: court, money, what the other parent did to you.
  • Don’t run down the other parent, even when they run you down. It forces children to defend half of themselves.
  • Don’t make them choose.

The Decoder’s page on parentification describes what happens when children are made to carry adult worries. This applies whatever the other parent is doing. Matching a harmful tactic costs the children, not the ex. See Children as the channel.

What if they ask questions I can’t answer?

Say: “That’s a good question. I don’t know yet, but I’ll tell you when I do.”

Or: “That’s a grown-up thing I’m sorting out. You don’t need to worry about it.”

What if they want to talk about the other parent?

Let them. Listen without reacting. If they love the other parent, let them. That isn’t disloyalty to you, and it isn’t a sign they didn’t see what happened. When your child is pulled into it covers what to do when what they say worries you.

How children react, and what helps them

What are the effects of domestic abuse on children, and how do I help?

The short answer

The effects of domestic abuse on children can include anxiety, sleep problems, anger, regression, trouble at school, and taking care of a parent. Many children recover well with a safe, steady home, a parent who listens, and help when they need it. Children who still love the other parent aren’t confused; they’re children.

You left partly for them. Now you’re watching them, worried about every tantrum and every quiet day. This chapter helps you know what’s common, what’s worth more attention, and what helps.

If a child is in danger, or talks about wanting to hurt themselves, contact emergency services or a crisis line now. If you’re not safe right now has the numbers.

What are the effects of domestic abuse on children?

Children don’t have to see violence to be affected by it. Research on coercive control finds children are harmed by living inside it: the fear and the tension count as much as anything they witness. In England and Wales, the law now recognises children as victims of domestic abuse in their own right.

Common reactions by age:

Babies and toddlers: sleep and feeding problems, clinginess, distress when separated, startling easily.

Young children (3 to 6): going back to earlier behaviour (bedwetting, baby talk), tantrums, fears, worry about you, playing out what they saw.

School-age children (6 to 11): stomach aches and headaches, trouble concentrating, anger or sadness, blaming themselves, trying to be “perfect,” worrying about the parent who isn’t with them.

Teenagers: withdrawal, anger, risk-taking, trouble at school, protecting a parent, taking on adult roles, mistrust in their own relationships.

The Decoder describes two patterns that can carry on after separation: parentification, when a child becomes a parent’s carer or confidant, and golden child elevation, when one child is favoured and another blamed.

Many children recover well. What helps most is a safe, steady home and at least one adult who listens.

What helps children recover?

  • Safety and routine. Regular meals, bedtimes and school. Predictability tells a child’s body the danger has passed.
  • Your calm, as much as you can manage. Children read your mood. You don’t have to be perfect; you can say “I’m sad today, but I’m okay, and it’s not because of you.”
  • Permission to feel. Let them be angry, sad, or happy to see the other parent.
  • Listening without leading. Ask open questions: “How was the weekend?” Not “Did Dad shout?”
  • Telling school. A teacher who knows what’s happening can support them and notice changes.
  • Fun. Ordinary play and laughter are part of healing, not a distraction from it.

When should I get professional help?

Talk to your child’s doctor or school counsellor if:

  • reactions are getting worse, not easing, after a few months
  • they talk about hurting themselves, or about not wanting to be alive
  • they can’t go to school, or can’t sleep, for long stretches
  • they’re hurting others or animals
  • they tell you about being hurt, threatened or asked to keep secrets

Therapies with good evidence for children after abuse include trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, and child-parent psychotherapy for young children. Ask for a therapist with experience of domestic abuse. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

What if my child still loves the other parent?

Most do. That isn’t a sign they didn’t notice, or that you were wrong to leave. Children can love a parent and be hurt by them at the same time. Let them love both. Don’t ask them to see the other parent as you do.

What if my child is angry with me?

Children often direct anger at the safe parent, because it’s safe to. It can feel deeply unfair. Hold steady, name the feeling (“You’re so angry that we moved”), and keep the rules. It usually eases as they feel more secure.

What about me?

Your recovery helps theirs. Looking after yourself isn’t selfish. See What your nervous system is doing, and When your child is pulled into it for problems that come from the other home.

When your child is pulled into it

What do I do when my child repeats what the other parent says about me?

The short answer

When a child is repeating what the other parent says about you, stay calm, correct the fact gently, and tell them it’s not their job to sort out grown-up problems. Don’t question them about the other home. If they’re asked to spy, keep secrets or refuse visits, keep a record and get advice.

Your seven-year-old says, “Daddy says you broke up our family because you’re selfish.” Your teenager won’t come back after a weekend away. Your child asks why you “don’t let” them see their other parent more.

A child repeating what the other parent says about you is one of the most common ways children get pulled into the conflict. This chapter helps you respond without making it worse for them.

If your child talks about being hurt or threatened, or about harming themselves, contact the police, child protection services or a crisis line now. If you’re not safe right now has the numbers.

What do I say when my child repeats something cruel about me?

Take a breath first. Your reaction teaches them whether it’s safe to tell you things.

Say: “I can see that’s confusing. That isn’t true, and you don’t need to worry about it. Grown-ups sort out grown-up things. I love you, and that won’t change.”

Then move on. Don’t ask what else was said. Don’t attack the other parent, even though you may want to. Write it down later, in your child’s words, with the date. See What to document and how.

What if my child is being asked to spy or keep secrets?

Children are often asked “Who’s at Mum’s house?” or “What did she buy?” Tell your child, without blaming:

Say: “You don’t have to answer questions about my house. You can just say ‘I don’t know’ or ‘ask Mum.’ And you never have to keep secrets from me that make you feel worried.”

What about gifts and promises?

Big gifts, special treats, or promises like “you can live with me full time” are common, especially around court dates. Don’t compete. Don’t criticise the gift. Keep your home steady and your promises small and kept.

What if my child refuses to see the other parent?

Children refuse visits for many reasons: fear, something that happened, loyalty pressure, wanting to stay with friends, or not wanting to leave you alone. Find out gently, without leading questions.

  • If you believe the child is unsafe there, get legal advice urgently, and contact child protection services if there’s immediate risk.
  • If there’s a court order, don’t stop contact on your own without advice. In many places that can count against you. Ask your lawyer about asking the court to review it.
  • Keep a record of what the child says and does around visits.

What if my child refuses to see me?

This is one of the most painful things a parent can go through. Keep the door open: send short, warm messages, turn up when you’re supposed to, don’t blame the child. Get legal advice, and keep a record. If you’re accused of being the cause, see When they say you’re the abuser and the parental alienation section of Family court, honestly.

When should I get help?

If your child seems frightened of the other parent, talks about being hurt, shows sudden changes in behaviour, or is struggling for weeks, talk to their doctor, school or a child therapist. See How children react, and what helps them.

Apps, calendars and the parenting plan

Which co-parenting app is court approved, and what goes in a parenting plan?

The short answer

A court-approved co-parenting app keeps every message timestamped and undeletable, which protects you and calms things down. Courts often order them in high-conflict cases. Pair the app with a detailed parenting plan that settles schedules, holidays and decisions in advance, so there’s less to argue about.

The less you have to negotiate, the less there is to fight about. Two tools do most of that work: a co-parenting app and a detailed parenting plan.

Which co-parenting app is court approved?

Courts don’t usually “approve” a single brand, but many order parents to use a dedicated co-parenting app in high-conflict cases. Well-known options include OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents and AppClose. Look for:

  • messages that can’t be edited or deleted, with timestamps
  • a record you can export for your lawyer or the court
  • a shared calendar for the schedule
  • an expense tracker for shared costs
  • tone-checking features, in some apps, that flag hostile wording before it’s sent

Some apps charge a fee; some courts can waive it. Ask your lawyer which apps your local court is used to.

Moving, passports and travel with children

Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child, and how do I stop them taking the children abroad?

The short answer

Moving away from an abusive ex with your child usually needs the other parent’s agreement or a court’s permission, especially across borders, even when you’re the one escaping abuse. If you fear your ex will take the children abroad, passport alerts and court orders can help. Get legal advice before you move.

You want distance. A fresh start near family, or simply further away. But moving with children after separation has legal rules, and getting them wrong can hurt your case.

Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child?

Often yes, but usually not on your own say-so once the other parent has rights:

  • Within the same country: many places need the other parent’s agreement, or a court order, for a move that affects contact. Courts look at the reasons for the move, the effect on the child, and how contact would work.
  • To another country: almost always needs the other parent’s written consent or a court order. Taking a child abroad without it can be child abduction, even for a parent fleeing abuse.

Courts can and do allow moves where abuse is part of the reason. Explain the abuse, the reasons for the move, and how contact with the other parent will work, if it’s safe. Your record helps. See What to document and how.

What if I’ve already moved to escape?

How do I stop my ex taking the children abroad?

If you fear abduction:

  • United States: enrol your children in the State Department’s Children’s Passport Issuance Alert Program, so you’re told if anyone applies for a US passport for them. Both parents usually have to consent to a passport for a child under 16.
  • United Kingdom: apply for a prohibited steps order to stop a child being removed from the country. If removal is imminent, contact the police, who can issue an all-ports alert. You can also ask HM Passport Office not to issue a passport.
  • Elsewhere: ask a family lawyer about court orders that stop travel, and whether passports can be held by the court.

What if the children are taken abroad?

Act fast. Contact the police and a family lawyer. If the country is part of the 1980 Hague Convention on child abduction, there’s a process to ask for the children’s return. The trauma bond: why you miss someone who hurt you

What is a trauma bond, and how do I break it?

The short answer

A trauma bond is a strong attachment to someone who hurt you, built by cycles of harm and warmth. It explains why you can miss an abusive ex, defend them, or want to go back, even when you know you shouldn’t. It isn’t weakness, and it loosens with distance, time and support.

You know what they did. You’ve listed it. You’d never tell a friend to go back to someone like that. And still, at 11pm, you want to hear their voice.

That pull has a name.

What is a trauma bond?

A trauma bond is a powerful emotional attachment that forms between a person and someone who harms them, especially when the harm comes in cycles with warmth in between. The term comes from research in the early 1980s by psychologists Donald Dutton and Susan Painter, who studied why people stayed attached to abusive partners.

Two things build it:

  • A power imbalance. One person holds more control.
  • Intermittent harm and kindness. Cruelty, then relief. Coldness, then the person you fell for. You never know which you’ll get.

That unpredictable mix creates a stronger bond than steady kindness would. Behavioural scientists have known since the 1950s that rewards that come unpredictably are the hardest habits to break. The Decoder covers this as intermittent reinforcement and the bond itself as trauma bonding.

“Trauma bond” is a widely used term, not a medical diagnosis. It describes something real that many people recognise.

The pull you feel doesn’t mean you should go back. It means the bond was built well.

What are the signs of a trauma bond?

  • You miss them intensely, even remembering what they did.
  • You defend them, or explain their behaviour, to others or to yourself.
  • You remember the good times more vividly than the bad.
  • You feel you’re the only one who understands them.
  • You believe they’ll change if you’re patient enough.
  • You feel relief, not just anger, when they’re kind after being cruel.
  • You keep checking their social media. See Checking their profile.
  • Leaving felt, or still feels, like losing part of yourself.

What does trauma bond withdrawal feel like?

After leaving, many people describe something like withdrawal: obsessive thoughts, cravings to make contact, anxiety, low mood, poor sleep. Brain scans of people recently rejected in love show activity in reward and craving areas that overlap with addiction. That doesn’t mean love is a drug, but it explains why the urge can feel physical.

It peaks in the early weeks and months for many people, and it often spikes when they reach out. See Hoovering.

How do I break a trauma bond?

There’s no quick method. These are the things that tend to loosen it:

  • Distance. No contact, or the least contact you can manage. Every contact restarts the cycle. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • No intermittent contact. Occasional replies feed the bond more than steady silence.
  • Tell the story straight. Write down what happened, plainly, in order. Read it when the good memories crowd in.
  • Name the pull. “This is the bond, not a sign.” Naming it puts a little space between you and it.
  • Replace the comfort. The relationship met needs, even badly. Find other sources: friends, routine, rest, things you enjoy.
  • Support. A therapist who understands coercive control can help, especially if the bond keeps pulling you back. See Finding a therapist who understands this.
  • Patience with yourself. You’re unlearning something your body learned well.

How long does a trauma bond last?

There’s no reliable number, and you’ll be told many. It depends on how long the relationship lasted, how intense it was, whether you still have contact, and what support you have. Most people find the pull weakens noticeably once contact stops, with flare-ups on anniversaries or when the person reappears. It fades in waves, not in a straight line.

Trauma bond vs love: how do I tell the difference?

Love tends to feel safe and steady, even when it’s passionate. A trauma bond tends to feel urgent, anxious and consuming. Love survives your “no.” A trauma bond punishes it. You can have loved them and also be trauma bonded to them. The two often sit together, which is part of why it’s confusing.

Can a trauma bond become a healthy relationship?

Rarely, and only if the person who caused harm changes deeply, over a long time, with accountability and without pressure on you. Going back to test whether they’ve changed usually restarts the cycle. If you’re considering it, read If you go back and Trust as a practice first.

Is something wrong with me for feeling this?

No. Trauma bonds form in ordinary people under particular conditions. They say more about the pattern you were in than about your judgment or strength. What you’re allowed to feel about someone you left covers the mix of feelings this leaves behind.

What you're allowed to feel about someone you left

Why do I miss my abuser, and is it normal?

The short answer

If you’re asking “why do I miss my abuser,” you’re not alone. Missing someone who hurt you, feeling tenderness, relief and grief at once, is common and doesn’t mean leaving was wrong. Feelings don’t need to match the story other people expect. You’re allowed all of them.

You might miss them. At certain times: a time of evening, a smell, a song in the supermarket. You might remember something kind and feel a rush of warmth, alongside a clear knowledge that leaving was right. You might feel relief and grief in the same hour.

Why do I miss my abuser?

Because the relationship wasn’t only harm. There were real moments of closeness, real plans, maybe real love on your side. And because the bond was built by cycles of hurt and warmth, which make attachment stronger. The trauma bond explains that part.

Missing someone isn’t the same as wanting them back. You can hold both without either one cancelling the other.

Human attachment doesn’t switch off because the person turned out to be harmful. That isn’t a flaw in you.

What feelings are normal after leaving?

All of these are common, alone or together:

  • missing them, sometimes intensely
  • relief, even joy
  • grief for the relationship you hoped for. See The grief nobody will let you have.
  • anger, sometimes much later. See Anger, arriving late.
  • guilt and shame. See Guilt and shame.
  • fear
  • numbness
  • tenderness, or pity for them
  • loneliness, even in a full room

None of these feelings is evidence that the relationship wasn’t harmful, or that you secretly want to go back.

Why does this need saying?

Because the people around you often expect a simpler story: relief, a bit of sadness, then fine. When your feelings don’t match, it’s easy to decide something’s wrong with you, when the story was simply too simple.

When will these feelings settle?

This book won’t give you a date. Any number would be more comforting than true. Feelings tend to soften in waves, with harder days around anniversaries, contact and change. However long it takes, and however contradictory it feels, it isn’t a sign you’re doing it wrong.

What helps?

  • Let the feelings be there without acting on them. Missing them doesn’t mean you have to text.
  • Talk to someone who can hear the complicated version without telling you what to feel.
  • Write the full story down, good and bad, so neither side takes over.
  • Be wary of people who rush you. Pressure to “move on” usually comes from their discomfort, not from your progress.

What if I feel nothing at all?

Numbness is common. It’s often the nervous system protecting you while it’s overloaded. Feelings usually return as things settle. See What your nervous system is doing.

What if I feel happy, then guilty for feeling happy?

Happiness after leaving isn’t a betrayal of anyone, including your children. You’re allowed good days in the middle of a hard year.

What if I want to reach out to them?

Notice the urge, and wait. Tell someone first. Read Hoovering and The trauma bond. The urge usually passes, and it passes faster each time you don’t act on it.

The grief nobody will let you have

Is grief after leaving an abusive relationship normal?

The short answer

Grief after leaving an abusive relationship is real grief. You may be mourning the person you thought they were, the future you planned, and years of your life. Others often only see the harm and expect relief, which can leave you grieving alone. You’re allowed to mourn something that also hurt you.

There’s a grief in this that almost nobody around you will let you have. Everyone who knows what happened sees the harm, and expects relief.

What am I grieving?

Not always the person, though sometimes that too. More often:

  • the relationship you hoped it would be
  • the future you planned: the house, the children, growing old
  • the version of them you fell for, who may have been real some of the time
  • the years you gave it
  • the family as it was, for your children
  • the person you were before

Why do people dismiss this grief?

Usually with good intentions: “But they were awful to you,” as if that settles it. It’s as if grief needs its object to have been good. It doesn’t. People grieve difficult parents and painful marriages all the time.

Grief that other people don’t recognise has a name: disenfranchised grief. It’s harder to carry because you carry it alone.

You can grieve the loss of something that was also harming you. Mourning one doesn’t mean you’re confused about the other.

Is grief after leaving an abusive relationship normal?

Yes. It’s one of the most common experiences after leaving, and one of the least talked about. It can arrive straight away, or months later, once the practical crisis has passed and there’s room to feel it. It’s often tangled with relief, anger and longing. See What you’re allowed to feel about someone you left.

What do I do with it?

  • Let it be grief. You don’t need anyone’s permission for it to be real.
  • Find one person who can hold it. If friends can’t, a therapist or support group can. See Finding a therapist who understands this.
  • Mark the losses. Some people write a letter they never send, or mark the day the future they planned would have happened.
  • Watch for hard dates. Anniversaries can bring grief back with force. See The anniversary effect.
  • Don’t confuse grief with a reason to return. Missing the hoped-for future is not the same as that future being available. See The trauma bond.

When should I get more help?

If grief stops you functioning for a long time, or turns into hopelessness that won’t lift, talk to a doctor or therapist. If you’re thinking about harming yourself, go to When it’s more than aftermath.

Can I grieve while I’m still fighting them in court?

Yes. Many people grieve and fight at the same time. The grief may surface in odd moments: in the car after a hearing, or at night. It’s part of the same experience, not a contradiction.

How long does grief like this last?

It doesn’t follow a timetable. It comes in waves that tend to get further apart. Some losses, like the family you hoped your children would have, may be felt again at milestones for years. That’s grief doing its job.

What about grieving for my children’s family?

Many parents grieve the family their children didn’t get. Your children can still have a loving home, or two homes, even if it isn’t the one you pictured.

Guilt and shame

Why do I feel guilty after leaving an abusive relationship?

The short answer

Guilt after leaving an abusive relationship is very common: guilt for leaving, for staying so long, for the children, for not seeing it sooner. Much of it was planted by the relationship itself. Guilt says you did something wrong; usually you didn’t. Shame says you are something wrong; you aren’t.

Guilt after leaving an abusive relationship is one of the most common feelings there is. You left, and you feel guilty. For breaking up the family. For staying so long. For the times you went along with it. For not seeing it sooner. For feeling relieved.

Why do I feel guilty after leaving?

Because guilt was often part of how the relationship worked. Controlling people often teach their partners that everything is their fault: the moods, the arguments, the children’s behaviour. That training doesn’t stop when you leave.

The Decoder describes the planting of shame in shame installation, and the steady pressure of guilt in guilt-tripping.

Guilt was one of the tools. It’s still working, even though the person using it is gone.

Was it my fault?

No. Abuse is the responsibility of the person who chose to do it. You may have made choices you’d make differently now. Everyone does. But choices made under pressure, fear and confusion aren’t the cause of someone else’s abuse.

Guilt or shame: what’s the difference?

Guilt says: “I did something wrong.” It can be useful when it’s accurate, because it points to something to repair.

Shame says: “I am something wrong.” It’s never useful. It makes you hide, isolate and believe you deserve bad treatment.

Most of what people feel after abuse is shame wearing guilt’s clothes.

What about the guilt of staying so long?

People stay for many reasons: love, hope, fear, money, children, nowhere to go, a bond built by the abuse itself. See The trauma bond. Staying wasn’t a failure of intelligence or courage. And many people leave more than once before it holds. See If you go back.

What about guilt about the children?

You may feel guilty that they saw things, or that they now have two homes. Children do better in one calm home than in one tense one. Leaving to protect them, or yourself, is a choice made for them, not against them. How children react, and what helps them covers what helps them now.

How do I loosen guilt and shame?

  • Check the facts. Write the guilty thought down. Then ask: what did I know at the time? What choices did I have? What would I say to a friend who wrote this?
  • Say it out loud to someone safe. Shame shrinks when it’s spoken and met with understanding.
  • Notice whose voice it is. Often the critical voice in your head sounds like them.
  • Make repairs where they’re due, and only there. If you snapped at your children, apologise to them. That’s guilt doing its job.
  • Get support if shame is heavy or constant. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

Anger, arriving late

Why am I so angry now, long after the narcissistic abuse ended?

The short answer

Anger after narcissistic abuse or any coercive relationship often arrives late, months or years afterwards, when you finally feel safe enough to feel it. It’s a common, healthy sign that you’ve seen what happened clearly. It needs somewhere to go that doesn’t hurt you or the people around you.

In the early months there was fear, relief, grief, exhaustion. Then, a year or more later, something small happens, and you’re furious. Shaking-hands furious.

Why does anger arrive so late?

Because survival came first. In the early period, staying safe and getting through each day took everything you had. Anger needs room, and room needs safety.

Anger also needs a settled sense that what happened was wrong. If self-doubt was part of the abuse, that certainty can take a long time to arrive. When it does, anger often comes with it.

Late anger often means you can finally see what happened clearly.

Is anger after narcissistic abuse normal?

Yes. Anger is common after any kind of trauma, and research links it with post-traumatic stress. Many people describe it as a turning point: the moment they stopped blaming themselves.

What do I do with it?

Anger is a physical state as much as a feeling, so it helps to move it through your body:

  • Move: walking fast, running, swimming, hitting a pillow, anything that uses big muscles.
  • Write: an angry letter you never send. Say everything.
  • Say it: to a friend or therapist who can hear it without flinching.
  • Make something: art, music, a garden, anything that turns energy into form.

What doesn’t help:

  • aiming it at people who weren’t responsible, including your children
  • using it as fuel to contact or confront the person you left. That usually restarts the cycle. See Hoovering and No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • drinking or using drugs to quieten it. See Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through.

What if my anger frightens me?

If you’re worried you might hurt someone, or you’re lashing out in ways you regret, get support. A therapist can help you understand where it’s coming from and what to do with it. See Finding a therapist who understands this. If you’re being accused of being the angry one, see When they say you’re the abuser.

Will it pass?

For most people, it settles over time into something quieter. It may come back briefly, years later, on an anniversary or when something reminds you. That doesn’t mean anything has gone wrong.

Is it normal to be angry with myself?

Very. Many people are angry that they stayed, that they didn’t see it, or that they went back. That anger usually belongs to the person who did the harm. See Guilt and shame.

What if I’m angry at the people who didn’t help?

Friends who didn’t believe you, family who stayed neutral, services that let you down. That anger makes sense too. You get to decide what to do with those relationships. See The friends who didn’t believe you.

Can anger be useful?

Yes. Anger can power a boundary, a complaint, a court application, or help for someone else. Used that way, it becomes energy for your life, not fuel for theirs.

Checking their profile

Why do I keep checking my ex’s social media, and how do I stop?

The short answer

Checking your ex’s social media after an abusive relationship is common and hard to stop, because it feeds the same craving as contact. It also slows recovery. Muting, blocking, asking a friend to tell you anything you need to know, and replacing the habit with something else all help.

It’s late. You tell yourself you’ll just have a quick look. Forty minutes later you’ve seen their new partner’s holiday photos, read their cryptic post, and feel worse than you have all week.

Why do I keep checking my ex’s social media?

For the same reasons you miss them. The bond is still there, and checking is a way of getting a little contact without making contact. Each check gives a small hit: relief, anger, curiosity, hurt. Unpredictable rewards are the hardest habits to break. The Decoder explains this pull in push-pull.

There’s also fear. You want to know what they’re doing, who they’re telling about you, whether they’re coming for you. And some people check hoping for a sign they’ll come back, which is the opening hoovering relies on.

Every check keeps them living in your head rent-free.

Does checking slow recovery?

Research says yes. A study of people after a break-up found that those who kept looking at an ex’s profile had more distress, more longing and less personal growth than those who didn’t.

Why can’t I stop thinking about my ex?

Obsessive thoughts are common after a trauma bond, especially in the early months. They’re part of the withdrawal described in The trauma bond. They do ease with distance.

How do I stop checking?

  • Mute, unfollow or block them, their new partner and the friends who post about them. It’s the online version of no contact.
  • Remove the shortcut. Delete the app for a while, log out, or use an app blocker at night.
  • Ask a friend to watch for you. If you need to know something for safety or court, a friend can check and tell you only what matters.
  • Notice the trigger. Late at night? After a hard day? When you’re lonely? Plan something else for that moment: a call, a show, a bath, a walk.
  • Delay. When you want to check, wait 20 minutes. The urge usually passes.
  • Count the cost. After a check, write down how you feel. Read it next time you want to look.

What if I need to monitor them for safety?

If they’re threatening you or posting about you, ask someone else to monitor and screenshot for you. That keeps the evidence without keeping you hooked. See What to document and how.

What if I slip?

You will, probably. Everyone does. Notice it, don’t punish yourself, and start again. The aim is fewer checks, not a perfect record.

Closure you give yourself, and whether you have to forgive

How do I find closure after narcissistic abuse? Do I have to forgive?

The short answer

Closure after narcissistic abuse rarely comes from the person who hurt you: the apology, explanation or final conversation usually never arrives, or makes things worse. Closure is something you build yourself. Forgiveness is a choice, not a duty, and you can heal without it.

You want them to understand what they did. To say sorry and mean it. To explain why. You imagine the conversation that would finally let you put it down.

Will I get closure from them?

Probably not. People who control others rarely give honest accounts of what they did. The longing for that conversation is often the trauma bond talking. A “closure conversation” often becomes another round: blame, denial, tears, a fresh hook. Even a real apology may not give you what you need.

The Decoder describes apologies that change nothing in apologies without repair.

Waiting for them to give you closure keeps them in charge of your ending.

How do I find closure after narcissistic abuse?

Closure is something you make, not something you’re given. Things that help:

  • Write the true story, start to finish, in plain words. Not for anyone else. For you.
  • Write the letter you’d want to send, then don’t send it. Burn it, bury it, keep it in a drawer.
  • Name what you learned, about patterns, about yourself, about what you’ll never accept again.
  • Mark an ending. Some people choose a date, a walk, a small ritual.
  • Let the questions stay open. “Why did they do it?” may never be answered. You can live with an open question.

Should I send a closure letter to my ex?

Usually not. It reopens contact and hands them a reaction. If you need to say something, write it and keep it. If you must send something, keep it short and final, and don’t expect a reply. See Words you can use.

Do I have to forgive my abuser?

No. Forgiveness is a choice, not a requirement for healing. Some people find forgiveness brings peace. Some never forgive and heal fully. Both are legitimate.

Forgiveness also doesn’t mean:

  • excusing what happened
  • reconciling, or letting them back in
  • trusting them again
  • saying it was okay
  • stopping legal action or keeping quiet

Be wary of anyone who pushes you to forgive, especially the person who hurt you. Pressure to forgive can be another form of control; the Decoder calls this forced forgiveness.

What if I want to forgive?

Then do it on your terms, in your time, privately if you like. You can forgive someone and still keep no contact. The Repair, in this library, looks at forgiveness from the other side in If they never forgive you.

What about forgiving myself?

Often that’s the forgiveness that matters most. See Guilt and shame.

The anniversary effect

Why do certain dates bring it all back?

The short answer

An anniversary reaction to trauma is when a date, a season or a reminder brings old feelings back with surprising force, sometimes years later. It’s common across many kinds of loss and trauma. It doesn’t mean you haven’t moved on. Planning for hard dates softens them.

A date arrives: the anniversary of the day you left, a wedding anniversary, a birthday, the day something happened. And the past comes back with a force that surprises you, sometimes years after you thought it had settled.

What is an anniversary reaction?

It’s the return of feelings, memories or body reactions around a date, a season or a reminder linked to a loss or trauma. Grief and trauma researchers have recorded it across many kinds of loss. Less obvious triggers count too: a season, a song, a smell, a quality of light.

This isn’t a sign you haven’t moved on. It happens to people who are otherwise doing well.

What might I notice?

  • low mood, tearfulness or irritability in the days before the date
  • poor sleep or nightmares
  • feeling on edge, or numb
  • old memories arriving uninvited
  • a strong urge to contact them, or to check on them. See Checking their profile.

Why does it happen?

Memory links feelings to time and place. Your body remembers the season it was hardest, even before your mind notices the date. See What your nervous system is doing.

Hard dates can also bring contact from the person you left. Hoovering often clusters around birthdays and holidays. See Hoovering.

How do I plan for a hard date?

  • Put it in your calendar a week ahead, so it doesn’t ambush you.
  • Plan something gentle for the day: time with someone safe, a walk, a favourite meal.
  • Lower your expectations of yourself that week.
  • Tell one person, so someone knows why you’re quiet.
  • Decide in advance what you’ll do if they make contact. Usually: nothing.
  • Make a new tradition for that date, if you’d like it to mean something else.

Does it get easier?

For most people, yes. The intensity usually softens over the years, even if it never disappears. Holidays and the first year’s calendar covers the first round of dates in more detail.

Which dates might catch me off guard?

  • the day you met, married or moved in together
  • the day you left
  • the date of a serious incident
  • a court date, a year later
  • their birthday, and yours
  • holidays you spent together
  • the season when it was worst

What if the date is also a happy one?

A child’s birthday can also be the anniversary of something terrible. You can hold both: be fully present for your child, and give yourself room for the rest later.

What about the first anniversary of leaving?

Many people mark it. Some celebrate. Some grieve. Many do both. There’s no right way.

Memory, and doubting yourself

Am I making it up? Why is my memory so patchy?

The short answer

If you keep asking “am I making it up?”, you’re not alone. Stress makes memory patchy, and gaslighting teaches you to distrust your own account. Gaps don’t mean it didn’t happen. A written record, made at the time, protects you from both.

Some parts are vivid. Others are fog. The order of events won’t line up when you try to tell it. And a voice keeps asking: maybe it wasn’t that bad. Maybe you’re exaggerating. Maybe you made it up.

Why is my memory so patchy?

Stress and trauma affect how memories are stored and recalled. Intense moments can be remembered in sharp detail, sometimes too sharp. Long stretches can feel blurred or empty. Sequences get jumbled. This is a common, well-studied effect of stress on memory, not a sign that you’re unreliable.

Gaps in memory don’t cancel what you do remember. Both can be true about the same years.

Am I making it up?

The doubt itself is often a clue. Many people who were controlled were told for years that their memory was wrong: “That never happened.” “You’re too sensitive.” “You’re crazy.” That’s gaslighting, and it leaves a habit of second-guessing that outlasts the relationship.

The Decoder describes gaslighting and the self-doubt it trains, self-gaslighting induction.

People who invent abuse don’t usually lie awake wondering if they did.

How do I trust my own account again?

  • Write things down, from now on. A note made at the time beats memory made later. See What to document and how.
  • Collect anchors. Old messages, emails, photos, diary entries, things you told friends at the time. They show what happened when your memory wobbles.
  • Talk to people who were there, or who you told back then.
  • Notice the voice. When doubt arrives, ask whose voice it sounds like.
  • Accept some gaps. You don’t need a perfect timeline to know what happened.

Will patchy memory hurt me in court?

It can feel that way. Inconsistent dates can be used to question your credibility. That’s exactly why a record made at the time matters so much: it doesn’t depend on memory. Tell your lawyer about any gaps early, so they’re not a surprise. Courts and trained professionals increasingly understand that trauma affects memory. See Family court, honestly.

When should I get help?

If memories intrude often, if you have flashbacks or nightmares, or if large parts of your life feel missing, talk to a therapist who understands trauma. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

What if I remember new things later?

Memories can surface months or years afterwards, often set off by a smell, a place or a conversation. Write them down with the date you remembered them. Tell your lawyer if there’s a case. Remembering late doesn’t make a memory less true.

How do I tell my story if parts are missing?

Use what you know. Give approximate times (“spring 2022”), tie events to anchors (“just before her fifth birthday”), and say clearly what you don’t remember. A clear “I don’t remember that part” is more credible than a guess.

What your nervous system is doing

Why am I still on edge? What is hypervigilance after abuse?

The short answer

Hypervigilance after abuse is your nervous system staying on high alert after the danger has passed. A jumpy startle, scanning every room, a racing heart when your phone buzzes: these come from a body that learned alertness kept you safe. It eases with safety, time, small daily practices and, when needed, trauma-focused help.

A door slams down the street and your whole body jolts. Your phone buzzes and your heart races before you’ve even looked. You sit facing the door in every café. Your mind knows you’re safer now. Your body hasn’t heard.

What is hypervigilance after abuse?

Hypervigilance is a state of constant alert: scanning for danger, reacting fast and hard to small things. After abuse it’s very common. You might notice:

  • a strong startle at noises, raised voices or sudden movement
  • watching moods and faces closely, ready for a shift
  • trouble relaxing, even in safe places
  • a racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing
  • trouble sleeping. See Sleep.
  • feeling numb or shut down, which is the other side of the same system
  • going along with people to keep the peace, sometimes called fawning

Why doesn’t it switch off now that I’m safe?

For a long time, being alert was useful. Reading their mood kept you safe. Your nervous system learned that lesson thoroughly, and it doesn’t unlearn it because you moved out. As far as your body is concerned, vigilance worked.

It retrains slowly, through repeated experiences of real safety, not through one realisation.

Your body did its job well for a long time, and it’s still doing it.

What helps my nervous system settle?

Two practices you can do anywhere

A longer breath out. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of six or seven, as if blowing through a straw. Repeat five times. A longer out-breath tells your body to slow down.

Five things. Look around and name five things you can see. Then four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, one you can taste. Say them out loud if you can. It pulls your attention into the present room.

Things that help over weeks and months

  • Regular sleep and meal times, so your body has predictable rhythms.
  • Movement you like: walking, swimming, dancing, stretching.
  • Time with people who feel safe.
  • Less contact with the person you left, and fewer reminders. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • Small, safe experiences of calm, repeated often.

The Root, a guided practice in this library, has breathing and body exercises built for this. You can find it at The Root.

Could this be PTSD or complex PTSD?

Maybe. Post-traumatic stress disorder includes reliving what happened (flashbacks, nightmares), avoiding reminders, and feeling constantly on alert. Complex PTSD, recognised in the World Health Organization’s diagnostic system, adds long-lasting problems with emotions, self-worth and relationships, and is linked to prolonged trauma such as ongoing abuse.

Only a qualified professional can diagnose either. If these sound familiar and they’re getting in the way of your life, there are effective treatments. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

When should I get help quickly?

If you feel unable to cope, can’t function day to day, or have thoughts of harming yourself, go to When it’s more than aftermath.

Sleep

Why can’t I sleep after the breakup, and what helps?

The short answer

If you can’t sleep after a breakup with an abusive partner, your body is likely still on alert, and nights are when grief, fear and stress have room to surface. Steady sleep and wake times, a wind-down routine and a plan for 3am help. If it lasts for weeks, see a doctor: there are effective treatments.

You’re exhausted all day. Then you lie down and your mind starts: replaying conversations, imagining court, listening for the car. Or you fall asleep and wake at 3am, wide awake and afraid.

Why can’t I sleep after the breakup?

  • Your nervous system is still on watch. A quiet, dark room can be when alertness is strongest, because there’s nothing else to focus on. See What your nervous system is doing.
  • Grief and fear surface at night, when the day’s distractions are gone.
  • Practical stress lands hardest in the dark: money, court, the children.
  • Your body may be worn down, which Getting sick after leaving explains.
  • The relationship may have trained you not to sleep deeply, if nights were when things happened. The Decoder describes sleep deprivation used as control.

Disrupted sleep after abuse is so common it’s almost expected. It’s not a separate thing going wrong.

What helps me sleep?

  • Keep the same wake time every day, even after a bad night. It anchors your body clock more than bedtime does.
  • Go to bed when sleepy, not just tired.
  • Wind down for 30 to 60 minutes: dim lights, no news, no checking their social media. See Checking their profile.
  • Make the room feel safe: a lock you trust, a light you can reach, your phone charged nearby. See A home that feels like yours.
  • Use the bed for sleep. If you’re awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up, do something dull in low light, and go back when sleepy.
  • Cut caffeine after midday, and be careful with alcohol: it helps you drop off but wrecks the second half of the night. See Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through.

What do I do at 3am?

  • Don’t check the clock or your phone.
  • Try the longer out-breath from What your nervous system is doing.
  • Keep a notebook by the bed. Write the worry down and tell yourself you’ll deal with it in the morning.
  • If fear is high, check the doors once, then go back to bed. Checking again and again feeds the fear.

When should I see a doctor?

If poor sleep lasts more than a few weeks, or affects your work, driving or parenting, talk to a doctor. The best-tested treatment for long-term insomnia is a short course of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, which works without medication and is often available online or through health services. Nightmares can also be treated.

What about nightmares?

Nightmares are common after abuse, and they can be treated. One method, imagery rehearsal therapy, has you rewrite a recurring nightmare while awake and rehearse the new version. It has good evidence for trauma-related nightmares. Ask your doctor or a therapist.

Should I take sleeping pills?

Talk to a doctor first. Some sleep medicines help for a short time, but they’re not usually a long-term answer, and some don’t mix with alcohol or other medicines. The talking treatment for insomnia often works better in the long run.

Getting sick after leaving

Why am I getting sick after leaving an abusive relationship?

The short answer

Getting sick after leaving an abusive relationship is common. Long-term stress affects the immune system, sleep, digestion and pain, and some people only fall ill once the crisis eases. Abuse can also leave injuries you didn’t know about. See a doctor, and tell them what you’ve been through.

You finally got out. And then you got sick. Colds that won’t shift, headaches, stomach trouble, exhaustion, pain that moves around. It feels unfair.

Why do people get sick after leaving?

  • Long-term stress wears the body down. Months or years of stress hormones affect the immune system, digestion, blood pressure, sleep and pain. Research links prolonged stress to weaker immune function.
  • The crash after the crisis. Many people hold together through the worst of it, then fall ill once things calm down.
  • Poor sleep, poor food, no time to rest. All common after leaving.
  • Injuries you didn’t know about. Head injuries and strangulation can have effects that show up later, such as headaches, dizziness, memory problems, or changes to your voice or swallowing.

Studies of people who’ve experienced partner abuse find higher rates of chronic pain, stomach problems, headaches and other health conditions, often for years afterwards.

Your body kept going while it had to. Now it’s asking for care.

What should I tell my doctor?

You don’t have to give every detail. Something like this is enough:

Say: “I’ve recently left an abusive relationship. I’ve been getting ill a lot and I’m exhausted. I also want to mention that I was [strangled / hit on the head] at some point, in case that matters.”

Doctors are increasingly trained to ask about abuse, and what you tell them can also become a record if you need one. See What to document and how.

If you were ever strangled or choked, mention it even if it was long ago. See Warning signs to take seriously.

What helps the body recover?

  • Sleep first. See Sleep.
  • Regular, simple meals.
  • Gentle movement.
  • Fewer stressors where you can, including less contact. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.
  • Follow up on anything that doesn’t settle.

Which symptoms should I never ignore?

Get urgent care for:

  • a sudden, severe headache
  • confusion, or trouble speaking or seeing
  • weakness or numbness on one side of the body
  • fainting or seizures
  • trouble breathing or swallowing
  • chest pain

After strangling, some injuries show up days later. Tell the doctor it happened.

What about check-ups I’ve missed?

Abuse often means missed appointments and injuries that were never treated. Book the dentist, an eye test, any screening you’ve missed, and a check on any old injury that never healed right.

What about my medication?

If the person you left controlled your medicine or your appointments, ask your doctor to review everything you take. Ask the pharmacy to keep your details private.

Can stress cause physical illness?

It can raise the risk and make many conditions worse: headaches, gut problems, high blood pressure, skin flare-ups, pain. That doesn’t mean it’s “all in your head.” It means your body carried what happened, and it deserves treatment like any other illness. See What your nervous system is doing.

If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion

Is sexual abuse in a relationship still abuse? What do I do now?

The short answer

Sexual abuse in a relationship is abuse, and in most places sex without consent is rape or sexual assault even between partners or spouses. Reproductive coercion, such as interfering with contraception or pressuring a pregnancy, is abuse too. Health care, specialist support and reporting options exist, and the choices are yours.

This chapter is about sex that wasn’t freely chosen, and about control over your body and fertility. It may be hard to read. Take breaks. Skip parts if you need to.

If you’re in danger, or you’ve just been assaulted, go to If you’re not safe right now. If you’re having thoughts of harming yourself, go to When it’s more than aftermath.

Is sexual abuse in a relationship still abuse?

Yes. Being in a relationship, or married, doesn’t mean consent to sex. Sex you didn’t want, were pressured into, felt you couldn’t refuse, or couldn’t consent to because you were asleep, drunk or afraid, is sexual abuse. In most countries it’s also a crime, including within marriage.

It can include:

  • sex after you said no, or when you couldn’t say no
  • pressure, guilt or threats until you gave in
  • sex as a way to “make up” after violence
  • being made to do things you didn’t want to do
  • being filmed or photographed without consent. See Intimate images and online threats.

Many people only name it as abuse after they’ve left. That’s common, and it doesn’t make it any less real.

What is reproductive coercion?

Control over your fertility and reproductive choices:

  • hiding, damaging or refusing contraception, or removing a condom without agreement
  • pressuring you to get pregnant, or to stay pregnant
  • pressuring you to end a pregnancy
  • controlling access to doctors or sexual health care

Researchers recognise it as a form of abuse, and health services increasingly ask about it.

What health care might I need?

If the assault was recent:

  • Emergency contraception, ideally as soon as possible.
  • Medicine to prevent HIV (PEP), which needs to start within 72 hours.
  • A forensic exam, if you might want to report now or later. Specialist services can collect and store evidence even if you haven’t decided. In the US, ask a hospital for a sexual assault nurse examiner. In the UK, contact a sexual assault referral centre.

At any time:

  • Sexual health testing for infections.
  • Contraception you control, if that matters to you.
  • Support for your mental health. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

Where can I get specialist support?

  • United States: RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline, 1-800-656-4673.
  • England and Wales: Rape Crisis 24/7 Support Line, 0808 500 2222.
  • Scotland: Rape Crisis Scotland helpline, 08088 01 03 02.
  • Ireland: Rape Crisis Centre helpline, 1800 77 8888.
  • Australia: 1800RESPECT, 1800 737 732.
  • Canada: search “sexual assault centre [your city].”

Do I have to report it?

No. Reporting is your choice. You can report now, later, or never. You can get medical care and support without reporting. If you want to report, a specialist service can explain what happens and support you through it. See Police and reporting.

What about sex and intimacy later?

It may take time before sex or touch feels safe again. That’s common. Go at your own pace. A new partner who respects your pace is showing you something important. Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not covers this.

Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through

I’m drinking more since the breakup. Is that a problem?

The short answer

Drinking after a breakup from an abusive relationship, or using drugs, food, work or anything else to get through, is common. It’s a way of coping, not a moral failing. If it’s growing, or you can’t cut back, help is free and confidential, and there are safer steps you can take today.

A glass to sleep. Then two. Something to take the edge off before a handover. Scrolling until 3am. Working until you drop. Not eating, or eating everything.

You’re getting through. This chapter is about noticing whether the way you’re getting through is starting to cost you.

If you’re in crisis, or you’ve had thoughts of harming yourself, go to When it’s more than aftermath now.

Why do people drink more after a breakup or abuse?

Because it works, for a while. Alcohol and drugs quieten an alert nervous system, blunt grief and fear, and help you sleep. Research finds higher rates of drinking and drug use among people who’ve experienced partner abuse. Some people were also pressured to drink or use by the person they left.

There’s no shame in having reached for something. The question is whether it’s helping or starting to hurt.

How do I know if it’s becoming a problem?

Some questions to ask:

  • Am I drinking or using more than I used to, or more often?
  • Do I need it to sleep, to face a handover, or to get through the evening?
  • Have I tried to cut back and found it hard?
  • Is it affecting my work, my children, my health or my money?
  • Am I hiding how much?
  • Could the person I left use it against me in court?

If several are yes, it’s worth getting support now, before it grows.

Getting help takes back one more thing they took.

What can I do today?

  • Count it. Write down what you drink or use for a week. Seeing it clearly is a first step.
  • Have alcohol-free days. Two or three a week, to start.
  • Don’t drink to sleep. It breaks up the second half of the night. See Sleep.
  • Plan the hard moments. Before a handover or court date, decide what you’ll do instead: a walk, a call, the breathing in What your nervous system is doing.
  • Keep it out of the house for a while, if that helps.
  • Never drive after drinking or using.

If you’ve been drinking heavily every day, don’t stop suddenly without medical advice. Talk to a doctor first.

Where can I get help?

These services are free and confidential:

  • United States: SAMHSA National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357.
  • United Kingdom: Drinkline, 0300 123 1110 (Scotland: 0800 7314 314); FRANK (drugs), 0300 123 6600.
  • Australia: National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline, 1800 250 015.
  • Ireland: HSE Drugs and Alcohol Helpline, 1800 459 459.
  • Canada: search “[your province] addiction help line.”

A doctor can also help, and so can a therapist who understands trauma. Treating the trauma often makes it easier to cut back. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

What about other ways of getting through?

Overworking, not eating, overeating, gambling, shopping, scrolling, risky sex. The same questions apply: is it helping, or is it starting to cost you? The same help applies too.

Finding a therapist who understands this

How do I find a therapist for trauma bonding and abuse?

The short answer

A good therapist for trauma bond recovery and abuse understands coercive control, works in a trauma-informed way, and can offer approaches with evidence behind them. Ask about their experience before you start. Low-cost options exist in most places, and couples counselling with your ex is the wrong tool.

Not every therapist understands coercive control. A good one can help you loosen the trauma bond, settle your nervous system and rebuild trust in yourself. A poor fit can make you feel worse, or even take your ex’s side.

What should I look for in a therapist?

  • Experience with domestic abuse and coercive control, not just relationship problems.
  • Trauma-informed practice: they understand how trauma affects the body, memory and trust, and they go at your pace.
  • Comfort with mixed feelings, including missing the person. See What you’re allowed to feel about someone you left.
  • No pressure to forgive, reconcile or “see both sides” of the abuse.
  • You feel safe with them after a few sessions.

Which therapies help after abuse?

For post-traumatic stress, health guidelines recommend trauma-focused therapies, including:

  • Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, including cognitive processing therapy.
  • EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing).
  • Prolonged exposure, in some guidelines.

Other approaches can help with the wider effects: compassion-focused therapy for shame, and therapies that focus on relationships and boundaries. You don’t have to choose the method yourself. A good therapist will explain their approach and why.

If you’re looking for a therapist for trauma bonding in particular, ask how they work with attachment to an abusive partner. See The trauma bond.

Questions to ask: “How much of your work involves domestic abuse or coercive control?” “How do you work with someone who still misses the person who hurt them?” “What approach would you use, and why?”

Why is couples counselling the wrong tool?

Couples counselling assumes two people with roughly equal power, both acting in good faith. With coercive control, that isn’t the case. Joint sessions can be unsafe, and can make the abuse look like a shared problem. Many domestic abuse experts advise against couples counselling where there’s been abuse.

Be wary too of therapy language used against you: “you’re triggering me,” “I need you to respect my boundaries,” used to control. The Decoder calls this weaponized therapy-speak.

How do I find low-cost therapy?

  • Domestic abuse services often offer free counselling or support groups.
  • Public health services: in England, NHS Talking Therapies accept self-referral. In Australia, a GP can make a Mental Health Treatment Plan for subsidised sessions. In Ireland and Canada, ask your doctor about public mental health services.
  • Sliding-scale fees: many private therapists reduce fees on request. In the US, Open Path Collective lists low-cost therapists.
  • Training clinics: university clinics where supervised trainees offer low-cost therapy.
  • Support groups: free, and often run by people who’ve been through it.

What if the first therapist isn’t right?

Try another. Fit matters more than anything else in how well therapy works. You’re allowed to leave after a session or two.

What if I need help now?

If you’re in crisis, don’t wait for the right therapist. Any doctor, crisis line or mental health service is the right first step. See When it’s more than aftermath.

When it's more than aftermath

I’m not okay. I’ve had thoughts of ending my life. What do I do?

The short answer

If you’re suicidal after leaving an abusive relationship, please reach out now: call your local crisis line or emergency number. You don’t need to be sure it’s serious enough. Feeling this way is a sign of how much you’ve been carrying, not a sign you’ve failed, and help works.

If you’re thinking about ending your life, please call for help now.

  • United States and Canada: call or text 988.
  • United Kingdom and Ireland: Samaritans, 116 123.
  • Australia: Lifeline, 13 11 14.
  • Anywhere: your emergency number, or search “[your country] crisis line.”

You don’t need to be at a certain point to call. The people on these lines have heard it before. They’re there for exactly this call.

Read this chapter whenever you need it, in any order. You don’t have to read anything else first.

Is it normal to feel this way after leaving?

Feeling suicidal after leaving an abusive relationship is more common than people say. Many people go through very dark times after leaving abuse. Exhaustion, grief, fear, isolation and the weight of everything that happened can pile up until it feels like too much. Research finds that people who’ve experienced partner abuse are at higher risk of thoughts of suicide.

Feeling this way isn’t a verdict on you. It means you’re carrying more than one person should carry alone. It’s treatable, and people do come through it.

What’s ordinary aftermath, and what’s more?

Much of what this book describes is hard but expected: hypervigilance, poor sleep, late anger, patchy memory, hard anniversaries. That’s the range this book calls ordinary aftermath.

These signs suggest you need more support than a book, friends or family can give:

  • hopelessness that doesn’t lift, day after day
  • not being able to manage daily life (work, eating, looking after yourself or your children) for weeks
  • thoughts of harming yourself, in any form
  • drinking or using drugs much more than before to cope. See Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through.
  • feeling unable to cope in a way that feels different from the hard days you’ve had before

None of these means you’ve failed at recovering. They mean you need, and deserve, more help.

What do I do right now?

  1. Call a crisis line or your emergency number if you might act on thoughts of harming yourself.
  2. Make your surroundings safer for the next few hours: be with someone, go somewhere public, or ask someone to come to you.
  3. Tell one person. A friend, a family member, a doctor. Say it plainly: “I’m having thoughts of ending my life and I need help.”
  4. Contact a doctor today, or a mental health service. You don’t need to wait for the right therapist. See Finding a therapist who understands this for later.

What if the person I left is part of why I feel this way?

They may be. Ongoing abuse after separation, threats, court cases and being disbelieved can all wear you down. Getting safer from them is part of getting well. A domestic abuse service can help with both. See If you’re not safe right now and Find help.

What if I’m worried about someone else?

Ask them directly: “Are you thinking about suicide?” Asking doesn’t put the idea in their head. It often brings relief. Listen, stay with them if you can, and help them contact a crisis line or emergency services.

You are not a burden

That voice telling you everyone would be better off without you is lying. It’s one of the things deep pain says. You’ve already done one of the hardest things a person can do: you left. Getting through this is possible, and you don’t have to do it alone.

The friends who didn't believe you

My friends don’t believe me about the abuse. What now?

The short answer

When friends don’t believe you about abuse, it can hurt as much as the abuse itself. Disbelief usually comes from ordinary human reasons: discomfort, loyalty, one side of the story, abuse that didn’t look like they expected. Understanding why doesn’t mean you have to excuse it or keep them close.

You told them. You braced yourself, and you told them. And they said, “Are you sure?” Or, “But he’s always been lovely to me.” Or they said nothing, and things were never quite the same.

Why don’t my friends believe me?

Usually for reasons that are about them, not your credibility:

  • They don’t want it to be true about someone they know or like.
  • It didn’t look like abuse to them. Coercive control often doesn’t involve bruises. Patterns are harder to see than incidents.
  • They’ve heard the other version. See The narrative campaign.
  • They’re close to your ex, through work, family or history.
  • It touches something in their own life they don’t want to look at.
  • They don’t know what to do, so they do nothing.

The Decoder’s page on silent coalitions describes how groups go quiet around abuse.

Understanding why someone didn’t believe you is one thing. Deciding whether to keep them close is another. You can do the first without the second.

Does their disbelief mean I’m wrong?

No. Disbelief is common, especially with abuse that wasn’t physical. It says more about how hard abuse is to see from the outside than about what happened. If you’re doubting yourself because of it, read Memory, and doubting yourself.

Should I try to convince them?

Usually not. Arguing for your own experience is exhausting, and it rarely works. If you want to try once, keep it short:

Say: “I know this is hard to hear, and I know you like them. I’m not asking you to take sides. I’m asking you to believe that I’m telling you the truth about my life.”

Then let it rest.

What do I do about the friendship?

It’s your choice, made friend by friend:

  • Some are worth one honest conversation, after some time has passed.
  • Some may come round as they see more, or see the other person more clearly.
  • Some aren’t worth the energy now, or ever.

You don’t need to justify any of these choices to anyone. Your family and mutual friends covers people who get pulled into carrying messages.

Where do I find people who do believe me?

  • domestic abuse services and support groups, where people get it without explanation
  • online communities for survivors (choose moderated ones)
  • a therapist. See Finding a therapist who understands this.
  • new friends. See Making new people from scratch.

The friends who did, and are tired

My friends have supported me through this. How do I not wear them out?

The short answer

Supporting a friend after abuse takes a lot, and the friends who believed you are human too. Compassion fatigue is real, and it doesn’t mean they’ve stopped caring. Spreading support across several people, noticing what they give, and keeping some of the friendship about other things helps it last.

They believed you straight away. They showed up, took the calls, sat with you at 2am. They’ve been steady for months, maybe years. And lately you’ve noticed them pull back a little, or look tired when you start talking about it.

Why are my friends getting tired?

Because supporting someone through abuse and its aftermath is hard work. Hearing about it, worrying, wanting to fix it and not being able to: all of that takes a toll. There’s a name for it, compassion fatigue, and it happens to people who love you and mean every word of their support.

The Decoder describes the deliberate version, where an abuser wears out your supporters on purpose, in support system exhaustion. Ongoing post-separation abuse can do this even without anyone planning it.

Needing ongoing support isn’t shameful. Neither is your friend needing a rest from carrying part of it.

How do I keep the support going?

  • Spread it. Don’t rely on one person for everything. Add a support group, a domestic abuse service, new friends (Making new people from scratch), a therapist. See Finding a therapist who understands this.
  • Notice what they give. A thank-you, a message on a good day, remembering their birthday.
  • Ask before offloading. “Have you got space for a hard one tonight, or shall we just watch something?”
  • Keep other things in the friendship. Shared jokes, walks, films, their life. Ask how they are and listen to the answer.
  • Give back when you can, even in small ways.

What if a friend says they need a break?

It hurts, and it’s honest. Thank them. Let them know you understand. Lean more on your other supports for a while. Often the friendship comes back stronger for it.

What if I’m the friend reading this?

If you’re supporting someone, Helping someone in year one is written for you. Look after yourself too. You can’t pour from an empty cup, and a rested friend helps more than an exhausted one.

How do I ask for help without overloading one person?

Ask for something clear and small: “Could you sit with me while I read the court letter on Thursday?” A clear ask is easier to say yes to than “I need you.”

How do I tell if I’m leaning too hard?

Signs include slower replies, changes of subject, or tension when you call. Ask directly:

Say: “Am I leaning on you too much? Tell me honestly. I’d rather know.”

Most people appreciate the question.

What can I give back?

Ask about their life, and listen. Remember the dates that matter to them. Help with something practical when you can. Small things keep a friendship two-sided.

Your parents

My parents don’t support me after the divorce. Why, and what do I do?

The short answer

When parents don’t support you after a divorce or abusive relationship, the reasons range from shock and old loyalties to their own history. For some people, a parent’s behaviour is part of why the relationship felt normal in the first place. You decide how close to stay, and you don’t need their approval to heal.

Some parents are immediately on your side. Some struggle to understand. Some side with your ex, or tell you to go back. And for some people, a parent was part of the pattern that made the relationship feel familiar.

Why don’t my parents support me?

  • Shock, or disbelief, for the same reasons as friends. See The friends who didn’t believe you.
  • They like your ex, or the life you had.
  • Their values, about marriage, divorce, family or faith.
  • Embarrassment about what people will think.
  • Their own history. A parent who lived through something similar, or did something similar, may not be able to look at it.
  • Grandchildren. They may fear losing contact with the children.

What if my parent was part of the pattern?

Sometimes a parent’s control, criticism or volatility is part of why the relationship felt normal. If you grew up having to manage someone’s moods, a partner who needed managing could feel like home.

That’s real, and worth looking at, with a therapist if you can. It doesn’t rule out a good relationship with your parent now. It may mean that relationship needs its own honest look. The Repair, in this library, covers what gets passed down through families, from the other side.

The Decoder’s page on golden child elevation describes families where one child is favoured and another blamed, a pattern that can shape how you’re treated now.

What do I do?

No single script works. Choose for each parent:

  • A direct, calm conversation, once you’re steady enough:

Say: “I need you to know what was happening in my marriage. I’m not asking you to hate them. I’m asking you to support me and not pass things on.”

  • Boundaries, as with anyone who isn’t helping: less detail, shorter visits, topics that are off limits.
  • Distance, if they’re passing information to your ex or putting you at risk. See Your family and mutual friends.

What about my children and their grandparents?

Children often benefit from loving grandparents. If your parents are safe for the children but not on your side, you may be able to keep that relationship with clear limits: no talk about the other parent, and nothing passed on. If they’re not safe, put the children first.

What if I left my parents, not a partner?

Then much of this whole book applies to you. See If your story doesn’t look like the usual one.

What if my parents still see my ex?

Some parents keep a relationship with an ex, especially for grandchildren. You can ask them not to pass on news about you, and not to discuss the separation with your children. If they can’t keep to that, give them less information.

What if my parent was abusive too?

Then you may be dealing with two patterns at once. Much of this book applies to leaving a controlling family. See If your story doesn’t look like the usual one, and think about support from a therapist who understands both.

Telling someone new

When should I tell a new partner, or a new friend, about the abuse?

The short answer

When to tell a new partner about abuse depends on how serious the relationship is and what feels right to you. Telling everything too early can overwhelm a new connection; never telling can leave you carrying it alone. Share what fits the relationship, at your own pace.

At some point you’ll meet someone new: a friend, a colleague, a partner. And you’ll wonder how much to tell them about what happened.

When should I tell a new partner about the abuse?

There’s no rule. Two things to weigh:

Telling everything very early can overwhelm a new connection before it has its own footing.

Never telling can mean carrying it alone, and can leave a serious partner making decisions without information that affects them, especially if your ex may contact them or if there are court cases. See The new partner.

Neither is a moral failing. Both have costs. The workable path sits between them.

How much should I share?

Match it to the relationship:

  • A new friend or colleague: usually very little at first. “I went through a hard separation” is complete.
  • Someone you’re dating: enough to explain anything that might affect them. “My ex didn’t take the break-up well and sometimes contacts people in my life.”
  • A serious partner: more, at a pace that feels right. They deserve to understand what you’re carrying and what might come up.

You don’t have to share details you’re not ready to share, ever.

How do I say it?

Say: “There’s something about my past I’d like you to know. My last relationship was abusive, and I’m still dealing with some of the after-effects. I don’t need you to fix anything. I just wanted you to know.”

Watch how they respond. Curiosity, patience and respect for your pace are good signs. Pressure for details, pity, or making it about them are worth noticing.

What if I get it wrong?

There’s no wrong way that can’t be mended. You can only learn what works for you. If you shared too much too soon, that’s fine. If you waited too long, you can still share. See Trust as a practice and Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not.

What about a new doctor or therapist?

Tell them enough to help you: that you’ve left an abusive relationship, and anything that affects your health. Ask them to note any safety concerns on your record.

What about my employer?

You don’t have to tell them anything beyond what you need. See Work again.

What about the children’s new school or club?

Only what affects the child’s safety: who may collect them, any court order, and who to call. See Handovers.

What if they react badly?

Some people respond with disbelief, questions that feel like an interrogation, or advice you didn’t ask for. You can close the conversation:

Say: “I’d rather not go into details. I just wanted you to know.”

Their reaction tells you how much to share in future.

What if my ex tells them first?

It happens. A calm, short account from you, with no attack on your ex, is usually believed over time. See The narrative campaign.

Making new people from scratch

How do I make friends after an abusive relationship isolated me?

The short answer

Making friends after an abusive relationship is a skill you can learn, not luck. Isolation is common in coercive relationships, so many people rebuild a social life from very little. Showing up to the same place again and again, and getting through the awkward early weeks, is how most adult friendships form.

Making friends after an abusive relationship can feel impossible at first. During the relationship, friends fell away. Maybe they were pushed away. Maybe you stopped going out because it caused trouble. Now you’ve left, and the phone is quiet.

Why am I so lonely after leaving?

Because isolation is one of the most common features of coercive control. Cutting someone off from friends and family makes them easier to control. The Decoder describes it in isolation. Many people leave with a much smaller social world than they started with.

Loneliness after leaving shows what the relationship took from you. It says nothing about whether you’re likeable.

How do I make friends as an adult?

Research on adult friendship points to a few plain truths:

  • Repetition beats intensity. Seeing the same people regularly does more than one great conversation. A weekly class, a club, a volunteering shift, a running group.
  • It takes time. One study estimated that turning an acquaintance into a casual friend takes around 50 hours together, and a close friend more than 200.
  • The first few times feel awkward. That’s normal. It passes if you keep going.
  • Someone has to go first. “Do you want to grab a coffee after this?” feels risky and usually lands well.

This is hard, and it’s possible, at any age and after any amount of isolation.

Where do I start?

  • something you used to enjoy before the relationship. See Your own tastes, rediscovered.
  • a survivors’ support group, where you won’t have to explain, unlike the friends who didn’t believe you
  • volunteering, which gives you a reason to show up and something to talk about
  • keeping the friends you have, without wearing them out. See The friends who did, and are tired.
  • reconnecting with old friends. A short message is enough: “I’ve been through a lot and I’ve missed you. Fancy a catch-up?”

What if I find it hard to trust new people?

That’s common. Trust can be built slowly, in small steps. See Trust as a practice.

What if meeting people makes me anxious?

Start where there’s an activity to focus on: a class, volunteering, a walking group. Listen more than you talk at first, and keep showing up. Anxiety usually eases with repetition.

What about online groups?

Moderated survivor groups can help, especially late at night. Share as little personal detail as you can, and check privacy settings. Leave any group that feels like pressure or a pile-on.

How do I tell a good new friend from someone who’ll take advantage?

Use the same slow approach as in Trust as a practice. Good friends are steady, respect your “no,” and don’t rush closeness.

What about old friends I lost touch with?

Many will be glad to hear from you, especially if they wondered what happened. A short, honest message is enough:

Say: “I was in a controlling relationship and I lost touch with a lot of people. I’ve missed you. Would you like to catch up?”

When you share a town, a workplace or a community

What do I do when my ex is in the same friend group, workplace or church?

The short answer

When your ex is in the same friend group, workplace, church or small town, you can’t simply avoid them. Plan for run-ins, decide which spaces to keep and which to give up for now, ask for practical help at work or in the community, and keep a record if their behaviour crosses a line.

You left, but you still see them. At the school gate. In the office kitchen. Across the room at church. At every party your friends throw. In a small town, at the supermarket.

What do I do if my ex is in the same friend group?

  • Decide what you’ll do at events before you go: arrive with a friend, have an exit plan, keep conversations short.
  • Don’t ask friends to choose. Some will, on their own.
  • Tell hosts if you need to, quietly: “I’d rather not be seated near them.”
  • Accept that the group may change. Some people will drift. The Decoder’s page on silent coalitions describes how groups go quiet. See Your family and mutual friends.

What if we work together?

  • Tell HR or a manager you trust, especially if their behaviour affects your work or safety.
  • Ask for practical changes: different shifts, a different team, separate meetings.
  • Keep work contact strictly professional and in writing.
  • Keep a record of anything that crosses a line. Workplace harassment rules may apply.
  • At Will, in this library, covers coercion and protection at work in depth.

What about church or a faith community?

A congregation can be a lifeline or a pressure. Some communities support the person who left; others push reconciliation or side with the person who seems more established. If you’re being pressured, you can step back, attend a different service, or find a different community. The Sacred Divide, in this library, looks at how control works inside religious institutions.

What about a small town?

  • Plan routes and times to reduce run-ins: a different supermarket, a different time.
  • Prepare a short line for when you meet: “Hi,” and keep walking.
  • Remember that gossip spreads both ways. Steady, calm behaviour over time is your best answer. See The narrative campaign.
  • If the run-ins feel deliberate, or you feel unsafe, keep a record. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

Do I have to give things up?

Sometimes, for a while. That’s a real loss. Choose the spaces that matter most to you and protect those. You may get others back later.

What if they keep turning up where I am?

Once could be chance. A pattern isn’t. Keep a record of each time, and see Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

What if mutual friends invite us both?

Ask the host who’s coming. You can decline, go for a short time with a friend, or ask the host to choose a different day for you. You don’t owe anyone a shared room with the person you left.

Can I ask a group or club to act?

Many groups, teams and congregations have a code of conduct or a safeguarding lead. If their behaviour at shared events crosses a line, tell the organiser in writing, briefly, with dates.

Money from zero

How do I start rebuilding my finances after an abusive relationship?

The short answer

Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship starts with an honest list of what you have and owe, a budget built on today’s numbers, and small steps on credit and savings. Coerced debt can often be disputed. Starting from little is a common result of financial abuse, not a personal failing.

Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship often starts from very little. You may have left with far less than you had, or nothing at all. Maybe you’d never been allowed to see the bank statements. Maybe there are debts in your name you didn’t choose. Starting over financially is unglamorous, and that’s said on purpose: slow and boring is what works.

Where do I start rebuilding my finances?

  1. List what you have: income, benefits, savings, anything you own.
  2. List what you owe: every debt, with the lender, the amount and the minimum payment.
  3. Get your credit report from each credit agency (free in most countries) and check every entry.
  4. Build a budget on today’s numbers, not the life you hope to have next year. Essentials first: housing, food, utilities, transport, the children.
  5. Open accounts in your name only, at a bank they never used. See Money as the channel.
  6. Protect your income at work, including any leave you’re entitled to. See Work again.

Boring works. A slow, steady rebuild beats a dramatic fix almost every time.

What about debts I didn’t agree to?

This is called coerced debt: loans, cards or bills run up in your name through pressure, deception or fraud. You can often dispute them with the lender and the credit agency. A police report, a protective order or a letter from a domestic abuse service can help. In the UK, Surviving Economic Abuse has guidance and templates. In the US, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how to dispute errors. The Decoder describes the wider pattern in financial control.

How do I rebuild credit?

  • Pay every bill on time, even the small ones.
  • Keep any credit card balance low.
  • Consider a credit-builder card or loan, if the fees are low.
  • Make sure you’re on the electoral roll or equivalent, where that affects credit.
  • Give it time. Credit scores recover over months and years.

How do I start saving?

Start tiny. A few pounds or dollars a week into a separate account builds a habit, and a small buffer against emergencies. Many domestic abuse services can point you to emergency grants and matched-savings schemes.

Where can I get free money advice?

  • United Kingdom: Citizens Advice, StepChange, National Debtline.
  • United States: non-profit credit counselling agencies (look for ones accredited by the National Foundation for Credit Counseling).
  • Australia: the National Debt Helpline, 1800 007 007.
  • Ireland: MABS, the Money Advice and Budgeting Service.
  • Canada: non-profit credit counselling agencies.

Why does starting from zero feel so shameful?

Because it can feel like proof you failed. It isn’t. Financial abuse leaves people with less money, damaged credit and debts they didn’t choose. Starting again from very little is one of the most common results of a controlling relationship. It’s evidence of what was done to you, not of who you are.

Work again

Can I take domestic violence leave, and how do I explain a gap?

The short answer

Domestic violence leave exists in a growing number of places, sometimes paid: Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, Northern Ireland and parts of Canada and the US. Whether or not you have a legal right, many employers will help with time off and safety. You never have to tell an employer the whole story.

Going back to work, or staying in work, after leaving can be hard. Court dates, moving house, children’s needs, exhaustion. And you may have a gap to explain.

Can I take domestic violence leave?

In some places, yes:

  • Australia: employees can take 10 days of paid family and domestic violence leave a year.
  • New Zealand: 10 days of paid domestic violence leave a year.
  • Ireland: 5 days of paid domestic violence leave a year, since November 2023.
  • Northern Ireland: a 2022 law will give 10 days of paid domestic abuse leave a year once its regulations take effect. Check whether it’s in force yet.
  • Canada: federally regulated employees have paid leave, and most provinces offer some, partly paid or unpaid.
  • United States: no federal right, but several states require time off for survivors to go to court, see a doctor or move.
  • England, Wales and Scotland: no general legal right to domestic abuse leave yet, but many employers have policies. Ask HR.

Search “[your country or state] domestic violence leave” for the current rules.

Do I have to tell my employer what happened?

No. “I’m dealing with a personal family matter” is enough in almost every situation. You choose what to share. You might share more if you need leave, flexibility or safety measures, but even then you can keep the details private.

What you share about this part of your life at work is your call, based on what helps you, not on what you think you owe.

How do I stay safe at work?

If the person you left knows where you work:

  • tell security or reception, and give them a photo
  • ask to have calls screened, and your details removed from public directories
  • ask about changing your hours, location or parking
  • keep a record of anything that happens at work. See What to document and how.

See the work section of A safety plan for after you’ve left.

How do I explain a gap in my CV?

Keep it short and forward-looking:

Say: “I took some time out to deal with a family matter, which is now resolved. I’m ready to focus on work, and I’ve kept my skills up by [anything you did].”

Interviewers rarely push further. If they do, you can say, “It was personal, and it’s behind me.”

What if my ex is disrupting my job?

Calling your employer, turning up, or making handovers clash with your shifts are forms of financial abuse. Tell your employer what’s happening in brief, and keep a record. See Money as the channel. At Will, in this library, covers workplace rights and protections in depth.

A home that feels like yours

How do I start feeling safe at home after abuse?

The short answer

Feeling safe at home after abuse can take time, whether you’ve moved somewhere new or stayed in a place you shared. Practical security, small changes that make the space yours, and calm routines all help your body learn that home is safe now.

Feeling safe at home after abuse takes longer than changing the locks. You have a door that locks now. But you still lie awake listening. Or you’re in the home you shared, and every room holds a memory.

Why doesn’t home feel safe yet?

Your nervous system learned that home was where danger lived. It takes time, and many calm days, for it to learn otherwise. See What your nervous system is doing.

What practical steps help?

  • Change the locks if they ever had a key, and the alarm and garage codes.
  • Add what makes you feel secure: a door chain, a video doorbell you control, outdoor lights, a window lock.
  • Know your exits and keep your phone charged near you at night.
  • Check for devices they set up. See Surveillance and technology.
  • Ask about help with costs. Some domestic abuse services and councils fund security improvements, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.

How do I make it feel like mine?

  • Change something you can see: move the furniture, paint a wall, new bedding.
  • Remove or store reminders. You don’t have to keep their gifts, photos or the chair they always sat in.
  • Bring in things you love. Plants, music, colours, smells. See Your own tastes, rediscovered.
  • Make a calm corner where you go to rest: a chair, a blanket, a lamp.
  • Set your own rules: eat when you like, play music loud, leave the dishes.

Every small choice that’s yours alone tells your body something new about home.

What if I’m still in the home we shared?

Reclaiming a shared home is hard. Some people change one room at a time. Some do a deliberate “clearing” day with a friend. Some find they need to move eventually. All are fine.

What helps at night?

A routine, a light within reach, a door you’ve checked once, and something calming to listen to. See Sleep.

What if I’ve had to move somewhere I don’t like?

A refuge, temporary housing, a smaller flat, a relative’s spare room. It can feel like a step back. Make one corner yours: a photo, a lamp, a blanket, a smell you love. Home can start with one safe spot.

What if they know where I live?

Keep the security steps from your safety plan in place, and record any visits. If they turn up or watch the house, that can be stalking. See Stalking and harassment after a breakup. Ask about a protective order that covers your address.

What if the children bring the other home with them?

Bags, gifts and devices travel between homes. Check devices that come back from visits. See Device and account audit. Let children keep comfort items from both homes. Your rules apply in your house.

How do I sleep alone after years on alert?

Some people find a night light, white noise or a pet helps. Sleep has more.

Pets and the things left behind

My ex took my dog, or won’t give back my things. What can I do?

The short answer

If your ex took your dog or is holding your belongings, you have options. Some protective orders can cover pets, pet foster schemes help people leaving abuse, and police in many places can attend while you collect belongings. Threats to pets are a recognised warning sign.

You left in a hurry, or you couldn’t take everything. “My ex took my dog” is one of the most common things people say after leaving. Now they have your dog. Or your grandmother’s ring, your documents, your clothes. And they know exactly how much it matters.

Why are pets used against people leaving abuse?

Because pets are family, and controlling people use whatever matters most. Threats to hurt a pet, refusing to return it, or using it to make you come back are common. Researchers have found that many people delay leaving because they’re afraid for their animals. The Decoder describes the general pattern in children used as leverage; pets are used the same way.

Threats to harm or kill a pet are also a warning sign of wider danger. See Warning signs to take seriously.

What can I do about my pet?

  • Protective orders: in many US states, and some other places, a protective order can include pets. Ask when you apply. See Protective orders.
  • Ownership records: microchip registration, vet records, purchase receipts and licences in your name help show the animal is yours.
  • Pet foster schemes: some charities look after pets while their owners are in refuge or temporary housing. In the UK, Dogs Trust’s Freedom Project and Cats Protection’s Paws Protect. In the US, RedRover runs programmes and grants, and many shelters now accept pets. Ask a domestic abuse service.
  • Police: if a pet is being harmed or threatened, report it.

How do I get my belongings back?

  • Ask in writing, once, with a list and a proposed date. Keep it brief.
  • Ask the police to attend while you collect your things. Many forces will do this, sometimes called a civil standby or police-assisted collection.
  • Send someone else: a friend or family member, with your list.
  • Use your lawyer, especially for valuables, documents or anything covered in a divorce.

If they destroy or sell your belongings, that may be criminal damage or theft. Keep a record.

What about letting go?

Some things you’ll get back. Some you won’t. Write the losses down in your record if they might matter in court. Losing them is a real grief, especially things that held memories. Some people find it helps to write down what was lost, and then decide what they’re willing to keep fighting for and what to let go. Your safety is worth more than any object.

Holidays and the first year's calendar

How do I get through the first holidays after the divorce?

The short answer

The first holidays after divorce or leaving an abusive relationship are often among the hardest days of the year: old traditions, children away, family pressure, and contact from your ex. Plan them early, decide how the children’s time works in writing, and make a small new tradition of your own.

The first Christmas. The first birthday. The first school holidays. The first anniversary of the day you left. The first year is full of firsts, and each one can catch you off guard.

Why are the first holidays so hard?

  • Old traditions remind you of what’s gone.
  • The children may be with the other parent.
  • Family gatherings bring questions and pressure.
  • Your ex may use the day for contact, guilt or conflict. Hoovering often spikes around holidays. See Hoovering.

How do I plan the first holidays after divorce?

Settle the children’s time early, in writing. Who has them when, where handovers happen, and what happens about presents. A clear plan leaves less to fight about. See Apps, calendars and the parenting plan.

Plan your own day, especially if the children are away. Be with people who feel safe, or plan a quiet day that’s deliberately yours. Don’t leave it empty by accident.

Decide about contact in advance. If they message, what will you do? Usually: nothing, or a short reply about the children only. See Words you can use.

Lower the bar. You don’t have to make it perfect. A smaller, calmer holiday is fine.

You’re allowed to do this year differently. You’re allowed to do it badly. You’re allowed to skip it.

What about family pressure?

Prepare one line for questions you don’t want to answer:

Say: “I’d rather not talk about it today. Tell me about you.”

If gatherings feel unsafe or too much, you can leave early or not go.

How do I make new traditions?

Start small: a walk on the morning of the holiday, a meal you love, a film, a phone call with a friend. New traditions take a few years to feel like yours. That’s fine.

What about the other firsts?

Plan for anniversaries, the children’s birthdays, and the day you left. See The anniversary effect. Put them in your calendar a week ahead.

What about presents for the children?

Agree in writing who buys what, or let each parent do their own. Don’t compete. Children remember calm more than gifts.

What if the other parent uses the holiday to cause trouble?

Late returns, changed plans, guilt messages. Keep to the arrangement, write down what happens, and reply briefly. See Words you can use. If it breaks a court order, report it.

What about the holidays when I don’t have the children?

They can be the loneliest days of the year. Plan ahead: friends, volunteering, a trip, a film marathon, a long walk. Some people mark the day another way on purpose.

What about New Year and other fresh-start days?

Big milestone days can bring pressure to feel better. You don’t have to make resolutions. One small intention is enough.

Your own tastes, rediscovered

How do I find myself again after an abusive relationship?

The short answer

Finding yourself after an abusive relationship often happens in small surprises: music you’d stopped playing, food you love, an opinion you’d stopped saying out loud. Coercive control narrows what feels safe to want. Getting it back is allowed to be light, and even fun.

Finding yourself after an abusive relationship rarely happens all at once. One day you notice you’re humming a song you haven’t let yourself play in years. Or you order something you’d never have ordered with them. Or you realise you have an opinion, and you say it.

Why did I lose track of myself?

Coercive relationships often shrink what feels safe to want, prefer or enjoy. Your clothes, your music, your friends, your opinions: each one slowly bent towards what kept the peace. It happens so gradually you may not have noticed. The Decoder describes it as identity erosion.

How do I find myself again?

You don’t have to find a whole new self. Start with small curiosities:

  • Try things without a goal. A class, a recipe, a walk somewhere new, not because it’s therapy but because it sounds good.
  • Notice what you like, in a note on your phone: a colour, a song, a smell, a kind of day.
  • Go back to old loves. What did you enjoy before the relationship?
  • Say an opinion out loud, somewhere safe, and notice how it feels.
  • Let your home reflect you. See A home that feels like yours.

This part is allowed to be light. Not every piece of recovery has to be heavy.

What if I don’t know what I like?

That’s common. Treat it as an experiment. Try things and keep what works. Some of what you find will surprise you. That’s part of the point. People who were isolated often find new friends through the same experiments. See Making new people from scratch.

What if it feels selfish?

It isn’t. Rediscovering what you enjoy is part of getting your life back. And people who are happier in their own skin tend to be steadier parents, friends and partners. See The version of you from before.

What small experiments can I try?

  • Buy one thing in a shop that only you would choose.
  • Cook a meal they hated, or wouldn’t allow.
  • Make a playlist of songs from before the relationship.
  • Rearrange one room for your own comfort.
  • Say no to an invitation you don’t want, and yes to one you do.
  • Spend an afternoon with no plan, and notice what you drift towards.

What if what I liked before doesn’t fit any more?

Tastes change anyway, with age and experience. Some old loves will come back. Some won’t. New ones will turn up. You’re not restoring a museum. You’re living.

Why do I feel guilty enjoying things?

Some people feel they don’t deserve fun while life is still hard, or while a court case is going on. Enjoying something doesn’t prove you’re “fine,” and it doesn’t prove nothing happened. It’s part of getting your life back. See Guilt and shame.

Trust as a practice

How do I deal with trust issues after abuse?

The short answer

Trust issues after abuse make sense: trust was used against you. You don’t have to choose between total guard and total openness. Trust can be given in small amounts, matched to how people behave over time, and adjusted as you learn more.

You don’t trust easily any more. Or you trust too fast and get hurt. Either way, the way you used to trust doesn’t feel safe.

Why do I have trust issues after abuse?

Because trust was used against you. Someone you let in used what you shared, and used your good faith. Being careful now is a sensible lesson, not a flaw.

How do I learn to trust again?

Think of trust as a dial, not a switch. You can turn it up a little at a time:

  1. Start small. Share something low-stakes, or rely on someone for something minor.
  2. Watch what happens. Did they keep it private? Did they show up? Did they respect your “no”?
  3. Adjust. Turn the dial up a little if they handled it well. Turn it down if they didn’t.
  4. Repeat, over weeks and months.

Trust built slowly, on evidence, lasts longer than trust given all at once, and is easier to live with than trust never given.

The Weighing, in this library, sets out this way of judging people in Trust as a skill you rebuild.

What signs are worth noticing?

Signs to trust a little more: they keep small promises, respect your limits, are the same in private and in public, and accept “no” without sulking.

Signs to slow down: pressure, rushing intimacy, big promises early, anger when you set a limit, stories that don’t add up. See Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not.

What about trusting myself?

Often that’s the trust that took the biggest hit. It comes back the same way: small decisions, noticing they turned out fine, and building from there. See Memory, and doubting yourself.

What if someone from the past says they’ve changed?

Use the same dial. Real change shows over time, consistently, without pressure on you. See the section on telling hoovering from real change in Hoovering.

How do I trust my own judgment about people?

Write down your first impression of someone new. Look back after a month. You’ll often find your first sense was right. That record rebuilds confidence in your judgment, one person at a time.

What about doctors, lawyers and therapists?

The same dial works. Share a little first, watch how they respond, and change provider if they dismiss you. You’re allowed to ask for someone else.

What if I keep choosing people who hurt me?

Many survivors worry about this. Patterns from earlier life can make some kinds of attention feel familiar. Knowing the early warning signs and going slowly both help. See Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not. Therapy focused on relationships can help too. See Finding a therapist who understands this.

What if I want to keep records on someone new?

With the person you left, records protect you. If you notice an urge to document a new friend or partner, pay attention to it. It may be a sign to slow down, or a sign that something is wrong.

Dating, when you're ready and when you're not

When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?

The short answer

There’s no set time for dating after an abusive relationship. Two pressures get in the way: rushing to prove you’re fine, and waiting for a perfect “ready” that never comes. Better signs are curiosity about new people, and being able to handle ordinary uncertainty. Know the early warning signs, and go at your own pace.

Some people want to date straight away. Some can’t imagine ever doing it again. Most land somewhere in between, and wonder if they’re doing it wrong.

When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?

There’s no number of months. Two pressures can push you either way:

  • Rushing to prove you’re fine. Dating soon can be a way of saying “look, I’ve moved on,” to yourself or to others. That’s a different reason from wanting to meet someone.
  • Waiting for a perfect “ready.” The idea that one day you’ll be fully healed and allowed to date. For most people that clean moment never comes.

More useful signs:

  • You can be curious about a new person, instead of measuring them against your ex.
  • You can live with the ordinary uncertainty of early dating without it setting off your alarm system too strongly. See What your nervous system is doing.
  • You’d be okay if it didn’t work out.

None of these have to be perfect.

What are the early warning signs?

Abusive relationships often start with intensity. Watch for:

  • Love bombing: overwhelming attention, gifts, “I’ve never felt this way,” very fast. The Decoder’s love bombing.
  • Future faking: big plans very early: moving in, marriage, children. The Decoder’s future faking.
  • Pushing past “no,” even in small things.
  • Jealousy framed as love.
  • Running down every ex as crazy.
  • Wanting you to themselves, and resenting your friends.
  • Your gut saying “slow down.”

A good partner is fine with slow.

How do I date safely?

  • Meet in public, and tell a friend where you’ll be.
  • Keep your address private until you trust them.
  • Go at your own pace with physical intimacy. See If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion if that’s relevant to you.
  • Use the trust dial in Trust as a practice.

When do I tell them about my past?

When it feels right, and in proportion to how serious it’s getting. See Telling someone new. If your ex might contact them, tell them earlier. See The new partner.

What if I never want to date again?

That’s a valid choice. A full life doesn’t require a partner.

What if I’m drawn to the same type?

Notice it without judging yourself. The pull towards intensity or charm can feel like chemistry when it’s familiarity. Go slower with people who feel exciting in the same way your ex did, and give steadier people a second date.

How do I date online safely?

  • Use a first name only, and a photo that isn’t on your other profiles.
  • Do a video call before meeting.
  • Meet in a public place, and tell a friend where you’ll be and when you’ll check in.
  • Keep your address, workplace and children’s school private until you trust them.
  • Search their name and photos if anything feels off.

What if a new partner does something that reminds me of my ex?

Ask whether it’s a pattern or a single moment that set off an old alarm. Talk about it calmly. A good partner will listen and adjust. If it’s a pattern, take that seriously. See Trust as a practice.

The version of you from before

Will I ever be the same after abuse?

The short answer

Will you ever be the same after abuse? Not exactly, and that isn’t bad news. Much of what felt lost comes back: humour, joy, confidence, the ability to connect. What’s available isn’t the old you, but a third version that carries what you’ve learned and isn’t less for it.

You look at old photos and wonder where that person went. Will you get back to who you were before?

Will I ever be the same after abuse?

The honest answer is more hopeful than a flat “no,” and more honest than a comforting “yes.”

Much of you comes back. Your sense of humour. Your capacity for joy. Your ability to trust and connect. Your confidence in your own judgment. For most people these return, rarely in a straight line and rarely on a schedule. The Decoder describes the wearing-away in identity erosion. It can be reversed.

Some of it doesn’t. A version of you who never knew what you know now, who has no memory of it, isn’t coming back. Nobody who’s lived through something big gets to be untouched by it.

What’s available instead?

A third version. One who carries the experience, has learned hard things from it, and isn’t lesser for it. Often wiser about people. Clearer about what you will and won’t accept. Kinder to others in trouble.

“Getting back to normal” is the wrong goal. The person you’re becoming is the right one.

Why does this reframe matter?

Because chasing your old self sets a target you can’t reach, and leaves you feeling you’re always falling short. Building forward from what’s come back, plus what you’ve learned, is both truer and more achievable.

How do I meet this new version?

  • Notice what’s returned. Write it down. It’s easy to miss.
  • Notice what’s new: limits you now hold, people you now spot.
  • Try things. See Your own tastes, rediscovered.
  • Be patient with what’s still healing. See What “recovered” means.
  • Let people meet the new you. See Making new people from scratch.

What tends to come back first?

Many people notice small things first: laughing at something on TV, enjoying a meal, sleeping through the night. Bigger things, like confidence in your decisions or trust in new people, usually return more slowly. See Trust as a practice.

What about the parts of me the relationship used?

Kindness, loyalty, patience, a habit of forgiving. Controlling people often lean on exactly these. You don’t have to give them up. You can keep them and add limits: kind, with a boundary; loyal, to people who earn it.

What if people say I’ve changed?

You have. Some people will welcome your new limits. Some won’t, especially people who were used to you giving way. Their reaction tells you about them. See Your family and mutual friends.

Can anything good come out of this?

Researchers call it post-traumatic growth. Some people report closer relationships, new priorities or a stronger sense of their own strength after trauma. It isn’t required, and it doesn’t make what happened worth it. Growth and pain can sit side by side.

What “recovered” actually means

What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?

The short answer

Recovery from narcissistic abuse, or any coercive relationship, means getting back choice and capacity, with hard days still allowed: making decisions from your own wishes, trusting your judgment, wanting things, and being present without the past running the show.

“You’ll know it when you feel it” isn’t something you can measure. So here’s a working definition of recovery, stated plainly.

What recovery is not

  • Never having a hard day again. Nobody gets there, including people who’ve recovered by any reasonable measure.
  • Never thinking about them.
  • Forgiving them. See Closure you give yourself, and whether you have to forgive.
  • Being exactly who you were before. See The version of you from before.
  • A finish line on a date. This book won’t give you one.

What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?

Recovery is getting back choice and capacity: making decisions from your own wishes rather than fear or old habit, trusting your judgment, wanting things and going after them, and being present in new relationships without the past constantly breaking in.

How do I know I’m recovering?

Some markers people find useful:

  • You make decisions without checking what your ex would have thought.
  • Hard days come and go, instead of becoming hard months.
  • You’re curious about new people and situations, as well as careful.
  • Your sense of your own judgment has mostly come back.
  • You laugh more.
  • You can talk about what happened without being swept away by it.
  • They take up less of your thinking.

None of these need to be perfect. None mean the hard feelings never return. They mean those feelings have stopped running the show.

What if I’m not there yet?

Then you’re somewhere on the way, which is where most people are. Recovery happens in waves, with good stretches and setbacks. A setback doesn’t erase progress. See The long after, and if you’re struggling more than a hard week, When it’s more than aftermath.

What slows recovery down?

  • Ongoing contact or court cases that keep your alarm running. See When it stops.
  • Having no one to talk to. See Making new people from scratch.
  • Poor sleep, or depression that hasn’t been treated. See Sleep.
  • Money stress. See Money from zero.
  • Going back and forth with the person you left. See The trauma bond.

None of these mean you’re failing. They’re weights. Some can be lifted. Some have to be carried for a while.

What helps it along?

Safety first, then routine, then people. Research on recovery after trauma describes three stages: getting safe, telling the story and grieving, and reconnecting with ordinary life. They overlap and loop back. You can be making new friends while you’re still grieving.

How do I measure progress without a timeline?

Compare yourself with yourself, months apart. Not with other people, and not with a date. Once a season, write down your answers to three questions:

  1. What takes less effort now?
  2. What scares me less?
  3. What do I want that I couldn’t let myself want before?

Keep the answers. On bad days, read them.

How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?

The short answer

How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship? There’s no single number, but many people leave and return at least once before they leave for good. Going back isn’t a moral failure, and it doesn’t rule out leaving for good later.

This chapter is written without judgment, because that’s the only useful way to write it.

You left. Then you went back. Maybe more than once. Maybe you’re back now, or thinking about it, and you’re ashamed to be reading a book about leaving.

You’re welcome here.

How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?

You’ll often see the figure “seven times.” It’s widely repeated, but research doesn’t settle on a single number. Different studies of different groups find different patterns. What’s consistent is that leaving more than once is common. For many people, leaving is a process that takes several attempts, not a single act.

Why do people go back?

  • Hope that things have changed, or will.
  • The trauma bond, which pulls hardest in the early weeks. See The trauma bond.
  • Hoovering that arrives at a low moment: the apology, the promise, the crisis. See Hoovering and the Decoder’s hoovering.
  • Practical pressure: money, housing, the children, nowhere to go. See Housing and benefits.
  • Isolation and loneliness after leaving.
  • Fear of what they’ll do if you don’t.
  • Missing them, in a way that briefly outweighs what you know. See What you’re allowed to feel about someone you left.

What tends to be different about the leaving that lasts?

Often, though not always:

  • Clearer information, sometimes gained by going back and seeing that nothing had changed.
  • Stronger supports already in place: a record, legal knowledge, money set aside, people who know the truth.
  • A safety plan. See A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  • Less contact afterwards, so the bond has room to loosen. See No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.

If you’ve gone back before, it doesn’t disqualify you from leaving for good. It doesn’t make this attempt less likely to hold.

What if I’m back now?

  • Keep your record going, quietly. See What to document and how.
  • Keep one or two people close who know the truth.
  • Keep some money and copies of documents somewhere safe.
  • Watch for the warning signs. See Warning signs to take seriously.
  • Know that services will help you whether you’re staying, leaving or unsure.

What this chapter is not

It isn’t encouragement to go back to “get more information.” It’s here so that anyone who has gone back doesn’t carry extra shame on top of everything else.

What if friends and family are angry that I went back?

They may be scared for you, or worn out. Tell one or two of them that you’re safe for now and that you’d like them to stay in touch. If they can’t, a domestic abuse service will stay with you whatever you decide.

What if I leave again?

Many people do, and the second or third time is often better prepared. Use what you learned: your record, your documents, your safety plan, the people who know. See The first 30 days.

Helping someone in year one

How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?

The short answer

To help a friend who left an abusive relationship: believe them, let their feelings be complicated, don’t push a timeline, don’t constantly attack their ex, and stay around for the long haul. What feels helpful and what helps aren’t always the same thing.

This chapter is written to be forwarded, in full, to whoever is supporting someone in the first year after leaving. It’s short on purpose.

How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?

Believe them, fully, the first time. Not with careful questions that sound like you’re checking their story. See The friends who didn’t believe you for why this matters so much.

Let their feelings be complicated. They may grieve, miss the person, feel relief and sadness at once. None of this means they made a mistake, and none of it needs correcting. See What you’re allowed to feel about someone you left.

Don’t push a timeline. Questions about whether they’re “over it yet,” or about dating, land as pressure, however kind the intention.

Don’t keep attacking their ex, even if you’re furious on their behalf. It can make some people defend or minimise, because it’s easier than managing your anger on top of their own.

Help with the practical. Meals, lifts to court, an hour with the children, sitting with them while they read messages.

Learn the safety basics. Don’t share their location or news with mutual friends. Don’t pass on messages from their ex. See A safety plan for after you’ve left.

What feels helpful and what helps are sometimes different things. Believing them, being steady, and following their lead beats advice, deadlines and even righteous anger.

What if they go back?

Stay in touch. Don’t say “I told you so.” People often leave more than once, and they’re more likely to leave for good if they still have people to come back to. See If you go back.

What if I’m worried about their safety?

Tell them, once, calmly. Share Warning signs to take seriously. If you think they’re in immediate danger, call emergency services. If they talk about harming themselves, see When it’s more than aftermath.

The single most useful thing you can do

Stay. Check in next month, and the month after, not just in the first hard week. If you support survivors for work, see For professionals. And look after yourself too. See The friends who did, and are tired.

What should I say, and what should I avoid?

Helpful:

  • “I believe you.”
  • “That sounds exhausting.”
  • “What would help this week?”
  • “You don’t have to decide anything today.”

Best avoided:

  • “Why didn’t you leave sooner?”
  • “Just ignore them.”
  • “You need to move on.”
  • “At least you’re out now.”

How can I help with the practical side?

Offer lifts to court or appointments, childcare, a meal, an hour sitting with them while they read messages, help finding a lawyer, or a safe place to keep documents and a ready bag. See Find help.

The long after

What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years on?

The short answer

Life after narcissistic abuse, years on, isn’t a straight line, and it doesn’t have to be. What’s available is a life that isn’t organised around what happened: relationships built on trust, tastes that are yours, anger that has mostly settled, and the capacity to choose.

This isn’t a straight line, and it was never going to be. Some weeks feel like real progress. Some feel like every chapter of this book arriving at once, out of nowhere. Both are part of the same process. Neither one is the “real” direction.

What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years later?

People who are years out describe things like this:

  • A life that isn’t organised around what happened. Days go by without thinking about them.
  • Relationships built on trust that was given slowly and earned. See Trust as a practice.
  • Tastes and habits that are simply yours, no longer a recovery project. See Your own tastes, rediscovered.
  • Anger that has mostly settled, with an occasional brief return that doesn’t mean anything has gone wrong. See Anger, arriving late.
  • A steadier body, with fewer jolts and better sleep.
  • Clearer judgment about people, and limits held without effort.
  • For some, contact that still exists because of children, but takes up a small corner of life instead of the centre.

Recovery leaves the past in place and gives you back your capacity to choose, to trust, to want things, and to build a life that isn’t defined by what you left.

  • ☐ Asked the court for protections: separate waiting room, screen or video link, separate entrance or times
  • ☐ Supporter arranged, and court asked if they can come in
  • ☐ Folder: documents in date order, timeline summary on top, key evidence flagged
  • ☐ List of the points I want to make, in order
  • ☐ Travel and parking planned; plan to leave without meeting them
  • ☐ Childcare arranged
  • ☐ Someone to check on me after

What to bring

  • ☐ Folder and a spare copy of key documents
  • ☐ Notebook and pen
  • ☐ ID and the hearing letter
  • ☐ Water, snack, charger, something to read
  • ☐ Tissues

On the day

  • ☐ Arrive early; tell the usher about protections
  • ☐ Don’t agree to anything outside the room I’m unsure of; ask for time
  • ☐ Speak slowly; answer the question asked; then stop
  • ☐ Facts, dates, effect on the children
  • ☐ If I freeze: ask for a moment, breathe, look at my notes
  • ☐ Speak only to the judge, not to them

After

  • ☐ Copy of the order, or know when it arrives
  • ☐ Order read; dates and conditions checked
  • ☐ Copies to school and anyone who needs them
  • ☐ Safety plan updated for the next few days. See A safety plan for after you’ve left.
  • ☐ Something gentle planned for myself

The short answer

How to reply to an abusive ex, in a few lines: keep it brief, factual, polite and final, and answer only what needs answering. These are the replies most people need most often.

Not sure how to reply to an abusive ex? Start here.

Accusation: “I don’t agree with that. Pickup is still 5pm Friday.”

Rant or insult: No reply, or “I’ll respond to anything about the children’s arrangements.”

Last-minute change: “The agreed time is 5pm. If you can’t make that, let me know by Thursday.”

“Answer me now”: “I’ve received your message. I’ll reply by Wednesday.”

Questions about my life: “That’s private. Is there anything about the children you need?”

Guilt: “I understand you’re unhappy with it. It stays as agreed.”

Threat of court: “That’s your choice. I’ll follow the current order.”

“Emergency”: “If it’s an emergency, please call [emergency number]. If it’s about the children, I’m happy to discuss it here.”

“Let’s just talk”: “I’ll only communicate in writing about the children.”

Apology or “I’ve changed”: “Thank you. I’ll keep our contact to the children’s arrangements.”

Closing contact: “I’ve read your message. I’m not going to reopen contact. Please don’t contact me again.”

To a friend passing messages: “Please don’t pass on messages. If it matters, they can contact me in writing.”

Before sending: Would I be fine with a judge reading this? Then wait an hour.

A to Z: the words, plainly

What do these post-separation abuse terms mean?

The short answer

The post-separation abuse terms people search for, from address confidentiality to vexatious litigant, each explained in a line or two, with a link to the chapter that covers it and, where there is one, the Pattern Decoder page.

These are the post-separation abuse terms people search for, explained plainly.

Address confidentiality program. A substitute address for public records, so an ex can’t find you through them. Your address, your records, your mail.

BIFF. A way of replying to hostile messages: brief, informative, friendly, firm. Words you can use.

Blame-shifting. Making you responsible for what they did. Guilt and shame; Decoder: blame-shifting.

Coerced debt. Debt in your name that you didn’t freely agree to. Money as the channel.

Coercive control. A pattern of behaviour that takes away someone’s freedom and sense of self. A crime in some places. What post-separation abuse is.

Complex PTSD. A recognised condition linked to prolonged trauma, with lasting effects on emotions, self-worth and relationships. What your nervous system is doing.

DARVO. Deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. When they say you’re the abuser; Decoder: DARVO.

Discard. Ending a relationship suddenly and coldly, often after blaming you for everything. If they left you; Decoder: blame and discard.

Economic abuse. Using money to control: restricting it, taking it, or running up debt. Money as the channel; Decoder: financial control.

Fawning. Going along with someone to keep the peace, a survival response. What your nervous system is doing; Decoder: fawning.

Flying monkeys. People who carry an abuser’s messages or pressure to you. Your family and mutual friends; Decoder: proxy recruitment.

Future faking. Big promises about the future to pull you in or back. Hoovering; Decoder: future faking.

Gaslighting. Making you doubt your memory or perception. Memory, and doubting yourself; Decoder: gaslighting.

Grey rock. Being as dull and unreactive as possible in necessary contact. Grey rock and written-only contact.

Hoovering. Trying to pull a former partner back into contact. Hoovering; Decoder: hoovering.

Hypervigilance. Staying on high alert after the danger has passed. What your nervous system is doing.

Intermittent reinforcement. Unpredictable warmth and cruelty, which builds a strong bond. The trauma bond; Decoder: push-pull.

Intimate image abuse. Sharing, or threatening to share, intimate images without consent. Intimate images and online threats.

Isolation. Cutting someone off from friends and family. Making new people from scratch; Decoder: isolation.

Litigation abuse. Using courts to harass or wear someone down. Systems abuse and litigation abuse; Decoder: legal abuse.

Love bombing. Overwhelming attention and affection early on, or on return. Dating, when you’re ready and when you’re not; Decoder: love bombing.

Minimisation. Making what happened sound smaller than it was. Abuse, or a hard breakup?; Decoder: minimization.

No contact. No communication of any kind. No contact, low contact, or contact you can’t avoid.

Parallel parenting. Raising children in two homes with minimal contact between parents. Co-parenting or parallel parenting?.

Parental alienation. A disputed term for one parent turning a child against the other. Family court, honestly.

Post-separation abuse. Control, fear or punishment that continues after a relationship ends. What post-separation abuse is.

Protective order. A court order to stay away and stop contact. Protective orders.

Reactive abuse. A popular term for provoking someone until they react, then using the reaction against them. When they say you’re the abuser.

Reproductive coercion. Control over contraception, pregnancy or fertility. If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion.

Silent treatment. Withdrawing communication as punishment. Messages you don’t have to answer; Decoder: silent treatment.

Smear campaign. A sustained effort to damage your reputation. The narrative campaign; Decoder: smear campaigns.

Stalking. A pattern of unwanted pursuit that causes fear. Stalking and harassment after a breakup.

Stalkerware. Hidden software that reports what you do on your phone. Surveillance and technology.

Stonewalling. Refusing to engage, as a tactic. Grey rock and written-only contact; Decoder: stonewalling.

Trauma bond. A strong attachment to someone who hurt you, built by cycles of harm and warmth. The trauma bond; Decoder: trauma bonding.

Triangulation. Bringing a third person in to unsettle or compete. The new partner; Decoder: triangulation.

Vexatious litigant. Someone a court has restricted from filing repeated cases without permission. Systems abuse and litigation abuse.

The post-separation abuse questions people ask most, each with a short, plain answer and a link to the chapter with the full detail.

These are the post-separation abuse questions people ask most.

What is post-separation abuse? Control, fear or punishment from a former partner that continues after the relationship ends, through children, money, courts, other people or technology. Read more.

Is post-separation abuse a crime? Parts of it are crimes almost everywhere (threats, stalking, breaking an order), and coercive control itself is a crime in some places. Read more.

Does post-separation abuse ever end? For many people it fades or stops; for some it continues in a smaller form for years. Read more.

Is leaving the most dangerous time? It can be, especially with a very controlling partner. Plan for safety. Read more.

What is hoovering? An attempt to pull you back into contact, often just as you’re doing well. Read more.

Will my ex come back? Many do, at least once. You don’t owe a reply. Read more.

What is a trauma bond? A strong attachment to someone who hurt you, built by cycles of harm and warmth. Read more.

How long does a trauma bond last? There’s no fixed time; it usually weakens once contact stops, in waves. Read more.

Why do I miss my abuser? Because the relationship wasn’t only harm, and the bond was built strong. Missing isn’t wanting back. Read more.

Is my ex a narcissist? Only a clinician can diagnose that, and you don’t need a diagnosis to protect yourself. Read more.

What do narcissists do after a breakup? Common patterns: silence, a smear campaign, fast replacement, and a return. Read more.

Can you co-parent with a narcissist? Usually not in the usual way; parallel parenting works better. Read more.

What is parallel parenting? Two homes, minimal written contact, and a detailed plan. Read more.

What is the grey rock method? Short, flat, factual replies that give them nothing to react to. Not for every situation. Read more.

Should I respond to my ex? Only when a message needs an answer: children, court, money with a deadline. Read more.

How do I document post-separation abuse? Four lines, the same day: when, what, who saw it, what it changed. Read more.

What is litigation abuse? Using the courts to harass or exhaust you. Courts can limit it. Read more.

Where can I get free legal help? Legal aid, domestic abuse legal services, law clinics and court help desks. Read more.

How does a protective order work? A court order to stay away and stop contact; breaking it can lead to arrest. Read more.

Can I report post-separation abuse to the police? Yes. Describe the pattern and ask for a reference number. Read more.

What is reactive abuse? Being provoked until you react, then having the reaction used against you. Read more.

Is my ex tracking me? Check accounts, location sharing, your phone and your car, safely. Read more.

What if my ex threatens to post pictures of me? It’s a crime in many places, and tools can block images from spreading. Read more.

How do I explain the divorce to my child? Simple, true facts for their age; not their fault; loved by both. Read more.

How do I make handovers safe? School handovers, a public place, a third person, or a contact centre. Read more.

Why can’t I stop thinking about my ex? Obsessive thoughts are part of the withdrawal from a trauma bond, and they ease with distance. Read more.

Do I have to forgive? No. Forgiveness is a choice, not a requirement for healing. Read more.

How many times does it take to leave? There’s no single number; leaving more than once is common. Read more.

Will I ever be the same? Not exactly, and much of you comes back, plus what you’ve learned. Read more.

What does recovery look like? Getting back choice and capacity, not never having a hard day. Read more.

How do I help a friend who left? Believe them, follow their lead, and stay. Read more.
