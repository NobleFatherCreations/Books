SCENARIO 6 — “It's Just A Costume”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. Not a predator. Hundreds of people, none of whom thought about it, wearing your grandmother's dress, a headdress, a bindi, a keffiyeh, a sacred pattern, as festival wear — and one person who will argue with you about it if you say anything.

Picture it. You are walking to the second stage and you pass a war bonnet, two bindis and somebody's idea of a kimono, and you are doing the thing you always do, which is deciding forty separate times an hour whether to spend energy on it. Then somebody in your own camp asks you — warmly, as the resident expert — whether her outfit is okay, and now it is your job.

Here are the signs. Sacred or ceremonial items worn as decoration. You are asked to adjudicate, because you are the nearest person of colour. Objecting is treated as policing rather than as information. “It's appreciation” offered as though it settles the question. The energy cost is continuous and invisible to everyone around you. Somebody wants absolution more than they want an answer.

Now here's what's really happening. This belongs in a safety guide for one reason: the cumulative cost. Any single instance is minor and arguable. Forty a day, every day, while also being asked to educate people about it, is a continuous background tax on the same attention you need for everything else in this guide, and depleted attention is the precondition every other pattern relies on. There is also a specific trap in being made the arbiter — once you are the person who rules on this, every conversation about it becomes work you did not agree to do, performed for an audience that wants reassurance rather than information.

So here's your check. I call it the T check. Ask: “Am I being asked for information, or for permission?” — Information is a conversation. Permission is a job, and you can decline it.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. You Are Not The Ethics Department. The sharpest version is the person who will not let it go until you approve. They are not asking what you think; they are looking for a member of the group to sign it off so that the discomfort goes away, and your reluctance reads to them as you being difficult rather than as you being tired. That is the moment to notice that a request for education has become an obligation to perform it, in public, for free, about your own culture, at a festival you paid to attend. You are allowed to hand it back. Declining to be the arbiter is not conceding the argument; it is refusing an unpaid role.

So here's your move. Decide your own policy in advance and apply it without deliberating each time — most people pick one thing they will always say something about and let the rest go, and that is a legitimate and sustainable choice. Hand the adjudication back with a resource rather than a ruling.

Here are the words. “I'm not the person to green-light that, and I'd rather not be. Have a look up why it's a thing — there's loads written by people who actually want to explain it.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Deciding forty times a day whether to say something is the cost nobody else can see. Spend it on purpose, not by default.
