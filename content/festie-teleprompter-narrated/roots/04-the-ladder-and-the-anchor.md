SCENARIO 4 — “The Ladder And The Anchor”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about the person you'll meet here. For anyone whose safety plan cannot be copied straight out of a generic guide — which, at some events, is most of the people reading this one.

Here's how it plays out. Thursday afternoon, before anything has happened, while the site is calm and the people you would need are all findable and not busy. Twenty minutes now buys you the thing that is impossible to assemble at two in the morning with a torch in your face, which is a set of names and a route you already trust.

So here's what to watch for. Two named non-security contacts: welfare, and a camp or community lead. Two people on site who will physically stand next to you on request. One person off-site who expects a check-in and knows where you are. Your own transport, or a lift that is not in anybody else's control. A note app for time, place, names — written the same hour, not the next day. The affinity camp or crew, found on day one rather than on the bad night.

Now here's what's really going on. Everything in this guide is cheaper to arrange in advance than to invent under pressure, and the reason is the same one that runs through this whole book: the moment is designed to be one where you cannot think. A prepared ladder converts a judgment call into a lookup. The witness rule does the most work of anything on that list — accounts of these encounters diverge sharply depending on whether anyone else was present, and a second person standing beside you changes both what happens and what can be said about it afterwards. None of this is about expecting trouble. It is the same logic as knowing where the medical tent is.

So run this check. I call it the R check. Ask: “Can I name my two non-security contacts right now?” — If not, that is the twenty minutes, and it is best spent on Thursday.

And here's the darker reality. Write It The Same Hour. Documentation is the part people skip and the part that matters most, because these situations are decided later by whose account is more specific. A dated note written within the hour — time, location, names, what was actually said, who else was there — outperforms any amount of remembered detail weeks on, and it does not require anyone to find you credible in the moment. Send it to someone off-site so it exists outside this weekend and outside your own phone. It takes ninety seconds. Do it even when you are fairly sure nothing will come of it, because the cases where something does come of it are exactly the ones where you did not expect it to.

So here's the play. Get your two non-security names in the first hour on site. Agree the stand-next-to-me signal with two friends. Write anything that happens down the same hour and send it off-site. Keep your own way home.

Here's exactly what to say. “Who's the welfare lead this year, and how do I reach them directly? — If I text you a pin, just come stand next to me, you don't have to say anything.”

And here's the truth. A plan built for the average reader is not built for you. Build yours on Thursday, out of real names.
