SCENARIO 3 — “They Called Security On You”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. Someone in a dispute with you who has correctly worked out that involving staff is a move that costs them nothing and costs you a great deal. Sometimes deliberate. Sometimes just a person who reached for the phone without thinking about who would be believed.

Picture it. There was an argument at your camp. It was nothing. Now there are two radios and a torch and the questions are coming at you rather than at the person who started it, and the specific thing you notice, standing there, is that everybody is being perfectly polite and the outcome has somehow already been decided before anybody asked you anything.

Here are the signs. Security arrives asking you the questions rather than asking everyone. The other party is treated as the reporter rather than as a participant. Your tone becomes the subject rather than what happened. Someone reaches for staff or police unusually fast over something small. You are asked to leave to resolve it while the other party stays. You are, or are close to, the only one of you in the immediate area.

Now here's what's really happening. This book's standard advice is to escalate — tell a ranger, tell security, involve staff. That advice assumes the escalation route is neutral, and for a lot of readers it is not: the same call produces different outcomes depending on who is standing where, and a police involvement that resolves one person's evening can be a materially dangerous event for another. Saying so is not cynicism about festival staff, most of whom are decent and trying. It is the difference between a safety plan built for an average reader and one built for you. A plan that ignores this is not neutral — it is a plan that works for other people.

So here's your check. I call it the O check. Ask: “If this escalates to staff, who does that help?” — Ask it before the moment, because in the moment there is no time.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Build A Ladder That Stops Where You Need It To. The practical version of this is not avoid all staff — it is have more rungs before that one, and know which specific humans they are. Camp leads, welfare, the harm-reduction tent, an artist liaison, a named accessibility or community lead: these are escalation routes that are generally safer and almost always more effective than a radio call, and they exist at nearly every festival. The other half is witnesses. The single biggest change in how one of these encounters goes is whether two other people are standing there, and the second biggest is whether somebody is recording. Neither requires a confrontation. Both require having thought about it before the torch is pointing at you, which is the entire reason this page exists.

So here's your move. On arrival, get two names that are not security — welfare and a camp or community lead. In any dispute, get a witness beside you before it escalates, stay still and boring, and write it down immediately afterwards with the time and the names. Do not resolve it alone in the dark.

Here are the words. “I'm happy to talk about this with welfare present. — [to a friend] Stand here with me a minute, would you.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Escalation is a tool, not a reflex. Know which rung is safe for you before you need to climb.
