SCENARIO 4 — “I Built This Camp”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. The specific dynamic where a camp founder or long-standing leader begins to treat the camp as personal property — where their vision overrides community input, their comfort overrides member wellbeing, and accountability flows downward but not upward.

Picture it. You have run this camp for seven years. You have poured extraordinary amounts of time, money, and love into building it. You know the infrastructure better than anyone. You also notice that your decisions rarely get questioned, that members seem careful about how they raise concerns with you, and that the last two people who pushed back significantly on a camp decision are no longer in the camp. You tell yourself they were not a good fit. You have not considered whether there is a pattern.

Here are the signs. Decisions are made unilaterally and questioning them is received as ingratitude or disloyalty. People who push back on decisions tend to leave or be pushed out. Your comfort and vision consistently override member input in conflicts. The camp's accountability structures apply to members but not clearly to you.

Now here's what's really happening. Founder syndrome in camp culture is the accumulation of unchecked authority over time — a process so gradual it is often invisible to the person experiencing it. The camp leader who started with genuine community intention can become, through years of carrying the weight of the camp, someone who confuses their investment in the camp with ownership of it and ownership of the people in it. The antidote is structural: accountability that includes you, decision-making that includes others, and the willingness to hear things about yourself that are hard.

So here's your check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Am I accountable to my camp in the same ways my camp members are accountable to me?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Structural Accountability For Camp Leadership. Practical accountability structures for camp leads: (1) At least one other person in the camp has authority to raise concerns about your behavior without social cost. (2) Major camp decisions involve input from members before they are made, not just communication after. (3) There is a clear process for raising concerns about camp leadership specifically. (4) Camp finances are transparent to at least two members beyond yourself. (5) Exit from the camp is genuinely free — members can leave without social consequences. (6) You actively seek honest feedback after events rather than only welcoming positive feedback. These structures do not diminish your leadership. They make it trustworthy.

So here's your move. Build accountability structures that include you. The camp that cannot hold its leader accountable cannot protect its members.

Here are the words. “I want to create a formal way for camp members to raise concerns about my decisions and my leadership. Here is what I am proposing.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The camp you built belongs to the community in it as much as to you. Lead accordingly.
