SCENARIO 11 — “Who's Holding The Camp Money”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. Whoever collects for the generator, the truck, the food, the sound system and the pitch — often the founder, often for years, often with no accounting anybody has ever seen.

Here's how it plays out. Everyone pays in two hundred at the start of the season and nobody has ever seen a spreadsheet. It is almost certainly fine. It is also the reason that when somebody wants to leave the camp, or disagrees with him about something, there is an unspoken question about what happens to what they have put in, and nobody asks it out loud.

So here's what to watch for. One person collects and spends with no reporting. No receipts, no ledger, no statement at the end of the season. Members do not know what they have collectively paid for. Assets — the shade structure, the sound system, the truck — are in one name. Leaving the camp means losing money nobody can quantify. Asking about it would be treated as an accusation.

Now here's what's really going on. Camp money is usually handled by a trusted person acting in good faith, and the problem is structural rather than moral: unaccounted collective funds create an exit cost that nobody can name, and an exit cost is leverage whether or not anybody intends to use it. It compounds with the housing-as-leverage dynamic elsewhere in this guide — if the same person controls where you sleep, what you have paid in, and the assets you helped buy, then disagreeing with them has a price that is real and unquantifiable, which is the worst combination.

So run this check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Could any member see where the money went, without asking a favour?” — If not, trust is doing a job that a spreadsheet should be doing.

And here's the darker reality. Transparency Protects The Person Holding It. If you are the one holding the money, the accounting is for you before it is for anybody else. The moment a camp has a falling-out — and camps do — an unaccounted fund becomes the thing people reach for, and years of genuine generosity get reinterpreted in a group chat by people doing arithmetic from memory. A shared sheet, receipts photographed into a folder, and a five-minute end-of-season summary makes that impossible. And put the significant assets somewhere collective, because a shade structure everybody paid for that lives in one person's garage is an ownership question waiting for a bad year.

So here's the play. Run a shared spreadsheet anybody can open, photograph receipts into a shared folder, and post a short end-of-season summary without being asked. Name a second signatory. Write down who owns the major assets and what happens to them if the camp splits.

Here's exactly what to say. “Putting the camp finances on a shared sheet this year — not because anything's wrong, just so it's never a question. Anyone want to be second pair of eyes on it?”

And here's the truth. Unaccounted money is an exit cost nobody can name, and it is leverage even when nobody means it to be.
