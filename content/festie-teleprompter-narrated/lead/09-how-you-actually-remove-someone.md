SCENARIO 9 — “How You Actually Remove Someone”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. You, holding a decision you have already made and have been postponing for eleven months because you do not know how to do the next part without it becoming a scene.

Here's the scene. You know he cannot come back. You have known since August. What you do not have is a way of saying it that does not turn into a two-hour argument in front of the camp, or a group chat meltdown, or him turning up anyway because nothing was ever said clearly enough to be enforceable.

So watch for these. The decision is made and only the delivery is missing. You are waiting for one more incident to make it defensible. The camp is quietly organizing itself around avoiding him. People have started not coming, and you know which people. There is no written camp agreement to point at. You are planning to do it in person, alone, at the event.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Most camp exclusions go badly for procedural reasons rather than emotional ones: delivered verbally, at the event, by one person, with no written basis and no defined scope, which makes them arguable, unenforceable and humiliating for everyone present. Every organization that does this well does the same four things — decide off-site, deliver in writing, be specific and short about what and for how long, and do not debate. The last one is the hardest and the most important: the decision is not being negotiated, it is being communicated.

So ask yourself this. I call it the A check. Ask: “Is this decided, and is it written down?” — If it is not written, it is a disagreement rather than a decision, and it will be treated as one.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Do It Before The Event, Not At It. Doing it on site is the mistake that produces every bad outcome. He arrives having paid, packed and travelled, in front of an audience, with nowhere to go and nothing to lose — and that is the version that becomes a confrontation, or a scene the whole camp remembers, or a safety issue at two in the morning. Send it weeks out, in writing, and tell the event's safeguarding lead that you have done it so somebody official knows before he turns up. Also protect the person who raised it: never name them, and never let the reasoning be specific enough to identify them, because the single biggest deterrent to anyone else ever reporting anything is watching what happened to the last person who did.

So here's what you do. Decide with two other people, not alone. Send it in writing weeks before the event, short, specific, with a clear scope and no invitation to discuss. Tell the event's safeguarding lead. Brief the camp on what is happening without the detail and without names.

Here's what to say, out loud. “You're not camping with us this year. This is a decision, not a discussion, and I'm not going to debate it over messages. If you turn up, we'll be asking the event to handle it.”

And remember this. Waiting for one more incident means one more person gets hurt. Decide off-site, write it down, and do not debate it.
