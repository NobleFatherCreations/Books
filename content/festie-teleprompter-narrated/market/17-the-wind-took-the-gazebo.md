SCENARIO 17 — “The Wind Took The Gazebo”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. The forecast, and a pitch fee that bought you a square of grass and no guarantees whatsoever.

Here's how it plays out. It went at four in the morning. The frame is in three pieces, half the stock is in a puddle, the rest blew toward the fence, and the event is running as normal at ten. Nobody is going to reimburse you, your pitch fee is not coming back, and you have just discovered that your policy covers theft but not weather.

So here's what to watch for. Gazebo weights instead of ground anchors, on a forecast you did not check. Stock stored at ground level in a field. No stock insurance, or a policy that excludes weather and unattended loss. No written terms about cancellation or refund with the organizer. No contingency float to survive a wiped weekend. Everything you own on the circuit is in this one gazebo.

Now here's what's really going on. Vendors carry all the risk at an outdoor event and almost none of the protection. Pitch fees are near-universally non-refundable, organizers rarely take liability for weather, and the standard trader policy people buy often excludes exactly the two things most likely to happen in a field — storm damage and loss from an unattended stall overnight. The result is a business that can lose a season's stock in one night with no route to recovery, sitting next to an event that continues around it.

So run this check. I call it the R check. Ask: “If my stall was destroyed tonight, what would I actually recover?” — Read the policy now, not at four in the morning.

And here's the darker reality. Concentration Is The Real Exposure. The structural danger is concentration rather than weather. Most makers put their entire inventory into one vehicle, one gazebo, one weekend, and a single night removes the business rather than a weekend's takings. Split the stock: keep a reserve in the van, and a reserve at home that never travels, so the worst night costs you an event instead of a livelihood. Then read the policy wording for storm and unattended exclusions, ask the organizer in writing what happens to the pitch fee if the event is cancelled or a trading day is lost, and keep a float that covers one wiped weekend. None of this is pessimism; it is the difference between a bad weekend and starting again.

So here's the play. Ground-anchor properly and check the forecast daily. Stock off the ground, in lidded boxes. Split inventory across stall, van and home. Get a policy that covers storm and unattended stock, and get the organizer's cancellation terms in writing before you pay the pitch fee.

Here's exactly what to say. “Before I pay the pitch — what are the terms if the event's cancelled or we lose a trading day to weather? Can you put that in the email? — Half the stock stays in the van, always.”

And here's the truth. The pitch fee buys you grass. Everything that happens on it is yours, so split the stock and read the exclusions.
