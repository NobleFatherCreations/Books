SCENARIO 8 — “Booth Placement As Punishment”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about the person you'll meet here. The promoter or market organizer who uses booth placement as a reward and punishment system — giving prime spots to compliant vendors and moving anyone who raises concerns or exercises independence to low-traffic locations.

Here's how it plays out. You raised concerns about an electricity fee discrepancy in a vendor group last season — politely, with documentation, requesting the refund you were owed. This season's placement confirmation arrives: spot 52, far corner, partial shade, foot traffic rated poor on the organizer's own map. Last season you were in spot 8. Nothing has changed about your product or professionalism. Everything has changed about your willingness to be quiet.

So here's what to watch for. Placement quality drops significantly after you raised a legitimate concern. The explanation for the change is vague or contradictory. Other vendors who also raised concerns have also received worse placements. You feel that raising future concerns would cost you even more placement quality.

Now here's what's really going on. Placement retaliation is a control mechanism that works because it is hard to prove and because the cost of fighting it feels higher than the cost of compliance. The organizer has plausible deniability — placement decisions involve many factors. The counter is documentation of the pattern across multiple events and multiple vendors, and building relationships with other organizers so your circuit access does not depend on any one event.

So run this check. I call it the K check. Ask: “Am I staying quiet about legitimate concerns because I am afraid of what it will cost my placement next season?” — an organizer who punishes standards is telling you exactly who they are.

And here's the darker reality. Building Circuit Independence. The most effective protection against any single organizer's retaliation is a circuit that does not depend on them. Practical steps: apply to at least three times more events than you plan to attend so you have real options. Build direct relationships with multiple organizers independently. Document your sales performance and vendor reviews so your reputation exists independently. Participate in vendor communities so other organizers know your work before they see your application. Independence is the protection.

So here's the play. Build circuit access that does not depend on any single organizer's approval.

Here's exactly what to say. “I am going to continue applying to a range of events so I always have real options each season.”

And here's the truth. The organizer who punishes you for having standards is telling you they are not the right partner for your business.
