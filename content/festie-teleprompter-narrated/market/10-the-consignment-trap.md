SCENARIO 10 — “The Consignment Trap”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. The shop, gallery, boutique, or pop-up that wants your work on consignment — and then does not pay, goes out of business with your inventory inside, or loses the reconciliation. Consignment is legitimate. Unprotected consignment is a gift.

Picture it. "We would love to carry your line — we do 60/40 in your favor, you would be in great company." You drop off $1,800 of wholesale inventory. Three months later you are still waiting for the first check. The shop is still open. Your work is still on the shelves. Phone calls go unanswered. Emails get vague responses. Six months in you have recovered $600 of $1,800 in product and the shop just posted new arrivals on Instagram.

Here are the signs. No written consignment agreement with payment terms and reconciliation schedule. First payment arrives late or partial — this sets the pattern for everything that follows. No inventory tracking system or signed receipt for what you left. The owner becomes progressively harder to reach as time goes on.

Now here's what's really happening. Consignment fraud is one of the most common forms of vendor exploitation because artists feel awkward being professional with people who seem enthusiastic about their work. The enthusiasm is real — your work is valuable. The payment intention varies. A consignment agreement is not distrust — it is the thing that makes the relationship honest and enforceable. Without it, recovering your inventory or your payment requires legal action that costs more than the inventory is worth.

So here's your check. I call it the M check. Ask: “Do I have a signed agreement with payment terms — or just a handshake and a hope?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Consignment Agreement Minimum Terms. Every consignment placement needs in writing: itemized list of pieces with your price and the split, payment schedule (monthly reconciliation is standard), what happens to unsold pieces and when (90-day return clause), who is responsible for lost or damaged inventory, and what access you have to check on your pieces. Bring a printed agreement to every drop-off and do not rely on theirs. If a shop will not sign your agreement — take your inventory back. That is your information about their payment intention.

So here's your move. No signed consignment agreement, no inventory drop-off. This policy saves you every time.

Here are the words. “I use a standard consignment agreement for all my placements — I have one here for us both to sign before I leave anything.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Enthusiasm about your work is not a payment plan. A signed agreement is.
