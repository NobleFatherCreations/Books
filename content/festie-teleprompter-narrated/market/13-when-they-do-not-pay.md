SCENARIO 13 — “When They Do Not Pay”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's someone you'll recognize. You did the event. You delivered the inventory. You completed the commission. The payment did not come. This page covers what you actually do next — step by step — because most predatory promoters and buyers rely on vendors being too tired or too non-confrontational to pursue it.

Picture it. It has been six weeks since the event. You have sent three polite emails. You have received two vague replies and one promise of "by end of week" that came and went. The promoter is still posting about upcoming events on Instagram. Your follow-up messages are being read but not replied to. You are owed $1,200 and you are running out of patience and confidence that it is coming.

Here are the signs. Payment delayed past agreed date without proactive communication from their side. Replies become progressively vague, slow, or one-word as time passes. "Accounting issues" or "cash flow" appear as explanations without a concrete resolution date. They are visibly active on social media and running their business while ignoring your payment.

Now here's what's really happening. Most non-payment situations resolve at the written demand stage because most bad-faith payers are not prepared to actually face legal consequences — they are relying on vendors giving up. A written demand with a specific deadline and a reference to your legal options is the trigger that moves most situations to resolution. The ones who still do not pay after a written demand are the ones you take to small claims. The process is simpler than most people think.

So here's your check. I call it the R check. Ask: “Do I have documentation of everything agreed and every follow-up I have sent?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The Non-Payment Escalation Sequence. Step 1: Formal written demand email with your contract attached, a specific payment deadline of 7-14 days, and a statement that you will pursue legal remedies if unpaid. Step 2: small claims court — handles disputes up to $10,000-$25,000 depending on state, filing fee usually under $100, you represent yourself. Step 3: attorney demand letter for larger amounts ($150-300 one-time) frequently resolves it immediately. Step 4: publicizing non-payment in vendor communities is legal and effective. Document everything before going public. Most situations resolve at Step 1 or 2.

So here's your move. Send the written demand. Most situations resolve there. Be prepared to file if they do not.

Here are the words. “Per our agreement, payment of [X] was due on [date]. Please remit by [new date] or I will pursue this through small claims court.”

And here's the truth underneath it. You did the work. You get paid. This is completely enforceable and simpler than they want you to think.
