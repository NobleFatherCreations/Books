There are three other painters doing the same thing and a crowd standing in it watching, and by the fourth hour you have the headache you always get and have stopped noticing the smell, which is the part that should worry you most.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Solvent and aerosol exposure is a genuine occupational hazard with cumulative effects on the nervous system and the airways, and live painting at festivals reproduces almost every condition that makes it worse: enclosed space, long consecutive days, no extraction, and no employer carrying a duty of care. The critical detail is that a dust mask does nothing against organic vapour — it is the wrong class of protection entirely, and it produces a false sense of safety that leads people to work longer in worse air.

So ask yourself this. I call it the E check. Ask: “Can I smell it, and is air actually moving through here?” — If you have stopped smelling it and nothing is moving, leave now rather than at the end of the piece.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Acclimatization Is Not Tolerance. The dangerous signal is the one that feels like good news. When the smell fades you have not adapted, your olfactory receptors have saturated — and you are now breathing the same concentration with the warning system switched off, which is why people work through it and why the worst exposures happen to the most experienced painters on the longest days. Cumulative solvent exposure over years of festivals is a known occupational pattern and it does not announce itself. Buy the right cartridge respirator, insist on open sides, and take the piece outside when the rain stops.

So here's what you do. Carry an organic-vapour cartridge respirator, not a dust mask, and know when the cartridges expire. Insist on cross-ventilation as a condition of the pitch. Break outside every hour. Use water-based paint where the piece allows it, and keep the public out of the immediate air.

And remember this. When you stop smelling it, your warning system has failed, not the fumes. That is the moment to go outside.
