SCENARIO 7 — “There Are Kids In That Frame”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about the person you'll meet here. You, in the family field, where the light is good and the pictures are genuinely lovely and every single one of them has a safeguarding dimension you are responsible for.

Here's how it plays out. A child on someone's shoulders at golden hour with her hands in the air is one of the great festival photographs and you have a clear shot of it. You do not know her, you have not spoken to her parents, and in about four hours you will be deciding whether to put a recognizable image of somebody else's child on a public account.

So here's what to watch for. A recognizable minor, no parent asked. The frame would identify them to anyone who knows them. You are relying on the setting being public as your permission. A child in distress, undressed, or in a medical context. You have no written consent and are about to publish commercially. You would not be comfortable if a stranger did this to your own child.

Now here's what's really going on. Photographing children is the one area of event photography where the legal position and the professional standard diverge most sharply. In most public settings it is not unlawful, and every festival's media policy, every safeguarding lead, and every reputable agency nevertheless requires parental consent for identifiable minors — because the risk is not to you, it is to a child whose image you are putting into circulation without anyone who is responsible for them having agreed. Custody arrangements, safeguarding orders and families who are not findable are all invisible from behind a lens.

So run this check. I call it the L check. Ask: “Has a parent said yes, by name, to this specific use?” — Public setting is not permission where a child is concerned.

And here's the darker reality. You Cannot See The Reason It Matters. The specific families this hurts are the ones you cannot identify from where you are standing: a child in a custody dispute whose location must not be public, a family with a safeguarding order, a parent who has moved away from someone. A photograph of a child geotagged at a named festival on a public account can undo arrangements that took years to build, and the photographer will never learn that they did it. That is the whole argument for asking, and it is why the standard is consent rather than judgement — you are not in a position to assess the risk, so you do not get to decide it is low.

So here's the play. Ask the parent before you shoot, and get a name and a contact if you intend to publish. Default to backs of heads, crowd-scale, or unidentifiable framing in family areas. Never shoot a child in distress or in a medical context. If in doubt, send it to the parent and let them decide.

Here's exactly what to say. “Lovely moment — do you mind if I take it? I'd only post it if you're happy, and I'll send it to you either way. Can I grab your number?”

And here's the truth. A public field is not consent for somebody else's child. Ask the parent, or shoot the back of the head.
