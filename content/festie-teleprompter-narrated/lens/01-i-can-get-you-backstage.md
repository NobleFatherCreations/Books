SCENARIO 1 — “I Can Get You Backstage”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about a specific type. Has a laminate, a long lens, and an unusual amount of freedom of movement. Offers to shoot you, offers to get you in, offers to introduce you — and the offers arrive in a sequence that keeps having a next step.

Here's how it plays out. He says your look is incredible and he wants to shoot you properly, and he can get you into the artist area because he shoots for them. The first shoot is professional and the pictures are genuinely the best you have ever had of yourself. The second one he suggests somewhere private for the light, and mentions the festival he shoots in the spring, and asks whether you would want to be involved with that.

So here's what to watch for. Access, introductions and opportunity all route through one person. Shoots move from public to private across sessions. Wardrobe or nudity is introduced gradually rather than agreed up front. The next opportunity is always just ahead and is always in his gift. No call sheet, no assistant, no third person, no written brief. The work is good, which is what makes the arrangement hard to leave.

Now here's what's really going on. A camera is one of the most efficient access tools at a festival — it explains proximity, justifies attention, licenses photographing strangers, and moves through restricted space. Nearly everyone carrying one is doing the job. But the same properties make it attractive to someone who wants proximity for other reasons, and the pattern is the classic one from the modelling and music industries: real opportunity, real skill, real photographs, with each step requiring slightly more and each step justified by the last. The tell is not creepiness. It is the absence of the ordinary professional furniture — no brief, no third person, no rate, nothing in writing.

So run this check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Is there a third person, a written brief, and a stated rate?” — Professionals have all three. Their absence is not informality, it is the design.

And here's the darker reality. The Photographs Are The Hold. The photographs themselves become the leverage, and that is often the point rather than a side effect. Once someone holds images of you — especially anything you agreed to when you were tired, altered, flattered, or halfway through a shoot you did not want to ruin — they hold something that can be released, hinted at, or simply remembered out loud. Nothing has to be threatened. If you are in this: the files existing is not the end of your options, and the fastest way to defuse a hold is to stop it being a secret — tell one person now, in writing, before anything is asked. A secret with two keepers is worth far less to the person holding it.

So here's the play. Insist on the professional furniture: a written brief, a rate or a clear trade, a third person present, and no private locations for a first shoot. Bring somebody. Somebody wanting to shoot you properly will not mind an assistant; somebody wanting the room will mind a great deal, and that is your answer.

Here's exactly what to say. “Sounds great — I'll bring a friend, and can you send me a quick brief and what the usage is? I keep it all written down, it's just how I work.”

And here's the truth. A real photographer wants an assistant, a brief and a release. The ones who want the room empty are telling you what the shoot is for.
