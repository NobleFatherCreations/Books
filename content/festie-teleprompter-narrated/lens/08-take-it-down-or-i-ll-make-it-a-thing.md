SCENARIO 8 — “Take It Down Or I'll Make It A Thing”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about someone you'll recognize. Someone in a frame you published — sometimes with an entirely legitimate grievance you should act on immediately, sometimes escalating to demands for money, and occasionally mobilizing a pile-on that arrives before you have read the first message.

Here's the scene. The message says take it down. You took it at a public stage, you asked at the time, and you have the reply. The second message says you owe them money for using their image. The third says they have screenshotted your account and people are going to hear about this, and by the morning there are forty comments on a photograph of somebody else entirely.

So watch for these. A takedown request — which you should simply honour, immediately. A demand for payment attached to the takedown. Threats about your reputation rather than about the image. Claims that shift each time you answer them. Public escalation before any private conversation. You have no record of the consent you actually obtained.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Two different things arrive in the same inbox and telling them apart is the whole skill. A person asking for their image to come down is exercising something you should grant without argument, whatever the law says and whatever you paid to be there — that is the profession's own standard and it is also the cheapest possible resolution. A demand for money, or a threat about your reputation, is a different transaction, and the correct response to that one is not negotiation but documentation.

So ask yourself this. I call it the N check. Ask: “Is this a request to remove, or a demand for something else?” — Remove instantly for the first. Do not pay the second.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Delete First, Document Everything. The sequence that keeps photographers out of trouble is counterintuitive: comply completely and immediately on the takedown, and only then deal with the rest. Arguing about whether you were entitled to publish is the thing that converts a two-minute fix into a public campaign, because to everyone watching you are the person with the camera refusing to delete a picture. Take it down, say you have, apologize for the distress without conceding any claim, and keep every message. And build the thing that makes all of this short next time: a consent log, with a name, a date and what was agreed, which turns a he-said-she-said into a record.

So here's what you do. Remove it immediately and say so. Do not pay, do not argue the law, do not respond publicly. Screenshot everything with timestamps. Keep a consent log from now on so the next one is a thirty-second conversation.

Here's what to say, out loud. “It's down — removed as soon as I saw your message, and I'm sorry it upset you. I'm not able to pay anything, but the image is gone and it won't be used again.”

And remember this. Take it down first and sort out who was right afterwards. The photograph was never worth the week.
