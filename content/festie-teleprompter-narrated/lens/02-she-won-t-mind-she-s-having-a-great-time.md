SCENARIO 2 — “She Won't Mind, She's Having A Great Time”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. You, on a good night, with a great frame in front of you and a legal right to take it and a rationalization already forming. This page is aimed at the photographer, not at the subject.

Here's the scene. It is four in the morning and she is beautiful and completely gone, sitting on the grass laughing at nothing with her top half off, and it is the best light of the entire weekend. Nobody would stop you. It is a public field. She is not in a position to object and will not remember either way, and you already know that is the reason the shot is available.

So watch for these. The subject could not consent right now if you asked. You are relying on them not noticing rather than on them agreeing. The frame's value comes from their state rather than from the moment. Long lens, from distance, on someone who has not clocked you. You are constructing a justification before you have taken it. You would not show them the shot tomorrow and ask.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Legality and ethics part company here completely. In most jurisdictions you may photograph people in public and publish it, and that permission is exactly why the profession needs a standard above it — the law is not doing the work of deciding whether a frame should exist. A person who is incapacitated cannot consent to being photographed any more than they can consent to anything else, and a photographer relying on their impairment has made the same move as everybody else in this book who waits for capacity to drop. The test is simple and it is the one the whole trade runs on: could you show them tomorrow, and would they say yes.

So ask yourself this. I call it the L check. Ask: “Could this person consent right now if I asked?” — If not, there is no frame here. The camera does not create a permission the situation does not contain.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. You Are The Risk On This Page. Most of this book is about somebody doing something to the reader. This page is the other way round, and it is in here because festival photography has a genuine, ongoing problem with it: the near-nude, the overwhelmed, the passed-out, the person in a crisis at the harm-reduction tent, shot at distance and posted as atmosphere. Those frames circulate for years. They cost people jobs, custody arrangements, and their sense of safety in a place they loved. The photographer is usually not malicious — they are a person who wanted a good shot and let the law answer an ethical question. Do not be that. Tomorrow-test every frame, and when a frame fails it, do not take it rather than not posting it, because files leak and drives get shared.

So here's what you do. Apply the tomorrow test before the shutter, not before the upload. If someone is impaired, in distress, or in a medical or harm-reduction context, put the camera down entirely — and if you have already shot it, delete it now rather than filing it.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Not shooting that one. — [to a colleague] Mate, she's gone, leave it. Get the crowd instead.”

And remember this. The law says you may. The law is not your ethics, and a photograph of somebody's worst night outlives both of you.
