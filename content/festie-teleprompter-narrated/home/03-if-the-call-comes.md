SCENARIO 3 — “If The Call Comes”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about the person you'll meet here. You, woken at four in the morning, hearing something in their voice before you have understood a single word.

Here's the scene. The call you hoped would not come has come. Everything you do in the next ten minutes either makes you someone they can use or someone they regret ringing, and you will be operating on no sleep and pure adrenaline. This is why it is written down.

So watch for these. First sentence: are you safe right now, and where exactly are you. Second: is anyone with you, and can you get to welfare or medical. No questions about what they took, what they were wearing, or whose fault it is. Do not say you told them so, at any point, for any reason, ever. Write down what they tell you — location, names, time — while they say it. Stay on the line until they are with a person who can help.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. In the first ten minutes there are exactly three useful things: establish safety, establish location, and get them to someone physically present who can help. Everything else — the explanation, the details, the feelings, the question of how this happened — belongs to a later conversation and will be available then. The reason to know this in advance is that your own fear will be enormous and will present itself as an urgent need for information, and answering that need is the single most common way these calls go wrong.

So ask yourself this. I call it the E check. Ask: “Am I getting them help, or getting myself information?” — Both feel identical at four in the morning. Only one of them is useful.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. What Not To Say, And The Thing To Know. Never, in that call, ask what they took or what they were wearing or why they went off alone. Not because it is rude — because it tells them this was their fault, and that judgment will shut down the disclosure mid-sentence and quite possibly permanently. Two practical things worth knowing before you ever need them. Every festival has a welfare or medical tent that operates day and night, and it is staffed by people who do this constantly and without judgment; getting your person to that tent is more useful than any advice you can give down a phone. And if it was an assault: in most places a forensic examination can be done without making a police report, the window is measured in days not hours, and the decision about police can be made later. That single fact is one most people do not know, and knowing it removes the choice that stops people getting care.

So here's what you do. Safety, location, get them to a human. Write down what they say as they say it. Then — and only then — ask what they need from you, and do that rather than what you think they need.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Are you safe right now? Where are you exactly? Is anyone with you? — Okay. I want you to walk to the welfare tent and stay on the phone with me while you do it. You've done the right thing ringing me. We'll sort everything else out after.”

And remember this. They rang you. Whatever else happens tonight, that means you got the week before right. Do not spend it in the first ten minutes.
