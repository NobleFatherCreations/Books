SCENARIO 6 — “When It's Your Partner, Not Your Kid”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. You, at home, while somebody you love is somewhere that is uninhibited, physically affectionate, full of strangers, and entirely outside your view for four days.

Here's the scene. You said have a great time and you meant it. It is Saturday night and you have checked their location three times, and you have looked at who is posting from that field, and you have drafted a message that is technically about whether they are okay and is actually about something else, and you are aware that you are doing it.

So watch for these. Checking location sharing repeatedly rather than once. Messages framed as concern that are asking a different question. Wanting proof of who they are with or what they are doing. Setting expectations for contact frequency that are about reassurance. Feeling relief when they answer and worse forty minutes later. You would not be comfortable if they described your behaviour to a friend.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Distance plus a scene with a reputation plus no information is an excellent generator of anxiety, and anxiety is not the same thing as a problem in the relationship — but the behaviours it produces are the same behaviours that constitute monitoring, and that is worth being honest about while it is still small. Frequent location checking, demands for contact, and requests for proof are on a continuum whose far end is coercive control, and nobody arrives at the far end on purpose. They get there in small, reasonable-feeling steps, each one justified by worry.

So ask yourself this. I call it the O check. Ask: “Am I asking for information, or for reassurance?” — Both are human. Only one of them is fair to send.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Say The Real Thing, Or Say Nothing. The honest version is better than the disguised one in every case. “I'm feeling insecure and I don't need you to fix it, I just wanted to say it” is a sentence that costs you something and does no damage. “Are you okay? Who's there? Why haven't you replied?” is a sentence that looks like care and functions as surveillance, and they will feel the difference even if they cannot name it — and what it produces is a partner who starts managing your anxiety from a field, which is the beginning of them telling you less rather than more. If the checking is compulsive, the work is yours and it is not about this weekend. Deal with it at home, not through their phone.

So here's what you do. Agree the contact plan before they go — one message a day, no expectation of a reply within any window — so neither of you is improvising. Then put the phone down and do something. If you are checking location repeatedly, turn the sharing off for the weekend; it is feeding the thing it is supposed to settle.

Here's what to say, out loud. “One message a day, whenever suits you, and no pressure to reply — I'll assume you're having a brilliant time. — I'm feeling a bit insecure this weekend. That's mine, not yours. Just wanted to say it rather than send you weird texts.”

And remember this. Anxiety and monitoring feel identical from the inside and completely different from the other end. Say the real sentence instead.
