SCENARIO 9 — “The Front Of The Crowd”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about a specific type. Physics. Not a bad actor, not a decision anybody made — a density figure, a pinch point, and a moment when a crowd stops being people and becomes a fluid.

Here's how it plays out. It is the headline act, the barrier is full, there is a surge from the back, and somewhere in the middle of it a person goes down and the people around them cannot bend to help because there is no room to bend. Nothing about this looks dangerous from the mixing desk and it is the single most lethal thing that happens at music events.

So here's what to watch for. Density above roughly four to five people per square metre. Pinch points, narrowing barriers, or one exit route into a viewing area. Crowd movement that propagates as waves rather than as individuals. No spotters at the barrier and no view from the front looking back. No agreed show-stop authority, or one nobody will use. Late-arriving surges toward a single popular act.

Now here's what's really going on. Deaths in crowds are almost never trampling, which is the popular misconception — they are compressive asphyxia, where density becomes high enough that people cannot expand their chests, and it is survivable for only a few minutes. It is entirely a design and management problem: density limits, monitoring from a position that can see the front, spotters at the barrier, and crucially a named person with unambiguous authority to stop the show. The single factor that turns a dangerous crowd into a fatal one is hesitation about stopping.

So run this check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Who can stop the show, right now, without asking anybody?” — If that person is not named and briefed, you do not have a crowd safety plan.

And here's the darker reality. Somebody Must Be Able To Stop It Without Permission. Every crowd disaster review finds the same failure: people could see it and nobody stopped it, because stopping meant overriding a headline performance and nobody was sure they were allowed. Name the person, tell them in writing that they have the authority, tell the artist and their management in advance that a show-stop may happen and that they are to stop when asked, and rehearse the call. Give your spotters the same authority to escalate. It costs a few minutes of a set and the alternative is the thing that ends people's lives and organizations. Nobody has ever regretted a show-stop.

So here's the play. Set and monitor density limits with a competent crowd safety adviser. Put spotters at the barrier facing the crowd. Name the show-stop authority in writing and brief artists that it exists. Design out pinch points and single-route viewing areas. Rehearse the stop.

Here's exactly what to say. “Show-stop authority tonight is Kerry, and it is not subject to anyone's approval including mine. If she calls it, the band stops mid-song. — [to management] Please brief the artist that a show-stop is possible and what it sounds like.”

And here's the truth. People do not get trampled, they get compressed, and it takes minutes. Name the person who can stop it without asking.
