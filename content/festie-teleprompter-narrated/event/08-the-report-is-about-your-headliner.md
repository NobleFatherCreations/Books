SCENARIO 8 — “The Report Is About Your Headliner”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's someone you'll recognize. You, holding a credible report about the one person on the bill you cannot replace, forty-eight hours out, with six thousand tickets sold on their name.

Picture it. It came through welfare last night and it is specific, it is not the first thing you have half-heard about him, and he is on at ten tomorrow. The maths is doing itself in your head before you have finished reading: refunds, the agent, the insurance, next year's booking relationships, and a field full of people who bought tickets for him.

Here are the signs. The commercial cost of acting is immediately obvious and the cost of not acting is not. You are looking for reasons the report might be unreliable. You are thinking about handling it quietly, after the event. The agent's likely reaction is in your reasoning. You have heard something before and it did not go anywhere. Nobody outside your commercial interest is involved in the decision.

Now here's what's really happening. This is the decision that defines an event, and the pattern of failure across the industry is consistent: not a promoter who decided to protect a predator, but a promoter who decided to handle it after the weekend, and then after the season, and then not at all. Everything about the moment pushes one way — the cost of acting is concrete and immediate, the cost of not acting is diffuse and deferred — and that asymmetry is the whole trap. The counter is structural: a decision rule written when nothing is at stake, and a person outside the commercial line who makes the call.

So here's your check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Would I act on this identical report if it were about a volunteer?” — If yes, then the only variable is money, and you already know that is not a reason.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Everything Comes Out Eventually, With Your Name On It. Every event that has been publicly destroyed by this was destroyed by the cover-up rather than by the incident. It surfaces two or three years later, with the timeline, and the sentence that ends the organization is always that they knew and booked him anyway — because that is provable from a welfare log and an email. The decision looks commercial in the moment and reputational in retrospect, and the reputational version is always the larger number. Act on it now, document the decision and the reasoning, and accept the refunds, because the refunds are the cheap option and you will not believe that until it is too late to choose them.

So here's your move. Take the decision out of the commercial line — your safeguarding lead decides, not you. Act on the current event: remove, or supervise and separate with named conditions. Document the report, the decision and the reasoning the same day. Tell the agent factually and in writing. Write the rule into next year's contracts before you need it again.

Here are the words. “This goes to our safeguarding lead and their decision stands, including if it costs us the headline slot. — [to the agent] We've received a report we're acting on. He's not performing. I'm putting the decision and the reasons in writing to you today.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The refund is the cheap option and it will never feel like it. What ends events is not the incident, it is the email showing you knew.
