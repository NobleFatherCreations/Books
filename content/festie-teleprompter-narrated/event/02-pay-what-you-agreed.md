SCENARIO 2 — “Pay What You Agreed”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. You agreed to a fee. You agreed to a payment timeline. Those agreements are binding regardless of how the event went, what your costs were, or what complications arose. Pay what you agreed, on the timeline you agreed. This is the entire baseline of a professional reputation.

Picture it. The event cost more than projected. The attendance was lower than you hoped. The performer you agreed to pay $2,000 is now getting a message that you can only do $1,200 "given how the event went." She had no say in how the event went. She delivered her performance. She incurred the travel costs. She fulfilled her side of the agreement. The event's financial performance is your risk, not hers.

Here are the signs. Post-event financial "adjustments" are contract violations — not conversations to negotiate. The event's costs and revenue are your business risk — not a risk that transfers to your artists. Payment timelines in contracts are enforceable — "end of month" is a legal commitment. Offering future booking as compensation for current non-payment is not compensation.

Now here's what's really happening. Late payment and post-event fee adjustments are so normalized in the independent event world that many promoters do not register them as the contract violations they are. They are. When you agree to a fee and a payment timeline, that agreement is binding. The event's financial performance does not change your obligation. Artists and vendors who absorb your losses are subsidizing your events with their own labor and financial risk. That is not a sustainable or ethical business model.

So here's your check. I call it the T check. Ask: “Am I paying what I agreed, to whom I agreed, on the timeline I agreed — every time?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. What Professional Payment Practice Looks Like. Professional payment practice: (1) Pay deposits on the agreed timeline before the event — not when you get around to it. (2) Reconcile and pay final amounts within the agreed timeframe after the event — 7-14 days is standard. (3) If you genuinely cannot pay on time: communicate proactively before the deadline, not after you have already missed it. (4) Do not offer future booking, exposure, or "a better deal next time" as substitutes for agreed payment. (5) If you had to adjust fees due to genuine crisis: negotiate BEFORE the event, not after. Late adjustment is a unilateral contract change. That is not how agreements work.

So here's your move. Pay what you agreed, on the agreed timeline. Every time. This is the whole standard.

Here are the words. "The payment will be in your account by [agreed date]. Let me know if you do not receive it by then."

And here's the truth underneath it. Your financial reputation is built one payment at a time. It is also destroyed one payment at a time.
