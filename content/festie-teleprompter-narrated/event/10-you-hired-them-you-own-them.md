SCENARIO 10 — “You Hired Them, You Own Them”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. A security company that you engaged, whose staff you did not select, did not brief on your values, and whose conduct the public will attribute entirely to your event.

Here's the scene. Two complaints come through about the same door team — a search that was handled badly, and someone spoken to in a way that was not acceptable. They are not your employees. They are wearing lanyards with your logo on them, and to everybody who was there they are your festival.

So watch for these. No briefing given beyond capacity and ejection procedures. No shared standard on searches, gender, or trans attendees. Complaints about security route to the security company, not to you. No mechanism for an attendee to report a guard. The same complaints recur each year with no record kept. You have never checked licensing, or spoken to the guards yourself.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Security are the most physically interventionist people on site and usually the least integrated into the event's stated values, because they arrive as a contracted service rather than as part of the team. Attendees do not distinguish: a bad search or an aggressive ejection is remembered as something your festival did. The fix is contractual and procedural — brief them as staff, set the standards in the contract, and provide a complaints route that does not run through the company being complained about.

So ask yourself this. I call it the N check. Ask: “Can an attendee complain about a guard to someone who does not work for the security company?” — If not, you will never hear about it and it will continue.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Searches Are Where This Actually Goes Wrong. The specific, recurring failure is the search, because it is the one moment where a stranger in a uniform makes physical contact with every single person who enters. Get it in writing in the contract: same-gender searching offered as standard, a stated option for trans and non-binary attendees including a private space and an officer of their choosing, no searching of minors without a guardian present, and a stated protocol for assistance dogs, mobility equipment and medication. None of that is exotic and almost no event specifies it, which is why it is improvised at the gate by whoever is on that morning — and improvisation at a gate is how a person's weekend starts badly and how your festival acquires a reputation.

So here's what you do. Brief security as staff, in person, on your values and your consent policy. Put the search standards, licensing requirements and conduct expectations in the contract. Publish a complaints route that bypasses the contractor. Log every complaint and review it before renewing.

Here's what to say, out loud. “You're briefed with our team, not separately — our consent policy applies to your staff. Same-gender searches offered as standard, private option available, and complaints come to our welfare lead, not to your supervisor.”

And remember this. They wear your logo and the public cannot tell the difference. Brief them like staff, or accept what they do in your name.
