SCENARIO 5 — “The Recording Rights Question”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. Your event produces content — video, audio, photography, social media. The question of what you own and what your artists and performers own is both a legal question and an ethical one. This page is about being honest about both.

Picture it. Your festival set videos are performing well on your YouTube channel. They are driving discovery for your next event. They are also generating streaming revenue through the platform's music content system. The artists in those videos were paid for their performance. They were not paid for the recording, the distribution, or the streaming revenue. Your standard booking confirmation contained a rights clause they did not negotiate. The content that is building your brand was created by people who are not sharing in its returns.

Here are the signs. Sweeping recording rights clauses in standard booking confirmation language. Live recordings generating streaming or licensing revenue without artist participation. Photography and video of artists used in commercial contexts without their agreement. Social media content featuring artists used to promote future events without compensation.

Now here's what's really happening. The gap between what promoters legally own through standard contracts and what they ethically owe the artists who created the content is significant. Artists whose performances are recorded, distributed, and monetized are entitled to participate in that revenue. A recording rights clause buried in a booking confirmation is not the same as a negotiated agreement. The ethical standard is transparency about what you are doing with the content and fair participation for the people who created it.

So here's your check. I call it the V check. Ask: “Are the artists whose recordings I am using being compensated fairly for the commercial use I am putting that footage to?” — the content that builds your brand was created by artists. Treat their contribution accordingly.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Ethical Recording Rights Practice. Ethical practice for event recordings: (1) Be explicit in booking agreements about what recording will happen and for what purpose. (2) Distinguish between archival recording (no commercial use) and commercial recording (streaming, licensing, promotion) — these are different agreements. (3) Share streaming and licensing revenue with performing artists — this is both the legal standard in many contexts and the ethical one in all contexts. (4) Use artist images and recordings for event promotion only with their explicit agreement. (5) When in doubt: ask before you use. The conversation is easier before the content is published than after.

So here's your move. Be transparent about what you record, how you use it, and share revenue with the people who created it.

Here are the words. “Here is what we record at our events and how we use it. Here is how we share revenue with performing artists.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The content that builds your brand was created by artists. Treat their contribution accordingly.
