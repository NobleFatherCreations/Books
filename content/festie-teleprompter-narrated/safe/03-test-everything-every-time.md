SCENARIO 3 — “Test Everything Every Time”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a specific type. Fentanyl is in the supply. It is in pills, powders, and substances where people are not expecting it. It is invisible, odorless, and a lethal dose is smaller than a few grains of salt. Testing is the single most effective harm reduction step you can take.

Here's the scene. "Someone in our camp tested their MDMA Saturday morning and it came back as something completely different." This happens at every major festival. Not occasionally — every festival, every season. The person who handed it to you may not have known either. Testing is not about distrust of the person who gave it to you. It is about knowing what is actually in what you are consuming. You would not eat a wild mushroom without identifying it first. Same exact energy. Same exact stakes.

So watch for these. Fentanyl test strips: dissolve a tiny amount in water, dip — ONE line means fentanyl present, do not use. Reagent kits (Marquis, Mecke, Simon's): color reactions identify MDMA, MDA, amphetamines, and adulterants. Start with a much smaller amount than you think is the dose — festival environments intensify everything. Wait before redosing — onset times vary and stacking doses before you feel the first one is how people get in trouble.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The recreational drug supply has been contaminated with fentanyl and fentanyl analogues at a scale that makes testing not optional — it is a basic safety step equivalent to checking that your tent stakes are in the ground before it rains.

So ask yourself this. I call it the A check. Ask: “Have I tested this — and does everyone in my group know what to do if something goes wrong?” — two minutes of testing is the difference between a good night and a medical emergency.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Every Time Means The Batch You Already Trust. Fentanyl and its analogues have turned up in pressed pills sold as MDMA, in cocaine, in ketamine, in counterfeit prescription tablets — substances where nobody in the chain intended an opioid to be present at all. Contamination is not evenly mixed: two pills pressed in the same batch can differ, so a strip that came back clean on Friday’s half says nothing about Saturday’s. This is the specific reason “it’s from a friend” is not a safety measure — your friend is not the chemist, he is another customer, and he is testing nothing either. Two strips and a reagent kit cost less than a night of parking. Carry naloxone even if you never touch an opioid on purpose, because the overdose you reverse will most likely belong to somebody who also thought they never had.

So here's what you do. Test your stuff. Carry Narcan. Know the overdose signs. These three things save lives.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Hold on — let me test it first. I have strips in my kit. Two minutes.”

And remember this. The two minutes it takes to test is not inconvenience. It is the difference between a good night and a medical emergency.
