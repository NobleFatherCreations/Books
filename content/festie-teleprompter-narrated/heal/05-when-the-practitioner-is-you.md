SCENARIO 5 — “When The Practitioner Is You”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about a specific type. You. Honest, well-trained, in this for the right reasons, and holding more power in every session than the frame of healing makes it easy to notice.

Here's how it plays out. A client tells you, three weeks later, that something in a session did not sit right. Your entire body wants to explain — what you actually did, what the modality is, what they may have misread. That impulse is normal, it is human, and it is the exact moment where good practitioners become bad ones. What you do in the next sixty seconds is your whole ethics, and it is not a feeling, it is a procedure.

So here's what to watch for. You are the only one who could say what happened in that room. Your client was undressed, prone, altered, or grieving — sometimes all four. Attraction to a client has arisen and you have not told a supervisor. You have extended a session, or a role, because it felt right in the moment. You have no supervisor, no insurance, and no complaints route. Your first instinct on hearing a concern is to explain rather than to hear.

Now here's what's really going on. The power differential in this work does not switch off because your intentions are good. You control the room, the interpretation, the duration, and the narrative afterwards, and your client is frequently in a state they cannot fully assess. That is why every regulated profession requires supervision, insurance, a scope statement, and a complaints route that does not run through you — not because practitioners are assumed to be predators, but because an unsupervised practitioner cannot self-detect drift. The false shaman this guide opens with relies on the entire field being unregulated. The way honest practitioners take that cover away is by visibly having the things he cannot have.

So run this check. I call it the L check. Ask: “If a client wanted to complain about me, could they — to someone who is not me?” — If not, you are running the same structure as the person you would never want to be.

And here's the darker reality. Nobody Decides To Become That Person. Read the misconduct literature and the pattern is remarkably consistent: almost nobody sets out to do it. It goes one session long, then one region unannounced, then one dual relationship, then a rationalization that the work required it — and each step was taken by somebody who believed themselves to be a good practitioner, because they were, right up until they were not. Supervision exists because that drift is invisible from inside. If you are reading this and one specific session came to mind: that is not proof you are a bad practitioner, it is the ordinary early warning this whole page is for. Take it to a supervisor this week. The ones who never take anything to anyone are the ones this ends badly for.

So here's the play. Build what the false ones cannot fake: insurance, a named supervisor you actually use, a written scope with limits, a spoken consent brief every session, and a complaints route that bypasses you — published. If a concern comes to you, the first response is thank you for telling me, and the second is a referral to that route. Not an explanation.

Here's exactly what to say. “Thank you for telling me — that took something. I'm not going to defend myself at you. Here's the person you can take it to independently, and I'll cooperate with whatever they ask.”

And here's the truth. The practitioner people can safely complain about is the one worth going to. Be the one with a route that does not run through you.
