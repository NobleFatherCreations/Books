SCENARIO 7 — “The Client Who Books You Every Day”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's the person you'll meet here. Books repeatedly, discloses heavily and fast, and treats the session as a relationship rather than an appointment. Sometimes genuinely in distress. Sometimes using the one structure on site where a stranger will touch you alone in a tent for an hour.

Picture it. Third booking in two days. He has started arriving early and staying after, and the disclosures have moved from his shoulder to his marriage to the fact that you are the only person who has ever really understood him, and today he asked whether you would have dinner, and when you said no he apologized so thoroughly that you ended up reassuring him.

Here are the signs. Books far more often than the presenting issue warrants. Rapid, heavy personal disclosure aimed at building intimacy. Arrives early, stays late, wants to be in your space outside the session. Gifts, or paying over the rate. Tests small boundaries and apologizes elaborately when they hold. You have started dreading the booking and have not told anyone.

Now here's what's really happening. The therapeutic frame is unusually exploitable in this direction: a practitioner is alone, often in a soft-walled space, professionally committed to warmth and non-judgement, and financially motivated to keep a repeat client. All four of those work against you. This is a known pattern in massage, bodywork and complementary practice, and the festival version is worse because there is no reception, no colleague next door, no booking system, and no employer — just you, a tent and somebody who booked an hour of unobserved access.

So here's your check. I call it the L check. Ask: “Is this a client, or is somebody building a relationship inside an appointment?” — Frequency, disclosure and after-time are the three that tell you.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Your Training Is Being Used Against You. Notice which of your professional instincts are working for him. Non-judgement stops you naming it. Holding space keeps you in the tent. Not wanting to shame a client stops you saying no plainly. Needing the booking stops you cancelling. Every one of those is a good quality and all four are load-bearing for him, which is why practitioners are so slow to act on this and why by the time they do it has usually escalated. Break it with structure rather than with a judgement about him: somebody else on site knows your schedule, the tent flap stays open, and the booking pattern is capped. You do not have to decide he is dangerous to stop being alone with him.

So here's your move. Cap repeat bookings and say the cap is policy. Tell one person your schedule and have them walk past at the end of each session. Keep the space semi-open. End the after-time at the door rather than inside, and write down what happened the same day.

Here are the words. “I keep it to one session a day per person so everyone gets a slot — book you in Sunday. — I'm going to wrap it there, I've got Mira coming in. [walking out with him]”

And here's the truth underneath it. Warmth is your professional tool. It is not an obligation to be alone with somebody who is using it.
