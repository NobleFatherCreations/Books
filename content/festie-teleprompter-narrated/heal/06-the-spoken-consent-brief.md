SCENARIO 6 — “The Spoken Consent Brief”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. For practitioners, and for anyone about to get on a table, into a container, or under someone's hands. Ninety seconds, said out loud, every session, including with the client you have seen forty times.

Here's the scene. The brief is the thing that makes the rest of this guide unnecessary. It is also the thing that gets skipped first, because it feels clinical and the work is supposed to feel sacred — and that feeling is precisely what every pattern in this guide has been exploiting. Said once, it converts an unspoken understanding into a shared agreement, and unspoken understandings are the medium predators swim in.

So watch for these. Name the modality and what it actually involves, in plain words. Name every area you will work on, and what stays covered. Name what is outside your scope and who you refer to. Say how long, and that any extension gets re-agreed out loud. Say that stop ends it instantly, with no explanation owed and no cost. Say who they can raise a concern with who is not you — and mean it.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Every documented mechanism in this guide — scope creep, unannounced contact, sessions that extend, agreements revised mid-state — fails against a brief that was given out loud beforehand. Not because a bad practitioner will not try, but because the client now has a stated baseline to notice the departure from, and noticing is the whole difficulty. This is the same logic as the pre-burn check in F.L.O.W. and the access plan in A.C.C.E.S.S.: front-load the decisions to a moment when everyone is clear-headed, so the moment of pressure only requires recognition rather than judgment.

So ask yourself this. I call it the H check. Ask: “Was the brief given out loud this session — not implied, not last time?” — If it was skipped, that is the first departure, and it happened before anything else did.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Who Objects To Ninety Seconds. There is diagnostic value in offering it. Honest practitioners are glad of the brief — it protects them as much as the client, and most will tell you it has saved them from a misunderstanding at some point. A practitioner who finds it cold, clinical, unnecessary between people who trust each other, or contrary to the flow of the work is telling you that they prefer the ambiguity, and ambiguity has exactly one beneficiary in a room where one person is dressed and standing. You are allowed to ask for it as a client. The answer you get is worth more than the ninety seconds.

So here's what you do. Practitioners: write yours, six lines, and say it every time until it is automatic. Clients: if nobody gives you one, ask — what will you be working on, what stays covered, how long, and who do I talk to if something feels off.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Before we start: shoulders and upper back only, everything else stays covered, fifty minutes, and if I want to go anywhere else I'll ask you first. Stop ends it immediately, no explanation needed. And if anything sits badly afterwards, here's who you can talk to who isn't me.”

And remember this. Ninety seconds of plain speech removes most of what is in this guide. That is why the people in this guide skip it.
