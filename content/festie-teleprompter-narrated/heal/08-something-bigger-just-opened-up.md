SCENARIO 8 — “Something Bigger Just Opened Up”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. A client who came in for tension and is now in the middle of something much larger — a flashback, a panic attack, a disclosure of abuse, or a psychiatric emergency — in your tent, on your table, with you.

Here's how it plays out. Bodywork opened something and she is not in the room any more. She is shaking, breathing badly, somewhere in her own history, and telling you something she has clearly never said out loud. You are a massage therapist. You have ninety seconds to decide what you are and are not going to try to do, and every instinct you have is pulling you toward staying with it.

So here's what to watch for. Dissociation, flashback, or panic on the table. A disclosure of abuse, self-harm or suicidal thinking. Signs of a psychiatric emergency rather than emotional release. You are considering doing something you were never trained to do. The session has run long because stopping feels like abandonment. You have no referral route and did not find one before the event.

Now here's what's really going on. Bodywork and altered-state contexts surface trauma routinely, and the single most consequential skill a practitioner has is knowing where their own edge is. Attempting to process trauma without that training can deepen it, and the moment where it feels most compassionate to continue is the moment it is most likely to do harm. The competent response is small and unglamorous: stabilize, ground, do not excavate, and hand over to someone whose job it is. Every large festival has welfare, and most have psychedelic support services staffed by people trained for precisely this.

So run this check. I call it the E check. Ask: “Am I still inside what I was trained to do?” — The honest answer arrives fast, and it is usually no, and no is the correct place to stop.

And here's the darker reality. Staying Is Not Always The Kind Thing. The mistake almost never looks like negligence — it looks like a caring practitioner who did not want to abandon somebody mid-crisis, and who therefore attempted trauma work they had no training for, in a tent, on a stranger, at a festival. That is how people are left more destabilized than they arrived, and it is committed by good practitioners with good intentions every season. Handing over is not abandonment, and it is worth saying out loud to the client that it is not, because they will experience it that way if you do not. Walk them there. Do not send them.

So here's the play. Stop the bodywork. Ground them — upright, feet on the floor, breathing, orient to the room and the day. Do not ask for more detail and do not interpret. Then walk them to welfare or the psychedelic support tent yourself, and debrief afterwards with your own person.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm going to stop the massage. Feet on the floor, sit up with me, look at the doorway. You're at a festival, it's Saturday afternoon, you're safe. — This is bigger than what I'm trained for, and that's not me leaving you. I'm going to walk over to welfare with you and stay while you get someone who does this properly.”

And here's the truth. Knowing where your training stops is your training. Walking somebody to the right person is competence, not failure.
