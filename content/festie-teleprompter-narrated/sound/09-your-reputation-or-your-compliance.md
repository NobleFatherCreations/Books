SCENARIO 9 — “Your Reputation Or Your Compliance”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a particular kind of person. The implicit or explicit threat that pushing back on a promoter, a manager, or an industry figure will result in being blacklisted, badmouthed, or frozen out of the circuit. One of the most effective control mechanisms in the music industry.

Here's the scene. You stopped working with the promoter who wasn't paying you. Two months later you start hearing through the circuit that you're "difficult to work with" and "unreliable." Your booking inquiries from other promoters in that network have slowed. You didn't do anything wrong. But the narrative is already in circulation and you don't know how to counter something you're not supposed to know is being said.

So watch for these. Implicit or explicit threats to your reputation when you exercise independence. Negative characterizations begin circulating after you end a bad deal. Professional contacts who were previously warm become suddenly distant. The person spreading the narrative directly benefits from your compliance.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Reputation threats work because the music and festival circuit is small, relationship-driven, and operates heavily on word of mouth. The goal is to make the cost of pushing back higher than the cost of compliance. It often works. The counter is: document everything before any dispute escalates, build independent relationships across the industry, and proactively communicate your version of events to people who matter to you. Being blacklisted by predatory promoters is often a feature, not a bug.

So ask yourself this. I call it the D check. Ask: “Have I documented the facts of this situation so I have something to point to if my version of events gets challenged later?” — the people worth working with will believe the documentation over the rumor.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. When Your Reputation Is Being Attacked. (1) Document: exactly what is being said, where, by whom, and when. (2) Communicate directly with your most important professional relationships — not to spin but to share facts. "I wanted to reach out directly because I understand there may be things being said about me — here's what actually happened." (3) Let your work speak. Predatory promoters lose credibility over time as more artists share experiences. (4) Musician-specific reputation defense: being known as someone who stands up for fair pay makes you MORE attractive to legitimate promoters.

So here's what you do. Document the dispute. Communicate directly with people who matter to you. Let your work speak.

Here's what to say, out loud. [To a trusted industry contact]: "I'm reaching out directly because I understand there may be things being said — I wanted you to have the facts."

And remember this. Being blacklisted by predatory promoters is often a feature, not a bug. Your real advocates will know the difference.
