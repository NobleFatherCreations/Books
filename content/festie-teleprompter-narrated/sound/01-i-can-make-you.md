SCENARIO 1 — “I Can Make You”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. The promoter with the network, the stages, the bookings. Positions themselves as the gatekeeper to your next level. Uses that position to extract below-rate fees, free promotion work, personal access, and silence. The next level stays perpetually 3 months away.

Picture it. "I'm building something with your sound specifically in mind — I need you for three dates this summer and if those go well we're talking main stage next year. We don't need a formal contract yet, this is about building a relationship first." Three shows later: you've covered your own travel, been paid 60% of what was agreed verbally, done promotional content for free, and the main stage conversation has vaporized.

Here are the signs. Future opportunity always slightly out of reach — dependent on your continued compliance. Contract discussions deflected as premature or as signs of distrust. Payment consistently below what was agreed verbally, framed as temporary. Any friction triggers implicit threat to the whole relationship and future bookings.

Now here's what's really happening. The festival and music industry has a specific power structure that predatory promoters exploit: artists need bookings, bookings require promoter relationships, and challenging a promoter feels like risking your entire access to the scene. The "building something together" frame removes professional clarity while the promoter retains all the structural power. A legitimate promoter makes money when you get paid fairly.

So here's your check. I call it the S check. Ask: “Is there a signed contract — or just a verbal promise and a sense that questioning it risks the whole opportunity?” — that feeling is the leverage working exactly as intended.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The Minimum Performance Contract. Every booking needs in writing: performance fee, payment timeline (50% deposit before, 50% at show is standard), cancellation terms, accommodation and travel responsibilities, set time and length, technical rider requirements, who owns live recordings or footage, and promotional obligations on both sides. American Federation of Musicians (afm.org) has standard contract templates. Music attorneys offer one-time consultations. A legitimate promoter will sign. The ones who won't are giving you your answer.

So here's your move. No contract, no soundcheck. This is a full sentence and a complete policy.

Here are the words. “I'm excited about these dates — let's get the agreement in writing so we're both protected. I can send my standard rider tonight.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Any promoter who won't put it in writing is showing you what the deal actually is.
