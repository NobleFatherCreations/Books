SCENARIO 6 — “This Is A Safe Space”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's someone you'll recognize. The assumption that queer-coded spaces, events, or communities at festivals are automatically safer — and the specific exploitation of that assumption by people who know how to appear to belong in those spaces.

Picture it. The tent has a progress pride flag and the DJ is playing music you know from queer spaces. The crowd feels like your people. You let your guard down more than you normally would at a festival because it feels safer. Later you realize: you were less cautious about who you were talking to, less alert to the dynamics around you, and one of the people you trusted because of the context turned out to be someone who specifically sought out queer spaces because they knew people would be less guarded there.

Here are the signs. You are less alert in a space because it is coded as queer or LGBTQ-friendly. Someone in the space is using queer aesthetics and language but their behavior does not match. The space feels safer than the general festival but you have not actually verified why. Your guard is down in ways that have more to do with the flag than with actual safety information.

Now here's what's really happening. Queer spaces at festivals can be genuinely safer in important ways — more consent-conscious culture, more gender-affirming, more familiar community norms. They can also be specifically targeted by people who know that queer festival-goers will be less guarded in those spaces. A rainbow flag is an aesthetic. Safety is built through behavior, through relationships, through actual community accountability. Verify through those channels — not through the decor.

So here's your check. I call it the D check. Ask: “Have I verified that this space is actually safer — or am I assuming based on aesthetics?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. What Makes A Space Actually Safer. Genuinely safer spaces have: clear consent culture that is actively maintained not just posted on a sign. People who intervene when norms are violated. Accountability for community members who cause harm. Staff or organizers who are actually known to the community. A reporting process that works. Space to leave without social cost. Queer aesthetics without these things are just aesthetics. When you enter a space that is billed as queer or LGBTQ-friendly: look for evidence of active culture maintenance, not just flags. Ask someone you already trust if they know this space and its organizers.

So here's your move. Let queer-coded spaces be a warm starting point. Verify actual safety through behavior and relationships.

Here are the words. “Is this a space you know? Who runs it and are they solid?”

And here's the truth underneath it. Safety is not an aesthetic. Verify through behavior and community relationships, not flags and decor.
