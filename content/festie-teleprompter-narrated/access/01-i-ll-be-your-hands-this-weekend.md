SCENARIO 1 — “I'll Be Your Hands This Weekend”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. Shows up early, helps beautifully, and keeps helping until helping is the only way anything gets done. Learns your routine faster than your friends did. Frames every act of assistance as devotion rather than logistics, so that by day three declining anything feels like rejecting a person instead of declining a task.

Picture it. He offered to push on the gravel stretch Thursday and he was genuinely good at it — read the terrain, asked before every curb, never grabbed the handles without saying so. By Friday he is carrying your meds because your bag was full. By Saturday he knows the schedule better than you do, and when you mention going to the silent disco with the two people from your camp, he says he doesn't mind, he'll come, it's easier, they don't know how to do the transfer properly.

Here are the signs. Helps with one thing, then quietly becomes the route to all things. Learns your routine and your medication times unusually fast. Frames assistance as love or devotion rather than as a task anyone could do. Discourages other people from helping — “they don't know how you like it” Is present for every interaction you have with anyone else. Reacts to you making a plan without him as though it were a betrayal.

Now here's what's really happening. This is dependency grooming with an accessibility costume on. The mechanism is not the helping — the helping is often real and skilled. The mechanism is consolidation: the steady narrowing of who can assist you until one person controls movement, medication, and access to everyone else. Disabled people are victimized at substantially higher rates than non-disabled people, and the perpetrator is disproportionately someone occupying a care or assistance role, because that role supplies proximity, privacy, physical contact, and a ready-made explanation for all three. The tell is never a single act. It is the direction of travel: every week he is load-bearing for one more thing, and every week there is one fewer person who could take over.

So here's your check. I call it the S check. Ask: “If this person left right now, would my weekend still work — or would it stop?” — If it would stop, that is not closeness. That is a single point of failure with feelings attached.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The Helper Nobody Questions. The reason this role is chosen is that it is socially armored. A man pushing your chair reads as a good man to every single person who walks past, which means he can stand closer, stay longer, and be alone with you more than anyone else at this festival — in full view, with everyone approving. If you later say something was wrong, you are contradicting a thing a hundred people watched and admired. That is the actual asset, and it is why the same role attracts the same people at every event. None of this makes the good ones bad. It makes the question worth asking early, while the answer is still cheap: who else could do this, and do they have my number?

So here's your move. Keep at least two people who can do any given task, and use both in the first twenty-four hours so it is normal rather than a statement. Split the functions on purpose — the person who pushes is not the person who holds meds is not the person who knows the plan. If someone objects to redundancy, that objection is the finding.

Here are the words. “I run a two-person system for everything — it's how I stay independent, not a comment on you. Can you take the morning and let Rae take the evening?”

And here's the truth underneath it. Help you can only get from one person is not help. It is a bottleneck, and bottlenecks are where people who want control go to stand.
