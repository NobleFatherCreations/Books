SCENARIO 10 — “Found Your People Vs Being Somebody's Project”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. The good version is other disabled and neurodivergent people, and the non-disabled people who have been around them long enough to have stopped performing. The other version means well, sometimes intensely, and needs you to be receiving something.

Picture it. Two things happen the same weekend. A woman with a cane and a folding stool sees you doing the arithmetic about the distance to the second stage, and says without ceremony that the north path is flat and there is a bench two-thirds of the way, and then talks to you about the lineup for twenty minutes and never mentions either of your bodies again. And a man tells you three times that his camp is a safe space for you and asks whether you are comfortable and asks again and asks whether you are sure.

Here are the signs. Real: gives you information and then moves on to the actual subject. Real: assumes competence — asks before helping, accepts no without a second attempt. Real: you are allowed to be annoying, wrong, or boring without losing standing. Project: needs you to confirm they are doing well at including you. Project: helps without asking, and is hurt when that lands badly. Project: your presence in their camp gets mentioned to other people.

Now here's what's really happening. Real community is identifiable by one thing: whether you can decline without it costing you. In genuine crip and ND space, refusing help, leaving early, going non-verbal, or simply being unimpressive changes nothing about your standing — there is no ledger. Tokenizing warmth is transactional even when it is sincere, and the transaction is that your comfort confirms someone's self-image, which means your discomfort becomes a problem for them rather than for you. The scene has an enormous amount of the real thing in it. It also has people for whom your inclusion is a credential, and the two can sit in the same camp.

So here's your check. I call it the S check. Ask: “Can I say no here and have it change nothing?” — If declining costs you standing, it was never community. It was an arrangement.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. When Your Inclusion Is Somebody's Credential. The uncomfortable version: a person whose self-image depends on being good to you has a stake in you staying in a position where they can be good to you. That rarely looks like malice. It looks like discouraging the other camp, being hurt when you make plans elsewhere, reminding people how welcome you are here. The consequence is the same as the deliberate version on the first page of this guide — your options narrow — and it is harder to name because everyone involved, including them, believes it is kindness. The test stays the same and stays cheap: decline something small and see whether the temperature changes.

So here's your move. Test cheaply and early: decline one small offer and watch what the relationship does. Then find the other disabled and neurodivergent people on site, because they are the network that makes everything else in this guide survivable — the platform regulars, the quiet-zone regulars, the ones with the good folding chair.

Here are the words. “Thanks — I'm good, I've got a system. — Hey, are you here every year? What's the terrain like round the back stages?”

And here's the truth underneath it. The people who are actually your people will let you be ordinary. That is the whole test, and it works in under a minute.
