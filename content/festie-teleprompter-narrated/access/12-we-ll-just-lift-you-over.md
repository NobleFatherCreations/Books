SCENARIO 12 — “We'll Just Lift You Over”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about someone you'll recognize. Helpful, strong, and already reaching for your chair's handles or your arms. Genuinely trying to solve a problem — the mud, the step, the gap — that the event should have solved and did not.

Here's the scene. The path has turned to mud and you are stuck, which was entirely predictable in October. Three men decide to help. Nobody asks how your chair lifts. Somebody takes the armrest, which is not a lifting point and now bends, and you are briefly airborne and entirely at the mercy of people who mean well and have no idea what they are doing, and your footplate is being used as a handle.

So watch for these. Hands on you or your equipment before anyone asked. Lifting by armrests, footplates or wheels rather than the frame. “We've got you” said while you are trying to explain how it works. Being carried somewhere you did not agree to go. Terrain the event never made passable, treated as your problem. The offer of help proceeds regardless of your answer.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Two failures stack here. The event's, which is that accessible routes are an obligation and mud is foreseeable; and the crowd's, which is that helping a disabled person is treated as something that does not require permission. Being lifted incorrectly causes real injuries to both people and to expensive, hard-to-replace equipment, and a chair damaged on Saturday cannot be repaired before Monday, which turns a bad moment into the end of your weekend and possibly your independence for weeks.

So ask yourself this. I call it the E check. Ask: “Did I plan a route I can actually use, in this weather?” — And in the moment: “Did anybody ask me before touching me?”

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Moved Is Not The Same As Helped. Underneath the practical risk is the one that matters more. A person who can be picked up and relocated by strangers without agreement has had their ability to leave taken away, and being carried somewhere you did not choose is the single most efficient way to isolate a disabled person — no coercion needed, no conversation, just a friendly group and a mud patch. Most of the time it is exactly what it looks like. The habit worth building is the one that holds in both cases: nobody moves you or your equipment without your instruction, and the instruction includes where you are going.

So here's what you do. Say stop first and instruct second — people follow clear instructions and freeze at arguments. Know your chair's actual lift points and say them out loud. Pre-plan hard-standing routes and ask the accessibility team about wet-weather alternatives on arrival, before the mud exists.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Stop — hands off, please. If I want lifting I'll tell you where to hold. It's the frame here and here, and we're going to the hard path, not the tent.”

And remember this. Help you did not agree to is just being moved. You decide whether, how, and to where.
