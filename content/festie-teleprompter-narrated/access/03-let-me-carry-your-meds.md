SCENARIO 3 — “Let Me Carry Your Meds”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about the person you'll meet here. Reasonable, practical, and correct that your bag is full. Offers to hold the thing you cannot go twelve hours without. Sometimes a partner, sometimes a friend, sometimes the helper from page one who has already been given three other jobs.

Here's the scene. Your bag is genuinely overloaded and he is genuinely trying to help and the pouch with the afternoon dose genuinely does fit better in his pocket. Then it is 4pm and he is at the other stage and not answering, and you are doing the arithmetic about how bad this gets, and discovering that the answer to “how long can I wait” is now a question about somebody else's schedule.

So watch for these. Offers to hold medication, insulin, inhalers, or a charger you medically depend on. Is slow to return them, with a good reason each time. Suggests you don't need a dose right now, or that you seem fine. Uses the meds as a reason you should come with him rather than bringing them to you. Gets wounded at the suggestion you keep your own. Is the only person who knows where they are.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Whoever controls access to something you cannot skip controls your movements, your timing, and eventually your willingness to argue. This is textbook coercive control and it is well documented in intimate-partner contexts involving disabled partners — withholding medication, mobility equipment, or assistive technology is one of the most common forms it takes, precisely because it is invisible to outsiders and easy to explain away as forgetfulness. At a festival it needs no cruelty to work. Ordinary flakiness produces the same dependency, and a person who wants leverage only has to notice how well it worked and do it again.

So ask yourself this. I call it the C check. Ask: “Am I holding everything I cannot skip — meds, dose, charger, spare?” — If the answer routes through somebody else's pocket, that is a leash regardless of who is holding it.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. The Dose You Have To Ask For. The second half of this is the part people miss: once the asking starts, the asking itself becomes the leverage. You begin choosing your battles — not raising the thing that annoyed you, not saying you want to go to the other stage — because you have to ask this person for something at six o'clock and you do not want it to be awkward. Nobody threatened anything. Nobody had to. That quiet self-editing around a person who holds something essential is the clearest early sign of coercive control there is, and at a festival it can install itself in a single weekend. Carry your own. Carry a spare somewhere else. Tell one other person where the spare is.

So here's what you do. Split your supply: primary on your body, spare in a second location with a second person, and a photograph of the label and dose on your phone. Never consolidate. If someone is already holding them, retrieve them in daylight, in company, cheerfully, with a logistics reason and no accusation.

Here's what to say, out loud. “I keep my own — pharmacy rules, and I've been burned before. Can I grab that back off you? I'll take the water bottle instead, that's the heavy bit.”

And remember this. Anything you cannot skip lives on your body. That is not distrust. That is the same reason you keep your own passport.
