SCENARIO 12 — “The Exit Is Always Yours”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. Men are socialized to stay in uncomfortable situations because leaving reads as weakness. It's not. Knowing when and how to exit — a crew, a deal, a tent, a transaction — is one of the most important skills you'll use this weekend.

Picture it. You're in a situation that feels wrong and the voice in your head is running through reasons to stay: it'd be rude to leave, you don't want to make it weird, maybe you're just being paranoid, the crew will think you can't handle it. None of those reasons are about your safety. All of them are about managing other people's opinions of you. Those are not the same thing.

Here are the signs. "I need to find my camp" works everywhere and requires zero explanation. Code word system with your crew before the festival: one text = come get me. Festival Rangers are there for uncomfortable situations — not just medical emergencies. You don't have to explain yourself. You don't have to make it clean. You can just go.

Now here's what's really happening. The difficulty of leaving is almost always manufactured — by social pressure, by sunk cost thinking, by not wanting to be the one who breaks the vibe. Men specifically are socialized to read the desire to leave as cowardice rather than wisdom. It is wisdom. Your body knows when something is off before your brain has fully processed it. The moment leaving feels complicated is the moment leaving becomes most important.

So here's your check. I call it the S check. Ask: “When did I last actually check in with my squad — my actual people, not this new crew?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Safety Infrastructure Before You Arrive. Before gates open: set a daily check-in time with at least one friend outside whatever crew you end up in. Pick a meeting spot your whole group knows. Establish a code word. Write two emergency numbers in pen on your arm. Download an offline festival map. Know where the medical tent and Harm Reduction tent are. Know where the Ranger station is. These feel like overkill until the one time they're not.

So here's your move. Stand up. Say "I'm out." Walk. Managing their feelings about your departure is not your job.

Here are the words. "I need to bounce — catch you later." Then go. No elaboration needed.

And here's the truth underneath it. Knowing when to leave and actually leaving is a skill. Practice it.
