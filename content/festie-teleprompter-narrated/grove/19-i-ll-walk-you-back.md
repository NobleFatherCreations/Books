SCENARIO 19 — “I'll Walk You Back”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. Someone who has been around all night, pleasant, unremarkable, and is now offering the single most normal thing at a festival — which is also the thing that ends with him knowing exactly which tent is yours.

Here's how it plays out. It is late, the site is enormous and dark, and walking back alone genuinely is the thing everybody tells you not to do. He offers. The offer is correct. And somewhere between the last stage and the campsite the crowd thins out and it is just the two of you on a path, and the thing you were avoiding by not walking alone is now walking next to you.

So here's what to watch for. Insists on walking you specifically rather than the group. Discourages you from texting anyone that you are heading back. Wants to come in “just for a minute”, or to see your camp. Now knows your tent's exact location and that you sleep there alone. Times the offer for when everybody else has already gone. Waves off the alternative — a camp mate, a friend, walking with a group.

Now here's what's really going on. The walk back is the last unwitnessed stretch of the night and it is where a disproportionate amount of festival harm occurs, precisely because the safety advice — do not walk alone — creates a socially compulsory role that anybody can volunteer for. The escort is not a bad idea; being escorted by whoever happened to be standing there at 3am is. The other half of the cost is durable: after tonight, one more person knows which tent is yours, and tent location is the single most useful piece of information anyone can hold about you at a festival.

So run this check. I call it the O check. Ask: “Am I about to be alone with this person somewhere nobody is watching?” — The walk counts. That is the whole point of the walk.

And here's the darker reality. Who Knows Where You Sleep. Two separate things end here and it is worth keeping them apart. The first is tonight, which is about being alone on a dark path with somebody you have known for six hours. The second is every night after, which is about tent location — information that cannot be withdrawn once given, and that turns up again in the small hours later in the weekend when you had forgotten the conversation entirely. Handle both with the same move: walk back with people you arrived with, and if a stranger walks you, be walked to a landmark near your camp rather than to your door. It is not rude. It is the same reason you would not give a stranger your home address.

So here's the play. Pre-arrange the walk before you need it — leave when your camp leaves, or text a camp mate to meet you at a landmark. If someone unexpected is escorting you, stop at the food stall or the water point, thank them, and let them leave first.

Here's exactly what to say. “Cheers — Mel's meeting me at the big oak, so I'm good from there. — This is me, thanks, night!”

And here's the truth. Not walking alone is good advice. Walking with whoever volunteers at three in the morning is a different piece of advice entirely.
