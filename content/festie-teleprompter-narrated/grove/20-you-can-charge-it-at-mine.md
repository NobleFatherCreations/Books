SCENARIO 20 — “You Can Charge It At Mine”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a specific type. The person with the generator, the solar rig, the van with a cigarette lighter, or simply three power banks and a lot of generosity about them.

Here's the scene. You are on eight percent by Friday afternoon, which is the ordinary condition of every phone at every festival. He has power. Charging means leaving it in his van for a couple of hours, or sitting in his camp while it charges, and both of those are completely normal and both of them mean that for two hours the thing that connects you to everyone you know is in somebody else's hands.

So watch for these. Offers to take your phone away to charge rather than to charge it beside you. Your phone is repeatedly in his possession, briefly, with good reasons. Notices and comments on who is messaging you. Power access is framed as a favour that accrues. Discourages you from getting a power bank of your own. You have found yourself uncontactable for stretches and did not choose that.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A dead phone removes your map, your torch, your group chat, your ability to call anyone, your record of the weekend, and your capacity to prove where you were. At a festival it is the single most consequential piece of equipment you carry, and the site is designed — unintentionally — to drain it. Whoever solves that problem for you has a hold, and the hold does not require any intent at all to be dangerous: an unreliable friend with your phone produces the same eight hours of unreachability that a deliberate one does. The physical access is the second issue, and the more serious one, since a few unattended minutes is enough to install something or to read everything.

So ask yourself this. I call it the V check. Ask: “Is my phone in my own possession, charged enough to call someone?” — If it lives somewhere else for hours at a time, so does your ability to get help.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Uncontactable Is A Condition Somebody Can Arrange. Think about what an eight-hour gap in your reachability enables: nobody at home expects to hear from you at a festival, your friends assume you are at a stage, and you cannot be located by anyone who would come looking. Every isolation pattern in this book works better against a woman with a dead phone, which is why the offer to solve it is worth noticing rather than just accepting. The stalking guides make the same point from the other side — physical access to a handset, even briefly, is how location sharing gets quietly switched on. Charge it yourself, in your own hands, and set a PIN that is not your birthday.

So here's what you do. Carry two power banks and charge them before you travel — it is the cheapest safety equipment at any festival. If you must use somebody else's power, stay with the phone. Keep a paper note of two numbers and your camp location in your pocket for when it dies anyway.

Here's what to say, out loud. “I'll sit with it while it charges if that's alright — I'm paranoid about losing it. — Got my own bank, thanks though.”

And remember this. Your phone is your exit, your map and your witness. It does not go in anybody else's van.
