SCENARIO 9 — “Isn't That Still A Drug”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's the person you'll meet here. Somebody in your own corner — a recovery acquaintance, a camp mate who is proud of you, occasionally someone with a strong opinion about a programme — who has appointed themselves the judge of whether your sobriety counts.

Picture it. You take your prescribed medication in front of the camp, as you do every day, and somebody asks what it is. Then asks whether that is allowed. Then says, not unkindly, that they thought you were clean, and you find yourself standing in a field defending a prescription to a person with no medical training and considerable confidence.

Here are the signs. Being asked to justify a prescription socially. Talk of being properly clean, or real sobriety. Your psychiatric or substitution medication discussed as a loophole. Pressure to reduce or stop, from someone who is not your prescriber. You have started taking your meds privately to avoid the conversation. The same person is relaxed about their own caffeine, nicotine or painkillers.

Now here's what's really happening. Medication-assisted treatment and psychiatric prescriptions are medicine, and the evidence for them is strong — but purity narratives inside some recovery cultures treat them as a compromised form of sobriety, and the result is people quietly skipping doses to stay socially legitimate. That is the actual harm here and it is a serious one: interrupted psychiatric medication at a festival, with no sleep and heavy sensory load, is a genuine risk of crisis, and interrupted substitution treatment carries a risk of return to use with reduced tolerance.

So here's your check. I call it the R check. Ask: “Has my prescriber said this is fine?” — That is the only opinion in this conversation that has any standing.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Hiding Doses Is How This Hurts You. Watch for the behavioural tell rather than the argument: the moment you start taking your medication where nobody can see, you have accepted that it is shameful, and skipping becomes easy from there. That is the sequence that lands people in a medical tent on Sunday or in a crisis three weeks later, and it starts with one well-meaning question in a field. Your prescriber knows your history, knows what you are taking and knows why. Nobody in that circle does. If somebody keeps pushing after a flat answer, that is not concern about your recovery, it is a person who needs your compliance to feel right about their own framework.

So here's your move. Answer once, flatly, and do not debate. Take your meds on schedule, in the open, on the same routine you use at home. Tell one person in your camp what they are and what to do if you are unwell, so the information exists somewhere useful.

Here are the words. “It's prescribed, my doctor's across it, and I'm not discussing the dose. — Setting an alarm for it, same as at home.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The only person qualified to audit your prescription is the one who wrote it. Take it in the open and on time.
