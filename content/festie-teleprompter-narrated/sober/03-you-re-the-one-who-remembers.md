SCENARIO 3 — “You're The One Who Remembers”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a specific type. The whole group, gradually and without a conversation. Because you are the one who is sober, you become the one with the keys, the map, the phone battery, the plan, the medical decisions, and everybody's emergency.

Picture it. It is two in the morning and you are walking a stranger to medical because you are the only one upright enough to do it. This is the third night running. You came here for the music and you have seen about forty minutes of it, and somebody said earlier, as a compliment, that it is so good having you around because you always know what to do.

Here are the signs. You hold the keys, the cash, the phones, the plan — by default, never by agreement. Your sobriety is described as a resource the group has. You have not seen the sets you came for. Other people's medical decisions keep landing on you. Nobody has asked whether you want this role. Resenting it makes you feel like a bad friend, so you do not mention it.

Now here's what's really happening. Care work assigned by default rather than agreement is a well-documented route to burnout, and the festival version is unusually total: you are the group's driver, medic, treasurer, and institutional memory simultaneously, at exactly the time of year you came to enjoy yourself. It matters more than it sounds. Resentment plus exhaustion plus not having had any of the thing you came for is a genuinely recognized relapse-risk combination, and it builds quietly because the role arrives as a compliment. Choosing to look after your people is one of the good things about being the sober one. Being silently conscripted is not the same act.

So here's your check. I call it the B check. Ask: “Did I agree to this role, or was it assigned to me because I'm the one who'll remember?” — Those produce the same behaviour and completely different costs.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Resentment Is A Relapse Risk, Not A Character Flaw. The dangerous sequence is specific and it is worth being able to see coming: you do the caretaking, you miss the festival, you start resenting people you love, you feel guilty about the resentment because they are your friends and you chose this, and the guilt stops you saying any of it out loud. Depleted, resentful, isolated inside a group, and unable to name it — that is the state, and in recovery terms it is a familiar and serious one. Nobody engineered it. It assembled itself out of everyone's good intentions and the fact that you are reliable. The fix is a conversation on Tuesday, not heroism on Saturday.

So here's your move. Rotate it explicitly before the event — a named driver, a named phone-holder, a named person on duty each night, sober or not. Then take the sets you came for and say so in advance so it is a plan rather than an abandonment.

Here are the words. “I'm not on duty tonight — I'm watching the whole set, phone on if it's an emergency. Who's got the keys? Let's actually rota this.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Being sober makes you capable of holding everyone. It does not make you responsible for it. Those are different sentences.
