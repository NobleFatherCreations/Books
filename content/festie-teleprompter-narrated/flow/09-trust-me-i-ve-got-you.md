SCENARIO 9 — “Trust Me, I've Got You”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. Your base, your flyer, or the person spotting. The discipline requires hands on your ribs, hips and inner thighs, so the contact is legitimate, constant and unremarkable — which is the whole difficulty.

Picture it. You are inverted with your entire weight on his hands and he adjusts his grip, and the adjustment goes somewhere that was not necessary for the position, and you are upside down four feet off the ground and the only thing keeping you there is the person who just did that. You come down. Neither of you says anything. He books you for the next jam.

Here are the signs. Grip adjustments that are not required by the position. New moves introduced without describing the contact first. Contact continues after you have landed and are stable. Objecting is framed as not trusting your base, which is a safety word here. Sessions move from open practice to private space. You come out of a wash feeling odd and cannot point to a single thing.

Now here's what's really happening. Partner acro, aerial and any spotting discipline share a structural problem: consent to hold has to be given in advance and continuously, while the person giving it is frequently inverted, load-bearing, or in a position where objecting is physically unsafe. Every acro community that has addressed this has landed on the same protocol — name the contact before the move, no new contact mid-position, and trust means trusting the hold rather than trusting the person's intentions. The word trust is the thing to watch, because it is doing safety work and social work simultaneously and somebody can lean on the second to get the first.

So here's your check. I call it the W check. Ask: “Was this contact named before the move started?” — Unnamed contact is the finding, regardless of where it landed.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. You Cannot Object At Four Feet. The reason this is not like other unwanted contact is the physics. You cannot step back, you cannot pull away, and reacting sharply while inverted risks a genuine injury to both of you — so your body overrides the objection to keep you both safe, and afterwards you will read your own stillness as consent. It was not; it was self-preservation. This is exactly why the protocol lives before the move rather than during it, and why the right move afterwards is not to relitigate what happened in the air but to change the practice: named contact, open space, and a different base. You do not owe anybody an accusation to be allowed to stop flying with them.

So here's your move. Agree the contact map before any new move, out loud, every time — where hands go, and where they do not. Practise in open space. Change base without explaining yourself; you never owe a reason for who holds your body.

Here are the words. “Before we try it — where are your hands going? Ribs and hips, nothing else, and tell me if that changes. — I'm going to work with Sam on this one, cheers.”

And here's the truth underneath it. Trust in acro means trusting the hold. It has never meant losing the right to say where the hands go.
