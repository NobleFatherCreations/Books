SCENARIO 10 — “They Grabbed Me Mid-Set”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about the person you'll meet here. The crowd, at about one in the morning, drunk and delighted and not thinking — grabbing a hoop, stepping into a fire circle for a photograph, putting hands on a stilt-walker's legs, reaching for an aerialist's foot.

Here's how it plays out. You are three minutes into a fire set and a man steps inside the circle to film himself next to you. He is about a metre from a lit wick moving at speed and he has no idea, and you now have to decide, mid-spin, whether to stop the set or keep going while somebody stands in the burn radius filming.

So here's what to watch for. People entering the performance space for photographs. Props grabbed, touched or stepped on mid-set. Costume, wings, stilts or rigging treated as public property. No safety perimeter, no marshal, and no marked boundary. Contact that would be obviously unacceptable if you were not performing. The venue treats crowd management as your problem.

Now here's what's really going on. Performing in public gets read as an open invitation, and for fire and aerial that misreading is a physical danger to the audience as well as to you — a person inside a fire circle is inside a burn radius, and a grabbed hoop or a knocked stilt takes a performer down onto hard ground. The professional answer is that crowd management is the event's job and a perimeter is a condition of the booking, not a nicety. The individual answer is that you are allowed to stop, and stopping is safer than performing around an obstacle.

So run this check. I call it the O check. Ask: “Is there a real perimeter and somebody holding it?” — If not, that is a condition to fix before you light, not a thing to absorb during.

And here's the darker reality. Stopping Is The Professional Response. The trained instinct is to keep going, because performers are conditioned that stopping is failure. Unlearn that one. A set continued around a member of the public standing in the burn radius ends, when it ends badly, with a burned stranger, an insurance claim, and a performer explaining why they did not stop — and the explanation will be that it would have been awkward. The second half of this belongs to the event: a perimeter and a marshal are a condition of booking for fire, and if a promoter will not provide them the correct answer is that you do not perform, not that you perform more carefully.

So here's the play. Make the perimeter and a named marshal part of accepting the booking. Stop the set the moment the boundary is broken — kill it, say why, restart when it is clear. Agree a stop signal with your safety before you start.

Here's exactly what to say. “I need a marshal and a three-metre perimeter or I can't light — that's not negotiable for fire. — [stopping] Set's paused, mate, you're standing in the burn circle. Back behind the line and we'll go again.”

And here's the truth. You are working, not on display. A set that has to be performed around an obstacle is a set that should have stopped.
