SCENARIO 8 — “Whose Circle Is This”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a specific type. The good version has no owner, teaches sideways, and lets terrible beginners take up space. The other version has a centre, and the centre is a person, and everyone's position is measured by distance from him.

Here's the scene. Two circles at the same festival. In the first, a woman who has been spinning for twenty years spends eleven minutes helping somebody who picked up poi on Thursday, then goes back to her own practice and never mentions it again. In the second, everyone is subtly performing for one man at the edge, and when he leaves for ten minutes the energy of the whole circle changes, and everybody notices that it did.

So watch for these. Real: beginners get space and nobody is ranked. Real: teaching flows in every direction, including upward. Real: you can leave, be bad, or sit out with no cost. Owned: one person's approval organizes the room. Owned: access to gear, instruction, or gigs routes through him. Owned: people describe the circle by his name.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A practice circle is one of the genuinely great things this scene produces — skill transmitted for free, across ages and levels, because somebody once did it for you. It is also a structure with an obvious failure mode: circles with a centre concentrate access, and concentrated access is what every pattern in this guide runs on. The test is identical to the one in every other guide here, which is not an accident: can you decline, be unimpressive, or leave without it costing you? In a real jam the answer is yes and nobody registers it. In an owned one, everybody registers everything.

So ask yourself this. I call it the W check. Ask: “If I sat out tonight and said nothing, would anything change for me here?” — If sitting out has a price, this circle has an owner.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. When The Circle Is The Supply Chain. It gets serious when the circle is also where the gigs, the gear, and the instruction come from, because then leaving costs your practice and not just your evening. That is the configuration to watch for — not a charismatic person, which is normal and often lovely, but one person sitting across all of it at once. Everybody in that circle can feel it and nobody names it, because naming it is the thing that costs the most. If you are reading this and recognizing your own circle: the others already know, the conversation is easier than you think, and the fix is usually structural rather than confrontational. Two teachers. A second rigger. Somebody else who books.

So here's what you do. Keep your practice plural on purpose — more than one circle, more than one teacher, more than one route to a gig. Then hand skill downward for free, because a circle where everybody teaches cannot develop a centre.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Come spin with us, we're rubbish and nobody cares. — Here, let me show you the grip, it took me a year to work out and it shouldn't have.”

And remember this. A jam that needs a specific person to be good is not a jam. It is an audience with props.
