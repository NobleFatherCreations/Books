SCENARIO 11 — “It's A Great Look For Your Reel”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about someone you'll recognize. A promoter with a budget for sound, lights, security and bar staff, and a line about community for the people doing the dangerous thing in the middle of the field.

Here's the scene. He wants three fire performers for the Saturday headline changeover. There is no fee. There are wristbands, and he says it in a way that makes the wristbands sound generous. He also mentions that a lot of people get seen at this one, and that the photographer is really good, and every single other trade at this event is being paid.

So watch for these. Wristbands offered as the fee for skilled, insured, dangerous work. Every other supplier at the event is on a rate. No contract, no call time in writing, no fuel or travel budget. No mention of insurance, or an assumption that you carry it. “Exposure”, “the community”, “great for your reel” The photographer is paid and you are not.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Fire and aerial performance is skilled, insured, equipment-heavy, physically dangerous labour, and it is the single most reliably unpaid element of a festival's programme because performers love doing it and promoters know that. The comparison that settles it is on-site: the sound engineer, the bar staff, the security and the photographer are all on rates. If a budget exists for everyone who touches the event except the people doing the thing with fire in it, that is a decision rather than a constraint.

So ask yourself this. I call it the L check. Ask: “Who else working this event is unpaid?” — If the answer is only the performers, the budget is not the problem.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. The Insurance Question Is The Real One. The part that outlives the argument about money is liability. Unpaid, uncontracted, uninsured fire performance means that when somebody is burned — you, a colleague, or a member of the public who walked into the circle — there is no contract establishing who was responsible for the perimeter, no employer, and frequently no cover. Promoters who do not pay performers also, reliably, have not thought about any of that, and the person standing in the footage holding the lit tool is the one who will be answering for it. Ask about public liability in the first conversation. The answer tells you everything about the event's professionalism, before money is even discussed.

So here's what you do. Quote a rate like any other supplier and ask two questions early: what is the fee, and who carries public liability for this performance. Get the call time, perimeter and marshal in writing. Decline cheerfully and early rather than resentfully and late.

Here's what to say, out loud. “My rate's [x] per performer plus fuel and travel. Also — who's carrying public liability for the fire, you or us? I need that confirmed before I commit.”

And remember this. Everyone else at this event is being paid. Your work is more dangerous than most of theirs, and a wristband is not a fee.
