SCENARIO 6 — “The Clip's Already Up”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's the person you'll meet here. Always filming, often good at it, and operating on the assumption that a public performance is a public license. Sometimes a friend with a phone. Sometimes an account with two hundred thousand followers and a monetization deal.

Picture it. You burned last night. This morning there is a clip of it on an account you have never heard of, forty thousand views, your face clearly visible, your name nowhere, a brand tagged in the caption. The comments are mostly about your body. Somebody has stitched it. You did not know a camera was there, and the account owner, when you message, is genuinely baffled that you are not pleased.

Here are the signs. Films without asking and treats performing as blanket consent. Cannot say where footage will end up or who else gets it. Credits the venue and the brand but not the performer. Reacts to a takedown request as though you are being ungrateful. Monetized account, unmonetized subject. Costume, aerial, and fire performers get framed body-first.

Now here's what's really happening. Performing in public does not transfer your rights in your own image, and it never has — but the practical position is that enforcement is slow, cross-border, and expensive, so the operative protection is the one you set up in advance rather than the one you litigate afterwards. What makes this acute for flow and aerial performers is the combination of revealing costume, physical spectacle, and a distribution system that rewards exactly the framing you did not choose. The version that does real harm is not the friendly clip, it is the reposted one, where it travels without context, without your name, and often into spaces you would never have agreed to.

So here's your check. I call it the L check. Ask: “Have I said, out loud, before I start, what I consent to being filmed and posted?” — Silence gets read as a yes by people who wanted a yes.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. It Does Not Come Back Down. Once a clip is stitched, reposted, and mirrored, there is no version of this where you get it back. The realistic goal is containment, not removal — which is why the work is all front-loaded. State it before the burn, to the organizer and to anyone visibly filming, and get the organizer's media policy in writing when you agree to the gig. The second thing worth knowing is that this is often where a different kind of leverage starts: somebody holding footage of you, and mentioning it. Footage held over a performer is coercion, it is not a grey area, and the response is the same as for any other documentation threat — tell one person now, in writing, so the footage stops being a secret that can be spent.

So here's your move. Set your policy before you are lit, in one sentence, to the organizer and the circle. Get the event's media terms in writing when you take the booking. Watermark or claim your own footage first so the canonical version is yours.

Here are the words. “Happy to be filmed, not happy to be posted without a tag — credit me or don't put it up. Who's your media lead so I can send them that in writing?”

And here's the truth underneath it. A performance is not a release. Saying so takes six seconds and it is the only part of this you control.
