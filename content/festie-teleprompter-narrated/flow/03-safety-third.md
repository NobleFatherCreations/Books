SCENARIO 3 — “Safety Third”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's someone you'll recognize. Not a person — a room. The collective eye-roll when someone asks for a safety brief. The laugh when you check your fuel. The lightly impatient veteran waiting while you do a rig inspection everybody else skipped.

Picture it. You are about to burn. The safety is a guy you met an hour ago who is holding a duvetyne blanket like it is a beach towel, and when you ask whether he has done this before he says yeah, loads, and looks at his phone. The circle is waiting. Asking again in front of everyone would make you the difficult one. You have about four seconds to decide whether being the difficult one is worse than the other thing.

Here are the signs. Safety checks treated as a personality flaw rather than procedure. A designated safety who cannot describe what they would actually do. Fuel handled near lit tools, or depot uncovered. “Safety third” said in a context where it is not a joke. Nobody has asked who is calling stop, or what stop sounds like. The social cost of pausing is visibly higher than the cost of proceeding.

Now here's what's really happening. Normalized deviance is the documented process by which a group repeatedly gets away with skipping a step until the skipped step becomes the standard. Every fire and aerial community runs on it to some degree, because the consequence is rare and the friction is constant, and the people most exposed are the newest — who have the least standing to pause and the most to lose by being seen as green. The joke is doing real work here: it pre-frames anyone who insists on the check as humorless, which means the insisting costs something, which means fewer people insist. That is not culture, it is a mechanism, and it runs whether or not anybody in the circle intends it.

So here's your check. I call it the F check. Ask: “Have I personally checked my fuel, my depot, and my safety — by asking them what they would do?” — Secondhand assurance is not a check.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The Four Seconds. Burn injuries in this scene cluster around exactly this moment — not around difficult tricks, around skipped checks under social pressure with an audience waiting. The specific failure is almost always mundane: fuel too close, a depot uncovered, a safety who had never actually done it, nobody having agreed what the stop call was. And the reason it is mundane is the reason it is preventable. The four seconds of being the difficult one is the entire intervention. People who have been burned describe those four seconds with total clarity afterwards, every time — they remember deciding not to ask again. Ask again. The circle waits, and then forgets by morning, and you do not have a graft.

So here's your move. Run your own check out loud, every time, in the same words, so it becomes your known habit rather than a comment on this circle: fuel, depot, safety, stop call. Ask the safety one question they have to answer — what do you do if my wick catches my sleeve? Their answer is the check.

Here are the words. “Quick one before I light — what's the stop call, and what do you do if it goes up my arm? Not being difficult, I just do this every time.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The circle's patience is not a safety system. Being briefly annoying is cheaper than being briefly on fire.
