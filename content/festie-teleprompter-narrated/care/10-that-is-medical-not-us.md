SCENARIO 10 — “That Is Medical, Not Us”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a specific type. For everyone on a harm reduction or welfare shift, holding a role with no clinical registration and a queue of people whose conditions you are not qualified to assess.

Here's the scene. He has been sitting with you for an hour and is not improving, and you are not sure whether this is a long comedown, dehydration, a reaction, or something genuinely medical. Walking him to the medics feels like escalating, and you have a specific fear of being the volunteer who overreacts and looks green in front of the medical team.

So watch for these. Not improving, or getting worse, over time you would expect improvement. Temperature: very hot and not sweating, or cold and clammy. Breathing that is slow, irregular, snoring or noisy. Unrousable, or rousable only briefly, or seizing. Chest pain, a head injury, or an unexplained one-sided weakness. You are debating it with yourself, which is itself the threshold.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Harm reduction and welfare workers are the first point of contact for a great many situations and are not clinicians, and the most consequential skill in the role is a low, pre-decided threshold for escalation. Every serious festival incident review finds the same thing: the delay happened because somebody was not sure it was serious enough. The thresholds above are not judgement calls and should not be treated as any — temperature extremes, breathing changes, unrousability, seizures and head injuries go to medical immediately, every time, without a discussion about whether it warrants it.

So ask yourself this. I call it the R check. Ask: “Am I debating whether this is medical?” — The debate is the answer. Escalate while you are still discussing it.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Nobody Was Ever Criticized For The Early Call. Name the fear, because it is what causes the delay: you do not want to be the volunteer who walked someone over and was told it was nothing. Ask any festival medic and they will tell you the opposite — early calls are welcome, they would far rather see ten people who were fine than one an hour late, and the ones that go badly are always the late ones. The specific pattern to watch for is the person who is quietly deteriorating while the tent is busy, because a quiet person gets less attention than a loud one and deterioration is quiet by nature. Set a review time for everybody you are holding, and check them even when nothing has changed.

So here's what you do. Pre-decide the thresholds and escalate on any of them without deliberating. Set an explicit review time for every person you are holding. Walk them to medical rather than sending them. Hand over factually: what they took if known, when, what you have observed, what has changed.

Here's what to say, out loud. “I'm walking you over to the medics — not because it's dramatic, just because it's their call and not mine. — [handing over] Male, about twenty-five, in with us ninety minutes, reports MDMA around ten, temperature's up and he's not sweating, and he's less responsive than he was.”

And remember this. If you are debating whether it is medical, it is medical. No medic has ever complained about being called early.
