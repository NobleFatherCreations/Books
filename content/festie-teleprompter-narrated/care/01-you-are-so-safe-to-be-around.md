SCENARIO 1 — “You Are So Safe To Be Around”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about someone you'll recognize. Harm Reduction volunteers radiate something that most festival attendees are genuinely seeking — calm, presence, non-judgment, safety. These are real and valuable qualities. They are also specifically targeted by people who mistake your professional care for personal availability.

Here's the scene. He has been to your tent three times today. Each visit has a slightly different stated reason but the pattern of the conversation is the same — he lingers, he asks questions about you, he says things like "I have never felt so comfortable talking to someone." The fourth visit he asks if you want to get food after your shift. You are tired and his attention has felt kind throughout a difficult day. Something in you wants to say yes. Something else notes the pattern.

So watch for these. Multiple visits with escalating personal rather than support-related conversation. Compliments that specifically target your care qualities — your presence, your listening, your safety. Questions about your schedule, when your shift ends, what you are doing after. The line between appreciating your support and pursuing you personally has started to blur.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The Harm Reduction role creates a specific dynamic: you are at your most professionally present and emotionally available while participants are at their most open and vulnerable. Some participants experience the care you provide in your role as personal connection. Some deliberately target that dynamic for access. Maintaining the on-duty boundary — warm, caring, and professionally boundaried — protects both of you.

So ask yourself this. I call it the C check. Ask: “Am I maintaining the distinction between my care role and my personal availability?”

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. The On-Duty Boundary And Why It Exists. The on-duty boundary exists because: the power differential between a care provider and someone receiving care makes genuine consent complicated. Your ability to provide care effectively depends on maintaining the distinction between professional and personal. Crossing the boundary exposes you to real professional and sometimes legal risk. And it exposes the participant to the experience of someone they trusted professionally pursuing them personally — which is a harm regardless of your intentions. "I am on duty right now so I keep things professional during my role" is a complete and professional response. It is not a rejection.

So here's what you do. Warm, caring, professionally boundaried. All three at the same time. That is the standard.

Here's what to say, out loud. "I am on shift right now so I keep things professional during my role — I hope you have a great rest of your festival."

And remember this. Your care is a professional skill. Not everyone who benefits from it is entitled to more of you.
