SCENARIO 3 — “It's Just You Out There Tonight”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about a specific type. The site itself, during load-in. Dark, enormous, mostly empty, with the medical tent not up yet, security not started, no public, patchy signal, and people you have never met driving plant around in the dark.

Here's how it plays out. You are finishing a run of cable at the far end of a field at eleven at night. There is one work light. The nearest other human is about four hundred metres away and does not know exactly where you are. Your phone has one bar. Every single protection this festival will have by Friday — rangers, medical, welfare, crowds of witnesses — does not exist yet, and will not for six days.

So here's what to watch for. Lone working at night with nobody holding your location. Medical, welfare, and security not yet operational on site. Plant moving in the dark without a banksman. No radio, or a radio on a channel nobody is monitoring. Accommodation shared with people who have not been vetted at all. No headcount — nobody would notice by morning.

Now here's what's really going on. Everything else in this book assumes a populated festival: witnesses, a medical tent you can walk to, rangers, other people's phones. Build phase has none of that, and it is also when the workforce is smallest, least known to each other, and most exhausted. Two separate risks stack in that gap — the industrial one, where an ordinary injury becomes serious because nobody finds you for two hours, and the interpersonal one, where every pattern in this book operates without any of the infrastructure that usually constrains it. Crew accommodation in particular is often a field of strangers with no vetting and no lock, which is not a comment on crew, it is a description of the conditions.

So run this check. I call it the I check. Ask: “Does one named person know where I am and when I am due back?” — Not the crew. A person, with a time.

And here's the darker reality. The Six Days Before Anyone Is Watching. Say the plain version: the safest time to harm someone at a festival is before it is a festival. No public, no cameras, no rangers, no welfare tent, a small pool of people who all outrank you in tenure, and a culture that will close ranks around the build because the build is behind. The people this lands on hardest are new crew, and especially young or lone women and anyone who needed this gig — because raising anything means losing the accommodation you are currently sleeping in, on a site you cannot leave without a lift. That is the structural problem, and it is why the buddy rule is not fussiness. Two people, a stated return time, and one person off-site who expects to hear from you.

So here's the play. Buddy anything at height, with plant, with blades, or after dark — no exceptions, arranged as a crew norm rather than a personal request. Put a location and a return time to one named person every night. Know where the first-aider is, and where the vehicle keys live.

Here's exactly what to say. “Buddy rule after dark, I'm not doing the far field on my own. — Back by half eleven, if I'm not, come find me, I'm at the north cable run.”

And here's the truth. The festival's safety net goes up on Wednesday. You have been on site since the Thursday before. Build your own until then.
