SCENARIO 5 — “The Build Crew Kit”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's the person you'll meet here. For anyone taking a build gig, at any level, on any crew — including the good ones, because the good ones do all of this already and will respect you for it.

Picture it. You are packing. What goes in the bag now is what you will have at hour ninety-six on a dark field with no signal and a production office that has gone home. Everything on this page takes ten minutes to arrange before you travel and is impossible to arrange afterwards.

Here are the signs. The deal in writing: rate, days, what's covered, injury, transport home. A dated hours log you fill in daily, with photos of the work. Your own PPE — boots, gloves, hi-vis, glasses, ear protection, head torch. Your own route home that nobody else controls. One off-site person with the site address who expects a daily check-in. The name of the site first-aider and where the trauma kit actually is.

Now here's what's really happening. Every pattern in this guide is defeated by something arranged in advance rather than negotiated in the moment, and the moment is always the worst time — you are tired, you are isolated, the work is already done, and the other party holds the leverage. Bringing your own PPE is the clearest example: it removes the most common coercive sentence on any site, which is that there is not a spare and the job still needs doing. Owning your route home removes the second. These are not signs of distrust. Experienced crew arrive with all of it, and the crews worth working for notice and think better of you.

So here's your check. I call it the U check. Ask: “Can I refuse a task right now without losing my bed or my lift?” — If not, fix that before you need to refuse anything.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The Kit Is What Makes No Possible. Every item on that list exists to make one sentence survivable: no, I am not doing that. Your own boots mean nobody can tell you the job goes ahead in trainers. Your own transport means refusing does not strand you. The written deal means walking away does not cost you a fortnight's pay. The check-in means somebody notices. Refusal is not a matter of courage on a build site, it is a matter of what refusing costs — and everything on that list is there to lower the price until it is something you can actually afford to say.

So here's your move. Pack the kit, send the deal message, name your off-site person, and find the first-aider and the trauma kit in your first hour on site. Then do the work, which is some of the best work there is.

Here are the words. “Got my own PPE and my own van, so I'm sorted. Who's the first-aider this build, and where's the trauma kit living?”

And here's the truth underneath it. Every bit of that kit buys you the same thing: the ability to say no and still have a bed, a lift, and a wage.
