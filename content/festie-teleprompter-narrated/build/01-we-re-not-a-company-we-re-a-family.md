SCENARIO 1 — “We're Not A Company, We're A Family”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about the person you'll meet here. Warm, genuinely bonded, and absolutely running on unpaid hours. Recruits on love of the work. Uses family language exactly where a normal employer would use a contract, and only ever in the direction of asking.

Here's the scene. The build crew is the best group of people you have ever worked with and the first four days are the happiest you have been in a year. On day five you realize nobody has mentioned money since the first conversation, the sixteen-hour days have become the schedule rather than the exception, and when you ask about the rate the lead says, kindly, that this is not really that kind of thing, that everybody here is doing it for the same reason, and that the family looks after each other.

So watch for these. Family language appears exactly where terms should be. No written agreement, and asking for one reads as a trust problem. Hours expand without the deal changing. Asking about money is treated as a character flaw rather than a question. Looking after each other is invoked when asking, never when paying. Everyone senior has a real job title in the outside world.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Familial framing is the most efficient way to extract labor, because it converts a commercial question into a loyalty question — and once that conversion has happened, asking about your rate makes you the person who does not love the thing, in front of people whose respect you now depend on. Build crews are especially exposed because the work is genuinely joyful, the bonds are genuinely real, and the culture genuinely is one of mutual aid. All of that is true and none of it is a contract. The test is directional: notice whether family gets invoked when something is being asked of you, or also when something is owed to you.

So ask yourself this. I call it the B check. Ask: “If I could not work another shift from tomorrow, what would I actually be owed — in writing?” — If the answer is a feeling, you do not have a deal.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Family Is Load-Bearing In One Direction. Watch what happens at the edges, because that is where the frame reveals itself. Somebody gets injured on a family crew and discovers there is no insurance, no sick pay, and no employer, because they were never an employee — they were family, and family has no legal standing. Somebody raises a concern about a senior member and discovers the family closes, because a family protects its own and you are the newest one. That is the same mechanism the cult chapter of this scene's literature describes, arriving in work boots. It does not mean the warmth is fake. It means warmth is not a substitute for terms, and the crews that have both are the ones worth staying with.

So here's what you do. Get the deal in a message before the first shift — hours, what you get, what happens if you are injured, who pays for the ferry home. A text is a written agreement. Then keep working and enjoy the crew, which is genuinely one of the best things in this scene.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Love it here, in for the full build. Can you drop me a quick message with the hours and what the deal covers — mostly so I know where I stand if I get hurt.”

And remember this. A real family will write it down without being wounded about it. Only the extractive version treats the question as an insult.
