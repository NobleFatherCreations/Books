SCENARIO 2 — “The Ninety-Sixth Hour”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's someone you'll recognize. Nobody. This one is the schedule. Day nine of a build, fourth consecutive sixteen-hour day, everybody running on catering coffee and the fact that the gates open Thursday whatever happens.

Picture it. You have been awake for nineteen hours and you are about to make a cut you have made a thousand times. The thing is you have made it a thousand times, which is why you are doing it now instead of stopping, and which is also why your hands are doing it while your attention is somewhere else. Everyone around you is in the same state and nobody is going to be the one who says the crew should stand down, because the schedule is the schedule.

Here are the signs. Consecutive long shifts with no rostered rest. Experienced people making basic errors and laughing about it. Microsleeps, dropped tools, losing the thread mid-sentence. Safety-critical work scheduled at the end of shifts rather than the start. Nobody empowered to stop the job without it being a confrontation. The build is behind and that has quietly become everyone's justification.

Now here's what's really happening. Sustained sleep restriction degrades reaction time, judgment, and risk assessment measurably, and past roughly seventeen to nineteen hours awake the impairment is comparable to being over the legal driving limit — with the extra problem that self-assessment degrades alongside performance, so the more impaired you are the more confident you feel. Occupational injury data across construction and events points the same way: serious incidents cluster late in long stretches, among experienced workers, on routine tasks. The hazard is not the dangerous job you were nervous about. It is the easy one you have done a thousand times, at hour nineteen.

So here's your check. I call it the D check. Ask: “How long have I been awake, and is this task one I would let a drunk person do?” — If you would not, the honest answer is that the site currently has one.

And here's what sits underneath all of it. Competence Is Why It Gets You. The cruel part is that skill is the risk factor here, not the protection. Novices are slow and frightened, which keeps their attention on the task. Experienced hands run the cut on autopilot, which is exactly the faculty fatigue takes first, and it is why the amputations and the falls in this industry belong overwhelmingly to people who had done that job for years. There is a second layer specific to build: the incident happens on a site with no medical yet, no public, thin signal, and everyone else also exhausted. The margin you are relying on is not there. Stopping is not weakness on a build crew — it is the only intervention with any evidence behind it.

So here's your move. Name it as a crew rather than as an individual, because the social cost is what keeps it running: agree a hard stop hour for safety-critical work, and agree that anyone can call fatigue on anyone without argument. Schedule the height work, the plant, and the blades for the start of a shift.

Here are the words. “I'm nineteen hours in — I'm not cutting tonight, I'll do it first thing and it'll be better work. Anyone else past it should say now, nobody's keeping score.”

And here's the truth underneath it. The most experienced person on site is the one most likely to be hurt at hour ninety-six. That is not an insult. It is the data.
