SCENARIO 1 — “You Care So Much”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Here's a particular kind of person. The attendee who identifies that you are caring, available, and emotionally present in your role — and targets those qualities specifically as a hook for romantic or sexual access. Your caregiving energy is real. It is also visible and exploitable.

Picture it. You have been doing back-to-back shifts at the Harm Reduction tent and you are tired but still fully present with everyone who comes in. He has come by three times today — each time with a small question, each time staying a little longer to talk. The third time he says "I just feel so safe with you, I have never met anyone who listens like you do." He asks if you want to get a drink after your shift. Something about the sequence feels off but you cannot name it. You are also exhausted and the attention feels kind.

Here are the signs. Multiple visits to your station with escalating personal rather than service-related conversation. Compliments that specifically target your caregiving qualities — your presence, your listening, your safety. Asking about your schedule, when your shift ends, or what you are doing after. The line between appreciating your support and pursuing you personally starts to blur.

Now here's what's really happening. Staff and volunteers in caregiving roles at festivals — Harm Reduction, Rangers, medics, counselors — are specifically targeted by people who recognize that your role signals emotional availability, safety, and care. Those are genuine qualities. They are also qualities that certain people learn to use as a vector for access. The caregiving role creates a power dynamic you need to be aware of: you are in your element, you are giving, and some people use that specifically to get close.

So here's your check. I call it the H check. Ask: “Am I maintaining the boundary between my care role and my personal availability?”

And here's what sits underneath all of it. The On-Duty Boundary — Why It Exists. The on-duty boundary — not pursuing personal relationships with people you are supporting in your role — exists to protect both of you. It protects attendees from the power imbalance of a caregiver becoming a romantic pursuit. It protects you from the legal and professional risk of mixing your role with personal relationships. And it protects your ability to be effective — because once personal attraction enters the caregiver role the caregiving quality changes. "I am on duty right now" is a complete sentence and a professional standard, not a personal rejection.

So here's your move. Maintain the on-duty boundary. Warm, caring, and professionally boundaried are completely compatible.

Here are the words. "I am on shift right now so I keep things professional during my role — I hope you have a great rest of your festival."

And here's the truth underneath it. Your caregiving presence is a professional skill. Not everyone who benefits from it is entitled to more of you.
