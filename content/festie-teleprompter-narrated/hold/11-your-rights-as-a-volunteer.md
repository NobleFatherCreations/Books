SCENARIO 11 — “Your Rights As A Volunteer”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. Volunteers have rights. Not the same rights as employees in every jurisdiction but real rights nonetheless — the right to safe working conditions, the right to refuse unsafe tasks, the right to leave a role that is causing harm, and the right to documentation of what you were promised.

Here's how it plays out. You are being asked to cover a solo overnight shift in a remote area of the festival with no radio contact and no clear protocol for what to do if something happens. Your gut says this is not safe. Your supervisor says everyone does it and there is nobody else. You want to be helpful. You also want to go home in one piece. The question is whether you have the right to say no — and the answer is yes.

So here's what to watch for. Being asked to work alone in unsafe conditions without adequate communication or support. Being asked to cover tasks that are outside your training, role description, or competency. Being pressured to continue working when you have raised a safety concern about your conditions. Basic needs — food, water, breaks, sleep — being systematically denied for operational reasons.

Now here's what's really going on. Festival volunteer culture often blurs the line between genuine community contribution and labor exploitation. The "we are all in this together" framing can make it feel selfish or disloyal to refuse unsafe tasks or insist on basic working conditions. It is not. Volunteers who are placed in unsafe conditions and something goes wrong face real consequences — personal harm, trauma, and sometimes legal exposure. You have the right to refuse conditions that put you at unreasonable risk.

So run this check. I call it the H check. Ask: “Am I being asked to do something that my training, my safety, or my basic needs require me to say no to?” — saying no to an unsafe task isn’t letting the team down. It’s doing the job correctly.

And here's the darker reality. Volunteer Rights In Practice. In practice, volunteer rights include: the right to know what your role involves before you commit, the right to refuse tasks outside your role description or competency without losing your position, the right to safe working conditions including adequate communication, support, and backup, the right to leave a role that is actively harmful to you without legal consequence in most jurisdictions, and the right to documentation of what was agreed. After the event: formal feedback to the organization and to any bodies that oversee the event. Your experience matters and your feedback improves conditions for future volunteers.

So here's the play. Know your rights before you need them. Refusing unsafe conditions is not abandonment — it is professional judgment.

Here's exactly what to say. "I am not comfortable covering that alone without radio contact — what backup can you provide or what is the alternative?"

And here's the truth. Saying no to an unsafe task is not letting the team down. It is doing the job correctly.
