SCENARIO 3 — “The Predator Knows Where The Tent Is”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about someone you'll recognize. The reality that some people who seek positions of access at festivals — as staff, as volunteers, as practitioners in Harm Reduction or healing spaces — are specifically motivated by the access those positions provide to vulnerable people. This is not hypothetical.

Here's the scene. He has been volunteering in the Harm Reduction tent for three seasons. Everyone trusts him. He knows the layout, the protocols, the staff schedule, and most importantly he knows who comes in — who is most vulnerable, who is most altered, who is alone. He is warm, knowledgeable, and consistently present. He is also using that position and that knowledge in ways that the team has not yet named even though some of them have felt something was off.

So watch for these. A staff or volunteer member who shows unusual interest in specific attendees beyond the care role. Someone who seeks ongoing contact with attendees they supported after their interaction in the role. A pattern of being present when vulnerable attendees are alone in the space. Discomfort among other staff about a specific person that has not yet been formally named.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. People who want access to vulnerable people at festivals will seek roles that provide that access. Harm Reduction tents, medical stations, safe spaces, and security roles all provide access to people who are at their most vulnerable. This is a known and documented pattern in safeguarding contexts. If you have a gut feeling about a colleague in your space — that feeling is data. Name it to a supervisor. Document it. The culture of "but they have been here for years" is specifically what these patterns rely on.

So ask yourself this. I call it the L check. Ask: “Have I documented the specific behaviors that are making me uncomfortable about this person in an access role, with dates and specifics?” — the team looking out for each other matters as much as the team looking out for attendees.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. Safeguarding In Festival Staff Contexts. Basic safeguarding practices for festival staff spaces: no staff or volunteer should have unsupervised one-on-one access to impaired or vulnerable attendees. Staff contact with attendees ends when the support need ends — ongoing personal contact is a red flag. Any staff member who is seeking out specific attendees after interactions should be reported to a supervisor. All concerns about staff behavior should be documented in writing with date, time, and specific observed behavior. A genuine organization will take these concerns seriously. One that dismisses them is protecting the wrong person.

So here's what you do. Name what you see to a supervisor. Document it in writing. The culture of silence is what protects predators in these spaces.

Here's what to say, out loud. “I want to document a concern about a colleague's behavior — can I speak with a supervisor privately?”

And remember this. The most important safeguarding the team does is looking out for each other as much as for attendees.
