SCENARIO 5 — “The Tray On The Table”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let me tell you about someone you'll recognize. Your camp, and the eleven adults in it who are all lovely and none of whom is thinking about a four-year-old's sight lines.

Here's how it plays out. There is a tin on the low table with something in it, and a half-drunk cider at child height, and a bag on a chair. Your daughter is four and everything at that height is, to her, food. She has been putting things in her mouth for four years and nobody in this camp has had to think about that for a decade.

So here's what to watch for. Edibles, powders or pills at or below adult-knee height. Unlabelled food on a shared table in a camp with kids in it. Half-finished drinks left within reach. Bags left open on chairs and in tent doorways. The camp has never explicitly discussed having children present. “She won't touch it” offered as the safety measure.

Now here's what's really going on. Pediatric cannabis ingestion has risen sharply everywhere edibles have become common, and the reason is entirely mechanical: edibles look like sweets, a child's dose is a fraction of an adult's, and onset is delayed so the first sign is a small child who is unexpectedly unwell an hour later. At a festival the risk multiplies — more substances, more adults, more surfaces, and a medical tent rather than an emergency department. This is not a page about whether adults in your camp should use anything. It is a page about height.

So run this check. I call it the K check. Ask: “If I crouch to my child's eye level right now, what can I reach?” — Do it literally. It is a different camp from down there.

And here's the darker reality. Tell The Medics What They Might Have Taken. The failure that turns this serious is not the ingestion, it is the hour afterwards. A small child who has eaten an edible presents as unusually drowsy and unsteady, and a parent who does not know what happened reports a child who seems sleepy — which is a completely different clinical picture from a possible ingestion, and gets a completely different response. So the instruction is blunt: if there is any possibility, say so immediately and say exactly what it might have been. Nobody at that medical tent is interested in getting anybody in trouble and they will tell you so, and the treatment depends entirely on knowing. Guessing wrong costs hours, and hours matter at that body weight.

So here's the play. Run a waist-height rule as camp policy on day one — nothing above the ground and below the waist, everything in a closed bag in a tent. Label communal food. Do the crouch test yourself each morning, because camps drift by Saturday.

Here's exactly what to say. “Kids in camp this year, so can we keep everything above waist height and label the food? Not a judgement thing, just height. — [at medical] She may have eaten an edible, about an hour ago, I don't know how much.”

And here's the truth. Your camp is safe at adult eye level. Crouch down to three feet and look again.
