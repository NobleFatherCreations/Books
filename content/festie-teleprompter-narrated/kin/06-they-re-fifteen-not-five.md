SCENARIO 6 — “They're Fifteen, Not Five”
🎬 TELEPROMPTER SCRIPT

Let's talk about a specific type. Your fourteen-to-seventeen-year-old, who is too old for the kids' field, too young for everything else, and now navigating a site full of adults, substances and sexual attention with a child's experience and an adult's height.

Here's the scene. They want to go and watch a band with the two kids from the next camp and be back later, and that is a completely reasonable thing for a fifteen-year-old to want. It is also a fifteen-year-old, at night, in a crowd of adults, where nobody checks anybody's age for anything, and where a nineteen-year-old and a twenty-six-year-old will both read them as older than they are.

So watch for these. Adults who take an unusual interest in your teenager specifically. An older new friend who appears and is vague about their age. Substances offered to them because they read as older. They have no meeting point, no charged phone and no agreed check-in. They will not tell you if something goes wrong, because of what they were doing. You have not said the one sentence that makes telling you possible.

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A teenager needs a different regime from both a child and an adult — real independence, because refusing it produces a teenager who lies, and real structure, because the environment contains things they have no experience of. The single highest-value item is not a rule, it is a disclosure guarantee. Adolescents decide whether to tell a parent about a bad situation almost entirely on their prediction of the reaction, so a teenager who believes they will be punished for being where they were will handle it alone, at night, at a festival. That is the outcome to prevent.

So ask yourself this. I call it the K check. Ask: “Do they have a check-in time, a meeting point, and a promise from me that ringing costs them nothing?” — Three things, agreed before they go anywhere.

Now here's the part that's hard to hear. The Sentence That Actually Protects Them. Say this to them plainly, once, before the weekend: if anything goes wrong, whatever you were doing, ring me and I will not be angry and we will sort it out afterwards. Then keep it the first time it is used, over something small, because that is the test and they will run it. The alternative is a fifteen-year-old who has taken something, or is with somebody who will not let them leave, running the calculation about how much trouble they are in, and deciding to manage it themselves. Every safeguarding professional will tell you the same thing: the child who tells you is the one who thinks telling you is survivable.

So here's what you do. Give real autonomy with real structure — agreed zones, an hourly-ish check-in, a charged phone and a power bank, a meeting point, and the names of the friends they are with. Say the unconditional sentence and mean it. Tell them the two adult-world things nobody else will: watch your own drink, and an adult being very interested in you is a reason to leave.

Here's what to say, out loud. “Here's the deal: go, have a great time, text me at ten and midnight. And whatever happens, whatever you were doing, ring me — I won't be angry, I'll just come. That's a promise, not a trap.”

And remember this. You cannot supervise a fifteen-year-old at a festival. You can be the person they are not afraid to ring, and that is worth more.
