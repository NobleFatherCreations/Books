**Problem:** In the climax, "The bandana guys are gone" is listed alongside Hopper and the cashbox fleeing — but no bandana-wearing crew was ever introduced. The camp was described as a mandolin guy and a woman doing macramé. Nobody in a bandana.

**Proposed fix:** Plant them at the moment the tank job is actually set up, rather than back at the "warm camp" introduction — it's a smaller, more surgical insert and doesn't disturb the paragraph that establishes Hopper's camp as innocuous.

Insert this sentence right before *"Set break hits at 8:14 p.m. Pacific time"*:

> Two guys from the shade canopy — bandanas, the kind tied low and quiet, hadn't said ten words to Daniel all evening — get up without being asked and start running the balloon rig off the second tank. Hopper doesn't introduce them. They don't introduce themselves.

This gives "the bandana guys" a single, earlier, wordless appearance — consistent with how invisible Hopper's actual crew is meant to be (that's the point of the chapter) — so their later flight has something to refer back to.

**Your call:** approve as-is, want them planted even earlier (at the camp-arrival paragraph instead), or cut the phrase "the bandana guys" from the climax line entirely instead of adding setup?

# PART 2 — Voice, Vernacular & Landing Harder

## How to read this section

The book's prose has one very consistent authorial rhythm across all 26 chapters — short declarative fragments, the "That is the chapter" recap, the "Not X. Not Y. Z." construction, italicized one-line theses. That rhythm is clearly a deliberate house style, and I'm not proposing you sand it off. But it means every POV — a scared 22-year-old, a predator justifying himself, a tired camp veteran, the author breaking the fourth wall — currently thinks in the *same* sentence shapes. Real distinctiveness would come from layering **actual scene vocabulary** on top of that shared rhythm, so each POV sounds like a specific kind of person who happens to share the book's narrative cadence, not like the same narrator wearing five different name tags.

Below: a vernacular pass per POV bucket, then chapter-specific "raw detail" ideas.

---

## FESTIE POV (Chapters 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 15, 16, 19, 20, 22, 23, 26)

This is the book's default voice, and it currently reads a little "explained" — a Festie thinking to themselves rarely needs the parenthetical definitions a non-scene reader needs. Real Festies don't think "the nod — the slow deliberate one that says I have been around" as an aside to themselves; they'd just do the nod and have a private, unspoken vocabulary for it.

**Vocabulary to actually put in these characters' heads (not the narrator's explanations, their own private shorthand):**
- **Lot rat** for the type already named as an archetype — a Festie would use this as a snap-judgment word, not wait for the chapter to define it.
- **Shakedown** / **shakedown street** — the vendor gauntlet outside a lot. Good texture for any scene that currently just says "vendor row" or "the merch tent."
- **Sherpa** — scene slang for someone who shows a newbie the ropes (exactly Hopper's cover story in Ch1 — using this word *ironically* in Daniel's own head right before the OD would land hard: *He thinks: Hopper's basically my sherpa tonight. He will think about that word again in a courthouse hallway in October.*)
- **Spun** / **spun out** — over-amped on stimulants, distinct from "rolling" (on MDMA) or "tripping" (psychedelics). The book currently uses generic phrasing ("high and happy") where a Festie would use the specific word for the specific substance.
- **PLUR** and its ironic inversions — the book already uses PLUR-adjacent language (the whole "PLURth Angel" persona) but individual Festies rarely say the actual word or subvert it themselves. A line like *"This didn't feel very PLUR"* in a moment of quiet dread would be more authentic than an internal essay about boundaries.
- **JNCOs / kandi cuffs / flow toys** — small, brand-specific gear nouns. "She had a hoop" reads generic; "she had a two-and-a-half-minute LED hoop she'd built herself, the kind that costs more in batteries than it did to make" is a Festie detail.
- **"The comedown" / "Tuesday brain"** — already coined well in-book (Ch20). Worth reusing this exact phrase in other chapters that currently reinvent the concept from scratch (Ch3's "Day Three Brain" is a great parallel coinage — lean into having characters cross-reference each other's coined terms as if they're real scene slang everyone already knows, the way "PLUR" itself became universal.)
- **Default world** — already used well and consistently. Keep it; it's doing real work.
- **"The rail"** — already used for stage-front. Good, authentic, keep leaning on it.

**Raw detail lever:** Festie-POV chapters are strongest when the danger detail is mundane and specific (Ch1's brake-light-adjacent details: the trail mix down to raisins, $340 as actual cash in a wallet). Where a chapter goes abstract too fast (Ch2, Ch19), the fix is almost always "what's the one object in her hand or pocket right now" rather than "what is she feeling."

## WOOK POV (Chapters 5, 8, 12, 13, 14, 17, plus Ch18's "Secondary Drop")

This is the book's best-executed voice already — the code-switching between warm scene-speak and cold accounting language (Ch5's "eighty-nine or ninety" targeting math, Ch14's "outcomes" and "close rate") is genuinely sharp and doesn't need much help. Two places it can go further:

**Vocabulary to lean into harder:**
- **"The mark"** — none of the Wook-POV chapters actually use this word, which is odd, since it's the single most authentic piece of con-artist vernacular available. Every one of them dances around it ("target," "the girl," "the guy") without ever landing on the word a real long-con operator would actually use privately. One deliberate use — late, at the moment of maximum self-justification — would be a gut-punch: *I don't think of her as a mark. I want to be clear about that. (He thinks of her as a mark.)*
- **"Warming up," "the pitch," "closing," "objection handling"** — Ch14 (the undercover) already nails law-enforcement-specific jargon (training manual, close rate, expense report). The other Wook chapters could borrow a little of that same *procedural* vocabulary — not to make every predator sound like a cop, but because predators who've run the play for years genuinely do develop a private operations vocabulary, and right now only Ch14 has one.
- **Ch17 (Wedge, the "good" dealer)** is the chapter most ready for this: he already thinks in ledgers and margins. A few more actual drug-economy terms — "step on it," "the cut," "front," "re-up," "the count" — would sharpen a voice that's currently more generic-businessman than actual scene dealer.

**Raw detail lever:** the strongest Wook chapters (5, 12) work because the predator notices *one small true thing* about the victim that humanizes them right before the harm (Ch12's Santa hat memory). Ch8 (Sterling) and Ch13 (Cosmo) currently keep their victims more generic ("Asha," "Sasha" — a name and a job, not a specific memory). Adding one small, specific, human detail Sterling or Cosmo notices and discards would sharpen the "he saw her clearly and chose to profit anyway" gut-punch these chapters are going for.

---

## WITNESS POV (Chapters 6, 7)

Currently the least distinct voice in the book — it reads almost identically to Festie POV, just with slightly more authority. A Witness (someone who's been in the scene 15 years, watching from the outside of the immediate danger) should sound *tireder* and more clinical than a Festie in the moment of their own danger.

**Vocabulary to add:**
- **"Green flag / red flag"** — scene shorthand a longtime Witness would use constantly and casually, almost as punctuation. Currently the book spells out the assessment in full sentences every time ("Green: accepts and redirects...") — that's fine for the *teaching* material (the Counter-Drop boxes), but in the Witness's own internal narration during the scene, she'd just think "red flag" and move, not run the whole rubric.
- **"Camp mom / camp dad"** — used narratively already in other chapters (Lorelei in Ch7) but a Witness-POV narrator describing a scene in progress would use this term more freely and more sardonically than the book currently allows Margo or Jules to.
- **Veteran weariness markers**: "seen this movie," "same guy, different flannel," "I could set my watch to this." Witnesses have pattern-recognition fatigue that Festies (experiencing their first version of the pattern) don't have yet — the prose should sound more bored-and-sharp, less freshly-alarmed.

**Raw detail lever:** Both Witness chapters (6, 7) resolve very cleanly and calmly. One additional beat that would sharpen the "cost of vigilance" theme already present: show the Witness doing this math internally — a half-second where Margo (Ch6) or Hugo (Ch7) considers *not* saying anything, because they're tired, because it's not their fight tonight, because they've done this so many times — before doing it anyway. Right now they act instantly and correctly, which is a little too clean for how real fatigue works.

---

## MONTAGE POV (Chapter 24)

This voice is closer to journalism/documentary than to any character's interiority — good instinct for a "here's the mechanism" chapter, and mostly landing already. It would benefit from picking up institutional/advocacy-world vocabulary more explicitly, since these vignettes are literally about institutions (safety coordinators, subreddits, legal clinics, HR-style camp governance):

**Vocabulary:** "advisory," "documented pattern," "MOU" (memorandum of understanding — already half-used in vignette 5, worth using the acronym explicitly since that's how these actually get referred to), "case," "consultation," "whisper network" (already used and good — keep it central).

---

## AUTHOR DIRECT ADDRESS (Chapter 25)

This one should probably use *less* scene slang, not more — the whole conceit is the author stepping outside the Festie world to talk to the reader as a craftsperson confessing technique. Where it currently still uses scene vocabulary ("PLURth Angel," "the magic"), that's fine as the author's own persona bleeding through, but the confession content itself is strongest when it's plain, structural, editorial language — which it mostly already is. No major vocabulary change recommended here; this chapter's authenticity comes from precision, not slang.

## One cross-cutting naming issue worth a decision

Beyond the six discrepancies you asked me to resolve, my original read also surfaced a real copyediting issue: several character names are reused across unrelated Save-story protagonists in a way that could confuse a reader trying to track "wait, is this the same person":

- **"Priya"** is used for three unconnected characters: Ch1 (Priya Chandrasekhar), Ch12 (Priya Suresh), Ch22 (no surname given).

None of these break the plot the way the six discrepancies did, but if you want a clean copyedit pass, I'd suggest: rename Ch22's Priya, rename Ch18's Save-only Cara, and rename either Ch9's or Ch16's Okonkwo.
