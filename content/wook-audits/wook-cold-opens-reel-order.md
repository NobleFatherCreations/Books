# Wook in Sheep's Clothing — The Cold Opens
All 33 of them.
Every cold open as it currently stands live, plus a running order built for a promotional video series rather than for the book.
Generated from the live book source, so this file cannot drift from what shipped. Regenerate with `python3 content/wook-audits/build-coldopen-reel.py`.

## How this order was built
Chapter order is built for a reader who starts at the beginning and goes forward. A video series has the opposite problem: every episode is somebody's first, most viewers arrive knowing nothing about festival culture, and the algorithm rewards whichever one gets finished. So this order optimizes for six things instead:

1. **Cold-start legibility.** Can someone who has never been to a festival
   follow it with no setup? The most legible episode goes first, even if it
   isn't the best one.
2. **Hook speed.** How many seconds to the first *wait, what?*
3. **Recognition transfer.** Does it make a viewer with no connection to the
   scene recognize somebody in their own life? That is what drives shares.
4. **Payload.** Does it actually land, or does it only explain?
5. **Rotation.** POV and tone alternate deliberately. Two victim episodes back
   to back flattens; victim → predator → breather does not.
6. **Ending on the right beat.** Most of these cold opens close with the
   chapter's thesis. For video you almost always want to cut on the last
   *narrative* beat and let the argument stay in the book. A suggested cut
   point is given for every episode below.

# Season One — the thirteen

## EP 1 — Chapter 12: “We've Been Having Problems”
*Festie POV · 1,715 words · ~11 min read-aloud*

**Open on:** “The bag is heavier than she thought.”

**Why here:** Needs zero festival knowledge. A boyfriend, a gas station, a rehearsed sentence. Every viewer knows someone who has been the person in that passenger seat, which makes it the most portable thing in the book -- it plays on a true-crime channel, a relationships page, or a harm-reduction feed without changing a word. It also has a hard, clean ending, which most of these don't. Lead with your most legible episode, not your best one.

**Cut point:** End on: "He knew what would happen. He rehearsed the sentence."

## EP 2 — Chapter 5: “The Eight-Ball”
*Wook POV · 1,844 words · ~12 min read-aloud*

**Open on:** “I bring an eight-ball to every party.”

**Why here:** The POV flip is the entire sell of this book, and it lands hardest as the second thing you see, immediately after watching it happen to someone. Now you are inside the guy, and he is likeable, and he sleeps great. New this round: he catches himself using the word 'mark' twice and corrects himself both times, which is the moment the self-deception cracks on camera.

# Production notes

**Runtimes.** Read-aloud estimates above assume 150 words per minute, which is
an unhurried narration pace. These are long for short-form: the shortest is
Chapter 23 at about seven minutes, the longest Chapter 18 at seventeen. Three
usable formats:

- **Full narration** (7–17 min) for YouTube or a podcast feed. Cut at the point
  noted for each episode.
- **The first 90 seconds** as the short-form hook, ending on the cliff, with the
  full version linked. Chapters 10, 5, 16, 14 and 23 all have a natural 90-second
  break already in them.
- **Single-line cards.** Several of these have one sentence that carries the
  whole episode — "He rehearsed the sentence," "I'm just generous," "nobody
  can remember her last name," "The wristband is real." Those work as standalone
  text posts pointing at the video.

**Content warnings.** Episodes 4, 11 and 22 need one on the front. Chapter 18
(EP 11) specifically depicts drugging and assault; Chapter 22 (Season Two)
depicts the aftermath. Put support resources in the description, not just at
the end of the video.

**What not to spoil.** Chapter 25 confesses the persuasion techniques the book
used on the reader. It is the best single piece of writing in the book and it
only works if the audience has already been through the others, so it must stay
last. Do not use it as a hook, a teaser, or a trailer.

**The one continuing character.** Cara appears in Chapters 18, 21, 24 and 26.
EP 11 and EP 13 are her, six months apart. If the series does nothing else with
continuity, do that pairing — it is the only arc in the book that resolves
across chapters, and it is what turns a set of cautionary tales into a story.

## Chapter 1: IT’S NOT DRAMA, IT’S WARFARE
*Festie POV · 2,129 words · ~14 min · reel position: EP 4*

🎬THE DROP

“The Tank”

The cold open📍 FESTIE POV

The Gorge Amphitheatre parking lot at four in the afternoon smells like two things: hot asphalt with a top note of sunscreen, and money leaving people faster than their brain can track it.

You get here at peak lot and you feel it immediately — the pressure of all those bodies having an experience, the way the sound from the main stage leaks over the ridgeline like a promise that costs thirty-two dollars in service fees. The Columbia River is down there somewhere, reflecting silver in the kind of light that makes Pacific Northwesters feel chosen by God. The lot is car-camping, RV-camping, shade structures made of PVC and prayer, a guy in a camp chair with a Coors Banquet who has been here since Wednesday and has watched the whole civilization rise up around him with the philosophical detachment of someone who has seen it all before and still finds it disappointing.

His name is Daniel Weiss. He is twenty years old. He has been on tour for eighteen days.

His Subaru — a ’99 Forester with a cracked rear window, a sleeping bag, one duffel, and a bag of trail mix with only raisins left because he ate all the M&Ms first like an idiot — is backed in at Lot D near the water truck. He has $340 to his name. Not in an account. In his wallet. Three hundred and forty actual dollars, the last of what he saved doing dish shifts at the breakfast spot on Valencia before he quit to do this thing his friends called a bad idea and he called a spiritual necessity.

He has been practicing the nod.

Not like a nod nod. The nod. The slow deliberate one that says I have been around, I know what’s good, I move through this world with the easy weight of someone who does not need to ask where the bathrooms are. He practices it in the Subaru window on I-84 somewhere between Hood River and The Dalles. He looks like a twenty-year-old practicing a nod.

He has been saying kind. As in: That’s kind of you, brother. He heard an OG say it and it sounded like a whole life philosophy compressed into four syllables, which is exactly what being twenty years old on your first real tour feels like.

Hopper finds him at 5:47 p.m.

Hopper is forty-one, wearing a bucket hat with a hand-sewn patch of a running deer that has been on that hat for eleven years. He has a gray-streaked beard and the kind of easy gait that comes from walking parking lots for decades. He has three gold rings on one hand and no watch. He smells like sandalwood and nag champa and somebody’s van, and he has the unhurried warmth of a man who has never been in a hurry because he has always been right where he needed to be.

He sees Daniel trying to light a camp stove with a match that has already gotten damp.

“Hold on,” Hopper says. He has a lighter before Daniel can say he’s fine.

He lights the stove. He watches Daniel’s instant rice start to do its thing. He says, “First tour?”

Daniel says yes before deciding whether he wants to.

Hopper smiles. Not a predatory smile. Not even a warm smile, really — more like the smile of someone watching something they recognize from the inside. He says, “Man, I remember that.” And then he says, “You eaten today?” And Daniel says the rice, which is not ready yet. And Hopper says, “Come on. My camp’s got rice and beans. Real ones. You can eat, rest, I’ll show you around later.”

Daniel Weiss, eighteen days into his first Dead summer, $340 in his wallet, a bag of raisin-only trail mix, practicing the nod — he says yes.

That is the first yes.

Somewhere in the back of his head, in the part still narrating his own tour to an audience that does not exist, Daniel thinks: Hopper’s basically my sherpa out here. He will think about that word again in October, in a hallway outside a courtroom in Douglas County, and it will not sound the same.

Hopper’s camp is three rows over and it is the real thing: a shade canopy stretched tight between two tent poles with actual stakes, a propane burner, a blue cooler with ice in it, a Coleman lantern, and four or five people in varying states of horizontal. There is a guy with a mandolin not playing the mandolin. There is a woman doing something with macramé. There is the smell of cumin, actual cumin, not incense pretending to be cumin.

Hopper makes a plate and hands it to Daniel without ceremony, which is the smartest move in the whole manual — ceremony would have put up Daniel’s guard. No ceremony is just Tuesday.

Over the rice and beans they talk. Not about the play. About music. About where Daniel grew up (Cedar Rapids, Iowa, yes the whole state is one big flat thing, yes he knows, thanks). About the Dead, the setlist from the night before, whether Phil’s bass was mixed wrong in the second set. Hopper laughs at Daniel’s joke about I-84. He remembers Cedar Rapids — passed through it in ’07, great diner on the main strip.

The thing about Hopper is: he is not performing warmth. He is warm. That is the whole engine of this. If he were performing, Daniel’s nervous system would catch the edge of it, the effort, the production of the thing. Hopper has been doing this for nineteen years and he has made warmth into muscle memory and he cannot tell the difference anymore either. That is the deepest version of the move — where the operator has run the play so long they have genuinely incorporated it into their personality. The exploitation and the kindness are not separate. He is not pretending to like Daniel. He does like Daniel. He also needs Daniel to hold a tank.

Those two facts coexist without contradiction and that is what makes Hopper difficult to spot and this chapter necessary.

At seven-thirty, during the set break, Hopper says: “Hey, I have a thing. It’s simple. Easy money.”

He explains it flat. No pressure in his voice, which is its own pressure. Just hold the tank during set break. Stand right here, look friendly, twenty an hour, I handle the balloons, you don’t have to do anything. He makes it sound like the easiest logistics problem in the world, which it is, on its face, and Daniel says yes — he says it like he’s been handed a kidney, like someone finally decided he was useful — and they shake hands and Hopper says good man, which lands in Daniel’s chest like a hand on his shoulder.

That is the second yes.

It is the one that matters.

Two guys from the shade canopy get up around eight without being asked — bandanas tied low, the same practiced way, neither of them having said ten words to Daniel all evening — and start running the balloon rig off the second tank. Hopper does not introduce them. They do not introduce themselves. Daniel reads this as them being busy.

Set break hits at 8:14 p.m. Pacific time.

Four thousand bodies spill out of the amphitheatre into the lot. The sound from the stage cuts and for thirty seconds the world sounds like: gravel underfoot, cooler lids, the river somewhere below, voices, voices, voices. Then the economy kicks in. The nitrous setup on the south end does not just have a line — it has a crowd, the kind that has its own gravity, that pulls people by the familiar logic of everyone else is already doing it.

Daniel is holding the tank.

He is standing where Hopper told him to stand. He looks friendly. He is twenty years old and he has twenty dollars an hour and his hands on the valve and he is telling himself this is fine, he is just holding it, Hopper is the one doing the transactions.

At 8:22 p.m. a girl in a white flowy skirt cuts the line.

She is not aggressive about it. She is high and happy and she wants a balloon. She gets one. She gets another one. She is standing up and she is laughing when the third balloon leaves her and her knees go — not a stumble, not a lean — they go, like a light switch. She drops straight down and her skull hits the asphalt with a sound that does not belong in that lot or any lot or anywhere near music, a sound like something that cannot be taken back.

The blood under her ear is dark.

The lot freezes.

One second.

Two.

Then:

Hopper is gone.

The bandana guys are gone.

The cashbox is gone.

The balloon crew at the setup twelve yards over has achieved a collective act of teleportation and their van is already pointing toward the exit. They do not run. Running is how you get chased. They walk with the calm efficiency of people who have executed this particular exit strategy multiple times and the walk itself is a performance of innocence.

Daniel Weiss is still standing with the tank.

He does not move for three full seconds because his brain has not yet told his body what has happened. He is still in the previous reality where he is just holding a tank for twenty an hour look friendly. The blood under the girl’s ear is real and his brain is receiving it but hasn’t decided yet what to do with it and during those three seconds a security guard comes out of the crowd from forty yards north at a dead sprint pointing directly at him yelling something that resolves, as he gets closer, into: don’t move, don’t fucking move.

The tank comes up to his hip.

His clean t-shirt has the name of a band on it.

His actual face is out.

Hannah Reyes. Twenty-three. From Irvine. She went to the medic tent by ambulance at 8:29 p.m. She had a bleed she never woke up from. She died Monday at 9:14 a.m. in a hospital in Wenatchee. Her parents, who drove seven hours from Southern California, were with her. There was a playlist on in the room. Someone decided that was right.

Daniel’s parents drove twenty-three hours from Cedar Rapids.

His mother cried the entire walk through the parking lot. Not at Daniel, at the parking lot — at the asphalt and the heat and the fact that she had driven twenty-three hours to get here and that this was where the drive ended. His father said nothing in the car. Not because he was angry, or not only because he was angry — also because he did not have the words for what he was watching happen to his son, who had driven to a concert in a used Subaru and was now being handed back to him by a public defender in a county he had never been to.

In the hallway outside the courtroom, Daniel does the nod. At his own public defender. Slow and deliberate, I have been around, I know what’s good — eighteen days of practice firing off on schedule at a man holding a folder with his name on it. He catches it half a second after his chin comes back up.

It is the last time he does it.

Hopper was at a Phish run in Keene Valley by Tuesday.

Different kid. Different tank. Same exit.

Here is the thing the chapter has to tell you, and it might be the only thing that matters in all thirty-three chapters:

That kid said yes for the exact same reason you have said yes to things that cost you.

He was new. He was broke. He was alone in a lot where everyone else seemed to already know the choreography. Someone competent and warm and unhurried walked over, fed him, learned his name, and made him feel like he was useful, which is — and write this down in whatever part of your brain is still readable at 3 a.m. — the most reliable access route into a person who has not yet learned to protect themselves.

Not threat. Not lust. Not drugs. Not money.

Belonging.

Hopper did not hack a stranger. He made a friend. The friend happened to have hands and a face and twenty years of not knowing who Hopper was.

The tank was twenty dollars an hour.

The price was not twenty dollars an hour.

That is what this book is for.

## Chapter 2: HOW THEY GET YOUR YES BEFORE YOU FINISH YOUR DAB
*Festie POV · 2,094 words · ~14 min · reel position: EP 9*

🎬THE DROP

“The Pre-Party”

The cold open📍 FESTIE POV

The Sprinter is a 2021 Mercedes passenger van, black, driven by a guy named Boyd who does not talk and does not make eye contact and has a cooler wedged between the front seats that nobody is supposed to touch. The cooler says FRAGILE on the side in tape. Whatever is fragile in the cooler is not being addressed.

Solenne Baptiste is twenty-six years old and she has been a DJ for four years and she has her first artist wristband on her left wrist and it is the plastic kind, not the paper kind, which means she is officially on the lineup and she can walk through the artist entrance and none of this has fully arrived in her body yet because she is sitting in a van on the way to a pre-party with five women she does not know, driven by a man who will not look at her, to a location she has a general sense of — hills, north of the festival site, big house — and that is all she knows.

Rex is not in the van. Rex sent the van.

Rex is the promoter who books two of the three festivals she needs to break the year. She met him at a warehouse show in Silver Lake in February. He stayed for her whole set, which almost nobody does on the second floor at a warehouse show where the main act is downstairs. He found her after and said: You’ve got something, I want to hear more from you. He gave her his card. She texted the next day. He took four days to respond, then called, talked for forty-five minutes about the scene, about what he was building, about how he had eyes for talent in a way that most promoters didn’t because most promoters were chasing plays instead of making them. He said he was thinking about putting her on the lineup at Meridian this summer. He said it like it was not quite certain yet, which made her want it more.

That is Lever One. She does not know it yet.

Her mother calls on Sundays at ten and Solenne has not missed one in four years, including the Sunday she had food poisoning and took the call sitting on a bathroom floor in Reseda.

She has been awake since five-thirty a.m. She drove three hours to the festival from her apartment in Echo Park, dropped her setup at the artist lot, found her camping spot, built nothing because she will deal with it after the party, and then the text from Rex came: pre-party at the house, sending a car, be at the artist entrance at 7. She had one hour. She ate half a protein bar, put on the outfit, said yes.

In the van, minute three, she looks at the other women’s wrists and does the math. She has the artist band. The others have general admission with a gold add-on. She is the only one with the plastic kind. She registers this — the way you register a word that doesn’t fit the sentence — and decides not to figure out what it means yet.

That is her first mistake. Not a catastrophic one. Just — the mistake of a smart person who wants something too much to ask the inconvenient question. She knows something is slightly off. She decides it can wait.

It can’t wait. But that’s chapter two. She’ll learn it here.

The hills house is the kind of place that exists in every music scene in every city with mountains and money adjacent to it: Mediterranean-ish, stucco, pool that someone is definitely not licensed to have this many people in, string lights, palm trees doing nothing wrong just standing there being palm trees while everything below them goes sideways. The kitchen has a marble island the length of a small runway. On the marble island, arranged in three neat parallel lines on a black slate tile like a catering display at an investor dinner, is cocaine. Not hidden. Not secreted away in a back bathroom like a normal guilty habit. Just: here it is, welcome to the pre-party, the coke is between the olives and the charcuterie.

Rex is at the door.

He is forty-three, which he presents as thirty-seven through the aggressive use of skin care and an asymmetric haircut. He has a Patagonia vest over a shirt that costs what Solenne makes in a week teaching Ableton to undergrads. He hugs her with one arm and a shoulder-press that says you belong here and I’m in a hurry simultaneously, which is the hug of someone who has perfected the hug as a tool.

“You made it. Good. Come on, there’s someone you need to meet.”

Someone you need to meet is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Rex uses it to create forward momentum. She is already following him before she has decided to follow him. The house has seven rooms visible from the entry and a hallway off the kitchen with a door labeled THE TALENT ROOM in a printed sign, laminated, like someone made it at an office supply store, which they did. ## Chapter 6: THE GROUP CHAT

Eleven minutes is how long it takes.

It is also the first move.

## Chapter 7: THE WEEP TENT HUSTLE
*Witness POV · 1,831 words · ~12 min · reel position: EP 21*

🎬THE DROP

“Where Are You Camped”

The cold open📍 WITNESS POV

It is 2:47 a.m. on a Friday at a transformational gathering in the Sierra Nevada foothills, and Margo Kwon is being talked at by a man named Asher and she has not said a word in twenty-two minutes.

This is notable because Margo is not a quiet person. She runs a ceramics studio in Portland. She teaches a community wheel class on Saturdays. She has a relationship with her own opinions that her friends describe as “robust” and her mother describes as “a lot, sweetheart, but it’s fine.” She has been in this scene for fifteen years. She knows the moves. She knows the moves the way you know the moves after fifteen years of watching them get run on people you love — from the cheap seats first, then from the seat next to the person getting worked, and finally, occasionally, from inside the work itself, which is where she is right now, aware that she is being worked and somehow still in the chair.

She got here forty minutes ago. The headliner ended at one-thirty and the crowd dissolved in the way crowds dissolve at one-thirty — some people toward the bass stage for the afterparty, some toward the workshop dome where a man named River is leading a “nervous-system unwinding journey” that is mostly just slow swaying with eyes closed and incense doing its thing, and some people toward bed, which is a deeply unfashionable choice at a transformational gathering and which Margo has been threatening herself with since eleven p.m.

She stopped at the lantern circle because the cushions looked welcoming and Devon, who runs stage left, was there with a thermos, and Devon’s thermos makes the kind of chamomile that Margo’s body has been asking for since approximately Thursday.

She sat down.

There were four others: Devon with the thermos; Pickle, whose real name is Patricia and who has answered to nothing else since 2011, even at the DMV; Jules, a sober social worker three years clean who is here as a harm-reduction volunteer technically off shift but never really off shift, the way doctors are never really off shift at parties; and Asher.

Asher is twenty-six. It is his second festival. He is beautiful in the way that some twenty-six-year-olds are beautiful — effortlessly, slightly carelessly, in a way that he is not unaware of but also has not fully weaponized yet. He has dark eyes and a flannel that fits well and the particular quality of emotional openness that the scene cultivates in young men who have found, perhaps for the first time, a social environment where feelings are currency rather than liability.

He started talking.

He started, technically, with a question about the headliner — did anyone catch the second set? — and then rerouted, via three distinct switchbacks, through his last relationship (she “didn’t understand his process”), his mother’s diagnosis (a real one, serious, spoken with genuine pain), his complicated feelings about his stepdad (it was complicated in a way that took nine minutes to establish), the time he saw a great blue heron on the Willamette and felt his grandfather’s presence (the grandfather had died six months ago; this detail arrived with the particular weight of something true and not yet fully metabolized), and a recent ayahuasca ceremony in Costa Rica that involved a man named Sebastián who Asher now thinks may have been “more than human.”

It has been twenty-two minutes.

Nobody has spoken.

Nobody has been able to.

Margo has tried four times. She has opened her mouth, drawn breath, and been rerouted by the avalanche. She is a polite person — raised by Midwestern Lutherans who considered interruption the social equivalent of jaywalking — and she once let a man talk at her for six hours on a cross-country flight and only fled to the bathroom on the descent. Her whole face is doing the thing. The smile-and-dying thing. The “I am a compassionate person who loves humanity and also what is happening” thing.

Devon has stopped pretending to listen. Devon is staring at the lantern with the philosophical distance of a man who built a stage over four days and is currently operating on three hours of sleep and the specific emotional bandwidth of damp cardboard.

Pickle’s blanket has migrated slowly, slowly up her face. It is now at her chin. It is clear that if Asher continues, the blanket will consume her entirely and she will achieve a kind of protective metamorphosis, and whatever emerges on the other side will be a wiser creature.

Jules is watching Asher’s eyes.

Jules has been doing harm-reduction and social work long enough to know the difference between someone who is processing — who is tumbling through feeling, whose eye contact is inward, whose narrative has the quality of someone genuinely trying to find the thing — and someone who is funneling. Funneling is when the story is being directed. When the eye contact is outward, checking receipts, landing on specific faces to gauge the reaction. When the narrative, for all its apparent spontaneity, keeps circling back to the same destination.

Jules is clocking the difference.

Asher takes a breath.

The circle thinks, for a beautiful four seconds, that it is over.

It is not over. It is loading.

He looks directly at Margo. Not Devon, not Pickle, not Jules. Margo. The one who has been trying to respond. The one whose face has been most actively doing the thing. He has been monitoring this. Without appearing to monitor this, he has been monitoring this.

He says: “I just feel like you really get me. I don’t usually open up like this.”

Margo, who has not said a single word in twenty-two minutes, who has been doing intensive facial labor to maintain the appearance of compassionate presence, panics quietly inside the cardigan she has had since college.

He says: “I think we have a real soul connection.”

Pickle’s blanket goes up another inch.

He says, soft and certain: “I don’t know what camp you’re in, but I feel like the universe wants us in the same orbit tonight. Where are you camped?”

There it is.

The hinge.

The vulnerable monologue was not vulnerability.

It was an audition.

Asher just delivered twenty-two minutes of real pain — his mother’s diagnosis is real, his grandfather is genuinely dead, the heron on the Willamette probably genuinely moved him — and he used that real pain as the preparation for a question he was going to ask regardless of whether any of it landed, regardless of whether the feeling he described was real or performed or somewhere in the complex middle territory where most human behavior lives.

The pain is real. The function is extraction. Both things are true simultaneously, which is what makes this harder than the obvious moves. The obvious moves are obvious. This one is wearing a flannel that fits.

Where are you camped?

Margo knows what it means. She has seen this conversation cost three women their tents over three separate festivals over three years. She knows the shape of the next six hours if she answers the question straight.

She also knows something else, which is the thing that makes fifteen years of scene experience feel, in this moment, like a graduate degree she never wanted:

Asher may not know he is doing this.

That is the hardest part of the Weep Tent. Not the predators who know. The people who have discovered, somewhere between their genuine pain and their genuine loneliness, that a certain kind of performance of the first produces relief from the second, and who have so thoroughly merged the performance with the feeling that they can no longer find the seam.

Asher cries and doors open. He has learned this. He did not learn it in a seminar. He learned it through repetition, the way everyone learns everything, and now the crying and the opening are so fused that he experiences them as the same thing.

This does not make him safe.

This does not make the open easier.

This does not make Margo responsible for staying.

Here is the part Margo does not say out loud, tonight or ever: she considers letting it go. For about a second and a half she runs the whole thing — that she is fifty-one hours into a four-day event, that Devon and Jules are both right there and either of them could take this, that she has done this exact thing at this exact hour for fifteen years and has never once been thanked for it. There is a version of tonight where she says oh, I’m up near the north gate and lets the next six hours belong to somebody else.

The tiredness is real. The tiredness has a case.

She does it anyway. That is what the fifteen years actually bought — not the seeing. Anybody can learn the seeing. The doing-it-anyway on the night you are too tired to want to.

Margo says: “Asher. I’ve really appreciated you sharing tonight. I’m going to be honest with you — I’m not in a place to go deep right now, and I’m not looking for someone to share a camp. I hope you find your crew. I’m going to have my tea.”

She lifts her chamomile.

She is warm. She is not unkind. She is absolutely done.

Asher’s face does the thing. The particular recalibration of someone whose expectation of a yes has collided with a no. A flash of something — not anger, not exactly; closer to confusion, the genuine bewilderment of a person who has run this play enough times that the no doesn’t compute — and then the tears.

Real ones. Or real enough. This is the thing about the Weep Tent: the tears are usually real. The question is whether the tears are doing something other than crying.

Jules says: “I can walk you over to integration if you want to talk to someone.”

Asher says: “I just — I feel like I was really open with you and you’re kind of closing the door on that.”

Margo says: “I receive your openness. I’m not inviting you to my camp.”

Devon says nothing. Devon is eating a date. Devon has decided, correctly, that his role in this situation is to eat dates and be a witness and let Margo run it, which is the right call.

Pickle’s blanket is at the bridge of her nose. She is watching Asher over it with the specific gaze of a woman who has seen this particular play and is taking notes.

Asher leaves at two fifty-nine.

The circle is quiet for about ten seconds.

Then Pickle lowers the blanket and says: “He’s good.”

Jules says: “He’s been doing it for a while. The grandfather is real though.”

Margo says: “I know. That’s the whole thing.”

Devon eats another date.

The chamomile is still warm.
