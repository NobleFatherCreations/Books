# Wook — Vernacular Word Bank

**Pass A3, 2026-09-15.** Built by measuring the live book, not by
recalling what festival slang sounds like. The B passes write against this
so twenty-six chapters do not each invent their own vocabulary.

Everything numeric here was counted off `library/wook/index.html`.

The stratification that exists is correct: the Wook narrators speak in
supply and logistics, the festies in harm reduction. The gap is the other
direction. **Six chapters of Wook POV, 58,000 words, and not one of them
says playa, decom, default world, PLUR, set break, nitrous or shakedown.**

These narrators are supposed to be scene lifers — that is the whole
premise, the predator who has been here fifteen years and belongs. A lifer
says *playa* and *decom* without thinking about it. Reserving the culture
words for the victims and the logistics words for the predators splits the
vocabulary along exactly the line the book argues does not exist.

**The biggest single opportunity is a Wook narrator weaponising PLUR in
first person.** Zero uses across six chapters. The book's thesis is that
the language of the scene is the tool; no Wook has yet been caught holding
it.

## 5. The word banks

Use these in the B passes. A word goes in a chapter because that narrator
would reach for it, not to hit a quota.

**Wook POV — chapters 5, 8, 12, 13, 14, 17.** Already has: the mark,
stepping on it, caps, Bunk Police, source, at cost, my people. **Add from:**
playa, decom, default world, set break, PLUR (used as a lever), the lot,
shakedown, regional, burn, sound camp, renegade, family camp, lifer, since
'09, before it got like this.

**Festie POV — 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 26.**
Already has: Zendo, kandi, the rail, porta, gate line, wristband, camp,
Sprinter. **Add from:** heady, doof, whomp, totem, Camelbak, moop, cuddle
puddle, silent disco, workshop stage, day two, shuttle, will call, tabs.

**Witness POV — 6, 7.** Crew and adjacent-professional vocabulary, and the
thinnest of the three at 22,000 words. Already has: caps, PLUR, glover.
**Add from:** FOH, monitor world, load-in, teardown, ring out, the snake,
crew shirt, laminate, comp list, day parking, golf cart, the pit.

**Montage (24) and direct address (25)** sit outside the banks. The
montage speaks in the book's own voice about people from earlier chapters;
the reveal is the PLURth Angel speaking as the author. Neither should
acquire vocabulary it has not earned.

## 6. Currency — what has dated

The book already jokes about this: a Field Specimen kicker reads *"Slang
current as of 2014."* The rule that gag implies is the right one.

- **Still current, use freely:** heady, wook, kandi, moop, playa, decom,
  default world, shakedown, Zendo, Bunk Police, regional, burn, doof
  (Australian, but in circulation), the rail, set break.
- **Dated, use only in a character's mouth to date them:** rage stick,
  bass face, spun, trainwreck as a noun for a person, PLUR used
  unironically by anyone under thirty.
- **Never, in the book's own voice:** EDM as a genre label for the jam
  scene, "ravers" for festival attendees generally, "molly" as though it
  were a synonym for MDMA rather than a street name the book should treat
  with the same precision it treats everything else.

## 7. The rules the B passes follow

1. Vocabulary is a property of the narrator, never of the paragraph. Ask
   who is speaking before reaching for a word.
2. A scene word replaces an explanation. If the sentence already explains
   what a decom spiral is, the word is doing the explaining and the
   explanation comes out.
3. Never add a word a chapter has to define. The reader learns from
   context or the word does not belong.
4. Do not backfill the thin chapters to a number. Chapter 6 is thin
   because it is set in a weep tent in the Sierra foothills with two
   people; the fix there is one or two words of the right register, not
   twenty of the wrong one.
