# In Sheep's Clothing — Tone, Humor & Voice Audit

**Date:** 2026-09-10 · **Source:** `library/wook/index.html` at v5 (live)
**Scope:** everything *except* the cold opens, which were audited and rewritten
in the previous two rounds.

This document does two jobs. Part One is **the method** — how to analyze the
non-cold-open material in any chapter, and the five things that analysis turns
up book-wide. Part Two is **the chapter-by-chapter synopsis** — what each one
does well, where it slips, and one specific humor or slang addition per chapter.
Part Three is **corrections**, which are factual errors, not taste calls.

Everything numeric here was measured off the live file, not estimated.

---

## Part One — The method, and what it found

### What the book is made of

Every chapter is built from the same component set, and each component has a
different job. Auditing tone means auditing each component **against its own
job**, not against a single house style.

| Component | Count | Its job | Current state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold open | 26 | Put you in a scene | **Fixed last round** |
| Soundboard Quote | 26 | A real voice with a real résumé | **Strongest slot in the book** |
| Track | 139 | Name and dissect one play | Solid, mechanical |
| VIBE / MIRROR / REFRACTION | 417 | The play from three angles | Consistent |
| RUNS IN EVERY DIRECTION | 139 | "This isn't only one kind of person" | **Collapsed after ch10** |
| SOBER TUESDAY | 139 | The same play at a desk job | **Drifts abstract after ch10** |
| Pocket Scripts | ~470 | Sentences you can memorize | **Format broken in 9 chapters** |
| Field Specimen | 5 | The funniest device in the book | **21 chapters don't have one** |
| Tales From… The Save | 26 | Somebody gets out | Working |

### The five findings

#### 1. RUNS IN EVERY DIRECTION stopped naming people and started reciting a disclaimer

This is the biggest tonal problem in the book, and it is measurable.

The card exists to say *this play isn't run by one demographic*. Early on it does
that by **naming the people**. Chapter 1:

> The Overt Lot Rat is not always a dude with a crusty beard and a staff he calls
> medicine. It can be the hot rave girl who keeps touching people after they
> freeze. The flow-arts guy who "adjusts your form" in the hoop circle.

That's funny, specific, and it makes the point *by illustration*. Now chapter 20:

> The Comedown Text runs in every direction — romantic, professional, financial,
> community. The window does not discriminate by the nature of the contact or the
> identity of the person making it.

Same point, zero people, zero jokes. It reads like a legal disclaimer.

I counted two things in every RUNS card: **formula phrases** ("runs in every
direction," "is not gendered," "applies universally," "regardless of identity,"
"every demographic," "gender-neutral") and **named person-nouns** (guy, auntie,
elder, camp lead, DJ, vendor, volunteer…).

| Chapters | Formula phrases per card | Named people per chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 1–6 | 1.3 – 2.3 | 7 – 15 |
| 9–17 | 1.6 – 2.6 | 0 – 1 |
| 20 | **4.3** | **0** |

Chapter 20's four cards contain seventeen formula phrases and not one human
being. Chapter 24's eight cards name one person total. The card also **halves in
length**: chapters 1–10 average 62 words, chapters 11–24 average 40.

**The fix is a rule, not a rewrite:** *every RUNS card must name at least three
specific people who run this play, and at most one universality sentence.* The
disclaimer is the conclusion, not the content. Chapter 13's entire card is
currently one line — "The invoice does not have a gender" — where it should be
naming the femme camp lead with the mason jar, the touring sound guy whose
generosity is a ledger, and the beautiful person whose beauty was the first free
thing.

#### 2. SOBER TUESDAY has one setting, and it's an open-plan office

The card's premise is perfect: *here is this exact play at noon on a Tuesday in
default world.* When it lands, it lands hard. Chapter 5:

> If a waiter refilled your wine glass twice after you said you were fine and
> then described the third refill as "your body wanted it," you would leave a
> different tip. The restaurant does not get a pass because the candles were nice.

That has a room, a body, a punchline, and a moral, in two sentences. Chapter 1's
is the same species — "the coworker who takes things off your desk while you're
in the bathroom."

But 96 of the 139 cards are set in an office. Chapter 24's eight cards contain
twenty-three corporate nouns (HR, manager, executive, Q1, performance review) and
zero physical images. Chapter 18's contain seventeen. They're *correct* — they're
just all the same joke told in the same room.

**The fix:** rotate the venue. The plays in this book run at the DMV, in a group
chat, at a family Thanksgiving, in a landlord's email, at a car dealership, in a
church volunteer committee, in a Facebook Marketplace meetup, in a CrossFit gym,
in a multi-level-marketing "team call," in a hospital waiting room. Chapter 17
already proves rotation works — it has the landlord, the gym membership, and the
free trial in the same chapter, and it's the funniest SOBER TUESDAY run in the
back half. Cap the office at roughly one card in three.

#### 3. Only 5 chapters out of 26 have a Field Specimen, and it's the best thing in the book

The specimen cards are the single funniest device here, and there are five.

> **FIELD SPECIMEN №05 — DEFINITELY NOT A COP**
> Asks three times. Wears the lanyard wrong. Slang current as of 2014.
> The clipboard is not for kandi inventory.
> → *Nobody at this campsite has ever called it 'the marijuana.'*

> **FIELD SPECIMEN №03 — KING DAVE (SELF-CROWNED)**
> Throne: two coolers. Court: whoever still owes him. Title: "family."
> Donations accepted — cash only. Exits taxed.
> → *The crown is bottle caps. The hierarchy is real.*

"Exits taxed." "Halo: load-bearing." "Slang current as of 2014." This is the
book's actual comic voice, and it appears in chapters 1, 2, 7, 13, 14 and then
never again except the mirror in 23 and the PLURth Angel bookend in 26.

**The fix, and it is the highest-value single change in this document:** write a
field specimen for every remaining chapter. The format is four to six fragments
plus an italic kicker, it costs about sixty words, and it does more tonal work
per word than anything else in the book. Candidates that write themselves:

- **Ch6 — THE WEEP TENT OPERATOR.** Linen: intentional. Tears: real. Timing:
  also real. Has a lantern and a very specific idea of where you're camped.
  → *The crying is not the ask. The crying is the invoice's cover letter.*
- **Ch8 — THE FOUNDER (VENDOR ROW).** Business card: letterpress. Payment terms:
  vibes. Will describe your labor as a contribution and his couch as Topanga.
  → *Exposure has never once paid for a booth fee.*
- **Ch15 — THE ROW 14 SITUATION.** Curtains: drawn. Generator: running. Distance
  from the nearest witness: forty feet and one corner.
  → *The geometry was decided before you were invited.*
- **Ch17 — THE PLUG WHO LOVES YOU.** First one: free. Second one: free.
  Third one: a conversation about loyalty.
  → *You are not a customer. You are a subscription.*
- **Ch18 — EL (NOT THEIR REAL NAME).** Bus: converted. Hierarchy: converted.
  Sister count: growing. Exit interviews: none on file.
  → *Family is a word here. It is also a fee schedule.*

#### 4. The Pocket Scripts card breaks in nine chapters

A pocket script should be a sentence you can remember at 2 a.m. Chapters 1–8 get
this exactly right: seven to eleven chips, five to eleven words each.

Then nine chapters blow the format open. Chapter 9 has **65** chips. Chapter 15
has 53. Chapter 16 has 48. And the long ones aren't scripts at all — the longest
in chapter 10 runs **64 words**, chapter 17's runs 57.

The cause is structural: the Safety Appendix sections reuse `<span class="chip">`,
so appendix paragraphs render as pill-shaped chips designed to hold six words.
A 64-word pill is a design failure and a usability one — the reader can no longer
tell which lines are the memorize-these and which are the reference material.

**The fix:** keep `chip` for anything under about fifteen words, and give the
Safety Appendix its own class with normal paragraph styling. That's a
presentation change, not a writing one, and it makes nine chapters legible again.

#### 5. The Soundboard Quote is your best voice — protect it and use it as the model

Nothing here needs fixing except the factual slips in Part Three. This slot
consistently produces the book's best writing, and the reason is worth naming
because it's copyable: **the humor lives in the résumé, not the quote.**

> — Nina "Patchbay" Alvarez, forty-one, stage manager, ex-raver, **current
> destroyer of clipboard frauds**, thirteen years between warehouse stages,
> mid-size festival circuits, and **one arena she's not allowed to name.**

> — Talia "Breaker Box" Nguyen, forty-four, production runner and **crisis tent
> auntie**, twenty-one years across desert burns, warehouse circuits, and **one
> deeply ill-advised outdoor psytrance three-day in Vermont where it rained
> sideways.**

> — Indigo "Spool" Marchetti, forty-one, ten years walking the lot with a wooden
> tray of hand-wrapped moonstone pendants, **a Square reader that overheats by
> two p.m.**, and a personal policy of "I do not Venmo strangers wearing new hats."

The quote is serious; the credential line is where the comedy goes. That
structure — earnest content, funny attribution — is the one you should port into
the Track intros and the Tales From… The Save headers, which currently have no
comic register at all.

### Festival vernacular: what the book already uses, and what it's leaving on the table

The book's lot vocabulary is real and correctly used where it appears: *shakedown,
kandi, heady, Wook, doof, whomp, the rail, set break, Zendo, Bunk Police, nitrous,
tabs, caps, Camelbak, totem, playa, decom, default world, the gate, family camp,
Sprinter, art car, PLUR, Wharf Rats, moop.*

What's missing is that the vocabulary is **shared across all 26 POVs**. Bear the
soundman, Spool the vendor, Sister Lou in recovery, and Beans the nineteen-year-old
cold-brew kid all reach for the same nouns. Real scene vernacular is stratified
by role and by era. Per-POV word banks worth installing:

- **Bear (soundman, 32 years, ch1/12):** FOH, monitor world, ring out, gain
  before feedback, the SPL guy, load-in, snake, "in the clear," patched, one-mix,
  Tie-dye-to-tech-bro. Talks in *signal path* metaphors. Should never say "vibe."
- **Patchbay (stage manager, ch2/15):** advance, day sheet, laminate tiers, the
  runner, hard out, "we're on a hard out at 2," pushed set, walk-up, AAA, "who
  cleared him." Talks in *access levels*. Every play she describes is a
  credentialing failure.
- **Spool (vendor row, ch8/19):** booth fee, load-in window, Square reader, the
  county, resale cert, wholesale vs. lot price, "heady but not headache,"
  consignment, the tray. Talks in *unit economics*.
- **Sister Lou (sober crew, ch16/20/21):** Wharf Rats, the meeting behind the
  medical tent, "twenty-three years," the yellow balloon, ICE contacts, the
  briefing, "government name." Talks in *protocol and count*.
- **Beans (19–23, cold brew, ch11/14/17/23/26):** the youngest voice and the one
  with the most current slang. Should be the only cast member using *rizz-era*
  phrasing, "he's yapping," "that's crazy," "not the—," "bro is not," "cooked,"
  "it's giving." His comedy is deadpan generational contrast with the old heads.
- **Mara (harm reduction, ch4/13/18/22):** integration, aftercare, set and
  setting, "come back to your body," electrolyte, the good tent, tapering,
  "what does your body remember." Talks in *nervous-system* terms.
- **Yo-Yo (camp kitchen → therapist, ch5/6):** portions, feeding forty, the
  dish gloves, prep list, "who's on dinner," and then clinical vocabulary
  underneath it — the joke is that she runs a group practice the same way she
  ran a kitchen.

**The single highest-leverage vernacular change:** stop letting every POV say
"the play," "the ask," "the invoice," and "the mechanism." Those are the *book's*
words — the narrator's. Give each speaker their own noun for the same thing.
Bear calls it *the gain structure*. Patchbay calls it *the advance*. Spool calls
it *the markup*. Sister Lou calls it *the count*. Beans just calls it *the move*.

### Where dark humor is available and currently unused

The book is at its funniest when it's specific about **objects and logistics**,
not when it's making jokes about people. The lot supplies infinite objects:

- The cooler that is nine-tenths melted ice and one Celsius nobody will claim.
- The generator that runs the whole camp's fairy lights and nothing that matters.
- The camp that has a label maker and no first aid kit.
- The four-dollar bag of ice at hour sixty.
- The porta at 3 a.m. as a moral test.
- The group chat named something unusable that is now the only emergency channel.
- The one person with a working phone charger and a personality about it.
- The tarp everyone agreed to bring and nobody brought.
- The wristband that survives three showers and one relationship.
- Somebody's dog that is definitely not a service animal wearing a service vest.

Three places these belong and currently aren't:
1. **Track intros** — currently the driest text in the chapter. One concrete
   object per intro, before the analysis starts.
2. **Tales From… The Save headers** — the Saves themselves are good; their
   headers are functional. A specimen-style detail line would frame each one.
3. **The Kandi/Fanny/Drill component headers**, which are pure label right now.

The rule that keeps this from turning into joke-stuffing: **the humor should
never be about the victim, and never about the scene being stupid.** The book's
comic target is always the *operator's props* — King Dave's bottle-cap crown, the
letterpress business card, the lanyard worn wrong, the 2014 slang. That's why
"Exits taxed" works and why a joke about somebody's bad trip wouldn't.
