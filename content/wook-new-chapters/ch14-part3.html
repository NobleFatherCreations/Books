HAVE YOU BEEN THE WOOK?

Mirror Check At One In The Morning

Almost nobody in this chapter is a Wook. That is the thing that makes it the hardest mirror in the book, because there is no villain to be relieved you are not.

The two security staff who walked up to Tee were doing their jobs and would fail no test anyone could design. The person at the fire who said “you got anything” loves him. The friends of nine years who do not know the protocol exists are good friends. Not one of them did a single thing that would survive being written up.

So the mirror here is not have you been the predator. It is: have you been one of the four seconds?

Have you decided, in under a second, which of two altered strangers was having a night and which was becoming a problem — and have you ever gone back and checked whether you sort evenly? Almost nobody has checked. The checking is the entire ask of this chapter.

Have you given safety advice that was autobiography? Counted the number of times you say just in front of an action that is only simple for you?

Have you handed the bag to whoever was closest?

Have you asked somebody if they were staff, and then, when it landed wrong, spent the next four minutes managing your own embarrassment in a way that made them comfort you?

Have you been in a room where a decision got made — a booking, a placement, a complaint, a hire — and not noticed who was not in it? That is the most consequential item on this list and it is entirely composed of an absence, which is the hardest thing in the world to see, because absences do not show up in a photo.

And the one for readers who are running the protocol, which is a different mirror and it belongs here too: have you decided, at a fire at 1:51 a.m., that it was cheaper to absorb it than to name it — and then been told later, by someone who loves you, that they had no idea? The absorbing is completely rational. It is also how nine years go by with people who would have carried it and were never asked.

There is no accusation in this section. There is one request, and it is small: go back and check the sort. Not your beliefs, which are probably fine. The four seconds, which nobody has ever audited, and which decide which door a stranger goes through on the worst night of their month.

THE WOOK DISCOG

Studio Debut — The Baby Wook learning the moves

Twenty. First year on a volunteer safety team, given a radio and about nine minutes of training. Learns within one weekend — from nobody, from no document, from watching two shifts — which people you walk over and check on and which people you call in about. Could not tell you the rule. Does not know there is a rule. Would sincerely and correctly say that he treats everyone the same, and he is not lying, because the sorting does not happen anywhere he can see it. The Studio Debut is the moment a reflex trained entirely outside this field walks through the gate wearing a hi-vis vest, and nobody notices, because nothing happened.

Live Album — The Seasoned Operator

Year eight. Shift lead. Makes the four-second call somewhere around two hundred times a weekend, and has never once been told that it is a call. Has genuinely de-escalated situations that would have gone badly. Has genuinely gotten people to the Zendo who needed the Zendo. Is, by every measure the event uses, very good at this job — and has never seen an aggregate of his own routing decisions, because no such aggregate exists at any festival in this country, because nobody has ever built one, because building one would require deciding to look.

Greatest Hits Compilation — The Institutional Scaled-Up Version

The aftermovie cut to be beautifully diverse, playing on a loop above an org chart that is not. The safety literature that says “alert a staff member” to forty thousand people and has never once marked the assumption inside it. The incident log showing fewer reports from the people who report least, cited in a meeting as evidence that things are fine. The scene built on Black American music whose booking room, production company, board and safety committee assembled themselves out of who was already there, in perfect good faith, every year, for fifty years. Nobody scaled this up on purpose. It scaled up because nothing was ever counted, and the thing about not counting is that it always produces the same answer.

Tales From The Walk — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Same festival. Year ten. Tee again.

The change happened in April, in a parking lot, over a text thread about who was bringing the canopy. A friend of six years named Hollis Yeun — twenty-nine, plays bass badly, has been at every one of these since 2021 — typed one sentence that had never been typed at Tee before by anyone in his life.

Hollis wrote: “random but — if something goes sideways out there, who do you want me to get? like which specific people.”

Tee looked at his phone for longer than the message warranted.

That is the watermelon moment in this Save, and it belongs to Hollis rather than to Tee, which is the whole design: the small internal note that a question exists which you have never asked, and that the not-asking has been costing somebody else something for six years.

The answer took four texts. Zendo, not security. The medic tent, not the gate. And a specific name: a woman named Dionne who runs volunteer coordination and is, per Tee, “the one who’s actually good.”

Hollis wrote them down. In his notes app. Under the heading GROVE, above a list of which friends do not eat dairy.

June. 12:52 a.m. Same walk. Different night — this time it is a guy from their camp named Petey, twenty-two, first festival, who has taken something at a dose that seemed reasonable to a twenty-two-year-old and has arrived somewhere he cannot presently get back from.

Tee has him by the elbow. Hollis is on the other side.

Two security staff angle toward them across the grass — the same angle, the same unhurried, entirely reasonable approach, the same two-and-a-half seconds in which eleven adjustments have historically been required.

Hollis steps forward.

He does not announce it. He does not look at Tee. There is no glance, no signal, no moment of meaningful eye contact, because the entire value of this is that it is not a moment. He just takes half a step diagonally so that he is the nearest body, and he starts talking before they arrive, which is the part that matters most:

Hollis says: “Hey! We’re taking him to the Zendo — he’s having a hard trip, we’ve got him. Can you radio ahead so they know we’re coming?”

Medical words. A named destination. And a task.

The staff member with the radio lifts it and calls it in, and in the four seconds it takes him to do that he stops being a person making an assessment and becomes a person on an errand, which is the one transformation this entire Track is built around.

The other one says, to Petey, kindly, “You’re good, brother, they’ve got you,” and they move on.

Eleven seconds, start to finish. Nothing happened.

And now the actual payoff, which is so small that it would be easy to skip past, and which is the reason this chapter exists:

During those eleven seconds, Tee was looking at Petey.

That is it. That is the whole Save. He had both hands on his friend and both eyes on his friend and the entirety of his attention on the twenty-two-year-old who was coming apart, and he noticed — because Petey’s jaw had gone rigid in a way that Blythe’s never did — that this was closer to a medical situation than a Zendo situation, and he said so, and they went to the medic tent instead, and the medic found a heart rate that mattered.

The protocol was off for eleven seconds and in those eleven seconds he caught something.

Nobody says anything about it afterward. Hollis does not mention it, that night or ever, because mentioning it would convert a piece of infrastructure into a favor, and a favor has to be acknowledged, and acknowledgment is work, and the work would land on Tee.

At 3:40 a.m. they are back at camp and Petey is fine and somebody at the fire says something about how long the line for water was.

Tee is not tired in the specific way. He notices that too.

THE FANNY PACK

Front pocket — chapter essentials

Chapter 14. The front pocket. The one with the map you made in daylight.

The Wellness-Threat Sort (medical door or security door): Identical symptoms, two doors, decided in four seconds by whoever is closest. Say the medical nouns first — Zendo, medic, harm reduction, hard trip. An empty category gets filled by whatever the observer brought with them.

The Credibility Gap (whose report moves): Document in real time, name witnesses immediately, bring a second body to report, ask for a case number and the intake person’s name. If you are the one who gets believed, your credibility is a resource, not a virtue — co-sign.

The Advice That Doesn’t Fit (unmarked assumptions): Three bins — transfers, needs modification, dangerous for me. Every removal needs a substitution. “Just call security” is not neutral information; it is autobiography.

The Pre-Loaded Tax (the protocol nobody sees): The cost is not the items, it is the running. Vigilance spends the same attention your friend needs. If you are not paying it, some of your friends are, and they will not raise it at a campfire.

The Only One In The Photo (welcome vs included): Compare the aftermovie to the org chart. Welcome is a feeling the event has toward you. Included is whether anything changes if you are not there.

The Handoff — for everyone else: take the interaction, carry the thing, drive at 1 a.m., say the medical words first, and never announce any of it.

Ask the one question in April: “If something goes sideways out there, who do you want me to get?”

And run the four-second audit on yourself, because that is the only part of this chapter that nobody else can do for you.

💬 POCKET SCRIPTS — SAY THESE

Routing the situation before anyone categorizes it: “We’re headed to the Zendo — she’s having a hard trip.”

Converting an observer into a participant: “Can you radio ahead so they know we’re coming?”

Attaching to a structure: “I’m with Camp Larkspur, we’re on the east side.”

Asking on the radio, if you carry one: “Welfare check, possible hard trip, requesting Zendo” — not “disturbance, requesting security.”

Turning a conversation into a record: “Can I get a case number and your name for my records?”

Co-signing: “I was there, I saw it, I want to be listed as a witness.”

The April question: “If something goes sideways out there, who do you want me to get? Like which specific people.”

Pricing the panel: “I’d love to — what’s the honorarium?”

THE SOUNDCHECK

The Thursday Map

On arrival day. In daylight. Before anybody needs anything. Twenty minutes, walking.

Stop 1 — Physically walk to the harm-reduction tent and the medic tent. Not on the app. Your feet. At 2 a.m. in the dark, on a bad night, “it’s near the second stage somewhere” is not a location and nobody in your group will be in a condition to read a map.

Stop 2 — Learn one staff member’s name. Ask at the volunteer tent who to ask for. Every event has someone who is genuinely good at this, and the information is freely given to anyone who asks on a Thursday and impossible to obtain at 1 a.m. Saturday.

Stop 3 — Agree the camp’s carrying policy out loud. Who holds what, and why, decided deliberately instead of handed to whoever is closest. Sixty seconds. It removes the single most dangerous improvisation of the weekend.

Stop 4 — Say the three bins out loud to each other. Each person names one piece of standard festival advice that does not work for them and what they want instead. This is not a heavy conversation and it should not be staged as one; it takes about ninety seconds and it is astonishing how much of it is news to everybody.

Stop 5 — Whoever is going to be the mouth, decide now. Not forever, not formally. Just: if staff approach the group tonight, who steps forward. Deciding it in daylight means nobody has to decide it in two and a half seconds.

Run this with the whole camp, including the people for whom none of it is necessary. Especially them. A camp where only some people do the Thursday Map has made the map into a marked activity, and a marked activity is one more thing to manage.

THE SUNRISE SET

Zero slang. Just the true thing.

Tee has been coming for ten years.

He is not writing an exposé and he is not leaving. He loves this event. He has met the best people of his life in that field and he has cried at a sunrise set with his hands in the air and he has driven eleven hours each way, ten times, on purpose, to do it again.

Both of these things are true at once and the whole chapter fails if you drop either one: this scene is one of the most genuinely welcoming places in American life, and it is not the same event for everyone who walks through the gate.

The gap is not made of villains. That is the finding, and it is harder than the alternative. There is no room where anyone decided this. It is made of four-second decisions by people who would be devastated to see them aggregated, safety advice written from a vantage point nobody marked, org charts that assembled themselves out of whoever was already standing there, and a thousand small warm interactions that are each individually fine.

Which means it does not get fixed by anyone becoming a better person. Most of the people in this chapter are already good people. It gets fixed the way every other problem in this book gets fixed: by boring acts, done deliberately, by people who love the thing enough to do the boring thing.

Say the medical word before anyone has to guess. Take the interaction. Carry the bag. Drive at one in the morning. Ask the one question in April and remember the answer for a decade. Count the room, not the photo. Put a name and a budget line where a statement used to be.

None of that is a sacrifice. All of it is free. Most of it takes under a minute.

And the return on it is not abstract, and it is not gratitude, and it is not anybody feeling good about themselves.

The return is eleven seconds in which a man gets to look at his friend.

That is what the protocol costs and that is what turning it off buys, and it turns out the person who can turn it off is almost never the person running it.

It is whoever is standing next to him, deciding, in the next half second, to be the one who talks.

THE KANDI TRADE

The vow

I will say the medical words first — Zendo, medic, hard trip — before anyone has to guess at a category.

I will ask the arriving person for a task, because a person with a task has stopped making an assessment.

I will audit my own four seconds, honestly, at least once, knowing nobody else can do it for me.

I will treat my credibility as a resource that belongs to whoever needs it, and I will co-sign.

I will stop saying “just” in front of advice that is only simple for me.

I will take the interaction, carry the thing, and drive at one in the morning, and I will not announce any of it.

I will ask, in April, in daylight: if something goes sideways out there, who do you want me to get.

And when I am in a room where something gets decided, I will look at who is not in it — because the photo has never once changed anybody’s weekend, and the room changes all of them.
