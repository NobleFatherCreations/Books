HAVE YOU BEEN THE WOOK?

Mirror Check At Six In The Morning

This chapter has two mirrors and the second one is the hardest thing in this book.

The first is for the friend.

Have you asked someone what they took, in the first ten minutes, before anything else? Have you asked what they were wearing, or why they went with him, or whether they said no, or whether they are sure — and felt, while asking, that you were helping them get clear?

Have you made it communal? Woken the camp, gathered the group, made it the thing everyone is dealing with, because that felt like love and like solidarity and like doing something?

Have you gone and found him? Confronted him, or organised a confrontation, or posted about him — before the person it happened to had decided anything at all?

Every item there is done out of love, by decent people, constantly, and every one of them takes options away from someone who has just had options taken away from them. The scene is magnificent at the first seventy-two hours and most of its instincts in the first ninety minutes are wrong.

The second mirror is the one this book has been building toward since Chapter 5, and it is not comfortable.

Some of the people reading this have been on the other side of an ambiguous night.

Not as a predator. Almost nobody reading this is Amaru from Chapter 17 or the men in the beige RV. The mirror is not are you a rapist, and framing it that way is how everybody gets to skip the question.

The actual question is smaller and much more answerable:

Was there a night when you proceeded because nothing stopped you?

Where the other person was very altered, and you were too, and they did not say no, and they did not fight, and you did not check — not because you decided not to, but because checking would have introduced friction into something that was going well, and the not-checking was not a decision you experienced yourself making.

Chapter 5 said it in six words and this chapter is what the other end of those six words looks like at 5:40 in the morning: a woman doing an inventory of objects and distances because her memory has a gap with things in it.

Tonic immobility means that they didn’t resist is not evidence of anything. Fragmentation means that she seemed into it may be your account of a person who cannot currently produce an account at all. And a yes at that level of altered is the same yes this book spent Chapter 17 taking apart, and it does not become a different kind of yes because the room had a string of lights instead of an altar.

If that landed somewhere, the response is not a confession and it is not a post. Those are for you, not for them.

It is: change what you do from here. Ask. Out loud. Every time. Accept that asking is unsexy and do it anyway. Stop at ambiguity rather than proceeding through it. Treat “they didn’t say no” as the absence of information rather than the presence of consent — because that is precisely what it is, and it always was, and the only thing that ever made it feel otherwise was that nothing stopped you.

THE WOOK DISCOG

Studio Debut — The Baby Wook learning the moves

Twenty-two, and it is not the assailant — it is the friend. Wakes the whole camp. Asks what she took within the first four minutes, because it seems relevant and because they are frightened. Goes to find him at nine in the morning with two other people. Posts a story at eleven with no name in it that everybody decodes by noon. Deletes the group thread later that week “so it isn’t out there.” Every single action taken out of genuine love, at speed, by a person who has never been taught a single thing about this and is doing the only things that feel like doing something. The Studio Debut is the reason Protocol 5 exists, and it is the most common failure in this entire chapter, and it is committed almost exclusively by people who would take a bullet for the person they are harming.

Live Album — The Seasoned Operator

The event, at year fifteen. Has a reporting email address on the website. Takes a statement at the info booth on Sunday from a volunteer with no training who is also managing a lost-and-found queue. Files an incident report that preserves nothing, collects nothing, and starts no clock. Removes him, sometimes, if there is enough. Sends a sincere and genuinely well-meant email in October. Has no relationship with the hospital ninety minutes away, does not know whether it has a trained examiner on weekends, has never printed the state coalition’s number on anything, and would be astonished to learn that the single highest-value thing it could do costs about forty dollars and is a laminated card at the medical tent.

Greatest Hits Compilation — The Institutional Scaled-Up Version

The rural hospital ninety minutes from three major festivals with no SANE-certified examiner on weekend shifts. The neighbouring county where the nearest trained examiner is fifty minutes further and nobody at the first desk knows it. The kits sitting untested in storage, in numbers that have been the subject of multi-year audits in multiple states. The prevention literature — thousands of pages of it, at every festival in the country, all of it about the before, almost none of it about the after. And a scene that will mobilise a hundred people to search a field for a missing friend, brilliantly, at 2 a.m., and has never once put the number for the state sexual assault coalition on the back of a wristband, where it would cost nothing and be there at 5:40 in the morning when the phone is at nine percent.

Tales From The Drive — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Protocols live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Rory Fenn is twenty-five and this happened to him, and the first thing that has to be said about this Save is that a substantial number of people reading this chapter have quietly assumed it did not apply to them, and Rory is the reason it is here.

Men are assaulted at festivals. So are nonbinary people. The percentage who ever tell anyone is lower, the percentage who ever reach a hospital is much lower, and the number of pages written for them in this scene’s entire fifty-year safety literature is approximately zero. Every protocol in this chapter applies unchanged.

Sunday, 7:10 a.m. Rory is sitting on the bumper of a friend’s car in the overflow lot with a hoodie on over nothing and his hands between his knees, and the loop he is running is Frankie’s loop with two extra items in it: this doesn’t happen to guys and I’m bigger than him.

His friend is a woman named Sunny, thirty, who has been coming to this event for six years and who is the person in their camp who reads things.

The watermelon moment is hers, not his, and it is very small: she notices that he has not said a single complete sentence in twenty minutes, and that he keeps starting them.

She does not ask what happened.

Sunny says: “I’ve got you. You don’t have to tell me anything. I’m going to make one phone call and then I’m going to tell you what the options are, and you don’t have to pick any of them.”

Then she does the step that the cold open of this chapter did not do, and it is the entire difference between Frankie’s morning and Rory’s: she calls before she drives.

Six minutes and forty seconds on the hotline, standing by the driver’s side door, with Rory in earshot on the bumper, which she checks is okay first.

What she learns in six minutes and forty seconds:

The county hospital fourteen miles away does not have a trained examiner on Sundays. The one in the next county over does, forty minutes in the other direction, and they have a nurse named Pet who works summer weekends specifically. A non-report exam is available in this state and the kit is stored under a number. Being intoxicated does not affect eligibility for anything. If there is a possibility he was given something, the toxicology window is the tightest thing on the board and they should go now rather than after breakfast.

She also learns the thing that ends up mattering most, and it is a sentence rather than a fact: the person on the phone says, twice, that he does not have to decide anything today.

Rory hears her say it back to him and something in his shoulders changes, and he says the first complete sentence in half an hour, which is: “Okay.”

They leave at 7:31. It is forty-one minutes. Sunny does not put music on.

At the hospital he says, at the desk, the exact sentence this chapter told him he was allowed to say, and which he had rehearsed twice in the car:

Rory says: “I don’t know what happened to me. I think I might have been drugged. I’d like to talk to someone about a non-report exam.”

Nobody blinks. That is the thing he will tell people afterward, over and over, for years — that he braced for the whole building to react and the building simply did its job. A urine sample in the first fifteen minutes, because of the clock. The exam, unhurried, with everything explained first. The medication offered, including the seventy-two-hour course, which he takes. The kit sealed, labelled with a number, and stored.

Clothes in paper bags that Sunny got from a gas station on the way, because she had read this chapter on a Tuesday in April and had remembered one thing from it, which was paper, not plastic.

They are out by 12:40. He sleeps in the car for the first hour of the drive back and then eats an enormous amount of fast food, which is not a detail anybody puts in these stories and is what actually happens.

Here is how this Save ends, and it is important that it ends this way:

Rory has not reported. It is eleven months later and he has not reported, and he may never, and he has not decided, and he thinks about it maybe twice a month.

The kit is in storage under a number. The state holds it. He knows the number.

That is a complete success. That is what this chapter is for. Not a conviction, not a confrontation, not closure, not a post — an option, still open, eleven months later, belonging entirely to him, because two people spent five hours on a Sunday keeping a door from closing on a decision he was not being asked to make that week.

And a phone call in a parking lot that took six minutes and forty seconds.

THE FANNY PACK

Front pocket — chapter essentials

Chapter 20. The front pocket. The one you hope stays closed.

The decision and the clock are two different things. Act on the clock, defer the decision. You can take every time-sensitive step today while remaining completely undecided about everything that matters.

The exam is not the report. In most US jurisdictions you can have a full forensic exam without making a police report, stored under a number instead of your name, for years. Almost nobody knows this and it is the single most consequential fact in this chapter.

Call before you drive. 800-656-4673. Four to seven minutes. They will tell you which facility actually has a trained examiner today, which is frequently not the closest one.

The tightest clock is toxicology — hours, not days. If there is any chance you were given something, the urine sample is the most time-sensitive thing on the board. Say specifically that you may have been drugged, so the right panel gets run.

Seventy-two hours for HIV post-exposure prophylaxis. Three to five days for emergency contraception. Commonly up to five days for forensic collection.

You are allowed to have showered. Everybody showers. Evidence is recovered after showers routinely. Go anyway.

Being high does not disqualify you — not medically, not legally. Incapacity is the basis of the offence, not a defence against it. Tell the clinicians what you took; it changes your care and the panel they run.

Paper bags, not plastic. Screenshot everything and email it to yourself. Write the timeline badly, now — fragments are the expected account, not a weak one.

The festival’s incident report preserves nothing. Do it as well, not instead.

If you are the friend: three sentences, then drive, then be quiet. Do not investigate, do not confront, do not post, do not take over the decisions. Make the phone call they cannot make. Then set a reminder for week four.

The test: is there any chance that in three months I will wish I had kept my options open? If the answer contains maybe in any form, the clock applies to you today and the decision does not.

“I don’t know what happened to me” is a complete and serious sentence to walk into a hospital with.

💬 POCKET SCRIPTS — SAY THESE

The three sentences, if you are the friend: “I’ve got you.” / “You don’t have to tell me anything.” / “I’ll drive you, you don’t have to decide anything when we get there, and we can turn around at any point and I will not be weird about it.”

On the hotline, from the parking lot: “I’m at a festival near [town]. Which hospital near here has a SANE or forensic examiner on shift today, and is a non-report exam available in this state?”

At the hospital desk: “I don’t know what happened to me. I think I might have been drugged. I’d like to talk to someone about a non-report exam.”

Protecting the toxicology window: “I may have been given something — can we do a urine sample now, before anything else?”

Declining the police, without declining the care: “I’m not ready to talk to law enforcement. I’d like the exam collected and stored as a non-report.”

Telling festival staff, in addition: “I want to make a report to the event about a person here. I understand this isn’t a police report and I’m handling that separately.”

Asking for privacy on site: “Is there somewhere with a door? The medical tent is a tent.”

What a friend says instead of a question: “I believe you.” Then nothing.

Week four, unprompted: “Thinking about you. No reply needed. I’m around Thursday if you want to do nothing in the same room.”

THE SOUNDCHECK

The Card In The Phone

Before the event. Four minutes, and you will almost certainly never use it, and it costs nothing to carry.

Everything in this chapter depends on information that is impossible to obtain at 5:40 in the morning with a phone at nine percent and no signal. So obtain it in April.

Step 1 — Put the hotline in your phone as a contact. In the US: 800-656-4673. Name the contact something you will find while distressed. Not “SA hotline.” Something plain, at the top of your favourites.

Step 2 — Look up the state coalition for wherever the festival physically is — not where you live, where the event is — and save that number too. They know which hospitals have examiners and what the non-report rules are in that state. Every state has one and they are free.

Step 3 — Screenshot all of it. Screenshots work with no signal. A saved webpage does not. This is the entire reason this step exists.

Step 4 — Find the nearest hospital with a forensic examiner, once, and write down the town. Just the town and the drive time. Four minutes of work that saves two hours on the worst morning of somebody’s year.

Step 5 — Agree the three sentences with your camp, out loud, in daylight. Not a heavy conversation — ninety seconds, at the same time as the Chapter 14 Thursday Map. “If anyone in this camp has a bad night, the three sentences are: I’ve got you, you don’t have to tell me anything, I’ll drive you and you don’t have to decide anything.” Say it once. Everyone will remember it.

Step 6 — Decide now who has a functioning car and full gas on Sunday morning. There is always one. Knowing which one is a four-second question in April and an hour-long problem at 6:31 a.m.

And if you run a festival: the whole of this drill is a laminated card at the medical tent and the harm-reduction tent with four lines on it — the hotline, the state coalition, the nearest facility with an examiner, and the sentence the exam is not the report. It costs about forty dollars. Nobody has done it. You could do it this year.

THE SUNRISE SET

Zero slang. Just the true thing.

This scene is extraordinary at some things and this is not one of them, and the gap is not made of indifference.

Forty thousand people will search a field at 2 a.m. for a stranger’s missing friend. A camp will carry a person to medical on a tarp. Somebody will sit with you for six hours in a canvas tent and hold your hands and never once look at their phone. The generosity in this culture is not performative and it is not shallow — it is one of the most reliable things in American life.

And almost none of it is pointed at the morning after, because the morning after is quiet, and it happens in a tent, and it does not look like an emergency, and the person it is happening to is doing an inventory of objects and distances and has not asked anyone for anything.

So the fifty years of safety material are about the before. Test your substances. Watch your drink. Buddy system. Every bit of it good, all of it necessary, none of it written for 5:40 a.m.

Here is the whole of what this chapter wanted to say.

You do not have to know what it was.

You do not have to decide anything this weekend, or this month, or this year.

You do not have to be certain, or consistent, or able to tell it in order. Fragments are what this looks like from inside. They always have been.

The only thing worth doing in the next twelve hours is keeping a few doors from quietly closing while you are not able to think about doors — and every single one of those doors can be held open without you committing to walking through any of them.

A phone call from a parking lot. A urine sample. A drive to the right hospital instead of the near one. A sealed kit with a number on it in a storage room in a county you will never visit again.

None of that is a decision. All of it is a door.

And in three years, if you want it, the door is still there — and if you never want it, then you spent one bad Sunday in a car with somebody who loved you and did not ask you a single question, and that is not a loss.

If it already happened and the doors are closed, that is not a failure either. Nothing in this chapter is a verdict on anybody who did not have it in time. Most people did not have it in time. That is the entire reason it is here now.

You were not stupid. You were not careless. You were not asking for it, and you were not too messed up to be believed, and you did not consent by failing to fight, because that is not what fighting is and it never was.

Something was done to you.

The rest — what you call it, what you do about it, who you tell, whether you ever think about it again — is yours, and it is yours on your own schedule, and it is the one thing in all of this that nobody gets to take.

THE KANDI TRADE

The vow

I will put the hotline and the state coalition in my phone before the event, and screenshot them, because screenshots work without signal.

I will call before I drive, so that we go to the hospital that actually has someone.

I will remember that the exam is not the report, and that a kit can be stored under a number for years while somebody decides.

I will not ask what they took, what they wore, or whether they are sure.

I will say the three sentences, and then I will drive, and then I will be quiet.

I will not confront him, gather a group, or post — because my rage is mine and their options are theirs.

I will set the reminder for week four, and I will send the text that asks for no reply.

And I will stop treating “they didn’t say no” as consent, because it was never anything but the absence of information — and the only thing that ever made it feel like more was that nothing stopped me.
