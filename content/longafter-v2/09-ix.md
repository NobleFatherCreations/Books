=== the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you
title: The friends who didn't believe you
question: My friends don't believe me about the abuse. What now?
seo: When Friends Don't Believe You About Abuse
short: When friends don't believe you about abuse, it can hurt as much as the abuse itself. Disbelief usually comes from ordinary human reasons: discomfort, loyalty, one side of the story, abuse that didn't look like they expected. Understanding why doesn't mean you have to excuse it or keep them close.
---
You told them. You braced yourself, and you told them. And they said, "Are you sure?" Or, "But he's always been lovely to me." Or they said nothing, and things were never quite the same.

## Why don't my friends believe me?
Usually for reasons that are about them, not your credibility:

- **They don't want it to be true** about someone they know or like.
- **It didn't look like abuse to them.** Coercive control often doesn't involve bruises. Patterns are harder to see than incidents.
- **They've heard the other version.** See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).
- **They're close to your ex,** through work, family or history.
- **It touches something in their own life** they don't want to look at.
- **They don't know what to do,** so they do nothing.

The Decoder's page on [silent coalitions](/playbook/silent-coalitions) describes how groups go quiet around abuse.

> Understanding why someone didn't believe you is one thing. Deciding whether to keep them close is another. You can do the first without the second.

## Does their disbelief mean I'm wrong?
No. Disbelief is common, especially with abuse that wasn't physical. It says more about how hard abuse is to see from the outside than about what happened. If you're doubting yourself because of it, read [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## Should I try to convince them?
Usually not. Arguing for your own experience is exhausting, and it rarely works. If you want to try once, keep it short:

> **Say:** "I know this is hard to hear, and I know you like them. I'm not asking you to take sides. I'm asking you to believe that I'm telling you the truth about my life."

Then let it rest.

## What do I do about the friendship?
It's your choice, made friend by friend:

- **Some are worth one honest conversation,** after some time has passed.
- **Some may come round** as they see more, or see the other person more clearly.
- **Some aren't worth the energy now,** or ever.

You don't need to justify any of these choices to anyone. [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends) covers people who get pulled into carrying messages.

## Where do I find people who do believe me?
- domestic abuse services and support groups, where people get it without explanation
- online communities for survivors (choose moderated ones)
- a therapist. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- new friends. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).
- and look after the ones who did believe you: [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* describes how a social circle gets turned against someone in [The Social Siege](/feminine/the-social-siege#core-concept).

## Why disbelief happens
It's rarely because your story doesn't hold together. More often:

- **The charm.** They only ever saw the person at their best: funny, generous, helpful.
- **The hidden pattern.** Control happens in private. Friends saw pieces, never the whole.
- **Their own fear.** Believing you means accepting that someone they like could do this, or that it could happen to them.
- **The story got there first.** They may have heard a version that made you the problem.
- **Discomfort.** Taking a side is awkward. Disbelief is easier than conflict.

Understanding why doesn't make it hurt less. It can help you stop asking what you did wrong.

## If they come round later
Sometimes a friend sees something years later and comes back: "I'm sorry. I see it now." You can decide what you want. Some people welcome them back fully. Some accept the apology but keep more distance.

> **Say:** "Thank you for saying that. It meant a lot. I need a bit of time to think about where we go from here."

## Protecting the children from disbelieving adults
If relatives or family friends don't believe you, they may say things in front of the children. Ask them directly not to discuss the separation or the other parent around the children. If they can't, keep visits short and supervised, or pause them for a while.

## When disbelief comes from professionals
Being disbelieved by a police officer, social worker or judge can feel like being abused again. Write down what was said, ask for decisions in writing, and ask about reviews or complaints. Find one professional who does believe you, often a domestic abuse advocate, and let them speak with the others.

## When an old friend didn't believe
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Rachel told her oldest friend what her husband had done, the friend said, "But he's always been so lovely to me." Rachel felt the floor go. She stopped confiding in her, and found a support group where people nodded before she'd finished a sentence. Two years later, the friend called. She'd seen him shout at his new partner in a restaurant. "I'm so sorry," she said.

Rachel took the apology. She also kept the group.

## Find one person who believes you
If the people closest to you don't believe you yet, find one person who does, a helpline, an advocate, a group, and lean there for now.

## Sources
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.
- Ullman, S. E. (2010). *Talking About Sexual Assault: Society's Response to Survivors.* American Psychological Association (on negative reactions to disclosure).
- Sylaska, K. M., and Edwards, K. M. (2014). Disclosure of intimate partner violence to informal social support network members: A review of the literature. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*, 15(1), 3–21.

=== the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired
title: The friends who did, and are tired
question: My friends have supported me through this. How do I not wear them out?
seo: Supporting a Friend After Abuse: When Your Supporters Get Tired
short: Supporting a friend after abuse takes a lot, and the friends who believed you are human too. Compassion fatigue is real, and it doesn't mean they've stopped caring. Spreading support across several people, noticing what they give, and keeping some of the friendship about other things helps it last.
---
They believed you straight away. They showed up, took the calls, sat with you at 2am. They've been steady for months, maybe years. And lately you've noticed them pull back a little, or look tired when you start talking about it.

## Why are my friends getting tired?
Because supporting someone through abuse and its aftermath is hard work. Hearing about it, worrying, wanting to fix it and not being able to: all of that takes a toll. There's a name for it, compassion fatigue, and it happens to people who love you and mean every word of their support.

The Decoder describes the deliberate version, where an abuser wears out your supporters on purpose, in [support system exhaustion](/playbook/support-system-exhaustion). Ongoing post-separation abuse can do this even without anyone planning it.

> Needing ongoing support isn't shameful. Neither is your friend needing a rest from carrying part of it.

## How do I keep the support going?
- **Spread it.** Don't rely on one person for everything. Add a support group, a domestic abuse service, new friends ([Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch)), a therapist. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- **Notice what they give.** A thank-you, a message on a good day, remembering their birthday.
- **Ask before offloading.** "Have you got space for a hard one tonight, or shall we just watch something?"
- **Keep other things in the friendship.** Shared jokes, walks, films, their life. Ask how they are and listen to the answer.
- **Give back when you can,** even in small ways.

## What if a friend says they need a break?
It hurts, and it's honest. Thank them. Let them know you understand. Lean more on your other supports for a while. Often the friendship comes back stronger for it.

## What if I'm the friend reading this?
If you're supporting someone, [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one) is written for you. Look after yourself too. You can't pour from an empty cup, and a rested friend helps more than an exhausted one.


## How do I ask for help without overloading one person?
Ask for something clear and small: "Could you sit with me while I read the court letter on Thursday?" A clear ask is easier to say yes to than "I need you."

## How do I tell if I'm leaning too hard?
Signs include slower replies, changes of subject, or tension when you call. Ask directly:

> **Say:** "Am I leaning on you too much? Tell me honestly. I'd rather know."

Most people appreciate the question.

## What can I give back?
Ask about their life, and listen. Remember the dates that matter to them. Help with something practical when you can. Small things keep a friendship two-sided.

## A support map
Instead of leaning on one person for everything, spread it out. Write a list:

- **Practical help:** who can give a lift, mind the children, help with forms?
- **Listening:** who can hear the hard stuff?
- **Fun and distraction:** who makes you laugh and doesn't need to talk about it?
- **Expert help:** domestic abuse service, lawyer, doctor, therapist.
- **Emergencies:** who would you call at 2am?

Some people appear in more than one place. No one should have to be everything.

## Asking clearly
Friends often want to help but don't know how. A clear request is easier to say yes to:

> **Say:** "Could you come with me to the court hearing on the 12th? You don't have to do anything; I just don't want to be alone."

> **Say:** "I'd love an evening where we don't talk about any of this. Can we watch a film on Friday?"

## What friends say helps them help
People supporting someone through abuse often say they find it easier when:

- they're told what's going on, briefly, without every detail every day
- they're asked for clear, concrete things
- they're allowed to say no sometimes
- the friendship still has other parts: their life, their news, shared fun
- they're thanked, even simply

## Support that takes the load off friends
Domestic abuse helplines, support groups, advocates, counsellors and online communities can carry some of what friends can't. Using them isn't replacing your friends. It means your friends can stay friends, not full-time crisis workers.

## Fill in the map
Write your support map today: names next to practical help, listening, fun, expert help and emergencies. If one name appears everywhere, ask someone new for one small thing this week.

## Sources
- Figley, C. R. (Ed.) (1995). *Compassion Fatigue: Coping with Secondary Traumatic Stress Disorder in Those Who Treat the Traumatized.* Brunner/Mazel.
- Coker, A. L., et al. (2002). Social support protects against the negative effects of partner violence on mental health. *Journal of Women's Health & Gender-Based Medicine*, 11(5), 465–476.

=== your-parents
title: Your parents
question: My parents don't support me after the divorce. Why, and what do I do?
seo: When Your Parents Don't Support You After Divorce or Abuse
short: When parents don't support you after a divorce or abusive relationship, the reasons range from shock and old loyalties to their own history. For some people, a parent's behaviour is part of why the relationship felt normal in the first place. You decide how close to stay, and you don't need their approval to heal.
---
Some parents are immediately on your side. Some struggle to understand. Some side with your ex, or tell you to go back. And for some people, a parent was part of the pattern that made the relationship feel familiar.

## Why don't my parents support me?
- **Shock, or disbelief,** for the same reasons as friends. See [The friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you).
- **They like your ex,** or the life you had.
- **Their values,** about marriage, divorce, family or faith.
- **Embarrassment** about what people will think.
- **Their own history.** A parent who lived through something similar, or did something similar, may not be able to look at it.
- **Grandchildren.** They may fear losing contact with the children.

## What if my parent was part of the pattern?
Sometimes a parent's control, criticism or volatility is part of why the relationship felt normal. If you grew up having to manage someone's moods, a partner who needed managing could feel like home.

That's real, and worth looking at, with a therapist if you can. It doesn't rule out a good relationship with your parent now. It may mean that relationship needs its own honest look. *The Repair*, in this library, covers what gets passed down through families, from the other side.

The Decoder's page on [golden child elevation](/playbook/golden-child-elevation) describes families where one child is favoured and another blamed, a pattern that can shape how you're treated now.

## What do I do?
No single script works. Choose for each parent:

- **A direct, calm conversation,** once you're steady enough:

> **Say:** "I need you to know what was happening in my marriage. I'm not asking you to hate them. I'm asking you to support me and not pass things on."

- **Boundaries,** as with anyone who isn't helping: less detail, shorter visits, topics that are off limits.
- **Distance,** if they're passing information to your ex or putting you at risk. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## What about my children and their grandparents?
Children often benefit from loving grandparents. If your parents are safe for the children but not on your side, you may be able to keep that relationship with clear limits: no talk about the other parent, and nothing passed on. If they're not safe, put the children first.

## What if I left my parents, not a partner?
Then much of this whole book applies to you. See [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

Leaving controlling parents brings its own guilt, often wrapped in duty and love. Relatives may press you to "make peace." The patterns in this book still apply: contact through other people, a story told about you, hoovering on birthdays and holidays. You can choose how much contact to have, if any, and change it over time. Written contact, limits on topics and support from people outside the family all help.

## What if my parents still see my ex?
Some parents keep a relationship with an ex, especially for grandchildren. You can ask them not to pass on news about you, and not to discuss the separation with your children. If they can't keep to that, give them less information.

## What if my parent was abusive too?
Then you may be dealing with two patterns at once. Much of this book applies to leaving a controlling family. See [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one), and think about support from a therapist who understands both.

## Parents who blame you
Some parents say, "What did you do?" or "Why didn't you just leave earlier?" or "You'll never find anyone better." These comments often come from shock, fear or their own beliefs about marriage.

> **Say:** "I need you to understand that I didn't cause this. If you can't support me right now, I need you to at least not blame me."

You can give them information, like [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one). You can't make them understand.

## Parents who want to "talk to them"
A parent may want to call the person you left, to "sort it out" or tell them off. It usually backfires, and it can raise the risk to you.

> **Say:** "Please don't contact them. It will make things harder and less safe for me. The most helpful thing is to be here for me and the children."

## Cultural and faith expectations
In some families and communities, divorce or separation is seen as shameful, or as something to avoid at almost any cost. Parents may feel caught between you and their community.

You can respect where they come from and still protect yourself. Some find it helps to involve a respected faith leader or community elder who understands abuse. Specialist services exist for many communities and can support you and, sometimes, your family.

## Setting limits with love
You can love your parents and still set limits:

- topics that are off limits
- not passing information to the person you left
- not criticising you in front of the children
- how often and where you see them

Say it calmly, once, and then act on it if the limits aren't respected.

## Rebuilding with a parent later
Relationships with parents sometimes repair after the worst is over. Some parents eventually say sorry, or show it through their actions. Go at a pace that feels safe, and keep the limits that matter until trust is rebuilt.

## How Tunde set a limit with his mother
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Tunde's mother kept in touch with his ex, and passed on comments about his parenting. He told her calmly: "I love you, and I'm not going to hear what she says about me. If it comes up, I'll end the call." The next time it came up, he did, gently. She was upset. The time after, she stopped herself mid-sentence.

Their relationship didn't become perfect. It became one he could stay in.

## One limit, said once
Choose the one limit with your parents that would make the most difference. Say it calmly, once, then act on it.

## Sources
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.
- Ehrensaft, M. K., et al. (2003). Intergenerational transmission of partner violence: A 20-year prospective study. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 71(4), 741–753.

=== telling-someone-new
title: Telling someone new
question: When should I tell a new partner, or a new friend, about the abuse?
seo: When to Tell a New Partner About Past Abuse
short: When to tell a new partner about abuse depends on how serious the relationship is and what feels right to you. Telling everything too early can overwhelm a new connection; never telling can leave you carrying it alone. Share what fits the relationship, at your own pace.
---
At some point you'll meet someone new: a friend, a colleague, a partner. And you'll wonder how much to tell them about what happened.

## When should I tell a new partner about the abuse?
There's no rule. Two things to weigh:

**Telling everything very early** can overwhelm a new connection before it has its own footing.

**Never telling** can mean carrying it alone, and can leave a serious partner deciding things without facts that affect them. That matters most if your ex may contact them, or if there are court cases. See [The new partner](ch:the-new-partner).

> Neither is a moral failing. Both have costs, and most people end up somewhere in between.

## How much should I share?
Match it to the relationship:

- **A new friend or colleague:** usually very little at first. "I went through a hard separation" is complete.
- **Someone you're dating:** enough to explain anything that might affect them. "My ex didn't take the break-up well and sometimes contacts people in my life."
- **A serious partner:** more, at a pace that feels right. They deserve to understand what you're carrying and what might come up.

You don't have to share details you're not ready to share, ever.

## How do I say it?
> **Say:** "There's something about my past I'd like you to know. My last relationship was abusive, and I'm still dealing with some of the after-effects. I don't need you to fix anything. I just wanted you to know."

Watch how they respond. Curiosity, patience and respect for your pace are good signs. Pressure for details, pity, or making it about them are worth noticing.

## What if I get it wrong?
There's no wrong way that can't be mended. You can only learn what works for you. If you shared too much too soon, that's fine. If you waited too long, you can still share. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice) and [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not).


## What about a new doctor or therapist?
Tell them enough to help you: that you've left an abusive relationship, and anything that affects your health. Ask them to note any safety concerns on your record.

## What about my employer?
You don't have to tell them anything beyond what you need. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

You might tell your employer if you need time off for court, a change of hours, or protection at work, such as reception not confirming you work there. You can keep it brief: "I've left an abusive relationship and there are some safety and legal matters. I may need some flexibility." Ask whether there's a domestic abuse policy, and who will know. You don't have to share details or names.

## What about the children's new school or club?
Only what affects the child's safety: who may collect them, any court order, and who to call. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).

## What if they react badly?
Some people respond with disbelief, questions that feel like an interrogation, or advice you didn't ask for. You can close the conversation:

> **Say:** "I'd rather not go into details. I just wanted you to know."

Their reaction tells you how much to share in future.

## What if my ex tells them first?
It happens. A calm, short account from you, with no attack on your ex, is usually believed over time. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).

## A one-line, a short and a full version
It helps to have three versions ready:

- **One line:** "I left a difficult relationship a while ago."
- **Short:** "My last relationship was controlling and abusive. I'm doing much better, but it still affects some things, like how I react to sudden changes of plan."
- **Full:** what happened, what it left behind, what helps. For people you trust deeply.

You choose which version each person gets. You can always share more later.

## Telling a friend
New friends don't need the full story at once. Many people share a little at a time, watching how it's received. A good friend will respond with care and curiosity, not judgment or pressure for details.

## Telling a landlord or neighbour
If there's a risk the person you left might turn up, a neighbour or landlord may need to know something.

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship. If you ever see this person around the building, please don't let them in or give them any information, and let me know."

You don't owe them more than that.

## After you've told: what to watch for
Notice how people respond over the following weeks:

- **Good signs:** they believe you, ask what helps, respect your privacy, and treat you the same as before.
- **Warning signs:** they tell others without asking, make it about themselves, use it against you in an argument, or minimise it.

Their response tells you a lot about whether they're someone to keep close.

## Practise your short version
Write your one-line and short versions now, and say them out loud a few times. When the moment comes, you'll have words ready instead of having to find them under pressure.

## Sources
- Sylaska, K. M., and Edwards, K. M. (2014). Disclosure of intimate partner violence to informal social support network members. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*, 15(1), 3–21.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.

=== making-new-people-from-scratch
title: Making new people from scratch
question: How do I make friends after an abusive relationship isolated me?
seo: Making Friends After an Abusive Relationship
short: Making friends after an abusive relationship is a skill you can learn, not luck. Isolation is common in coercive relationships, so many people rebuild a social life from very little. Showing up to the same place again and again, and getting through the awkward early weeks, is how most adult friendships form.
---
Making friends after an abusive relationship can feel impossible at first. During the relationship, friends fell away. Maybe they were pushed away. Maybe you stopped going out because it caused trouble. Now you've left, and the phone is quiet.

## Why am I so lonely after leaving?
Because isolation is one of the most common features of coercive control. Cutting someone off from friends and family makes them easier to control. The Decoder describes it in [isolation](/playbook/isolation). Many people leave with a much smaller social world than they started with.

Loneliness after leaving shows what the relationship took from you. It says nothing about whether you're likeable.

## How do I make friends as an adult?
Research on adult friendship points to a few plain truths:

- **Repetition beats intensity.** Seeing the same people regularly does more than one great conversation. A weekly class, a club, a volunteering shift, a running group.
- **It takes time.** One study estimated that turning an acquaintance into a casual friend takes around 50 hours together, and a close friend more than 200.
- **The first few times feel awkward.** That's normal. It passes if you keep going.
- **Someone has to go first.** "Do you want to grab a coffee after this?" feels risky and usually lands well.

> This is hard, and it's possible, at any age and after any amount of isolation.

## Where do I start making friends?
- something you used to enjoy before the relationship. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- a survivors' support group, where you won't have to explain, unlike [the friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you)
- volunteering, which gives you a reason to show up and something to talk about
- keeping the friends you have, without wearing them out. See [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired).
- reconnecting with old friends. A short message is enough: "I've been through a lot and I've missed you. Fancy a catch-up?"

## What if I find it hard to trust new people?
That's common. Trust can be built slowly, in small steps. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

After abuse, wariness is a reasonable reaction, not a flaw. You don't have to decide all at once whether someone is safe. Share small things first and see what they do with them: do they remember, keep it private, respect a "no"? Spend time in groups before one-to-one. Let people earn more of you over weeks and months. If someone pushes for closeness fast, that's worth noticing.

## What if meeting people makes me anxious?
Start where there's an activity to focus on: a class, volunteering, a walking group. Listen more than you talk at first, and keep showing up. Anxiety usually eases with repetition.

## What about online groups?
Moderated survivor groups can help, especially late at night. Share as little personal detail as you can, and check privacy settings. Leave any group that feels like pressure or a pile-on.

## How do I tell a good new friend from someone who'll take advantage?
Use the same slow approach as in [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice). Good friends are steady, respect your "no," and don't rush closeness.

## What about old friends I lost touch with?
Many will be glad to hear from you, especially if they wondered what happened. A short, honest message is enough:

> **Say:** "I was in a controlling relationship and I lost touch with a lot of people. I've missed you. Would you like to catch up?"

## A 12-week plan
Making friends takes repeated contact over time. A gentle plan:

- **Weeks 1 to 4:** pick one regular activity: a class, a club, a volunteering shift, a walking group. Go every week.
- **Weeks 5 to 8:** learn two or three names. Have a short conversation each time. Suggest a coffee with one person.
- **Weeks 9 to 12:** do something outside the group with someone you like. Keep going to the group.

By then, you may have one or two people who are starting to feel like friends. That's a real start.

## The first conversation
Small talk feels hard after years of being watched or criticised. Simple openers work:

- "How long have you been coming here?"
- "What got you into this?"
- "Have you tried the café next door?"

Ask questions and listen. Most people are glad to be asked about themselves.

## Keeping friendships going
New friendships fade without small, regular contact. Send a message after you meet. Suggest the next plan. Remember small details, like an exam they had or a pet that was ill, and ask about them. Friendships grow through ordinary moments repeated.

## Friendships with other survivors
Support groups often lead to friendships with people who get it without explanation. These can be deeply healing. Watch that the friendship has room for other things too, so it doesn't stay only about the abuse.

## Lonely without being alone
You can feel lonely in a crowd, especially at first. Loneliness after abuse is common, and it usually eases as connections grow. Meanwhile, small things help: a regular café where staff know your face, a phone call with a relative, a pet, a library, a class.

## How Nina built a circle from nothing
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Nina's ex had cut her off from everyone. After she left, she knew nobody in her new town. She joined a Saturday walking group and went every week, even when she didn't feel like it. By week six she knew three names. By week ten she had coffee with one of them. By the end of the year she had a small group that met on Thursdays.

None of it was dramatic. It was the same walk, every Saturday, until it became a life.

## Choose one regular thing
Pick one activity that happens every week, and go four times before deciding whether you like it.

## Sources
- Hall, J. A. (2019). How many hours does it take to make a friend? *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, 36(4), 1278–1296.
- Stark, E. (2007). *Coercive Control.* Oxford University Press (on isolation as a tactic).

=== sharing-a-town-a-workplace-or-a-community
title: When you share a town, a workplace or a community
question: What do I do when my ex is in the same friend group, workplace or church?
seo: Ex in the Same Friend Group, Workplace or Church
short: When your ex is in the same friend group, workplace, church or small town, you can't simply avoid them. Plan for run-ins. Decide which spaces to keep and which to give up for now. Ask for practical help at work or in the community, and keep a record if their behaviour crosses a line.
---
You left, but you still see them. At the school gate. In the office kitchen. Across the room at church. At every party your friends throw. In a small town, at the supermarket.

## What do I do if my ex is in the same friend group?
- **Decide what you'll do at events** before you go: arrive with a friend, have an exit plan, keep conversations short.
- **Don't ask friends to choose.** Some will, on their own.
- **Tell hosts if you need to,** quietly: "I'd rather not be seated near them."
- **Accept that the group may change.** Some people will drift. The Decoder's page on [silent coalitions](/playbook/silent-coalitions) describes how groups go quiet. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## What if we work together?
- Tell HR or a manager you trust, especially if their behaviour affects your work or safety.
- Ask for practical changes: different shifts, a different team, separate meetings.
- Keep work contact strictly professional and in writing.
- Keep a record of anything that crosses a line. Workplace harassment rules may apply.
- *At Will*, in this library, covers coercion and protection at work in depth.

## What about church or a faith community?
A congregation can be a lifeline or a pressure. Some communities support the person who left; others push reconciliation or side with the person who seems more established. If you're being pressured, you can step back, attend a different service, or find a different community. *The Sacred Divide*, in this library, looks at how control works inside religious institutions.

## What about a small town?
- Plan routes and times to reduce run-ins: a different supermarket, a different time.
- Prepare a short line for when you meet: "Hi," and keep walking.
- Remember that gossip spreads both ways. Steady, calm behaviour over time is your best answer. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).
- If the run-ins feel deliberate, or you feel unsafe, keep a record. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## Do I have to give things up?
Sometimes, for a while. That's a real loss. Choose the spaces that matter most to you and protect those. You may get others back later.


## What if they keep turning up where I am?
Once could be chance. A pattern isn't. Keep a record of each time, and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## What if mutual friends invite us both?
Ask the host who's coming. You can decline, go for a short time with a friend, or ask the host to choose a different day for you. You don't owe anyone a shared room with the person you left.

## Can I ask a group or club to act?
Many groups, teams and congregations have a code of conduct or a safeguarding lead. If their behaviour at shared events crosses a line, tell the organiser in writing, briefly, with dates.

## A run-in script
If you see them unexpectedly in a shop, at the school gate or in the street:

1. Keep your face neutral. Don't start a conversation.
2. If they speak, keep it short: "Hi." Or say nothing and move on.
3. Go somewhere with other people if you feel unsafe.
4. Afterwards, write it down if it was more than a passing moment.

Practising the script in your head makes it easier when it happens.

## Splitting spaces fairly
In a small community you may share gyms, cafés, parks and groups. Some people agree, directly or through friends, on who goes where and when. Others simply change their own routine. You don't have to give up everything, but you may choose to step back from some places for a while for peace.

## Online communities you share
Shared online groups, like school parent chats, local groups and hobby forums, can become another channel. Mute or leave groups where they're active, or ask the admin to remove posts that target you. Don't argue publicly.

## Moving away: deciding
Some people decide to move to get away from a small community. It's a big decision, especially with children and court orders. Weigh up safety, support, schools, work and what the courts will allow. Talk to a lawyer before moving with children. See [Moving, passports and travel with children](ch:moving-passports-and-travel-with-children).

## Practise the run-in
Picture the place you're most likely to meet them. Walk through the run-in script in your head: neutral face, short or no reply, move away, write it down. Practising once makes the real moment less of a shock.
