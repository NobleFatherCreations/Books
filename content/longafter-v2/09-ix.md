=== the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you
title: The friends who didn't believe you
question: My friends don't believe me about the abuse. What now?
seo: When Friends Don't Believe You About Abuse
short: When friends don't believe you about abuse, it can hurt as much as the abuse itself. Disbelief usually comes from ordinary human reasons: discomfort, loyalty, one side of the story, abuse that didn't look like they expected. Understanding why doesn't mean you have to excuse it or keep them close.
---
What do you do when you finally tell a friend, and they don't believe you?

You braced yourself and told them. And they said, "Are you sure?" Or the classic: "But he's always been so nice to me." Or they changed the subject, and things were never quite the same.

## Why don't my friends believe me?
Usually for reasons that have more to do with them than with you:

- **They don't want it to be true** about someone they know or like.
- **It didn't look like abuse to them.** Coercive control often leaves no bruises. A pattern is much harder to spot than a single incident.
- **They've heard the other version first.** See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).
- **They're close to your ex,** through work, family or years of history.
- **It touches something in their own life** they'd rather not look at.
- **They don't know what to do,** so they do nothing.

The Decoder has a page on how whole groups go quiet around abuse: [Silent Coalitions](/playbook/silent-coalitions).

> Understanding why someone didn't believe you is one thing. Deciding whether to keep them close is another. You can do the first and skip the second.

## Does their disbelief mean I'm wrong?
No. Disbelief is common, especially when the abuse wasn't physical. It tells you how hard abuse is to see from the outside. It tells you nothing about what went on inside your home. If it's got you second-guessing your own memory, read [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## Should I try to convince them?
Usually not. Arguing the case for your own life is exhausting, and it rarely changes a mind. You're not a barrister, and they're not a jury. If you want to try once, keep it short:

> **Say:** "I know this is hard to hear, and I know you like them. I'm not asking you to take sides. I'm asking you to believe that I'm telling you the truth about my life."

Then let it rest. What they do with it is up to them.

## What do I do about the friendship?
You decide, one friend at a time:

- **Some are worth one honest conversation,** once a bit of time has passed.
- **Some may come round** as they see more, or see your ex more clearly.
- **Some aren't worth the energy right now.** Maybe ever.

You don't owe anyone a reason for any of these. If some friends have started carrying messages back and forth, [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends) covers that.

## Where do I find people who do believe me?
- domestic abuse services and support groups, where people get it without the explaining
- online communities for survivors (pick moderated ones)
- a therapist. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- new friends. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).
- the friends who did believe you, who deserve some looking after. See [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine*, another book in this library, shows how a social circle gets turned against someone in [The Social Siege](/feminine/the-social-siege#core-concept).

## Why disbelief happens
Ah, "but he's always been so nice to me." Of course he was. Plenty of people who control a partner at home are lovely at barbecues. Your friends saw the charm, the one who helped them move house. The control happened in private. They saw pieces. You lived the whole thing.

Believing you also costs them something. It means accepting that someone they like could do this, or that it could happen to them. Disbelief is the easy way out of awkward.

Knowing all this won't make it hurt less. It might help you stop asking what you did wrong.

## If they come round later
Sometimes a friend sees something, months or years later, and comes back: "I'm sorry. I see it now." What you do with that is your call. Some people welcome them back fully. Some accept the apology and keep a bit more distance.

> **Say:** "Thank you for saying that. It means a lot. I need some time to think about where we go from here."

## Protecting the children from disbelieving adults
Relatives or family friends who don't believe you may say things in front of the children. Ask them plainly not to discuss the separation or the other parent around the kids. If they can't manage that, keep visits short and supervised, or pause them for a while.

If anyone calls that you being difficult, the request speaks for itself. You asked them to keep adult conflict away from the children.

## When disbelief comes from professionals
Being disbelieved by a police officer, social worker or judge can feel like being abused all over again. Write down what was said, and when. Ask for decisions in writing, and ask how to get them reviewed. Those routes depend on where you live, so start with [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body).

Then find one professional who does believe you, often a domestic abuse advocate, and let them talk to the others.

::: scene
## When an old friend didn't believe
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Rachel told her oldest friend what her husband had done, the friend said, "But he's always been so lovely to me." Rachel felt the floor go. She stopped confiding in her, and found a support group where people nodded before she'd finished a sentence.

Two years later, the friend called. She'd seen him shout at his new partner in a restaurant. "I'm so sorry," she said. Rachel took the apology. She also kept the group.
:::

::: action
## Find one person who believes you
Pick one: a helpline, an advocate, a support group, or the one friend who did believe you. Save their number in your phone now. If it's a person, send one line: "Can we talk this week?"
:::

And the friends who did believe you? They need a bit of looking after too. [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired) is next.

=== the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired
title: The friends who did, and are tired
question: My friends have supported me through this. How do I not wear them out?
seo: Supporting a Friend After Abuse: When Your Supporters Get Tired
short: Supporting a friend after abuse takes a lot, and the friends who believed you are human too. Compassion fatigue is real, and it doesn't mean they've stopped caring. Spreading support across several people, noticing what they give, and keeping some of the friendship about other things helps it last.
---
They believed you straight away. They showed up, took the calls, sat up with you at 2am. They've been steady for months, maybe years. And lately you've noticed slower replies, or a tired look when you start on the latest thing your ex did.

## Why are my friends getting tired?
Because supporting someone through abuse and its aftermath is hard work. They hear it, they worry, they want to fix it and they can't. That wears people down. It has a name, compassion fatigue, and it happens to people who love you and mean every word of their support.

The Decoder describes the deliberate version, where an abuser wears out your supporters on purpose, in [Support System Exhaustion](/playbook/support-system-exhaustion). Ongoing post-separation abuse can do the same thing with nobody planning it, simply by never stopping.

> Needing support for a long time is nothing to be ashamed of. Neither is your friend needing a rest from carrying part of it.

## How do I keep the support going?
- **Spread it.** One person can't be everything. Add a support group, a domestic abuse service, new friends ([Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch)) and a therapist. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- **Notice what they give.** A thank-you. A message on a good day. Their birthday, remembered.
- **Ask before offloading.** "Have you got space for a hard one tonight, or shall we just watch something?"
- **Keep the rest of the friendship alive.** Shared jokes, walks, bad films, their life. Ask how they are, and listen to the answer.
- **Give back when you can,** even in small ways.

## What if a friend says they need a break?
It stings. It's also honest, which is what you want from a friend. Thank them. Tell them you understand. Lean on your other supports for a while. Often the friendship comes back stronger for it.

## What if I'm the friend reading this?
Hello, and thank you. [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one) is written for you. Look after yourself too. A rested friend helps more than an exhausted one, and you're allowed an evening off.

## How do I ask for help without overloading one person?
Ask for something clear and small. "Could you sit with me while I open the court letter on Thursday?" is easy to say yes to. "I need you" is a lot for anyone to hold.

## How do I tell if I'm leaning too hard?
Slower replies, quick changes of subject, a bit of tension when you call. If you notice those, ask:

> **Say:** "Am I leaning on you too much? Tell me honestly. I'd rather know."

Most people appreciate being asked.

## What can I give back?
Ask about their life and listen, even when it's twenty minutes on their boiler. Remember the dates that matter to them. Help with something practical when you can. Small things keep a friendship running both ways.

## A support map
Rather than leaning on one person for everything, spread it out. Write a list:

- **Practical help:** who can give a lift, mind the children, help with forms?
- **Listening:** who can hear the hard stuff?
- **Fun and distraction:** who makes you laugh and doesn't need to talk about it?
- **Expert help:** domestic abuse service, lawyer, doctor, therapist.
- **Emergencies:** who would you call at 2am?

Some names will turn up in more than one place. That's fine. Nobody should be under every heading.

## Asking clearly
Friends often want to help and don't know how. A clear request gives them something to do:

> **Say:** "Could you come with me to the court hearing on the 12th? You don't have to do anything; I just don't want to be alone."

> **Say:** "I'd love an evening where we don't talk about any of this. Can we watch a film on Friday?"

## What friends say helps them help
People supporting someone through abuse often say it's easier when:

- they're told what's going on, briefly, without every detail every day
- they're asked for clear, concrete things
- they're allowed to say no sometimes
- the friendship still has other parts: their life, their news, shared fun
- they're thanked, even simply

## Support that takes the load off friends
Domestic abuse helplines, support groups, advocates, counsellors and online communities can carry some of what friends can't. Using them takes nothing away from your friends. It means they get to stay friends, instead of becoming full-time crisis workers.

::: action
## Fill in the map
Open your notes app and type the five headings: practical help, listening, fun, expert help, emergencies. Put a name under each. If one name sits under all five, text someone else today and ask for one small thing this week.
:::

Friends are one circle. Family can be a harder one. When you're ready, [Your parents](ch:your-parents) is next.

## Sources
- Figley, C. R. (Ed.) (1995). *Compassion Fatigue: Coping with Secondary Traumatic Stress Disorder in Those Who Treat the Traumatized.* Brunner/Mazel.
- Coker, A. L., et al. (2002). Social support protects against the negative effects of partner violence on mental health. *Journal of Women's Health & Gender-Based Medicine*, 11(5), 465–476.

=== your-parents
title: Your parents
question: My parents don't support me after the divorce. Why, and what do I do?
seo: When Your Parents Don't Support You After Divorce or Abuse
short: When parents don't support you after a divorce or abusive relationship, the reasons range from shock and old loyalties to their own history. For some people, a parent's behaviour is part of why the relationship felt normal in the first place. You decide how close to stay, and you don't need their approval to heal.
---
Some parents are on your side from the first phone call. Some struggle to understand. Some side with your ex, or tell you to go back and try harder. And for some people, a parent was part of the pattern that made the relationship feel familiar in the first place.

## Why don't my parents support me?
- **Shock or disbelief,** for the same reasons friends struggle. See [The friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you).
- **They liked your ex,** or the life you had. Some were very attached to the in-law who fixed their printer.
- **Their values** about marriage, divorce, family or faith.
- **Embarrassment** about what people will think.
- **Their own history.** A parent who lived through something similar, or did something similar, may not be able to look at it.
- **The grandchildren.** They may be scared of losing contact with them.

## What if my parent was part of the pattern?
Sometimes a parent's control, criticism or volatility is part of why the relationship felt normal. If you grew up managing someone's moods, a partner who needed managing could feel like home.

That's real, and worth looking at, with a therapist if you can. It doesn't rule out a good relationship with your parent now. It may mean that relationship needs its own honest look. *The Repair*, another book in this library, covers what gets passed down through families, from the other side.

The Decoder's page on [Golden Child Elevation](/playbook/golden-child-elevation) describes families where one child is favoured and another blamed. That old pattern can shape how you're treated now.

## What do I do?
No single script works on every parent. Choose for each one:

- **A direct, calm conversation,** once you're steady enough:

> **Say:** "I need you to know what was happening in my marriage. I'm not asking you to hate them. I'm asking you to support me and not pass things on."

- **Boundaries,** as with anyone who isn't helping: less detail, shorter visits, topics that are off limits.
- **Distance,** if they're passing information to your ex or putting you at risk. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## What about my children and their grandparents?
Loving grandparents are often good for children. If your parents are safe for the kids but not on your side, you may be able to keep that relationship going with clear limits: no talk about the other parent, and nothing passed on. If they're not safe, the children come first.

If anyone paints those limits as you cutting the children off, the answer is simple: the visits happen, with conditions that keep the children out of the middle.

## What if I left my parents, not a partner?
Then much of this book is about you too. See [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

Leaving controlling parents brings its own guilt, usually wrapped up in duty and love. Relatives may press you to "make peace." The patterns in this book still show up: contact through other people, a story told about you, hoovering on birthdays and holidays.

You choose how much contact to have, if any, and you can change it over time. Written contact, limits on topics, and support from people outside the family all help.

## What if my parents still see my ex?
Some parents keep a relationship with an ex, often for the grandchildren's sake. You can ask them not to pass on news about you, and not to discuss the separation with your children. If they can't stick to that, give them less to pass on.

::: bridge slowtake:index
And if it's the other way round, and you're watching an older parent being controlled by a partner, a carer or another relative, often through their money, [The Slow Take](/slowtake) is another book in this library. It's written for the adult child in that spot.
:::

## What if my parent was abusive too?
Then you may be dealing with two patterns at once. Much of this book applies to leaving a controlling family. See [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one), and look for a therapist who understands both.

## Parents who blame you
"What did you do?" "Why didn't you just leave earlier?" "You'll never find anyone better." These often come from shock, fear or their own beliefs about marriage. They still land hard.

> **Say:** "I need you to understand that I didn't cause this. If you can't support me right now, I need you to at least not blame me."

You can hand them information, like [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one). You can't make them read it, and you can't make them understand.

## Parents who want to "talk to them"
Ah, the dad who wants to "have a word." It comes from love. It usually backfires, and it can make things less safe for you.

> **Say:** "Please don't contact them. It will make things harder and less safe for me. The most helpful thing is to be here for me and the children."

## Cultural and faith expectations
In some families and communities, divorce or separation is seen as shameful, or as something to avoid at almost any cost. Parents may feel caught between you and their community.

You can respect where they come from and still protect yourself. Some people find it helps to involve a respected faith leader or community elder who understands abuse. Specialist services exist for many communities and can support you and, sometimes, your family.

## Setting limits with love
You can love your parents and still set limits:

- topics that are off limits
- no passing information to the person you left
- no criticising you in front of the children
- how often and where you see them

Say it calmly, once. If the limit isn't respected, act on it. No speech needed.

## Rebuilding with a parent later
Relationships with parents sometimes mend once the worst is over. Some parents say sorry, in words or actions. Go at a pace that feels safe, and keep the limits that matter until the trust is back.

::: scene
## How Tunde set a limit with his mother
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Tunde's mother kept in touch with his ex, and passed on comments about his parenting. He told her calmly: "I love you, and I'm not going to hear what she says about me. If it comes up, I'll end the call." The next time it came up, he did, gently. She was upset. The time after, she stopped herself mid-sentence.

Their relationship wasn't perfect after that. But it was one he could stay in.
:::

::: action
## One limit, said once
Write down the one limit with your parents that would make the most difference, as a single sentence you could say out loud. Read it out once now. Next time it's needed, say it calmly, once, then act on it.
:::

Sooner or later there'll be new people in your life too, and the question of what to tell them. [Telling someone new](ch:telling-someone-new) helps with that.

- Ehrensaft, M. K., et al. (2003). Intergenerational transmission of partner violence: A 20-year prospective study. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 71(4), 741–753.

=== telling-someone-new
title: Telling someone new
question: When should I tell a new partner, or a new friend, about the abuse?
seo: When to Tell a New Partner About Past Abuse
short: When to tell a new partner about abuse depends on how serious the relationship is and what feels right to you. Telling everything too early can overwhelm a new connection; never telling can leave you carrying it alone. Share what fits the relationship, at your own pace.
---
How much do you tell the nice new person at work? Or the nice new person you've been on three dates with?

At some point you'll meet someone new: a friend, a colleague, a partner. And somewhere around the third coffee you'll start wondering how much of the story they get.

## When should I tell a new partner about the abuse?
There's no rule, whatever the internet says. Two things to weigh:

**Telling everything very early** can swamp a new connection before it has found its feet.

**Never telling** can mean carrying it alone. It can also leave a serious partner deciding things without facts that affect them. That matters most if your ex may contact them, or if there are court cases. See [The new partner](ch:the-new-partner).

> Neither is a moral failing. Both have costs, and most people end up somewhere in between.

## How much should I share?
Match it to the relationship:

- **A new friend or colleague:** usually very little at first. "I went through a hard separation" is a complete sentence.
- **Someone you're dating:** enough to explain anything that might affect them. "My ex didn't take the break-up well and sometimes contacts people in my life."
- **A serious partner:** more, at a pace that feels right. They deserve to understand what you're carrying and what might come up.

You never have to share details you're not ready to share.

## How do I say it?
> **Say:** "There's something about my past I'd like you to know. My last relationship was abusive, and I'm still dealing with some of the after-effects. I don't need you to fix anything. I just wanted you to know."

Then watch how they respond. Curiosity, patience and respect for your pace are good signs. Pushing for details, pity, or turning it into a story about them are worth noticing.

## What if I get it wrong?
Nothing you get wrong here is beyond mending. If you shared too much too soon, that's fine. If you waited too long, you can still share. You're learning what works for you. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice) and [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not).

## What about a new doctor or therapist?
Tell them enough to help you: that you've left an abusive relationship, and anything that affects your health. Ask them to note any safety concerns on your record.

## What about my employer?
You don't have to tell them anything beyond what you need to. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

You might tell them if you need time off for court, a change of hours, or protection at work, such as reception not confirming that you work there. Keep it brief: "I've left an abusive relationship and there are some safety and legal matters. I may need some flexibility."

Ask whether there's a domestic abuse policy, and who will know. You don't have to share details or names.

## What about the children's new school or club?
Only what affects the child's safety: who may collect them, any court order, and who to call. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).

## What if they react badly?
Some people respond with disbelief, questions that feel like an interrogation, or advice you didn't ask for. You can close the conversation:

> **Say:** "I'd rather not go into details. I just wanted you to know."

Their reaction tells you how much to share in future.

## What if my ex tells them first?
It happens. Some exes get in early with a version of their own. A calm, short account from you, with no attack on your ex, is usually believed over time. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).

## A one-line, a short and a full version
It helps to have three versions ready:

- **One line:** "I left a difficult relationship a while ago."
- **Short:** "My last relationship was controlling and abusive. I'm doing much better, but it still affects some things, like how I react to sudden changes of plan."
- **Full:** what happened, what it left behind, what helps. For people you trust deeply.

You choose which version each person gets. You can always share more later.

## Telling a friend
New friends don't need the full story at once. Plenty of people share a little at a time and see how it's received. A good friend responds with care and curiosity. Judgment, or pressure for details, tells you something too.

## Telling a landlord or neighbour
If there's a chance the person you left might turn up, a neighbour or landlord may need to know something.

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship. If you ever see this person around the building, please don't let them in or give them any information, and let me know."

You don't owe them more than that.

## After you've told: what to watch for
Notice how people respond over the following weeks:

- **Good signs:** they believe you, ask what helps, respect your privacy, and treat you the same as before.
- **Warning signs:** they tell others without asking, make it about themselves, use it against you in an argument, or minimise it.

How they handle it tells you a lot about whether they're someone to keep close.

::: action
## Practise your short version
Open your notes app and type your one-line and short versions. Say them out loud twice, in the car or the kitchen or wherever nobody's listening. When the moment comes, the words will already be there.
:::

If the bigger problem is that there's nobody new to tell yet, [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch) is next.

=== making-new-people-from-scratch
title: Making new people from scratch
question: How do I make friends after an abusive relationship isolated me?
seo: Making Friends After an Abusive Relationship
short: Making friends after an abusive relationship is a skill you can learn. Isolation is common in coercive relationships, so many people rebuild a social life from very little. Showing up to the same place again and again, and getting through the awkward early weeks, is how most adult friendships form.
---
People say you can't make real friends as an adult. You can. It just takes more effort than it did at school, when sharing a glue stick was enough to make a best friend.

After an abusive relationship it can feel harder still. Friends fell away while you were together. Maybe they were pushed away. Maybe going out caused so much trouble that you stopped. Now you've left, and the phone is very quiet.

## Why am I so lonely after leaving?
Because isolation is one of the most common features of coercive control. Someone cut off from friends and family is easier to control. The Decoder covers it in [Isolation](/playbook/isolation). Many people leave with a much smaller world than they went in with.

Your loneliness shows what the relationship took from you. It says nothing about whether you're likeable.

## How do I make friends as an adult?
Research on adult friendship points to a few plain truths:

- **Repetition beats intensity.** Seeing the same people regularly does more than one great conversation. A weekly class, a club, a volunteering shift, a running group.
- **It takes time.** One study estimated that turning an acquaintance into a casual friend takes around 50 hours together, and a close friend more than 200. That's a lot of coffees, so spread them out.
- **The first few times feel awkward.** For everyone, not just you. It passes if you keep going.
- **Someone has to go first.** "Do you want to grab a coffee after this?" feels risky to say, and it usually lands well.

> This is hard, and it's possible, at any age and after any amount of isolation.

## Where do I start making friends?
- something you used to enjoy before the relationship. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- a survivors' support group, where you won't have to explain yourself, unlike with [the friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you)
- volunteering, which gives you a reason to show up and something to talk about
- keeping the friends you have, without wearing them out. See [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired).
- reconnecting with old friends. A short message is enough: "I've been through a lot and I've missed you. Fancy a catch-up?"

## What if I find it hard to trust new people?
That's common, and trust can be built slowly. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

After abuse, wariness makes sense. You don't have to decide all at once whether someone is safe. Share small things first and see what they do with them. Do they remember? Keep it private? Respect a "no"?

Spend time in groups before one-to-one. Let people earn more of you over weeks and months. If someone pushes for closeness fast, notice that.

## What if meeting people makes me anxious?
Start where there's an activity to focus on: a class, volunteering, a walking group. Listen more than you talk at first, and keep showing up. Anxiety usually eases with repetition.

## What about online groups?
Moderated survivor groups can help, especially late at night. Share as little personal detail as you can, and check your privacy settings. Leave any group that starts to feel like pressure or a pile-on.

## How do I tell a good new friend from someone who'll take advantage?
Use the same slow approach as in [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice). Good friends are steady, respect your "no," and don't rush closeness.

## What about old friends I lost touch with?
Many will be glad to hear from you, especially if they wondered what happened. A short, honest message is enough:

> **Say:** "I was in a controlling relationship and I lost touch with a lot of people. I've missed you. Would you like to catch up?"

## A 12-week plan
Friendship takes repeated contact over time. A gentle plan:

- **Weeks 1 to 4:** pick one regular activity: a class, a club, a volunteering shift, a walking group. Go every week.
- **Weeks 5 to 8:** learn two or three names. Have a short conversation each time. Suggest a coffee with one person.
- **Weeks 9 to 12:** do something outside the group with someone you like. Keep going to the group.

By then you may have one or two people who are starting to feel like friends. That's a real start.

## The first conversation
Small talk feels hard after years of being watched or criticised. Simple openers work fine:

- "How long have you been coming here?"
- "What got you into this?"
- "Have you tried the café next door?"

Ask questions and listen. Most people are glad to be asked about themselves.

## Keeping friendships going
New friendships fade without small, regular contact. Send a message after you meet. Suggest the next plan. Remember small details, like an exam they had or a pet that was ill, and ask about them. Friendships grow from ordinary moments, repeated.

## Friendships with other survivors
Support groups often lead to friendships with people who get it without explanation. These can mean a great deal. Make sure the friendship has room for other things too, so it isn't only ever about the abuse.

## Lonely without being alone
You can feel lonely in a crowd, especially at first. Loneliness after abuse is common, and it usually eases as connections grow. In the meantime, small things help: a regular café where the staff know your face, a phone call with a relative, a pet, a library, a class.

## How Nina built a circle from nothing
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Nina's ex had cut her off from everyone. After she left, she knew nobody in her new town. She joined a Saturday walking group and went every week, even when she didn't feel like it.

By week six she knew three names, and by week ten she was having coffee with one of them. A year in, she had a small group that met on Thursdays. None of it was dramatic. She just kept turning up on Saturdays.
:::

::: action
## Choose one regular thing
Search for one activity near you that happens every week: a class, a club, a walking group, a volunteering shift. Put the next date in your calendar now, and promise yourself four goes before you decide whether you like it.
:::

And if the person you keep bumping into is your ex, at the shops, at work or at church, [When you share a town, a workplace or a community](ch:sharing-a-town-a-workplace-or-a-community) is next.

You left, but you still see them. At the school gate. In the office kitchen. Across the room at church. At every party your friends throw. In a small town, it's the cereal aisle at 9am on a Saturday. Naturally, that's the day you popped out in the jumper you sleep in.

## What do I do if my ex is in the same friend group?
- **Decide what you'll do at events** before you go: arrive with a friend, have an exit plan, keep conversations short.
- **Don't ask friends to choose.** Some will choose anyway.
- **Tell hosts if you need to,** quietly: "I'd rather not be seated near them."
- **Accept that the group may change.** Some people will drift. The Decoder's page on [Silent Coalitions](/playbook/silent-coalitions) describes how groups go quiet. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## What if we work together?
- Tell HR or a manager you trust, especially if their behaviour affects your work or your safety.
- Ask for practical changes: different shifts, a different team, separate meetings.
- Keep work contact strictly professional and in writing.
- Keep a record of anything that crosses a line. Workplace harassment rules may apply.

## What about church or a faith community?
A congregation can be a lifeline or a pressure cooker. Some communities support the person who left. Others push reconciliation, or side with whoever seems more established.

If you're being pressured, you can step back, go to a different service, or find a different community. *The Sacred Divide*, another book in this library, looks at how control works inside religious institutions.

## What about a small town?
- Plan routes and times to cut down run-ins: a different supermarket, a different time.
- Have a short line ready for when you meet: "Hi," and keep walking.
- Gossip travels in every direction. Steady, calm behaviour over time is your best answer. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).
- If the run-ins feel deliberate, or you feel unsafe, keep a record. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## Do I have to give things up?
Sometimes, for a while. Pick the spaces that matter most to you and protect those. You may get the others back later.

## What if they keep turning up where I am?
Once could be chance. A pattern is something else. Keep a record of each time, and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## What if mutual friends invite us both?
Ask the host who's coming. You can decline, go for a short time with a friend, or ask the host to see you on a different day. You don't owe anyone an evening in the same room as the person you left.

## Can I ask a group or club to act?
Many groups, teams and congregations have a code of conduct or a safeguarding lead. If their behaviour at shared events crosses a line, tell the organiser in writing, briefly, with dates.

## A run-in script
If you see them unexpectedly in a shop, at the school gate or in the street:

1. Keep your face neutral. Don't start a conversation.
2. If they speak, keep it short: "Hi." Or say nothing and move on.
3. Go somewhere with other people if you feel unsafe.
4. Afterwards, write it down if it was more than a passing moment.

If the children are with you, a short "Hi" is usually a better choice than silence. It's hard for anyone to call a polite hello hostile.

## Splitting spaces fairly
In a small community you may share gyms, cafés, parks and groups. Some people agree, directly or through friends, on who goes where and when. Others change their own routine. You don't have to give up everything, but you might step back from some places for a while, for peace.

## Online communities you share
Shared online groups, like school parent chats, local groups and hobby forums, can become another channel. Mute or leave groups where they're active, or ask the admin to remove posts that target you. Don't argue in public, however good the reply you've drafted.

## Moving away: deciding
Some people move to get away from a small community. It's a big decision, especially with children and court orders. Weigh up safety, support, schools, work and what the courts where you live will allow.

Talk to a lawyer first. [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) can point you to the right kind of help. See also [Moving, passports and travel with children](ch:moving-passports-and-travel-with-children).

## Practise the run-in
Think of the place you're most likely to see them. Type your run-in line into your phone notes, even if it's only "Hi." Under it, write one nearby spot with other people where you could step away to. Say the line out loud once.
:::
