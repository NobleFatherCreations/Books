=== if-you-go-back
title: If you go back
question: How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?
seo: How Many Times Does It Take to Leave an Abusive Relationship?
short: How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship? There's no single number, but many people leave and return at least once before they leave for good. Going back isn't a moral failure, and it doesn't rule out leaving for good later.
---
> Nothing in this chapter judges you for going back.

You left. Then you went back. Maybe more than once. Maybe you're back now, or thinking about it, and you're ashamed to be reading a book about leaving.

You're welcome here.

## How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?
You'll often see the figure "seven times." It's widely repeated, but research doesn't settle on a single number. Different studies of different groups find different patterns. What's consistent is that leaving more than once is common. For many people, leaving is a process that takes several attempts, not a single act.

## Why do people go back?
- **Hope** that things have changed, or will.
- **The trauma bond,** which pulls hardest in the early weeks. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).
- **Hoovering** that arrives at a low moment: the apology, the promise, the crisis. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) and the Decoder's [hoovering](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait).
- **Practical pressure:** money, housing, the children, nowhere to go. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Isolation and loneliness** after leaving.
- **Fear** of what they'll do if you don't.
- **Missing them,** in a way that briefly outweighs what you know. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).

## What tends to be different about the leaving that lasts?
Often, though not always:

- **Clearer information,** sometimes gained by going back and seeing that nothing had changed.
- **Stronger supports already in place:** a record, legal knowledge, money set aside, people who know the truth.
- **A safety plan.** See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- **Less contact afterwards,** so the bond has room to loosen. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).

> If you've gone back before, it doesn't disqualify you from leaving for good. It doesn't make this attempt less likely to hold.

## What if I'm back now?
- Keep your record going, quietly. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- Keep one or two people close who know the truth.
- Keep some money and copies of documents somewhere safe.
- Watch for the warning signs. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).
- Know that services will help you whether you're staying, leaving or unsure.

## What this chapter is not
It isn't encouragement to go back to "get more information." It's here so that anyone who has gone back doesn't carry extra shame on top of everything else.


## What if friends and family are angry that I went back?
They may be scared for you, or worn out. Tell one or two of them that you're safe for now and that you'd like them to stay in touch. If they can't, a domestic abuse service will stay with you whatever you decide.

## What if I leave again?
Many people do, and the second or third time is often better prepared. Use what you learned: your record, your documents, your safety plan, the people who know. See [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

## Staying safer while you're back
If you've gone back, you can still protect yourself:

- Keep a safety plan, and keep it somewhere they can't find. See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- Keep emergency numbers saved under plain names.
- Keep copies of documents and some money somewhere outside the home.
- Know the warning signs that mean you need help fast. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

None of this means you have to leave again. It means you're keeping your options open.

## Keeping your record and your exits
Keep writing down what happens, privately. If things get worse, a record will help, and it will help you see clearly. Keep one person who knows the truth. Keep your bank account, if you have one. Keep your exits: a place to go, a way to get there, a bag you could take.

## Signs it's getting worse
Watch for:

- more control than before: over money, phone, friends
- anger about the time you left, brought up again and again
- threats, or violence that's new or increasing
- isolation from the people who helped you last time

If you see these, talk to a domestic abuse service, even just to think it through.

## Keeping friends while you're back
Friends may pull away if you go back, out of frustration or fear. Try to keep at least one connection, even quietly. A domestic abuse helpline can be that connection if no one else is.

## The children while you're back
If you have children, keep their safety plan going too: a code word, a safe person at school, how to call for help. Notice how they're doing. If you're worried about them, a children's service or helpline can advise.

## Keep one door open
If you're back, keep one link to help: a saved helpline number, one friend who knows, a small amount of money somewhere safe. You don't have to decide anything else today.

- Anderson, D. K., and Saunders, D. G. (2003). Leaving an abusive partner: An empirical review of predictors, the process of leaving, and psychological well-being. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*, 4(2), 163–191.
- Dutton, D. G., and Painter, S. (1993). Emotional attachments in abusive relationships: A test of traumatic bonding theory. *Violence and Victims*, 8(2), 105–120.

=== helping-someone-in-year-one
title: Helping someone in year one
question: How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?
seo: How to Help a Friend Who Left an Abusive Relationship
short: To help a friend who left an abusive relationship: believe them, let their feelings be complicated, don't push a timeline, don't constantly attack their ex, and stay around for the long haul. What feels helpful and what helps aren't always the same thing.
---
This chapter is written to be forwarded, in full, to whoever is supporting someone in the first year after leaving. It's short on purpose.

## How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?
**Believe them, fully, the first time.** Not with careful questions that sound like you're checking their story. See [The friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you) for why this matters so much.

**Let their feelings be complicated.** They may grieve, miss the person, feel relief and sadness at once. None of this means they made a mistake, and none of it needs correcting. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).

**Don't push a timeline.** Questions about whether they're "over it yet," or about dating, land as pressure, however kind the intention.

**Don't keep attacking their ex,** even if you're furious on their behalf. It can make some people defend or minimise, because it's easier than managing your anger on top of their own.

**Help with the practical.** Meals, lifts to court, an hour with the children, sitting with them while they read messages.

**Learn the safety basics.** Don't share their location or news with mutual friends. Don't pass on messages from their ex. See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

> What feels helpful and what helps are sometimes different things. Believing them, being steady, and following their lead beats advice, deadlines and even righteous anger.

## What if they go back?
Stay in touch. Don't say "I told you so." People often leave more than once, and they're more likely to leave for good if they still have people to come back to. See [If you go back](ch:if-you-go-back).

## What if I'm worried about their safety?
Tell them, once, calmly. Share [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously). If you think they're in immediate danger, call emergency services. If they talk about harming themselves, see [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

## The single most useful thing you can do
Stay. Check in next month, and the month after, not just in the first hard week. If you support survivors for work, see [For professionals](ch:for-professionals). And look after yourself too. See [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired).


## What should I say, and what should I avoid?
Helpful:

- "I believe you."
- "That sounds exhausting."
- "What would help this week?"
- "You don't have to decide anything today."

Best avoided:

- "Why didn't you leave sooner?"
- "Just ignore them."
- "You need to move on."
- "At least you're out now."

## How can I help with the practical side?
Offer lifts to court or appointments, childcare, a meal, an hour sitting with them while they read messages, help finding a lawyer, or a safe place to keep documents and a ready bag. See [Find help](ch:find-help).

## What year one tends to look like
- **The first weeks:** shock, practical crises, safety. They need help with tasks.
- **Months 2 to 6:** court, money, children, exhaustion. Ups and downs. They may miss the person they left.
- **Months 6 to 12:** some steadiness, then anniversaries and setbacks. People around them often expect them to be "over it" by now.

Your support matters at every stage, and often most in the middle months, when others drift away.

## Practical help by stage
- **Early:** a bed, a lift, childcare, meals, help with forms, keeping documents safe.
- **Middle:** company at court or appointments, help finding lawyers and services, regular check-ins.
- **Later:** invitations to ordinary things, celebrating small wins, remembering the anniversaries.

## Helping with the children
Offering childcare during court dates or appointments is one of the most useful things you can do. Keep the children's routines familiar. Don't ask them about the other parent or the separation, and don't criticise the other parent in front of them.

## What not to do online
- Don't post about the abuse or the person they left.
- Don't tag them in posts or photos that show where they are.
- Don't confront the person they left on social media.
- Don't share their story without permission.

## Looking after yourself as the helper
Supporting someone through abuse is draining. Set limits on what you can give, and say so kindly. Talk to someone about how it's affecting you. Many domestic abuse helplines also support friends and family.

## What a good friend did
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Zoe's friend left her husband, Zoe didn't give advice. She drove her to court, minded the children during appointments and sent a message every few days that didn't ask for anything back. When her friend went back to him for a month, Zoe didn't say "I told you so." She said, "I'm still here." When her friend left again, Zoe was the first person she called.

Her friend said later: "You never made me choose between you and him. That's why I could choose me."

## One practical offer
Offer your friend one concrete thing this week: a lift, a meal, an hour of childcare, a sit with them while they read the messages.

- Coker, A. L., et al. (2002). Social support protects against the negative effects of partner violence on mental health. *Journal of Women's Health & Gender-Based Medicine*, 11(5), 465–476.
- Sylaska, K. M., and Edwards, K. M. (2014). Disclosure of intimate partner violence to informal social support network members. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*, 15(1), 3–21.

=== what-this-book-might-have-wrong
title: What this book might have wrong
question: How strong is the research behind this book on post-separation abuse?
seo: Post-Separation Abuse Research: What's Known and Unknown
short: Post-separation abuse research is growing but still young. Some of what this book says is well supported, some is disputed, and some is this book's own judgment. Here is which is which, published rather than buried.
---
This library holds itself to a simple standard: say what you know, what's disputed, and what's your own judgment.

## What is well supported?
- **Separation is a time of raised risk,** especially from very controlling partners. See [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first).
- **Abuse often continues after separation** through children, money, courts and other people.
- **Past strangulation is a strong warning sign** of later serious harm.
- **Stress and trauma affect sleep, memory and the nervous system.**
- **Children are affected by coercive control directly,** whether or not they see violence.
- **Many people leave more than once** before leaving for good.
- **Ongoing conflict between parents harms children.**

## What is disputed?
- **How well family courts recognise coercive control,** and how fast that's changing. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).
- **"Parental alienation":** how common it is, how to judge it, and whether claims of it are being used against parents who report abuse.
- **"Trauma bond"** as a concept: widely used and recognised, but not a formal diagnosis, and not tested the way a clinical condition would be.
- **Grey rock:** widely recommended by survivors and practitioners, with very little formal research.
- **Exact figures:** how common post-separation abuse is, how many attempts it takes to leave.

## What is this book's own judgment?
- The exact wording in [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use), drawn from practice, not controlled trials.
- The conversational guidance on children, parents and new partners.
- The working definition of recovery in [What "recovered" means](ch:what-recovered-actually-means).
- The order of steps in [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

## What is the biggest risk this book carries?
That the general patterns won't match your life. Laws vary by country and region, and people vary even more. If your experience differs from what's described here, it isn't less real. Trust your own situation over this book's generalisations wherever the two disagree.

## What would change this book's mind?
- If separation were shown not to raise risk compared with staying, the safety chapters would need revising.
- If hoovering were shown to be rare, the hoovering chapter would be overstating it.
- If parallel parenting or written-only contact were shown to harm children, those chapters would change.
- If the scripts in this book were shown to escalate conflict more than other approaches, they'd be rewritten.

## How is this book checked?
Every chapter lists its sources. Legal notes are dated and need checking before you rely on them. Corrections are welcome. The book's version history is kept on its main page.


## How do I report an error?
If you find something wrong, especially a phone number, a law or a source, please report it through the Noble Father Creations homepage. Corrections are made and dated in the updates on the book's main page.

## What kinds of sources this book uses
The book draws on several kinds of evidence:

- **Research studies,** listed by author and year, mostly from peer-reviewed journals.
- **Laws and official guidance,** from government and court websites.
- **Specialist organisations,** like national helplines and charities, for practical advice.
- **Clinical guidelines,** like NICE in the UK, for health and therapy.
- **Experience,** of people who've lived through it and those who support them, where research is thin.

Each chapter lists what it used.

## Where things change fastest
Laws, helpline numbers, benefits, court procedures and technology change often. Always check the current position where you live, especially for anything legal or financial. If you find something out of date, please report it.

## What the book leaves out
This book can't cover every country's law, every culture, every situation. It's written mainly with English-speaking countries in mind. It doesn't give detailed legal advice for your case, and it can't replace a lawyer, doctor or therapist who knows your situation.

## Reading research claims online
When you see a claim online, like "it takes seven attempts to leave" or "narcissists never change," ask:

- Who said it, and how do they know?
- Is there a study, and how many people were in it?
- Is it a single study, or what most research finds?
- Is someone selling something?

Healthy scepticism protects you from confident claims that aren't true.

## Tell us what we got wrong
If you find an error, an out-of-date number or something that doesn't fit your experience, please report it. Every correction makes the book better for the next reader.

=== the-long-after
title: The long after
question: What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years on?
seo: Life After Narcissistic Abuse: What Years Later Looks Like
short: Life after narcissistic abuse, years on, isn't a straight line, and it doesn't have to be. What's available is a life that isn't organised around what happened: relationships built on trust, tastes that are yours, anger that has mostly settled, and the capacity to choose.
---
This isn't a straight line, and it was never going to be. Some weeks feel like real progress. Some feel like every chapter of this book arriving at once, out of nowhere. Both are part of the same process. Neither one is the "real" direction.

## What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years later?
People who are years out describe things like this:

- **A life that isn't organised around what happened.** Days go by without thinking about them.
- **Relationships built on trust** that was given slowly and earned. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).
- **Tastes and habits that are simply yours,** no longer a recovery project. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- **Anger that has mostly settled,** with an occasional brief return that doesn't mean anything has gone wrong. See [Anger, arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).
- **A steadier body,** with fewer jolts and better sleep.
- **Clearer judgment** about people, and limits held without effort.
- **For some, contact that still exists** because of children, but takes up a small corner of life instead of the centre.

> Recovery leaves the past in place and gives you back your capacity to choose, to trust, to want things, and to build a life that isn't defined by what you left.

## What about the hard days, years on?
They still happen: an anniversary, a court letter, a child's question, seeing them in town. They tend to pass faster, and to knock you down less far. If one turns into more than a hard week, [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath) is still here.

## Where to go from here
- When a week goes badly: [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) explains what your body is doing.
- When the anger comes late: [Anger, arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).
- When they come back: [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).
- When you're helping someone else: [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one).
- When you just need the short version: [The card](ch:the-card).

## The close
Leaving was the middle, not the end. The first chapter said so, and everything since has been the part that comes after the door. This is the rest of it: unglamorous, uneven, and yours.


## What do people wish they'd known in year one?
- It doesn't go in a straight line, and that's normal.
- The hard days get shorter, even when they still come.
- The person you left doesn't get to define the rest of your life.
- You can build a good life alongside a difficult co-parent.
- Asking for help sooner would have made it lighter.
- Writing things down saved them more than once.

## Will I ever stop thinking about it?
For most people it becomes a smaller part of life: remembered, sometimes painful, no longer in charge. You may think about it on certain days for a long time. That doesn't mean it still runs your life.

## What people say, years on
People who left abusive relationships years ago often say:

- the person takes up far less space in their mind
- they still have hard days, but they recover faster
- they trust themselves more than they did before the relationship
- they're proud of leaving, even though it was the hardest thing they did
- their life is full of things that have nothing to do with the abuse

## The long tail
Some things can last for years: co-parenting, occasional court, anniversaries, a memory that surprises you. That's not failure. It's the long after. Most people find that the long tail becomes background, not foreground.

## What you might do with what you know
In time, you may find you know things that could help someone else: how to spot the patterns, what helped, what to avoid. You might share it with a friend, volunteer, or just be a calmer, wiser presence for someone in the thick of it. Or you might simply live your life. Both are good uses of what you've learned.

## A last word
You left. Then you kept going, through everything that came after. Whatever today looks like, that's worth remembering.

## A note to come back to
Write down today's date and one sentence about where you are. Put it somewhere you'll find it in a year. When you read it, you'll see how far the long after has carried you.
