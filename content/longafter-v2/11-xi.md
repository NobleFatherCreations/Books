=== if-you-go-back
title: If you go back
question: How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?
seo: How Many Times Does It Take to Leave an Abusive Relationship?
short: How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship? There's no single number, but many people leave and go back at least once before they leave for good. Going back isn't a moral failure, and it doesn't stop you leaving for good later.
---
> Nothing in this chapter judges you for going back.

You left. Then you went back. Maybe more than once. Maybe you're back right now, or thinking about it, and you feel a bit ashamed to be reading a book about leaving.

Pull up a chair. You're welcome here, exactly as you are.

## How many times does it take to leave an abusive relationship?
You've probably seen "seven times" quoted somewhere, usually with great confidence. It gets repeated a lot, but research doesn't settle on one number. Different studies of different groups find different patterns. The part they agree on is that leaving more than once is common. For lots of people, leaving is a process that takes a few goes, not one big moment.

## Why do people go back?
Usually for reasons that make complete sense from the inside:

- **Hope** that things have changed, or will.
- **The trauma bond,** which pulls hardest in the early weeks. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).
- **Hoovering** that turns up at your lowest moment: the apology, the promise, the sudden crisis. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) and the Decoder's [Hoovering / Relief Bait](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait) (getting pulled back in).
- **Practical pressure:** money, housing, the children, nowhere to go. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Loneliness** after leaving, especially if they'd already cut you off from people.
- **Fear** of what they'll do if you don't.
- **Missing them,** in a way that briefly outweighs everything you know. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).

## What tends to be different about the leaving that lasts?
Often, though not always:

- **Clearer information,** sometimes gained by going back and seeing nothing had changed.
- **Stronger supports already in place:** a record, some legal knowledge, money set aside, people who know the truth.
- **A safety plan.** See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- **Less contact afterwards,** so the bond has room to loosen. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).

> If you've gone back before, that doesn't rule you out of leaving for good. Many people leave more than once before it sticks.

## What if I'm back now?
Then here's how to keep your options open without making a fuss:

- Keep your record going. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- Keep one or two people close who know the truth.
- Keep some money and copies of your documents somewhere safe.
- Watch for the warning signs. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).
- Know that services will help you whether you're staying, leaving or not sure yet.
- Keep your exits: a place you could go, a way to get there, a bag you could grab.
- Save helpline numbers under plain names, like "Pizza".

None of this means you have to leave again. It means the door doesn't lock behind you.

## What this chapter is not
Nobody here is suggesting you go back to "get more information." This chapter exists so that if you have gone back, you don't carry a load of shame on top of everything else.

## What if friends and family are angry that I went back?
They may be scared for you, or just worn out. Tell one or two of them that you're safe for now and that you'd love them to stay in touch. If they can't manage it, a domestic abuse service will stay with you whatever you decide.

## What if I leave again?
Lots of people do, and the second or third time is often better prepared. Use what you learned last time: your record, your documents, your safety plan, the people who know. See [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

## Signs it's getting worse
Watch for:

- more control than before, over money, your phone or your friends
- anger about the time you left, brought up again and again
- threats, or violence that's new or getting worse
- being cut off from the people who helped you last time

If you notice these, talk to a domestic abuse service, even just to think it through out loud.

## Keeping friends while you're back
Friends sometimes pull away when you go back, out of frustration or fear. Try to keep at least one connection, even a quiet one. If nobody else can be that person right now, a domestic abuse helpline can.

## The children while you're back
If you have children, keep their safety plan going too: a code word, a safe person at school, how to call for help. Notice how they're doing. If you're worried about them, a children's service or helpline can advise.

::: honest
## Questions to sit with before deciding
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

Going back is your decision, and nobody here will judge it. Choices made in loneliness, fear or exhaustion can feel a lot like love, so it helps to look at them in daylight:

- What has changed in their behaviour, over months, without me asking or pushing?
- Am I going back to them, or away from something hard: money, loneliness, court, the children's sadness?
- Could I leave again if I needed to? What would I need in place?
- Who knows about this decision, and what do they say?

If an answer stings, you don't have to act on it today. Whatever you decide, keep your safety plan and one person who knows the truth.
:::

::: action
## Keep one door open
If you're back, keep one link to help. Right now, save a helpline number in your phone under a plain name, or text one friend who knows. You don't have to decide anything else today.
:::

When you have a quiet moment, [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) works whether you're out, back, or somewhere in between.

=== helping-someone-in-year-one
title: Helping someone in year one
question: How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?
seo: How to Help a Friend Who Left an Abusive Relationship
short: To help a friend who left an abusive relationship: believe them, let their feelings be complicated, don't push a timeline, don't keep attacking their ex, and stick around for the long haul. What feels helpful and what actually helps aren't always the same thing.
---
Someone you love has left, and you want to help without making it worse. Good instinct. This chapter is written to be forwarded, in full, to whoever is supporting someone through the first year after leaving. It's short on purpose, because nobody has time for a manual right now.

## How do I help a friend who left an abusive relationship?
**Believe them, fully, the first time.** Skip the careful questions that sound like you're checking their story. [The friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you) explains why this matters so much.

**Let their feelings be complicated.** They may grieve, miss the person, and feel relief and sadness on the same afternoon. None of that means they made a mistake. None of it needs correcting. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).

**Don't push a timeline.** "Are you over it yet?" and "Have you thought about dating?" land as pressure, however kindly you mean them.

**Don't keep attacking their ex,** even if you're furious on their behalf. Some people end up defending or playing it down, because that's easier than managing your anger on top of their own. Save the ranting for the drive home.

**Help with the practical stuff.** Meals, lifts to court, an hour with the children, sitting beside them while they read the messages.

**Learn the safety basics.** Don't share their location or news with mutual friends. Don't pass on messages from their ex, however harmless they look. See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

> What feels helpful and what helps are sometimes different things. Believing them, being steady and following their lead beats advice, deadlines and even righteous anger.

## What if they go back?
Stay in touch. Bite back the "I told you so." People often leave more than once, and they're more likely to leave for good if they still have people to come back to. See [If you go back](ch:if-you-go-back).

## What if I'm worried about their safety?
Tell them, once, calmly. Share [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously). If you think they're in immediate danger, call emergency services. If they talk about harming themselves, see [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

## The single most useful thing you can do
Stay. Check in next month, and the month after, not just in the first dramatic week. If you support survivors for work, see [For professionals](ch:for-professionals). And look after yourself too, which [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired) is all about.

## What should I say, and what should I avoid?
Helpful:

- "I believe you."
- "That sounds exhausting."
- "What would help this week?"
- "You don't have to decide anything today."

Best avoided, however tempting:

- "Why didn't you leave sooner?"
- "Just ignore them."
- "You need to move on."
- "At least you're out now."

## How can I help with the practical side?
Offer lifts to court or appointments, childcare, a meal, an hour sitting with them while they read messages, or help finding a lawyer. You could also be the safe place for their documents and a ready bag. See [Find help](ch:find-help).

## What year one tends to look like
- **The first weeks:** shock, practical crises, safety. They need help with tasks.
- **Months 2 to 6:** court, money, children, exhaustion. Ups and downs. They may miss the person they left.
- **Months 6 to 12:** some steadiness, then anniversaries and setbacks. People around them often expect them to be "over it" by now.

Your support matters at every stage, and often most in the middle months, when other people tend to drift off.

## Practical help by stage
- **Early:** a bed, a lift, childcare, meals, help with forms, keeping documents safe.
- **Middle:** company at court or appointments, help finding lawyers and services, regular check-ins.
- **Later:** invitations to ordinary things, celebrating small wins, remembering the anniversaries.

## Helping with the children
Offering childcare during court dates or appointments is one of the most useful things you can do. Keep the children's routines familiar. Don't ask them about the other parent or the separation, and don't criticise the other parent in front of them.

## What not to do online
- Don't post about the abuse or the person they left.
- Don't tag them in posts or photos that show where they are.
- Don't confront the person they left on social media, however good the comeback.
- Don't share their story without permission.

## Looking after yourself as the helper
Supporting someone through abuse is draining. Set limits on what you can give, and say so kindly. Talk to someone about how it's affecting you. Many domestic abuse helplines also support friends and family.

::: bridge slowtake:this-is-written-for-the-son-or-the-daughter
If the person you're helping is an older parent, being controlled by a partner, a carer or another relative, [The Slow Take](/slowtake/this-is-written-for-the-son-or-the-daughter) is another book in this library. It's written for the son or daughter in that spot.
:::

::: scene
## What a good friend did
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Zoe's friend left her husband, Zoe didn't hand out advice. She drove her to court, minded the children during appointments, and sent a message every few days that didn't ask for anything back.

When her friend went back to him for a month, Zoe didn't say "I told you so." She said, "I'm still here." When her friend left again, Zoe was the first person she called.

Her friend said later: "You never made me choose between you and him. That's why I could choose me."
:::

::: action
## One practical offer
Text your friend one concrete offer for this week, with a day attached: a lift on Tuesday, a meal on Thursday, an hour of childcare on Saturday.
:::

Helping is tiring, and you're allowed to be tired. [The friends who did, and are tired](ch:the-friends-who-did-and-are-tired) is for you, next.

=== what-this-book-might-have-wrong
title: What this book might have wrong
question: How strong is the research behind this book on post-separation abuse?
seo: Post-Separation Abuse Research: What's Known and Unknown
short: Post-separation abuse research is growing but still young. Some of what this book says is well supported, some is disputed, and some is this book's own judgment. Here's which is which, out in the open instead of buried in a footnote.
---
How much of this book should you trust? Fair question, and you should ask it of anything you read about abuse online, this included. This library holds itself to a simple standard: say what's known, what's disputed, and what's just our own judgment.

## What is well supported?
- **Separation is a time of raised risk,** especially from very controlling partners. See [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first).
- **Abuse often carries on after separation** through children, money, courts and other people.
- **Past strangulation is a strong warning sign** of later serious harm.
- **Stress and trauma affect sleep, memory and the nervous system.**
- **Children are affected by coercive control directly,** whether or not they see violence.
- **Many people leave more than once** before leaving for good.
- **Ongoing conflict between parents harms children.**

## What is disputed?
- **How well family courts recognise coercive control,** and how fast that's changing. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).
- **"Parental alienation":** how common it is, how to judge it, and whether claims of it are being used against parents who report abuse.
- **"Trauma bond" as a concept:** widely used and recognised, but not a formal diagnosis, and not tested the way a clinical condition would be.
- **Grey rock:** widely recommended by survivors and practitioners, with very little formal research behind it.
- **Exact figures:** how common post-separation abuse is, and how many attempts it takes to leave.

## What is this book's own judgment?
- The exact wording in [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use), drawn from practice, not controlled trials.
- The conversational guidance on children, parents and new partners.
- The working definition of recovery in [What "recovered" means](ch:what-recovered-actually-means).
- The order of steps in [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

## What is the biggest risk this book carries?
That the general patterns won't match your life. Laws vary by country and region, and people vary even more. If your experience is different from what's described here, it isn't less real. Where your own situation and this book disagree, trust your situation.

## What would change this book's mind?
- If separation were shown not to raise risk compared with staying, the safety chapters would need revising.
- If hoovering were shown to be rare, the hoovering chapter would be overstating it.
- If parallel parenting or written-only contact were shown to harm children, those chapters would change.
- If the scripts here were shown to stir up more conflict than other approaches, they'd be rewritten.

## How is this book checked?
Every chapter lists its sources. Legal notes are dated and need checking before you rely on them. Corrections are very welcome. The book's version history is kept on its main page.

## How do I report an error?
If you spot something wrong, especially a phone number, a law or a source, please report it through the Noble Father Creations homepage. Corrections are made and dated in the updates on the book's main page.

## What kinds of sources this book uses
The book draws on several kinds of evidence:

- **Research studies,** listed by author and year, mostly from peer-reviewed journals.
- **Laws and official guidance,** from government and court websites.
- **Specialist organisations,** like national helplines and charities, for practical advice.
- **Clinical guidelines,** like NICE in the UK, for health and therapy.
- **Experience,** of people who've lived through it and those who support them, where research is thin.

Each chapter lists what it used, and [Every source in this book](ch:all-sources) gathers them all on one page, with links.

## Where things change fastest
Laws, helpline numbers, benefits, court procedures and technology all change often. Always check the current position where you live, especially for anything legal or financial. If you find something out of date, please tell us.

## What the book leaves out
This book can't cover every country's law, every culture or every situation. It's written mainly with English-speaking countries in mind. It doesn't give detailed legal advice for your case, and it can't replace a lawyer, doctor or therapist who knows your situation.

## Reading research claims online
The internet is full of confident claims, like "it takes seven attempts to leave" or "narcissists never change." When you meet one, ask:

- Who said it, and how do they know?
- Is there a study, and how many people were in it?
- Is it one study, or what most research finds?
- Is someone selling something? (A course, a book, a "healing package" for three easy payments?)

A bit of healthy scepticism protects you from confident claims that aren't true.

::: action
## Tell us what we got wrong
Spotted an error, an out-of-date number or something that doesn't fit your experience? Open the Noble Father Creations homepage now and send a one-line note. Every correction makes the book better for the next reader.
:::

That's the fine print done. The last chapter, [The long after](ch:the-long-after), is about what life can look like years from now.

=== the-long-after
title: The long after
question: What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years on?
seo: Life After Narcissistic Abuse: What Years Later Looks Like
short: Life after narcissistic abuse, years on, isn't a straight line, and it doesn't have to be. What's on offer is a life that isn't organised around what happened: relationships built on trust, tastes that are yours, anger that has mostly settled, and the freedom to choose.
---
Does it ever stop being the main thing? For most people, yes, slowly. But it isn't a straight line, and it was never going to be. Some weeks feel like real progress. Some feel like every chapter of this book turning up at once, uninvited, on a Tuesday. Both are part of the same process. Neither one is the "real" direction.

## What does life after narcissistic abuse look like, years later?
People who are years out describe things like this:

- **A life that isn't organised around what happened.** Whole days go by without thinking about them.
- **Relationships built on trust** that was given slowly and earned. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).
- **Tastes and habits that are simply yours,** no longer a recovery project. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- **Anger that has mostly settled,** with an occasional brief return that doesn't mean anything has gone wrong. See [Anger, arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).
- **A steadier body,** with fewer jolts and better sleep.
- **Clearer judgment** about people, and limits you hold without having to think about it.
- **For some, contact that still exists** because of children, but takes up a small corner of life instead of the middle of it.

> Recovery leaves the past where it is and gives you back the ability to choose, to trust, to want things, and to build a life that isn't defined by what you left.

## What about the hard days, years on?
They still happen. An anniversary, a court letter, a child's question, spotting them in the supermarket queue. They tend to pass faster and knock you down less far. If one turns into more than a hard week, [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath) is still here.

## Where to go from here
- When a week goes badly: [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) explains what your body is up to.
- When the anger comes late: [Anger, arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).
- When they come back: [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).
- When you're helping someone else: [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one).
- When you just need the short version: [The card](ch:the-card).

## The close
The first chapter said leaving was the middle, not the end. Everything since has been about the part after the door. What comes next is the rest of it: uneven, often boring, and all yours.

## What do people wish they'd known in year one?
- It doesn't go in a straight line, and that's normal.
- The hard days get shorter, even when they still come.
- The person you left doesn't get to decide what the rest of your life looks like.
- You can build a good life alongside a difficult co-parent.
- Asking for help sooner would have made it lighter.
- Writing things down saved them more than once.

## Will I ever stop thinking about it?
For most people it becomes a smaller part of life: remembered, sometimes painful, no longer in charge. You may think about it on certain days for a long time. That doesn't mean it still runs your life.

## What people say, years on
People who left abusive relationships years ago often say:

- the person takes up far less space in their head
- they still have hard days, but they bounce back faster
- they trust themselves more than they did before the relationship
- they're proud of leaving, even though it was the hardest thing they did
- their life is full of things that have nothing to do with the abuse

## The long tail
Some things can last for years: co-parenting, the odd court date, anniversaries, a memory that ambushes you in the car. That's what the long after looks like. Most people find the long tail slips into the background.

## What you might do with what you know
In time, you may find you know things that could help someone else: how to spot the patterns, what helped, what to avoid. You might pass it on to a friend, volunteer, or just be the calm one for someone still in the thick of it. Or you might simply get on with your life. Both are good uses of what you've learned.

## A last word
You left. Then you kept going, through all the forms and messages and hard nights that came after. Whatever today looks like, you're still here, and that counts for a lot.

::: action
## A note to come back to
Grab a scrap of paper or your notes app. Write today's date and one sentence about where you are right now. Put it somewhere you'll stumble on it in a year, then read it and see what's changed.
:::

That's the end of the book, but you can come back any time. If you ever need to find your way in again, start at [Where are you right now?](ch:where-are-you-right-now) and pick the line that sounds like you. Look after yourself. I mean that.
