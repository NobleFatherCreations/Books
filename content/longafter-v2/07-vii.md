=== the-trauma-bond
title: The trauma bond: why you miss someone who hurt you
question: What is a trauma bond, and how do I break it?
seo: Trauma Bond: What It Is, Signs, and How to Break It
short: A trauma bond is a strong attachment to someone who hurt you, built by cycles of harm and warmth. It explains why you can miss an abusive ex, defend them, or want to go back, even when you know you shouldn't. It isn't weakness, and it loosens with distance, time and support.
---
You know what they did. You've listed it. You'd never tell a friend to go back to someone like that. And still, at 11pm, you want to hear their voice.

That pull has a name.

## What is a trauma bond?
A trauma bond is a powerful emotional attachment that forms between a person and someone who harms them, especially when the harm comes in cycles with warmth in between. The term comes from research in the early 1980s by psychologists Donald Dutton and Susan Painter, who studied why people stayed attached to abusive partners.

Two things build it:

- **A power imbalance.** One person holds more control.
- **Intermittent harm and kindness.** Cruelty, then relief. Coldness, then the person you fell for. You never know which you'll get.

That unpredictable mix creates a stronger bond than steady kindness would. Behavioural scientists have known since the 1950s that rewards that come unpredictably are the hardest habits to break. The Decoder covers this as [intermittent reinforcement](/playbook/intermittent-reinforcement-push-pull) and the bond itself as [trauma bonding](/playbook/trauma-bonding).

"Trauma bond" is a widely used term, not a medical diagnosis. It describes something real that many people recognise.

> Feeling the pull is normal. You can feel it and still not act on it.

## What are the signs of a trauma bond?
- You miss them intensely, even remembering what they did.
- You defend them, or explain their behaviour, to others or to yourself.
- You remember the good times more vividly than the bad.
- You feel you're the only one who understands them.
- You believe they'll change if you're patient enough.
- You feel relief, not just anger, when they're kind after being cruel.
- You keep checking their social media. See [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile).
- Leaving felt, or still feels, like losing part of yourself.

## What does trauma bond withdrawal feel like?
After leaving, many people describe something like withdrawal: obsessive thoughts, cravings to make contact, anxiety, low mood, poor sleep. Brain scans of people recently rejected in love show activity in reward and craving areas that overlap with addiction. That doesn't mean love is a drug, but it explains why the urge can feel physical.

It peaks in the early weeks and months for many people, and it often spikes when they reach out. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## What are some examples of trauma bonding?
These are the kinds of moments people describe:

- After a cruel weekend, they cook your favourite meal, and the relief feels like love.
- You tell a friend what happened, then spend ten minutes explaining why it wasn't that bad.
- They threaten to leave, and you end up apologising for something they did.
- You've blocked their number twice, and unblocked it twice, the same night.
- Months after leaving, one kind message undoes a week of feeling clear.

Each one follows the same rhythm: harm, then relief from the same person. The Decoder's [trauma bonding](/playbook/trauma-bonding) page has more examples.

## How do I break a trauma bond?
There's no quick method. These are the things that tend to loosen it:

- **Distance.** No contact, or the least contact you can manage. Every contact restarts the cycle. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- **No intermittent contact.** Occasional replies feed the bond more than steady silence.
- **Tell the story straight.** Write down what happened, plainly, in order. Read it when the good memories crowd in.
- **Name the pull.** "This is the bond, not a sign." Naming it puts a little space between you and it.
- **Replace the comfort.** The relationship met needs, even badly. Find other sources: friends, routine, rest, things you enjoy.
- **Support.** A therapist who understands coercive control can help, especially if the bond keeps pulling you back. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- **Patience with yourself.** You're unlearning something your body learned well.

## How long does a trauma bond last?
There's no reliable number, and you'll be told many. It depends on how long the relationship lasted, how intense it was, whether you still have contact, and what support you have. Most people find the pull weakens noticeably once contact stops, with flare-ups on anniversaries or when the person reappears. It fades in waves, not in a straight line.

## Trauma bond vs love: how do I tell the difference?
Love tends to feel safe and steady, even when it's passionate. A trauma bond tends to feel urgent, anxious and consuming. Love survives your "no." A trauma bond punishes it. You can have loved them and also be trauma bonded to them. The two often sit together, which is part of why it's confusing.

## Is a trauma bond the same as Stockholm syndrome?
They're related, but they aren't the same thing.

"Stockholm syndrome" comes from a bank siege in Stockholm in 1973. Some of the hostages defended their captors afterwards, and a psychiatrist advising the police gave the reaction a name. It describes a bond with a captor during a short, extreme ordeal.

A trauma bond grows over months or years inside a relationship you chose, with real warmth mixed into the harm. That's why it can feel like love and not like fear.

Neither is a medical diagnosis. Stockholm syndrome has never been in the main psychiatric manuals, and researchers argue about whether it's a distinct thing at all. Trauma bond is the more useful term for what happens between partners.

## Is it codependency, or limerence?
You may meet both words online.

**Codependency** usually means a habit of putting someone else's needs first, at your own cost. Some people find it a helpful word. Others find it blames them for a pattern the other person built. A trauma bond is about how the harm was delivered, not a flaw in you.

**Limerence** is an intense, obsessive longing for someone, often someone out of reach. It can sit alongside a trauma bond. The difference is the harm. A trauma bond forms with someone who hurt you, in cycles.

Whatever word fits, what loosens it is much the same: distance, a straight story, and support.

## Can a trauma bond become a healthy relationship?
Rarely, and only if the person who caused harm changes deeply, over a long time, with accountability and without pressure on you. Going back to test whether they've changed usually restarts the cycle. If you're considering it, read [If you go back](ch:if-you-go-back) and [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice) first.

## A 30-day plan to loosen it
Thirty days won't break a bond built over years. It can loosen its grip enough that you feel some choice again.

- **Days 1 to 7: cut the supply.** Block or mute them everywhere you can. Put photos and gifts in a box out of sight. Tell one person you're doing this, and agree they're the one you message when the pull comes.
- **Days 8 to 14: write the true story.** Each day, write one thing that happened, plainly, with the date if you know it. Read the list when the good memories arrive.
- **Days 15 to 21: fill the gaps.** Notice the times of day you miss them most. Put something there: a walk, a call, a class, a programme you like.
- **Days 22 to 30: look at what you've done.** Count the days without contact, the urges you rode out, the nights you slept. Write down one thing you've done for yourself that they would have stopped.

If you slip, start again from the day you're on. Slips are part of how bonds loosen.

## When the bond is with a parent or family
Trauma bonds can form with a parent, a sibling or a whole family, as well as a partner. They can be even harder to loosen, because they started in childhood and come wrapped in duty and love.

The same things help: distance where you can, a written account of what happened, and support. Add patience with yourself for the guilt that comes with stepping back from family. See "Leaving a controlling parent or family" in [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

## The worst nights
Some nights the pull is overwhelming. For those nights:

- Don't decide anything after 10pm. Tell yourself you'll think about it in the morning.
- Put your phone in another room, or give it to someone for the night.
- Hold something cold, or take a warm shower, to bring your body down.
- Read the list of what happened.
- Call a helpline. They talk to people in this exact state every night.

The night passes. The feeling usually feels smaller in daylight.

## Explaining it to people who don't understand
Friends may say, "Why would you miss someone who treated you like that?" It's hard to explain, and you don't owe anyone a full answer.

> **Say:** "It's called a trauma bond. The ups and downs made the attachment stronger. I know it doesn't make sense from the outside. I don't need you to understand it; I just need you to not make me feel stupid for it."

You can also send them this chapter.

## Is something wrong with me for feeling this?
No. Trauma bonds form in ordinary people under particular conditions. They say more about the pattern you were in than about your judgment or strength. [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left) covers the mix of feelings this leaves behind.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* traces how the bond gets built, stage by stage, in [The Manipulation Cycle](/feminine/the-manipulation-cycle#core-concept).

## What loosening looked like for Dev
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Dev knew his ex had been cruel. He still checked his phone every hour, hoping for a message. He started the thirty days. The first week he slipped twice and unblocked her. In the second week he wrote the list of what had happened and kept it on his phone. In the third week he joined a five-a-side team that played on the evenings he missed her most.

By the end of the month the pull hadn't gone. But he noticed it, named it and let it pass most days. That felt like the first choice he'd made in years.

## Name it when it comes
When the pull arrives, say quietly, "This is the bond, not a sign." Then do one thing from your list.

=== what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left
title: What you're allowed to feel about someone you left
question: Why do I miss my abuser, and is it normal?
seo: Why Do I Miss My Abuser? Mixed Feelings After Leaving
short: If you're asking "why do I miss my abuser," you're not alone. Missing someone who hurt you, feeling tenderness, relief and grief at once, is common and doesn't mean leaving was wrong. Feelings don't need to match the story other people expect. You're allowed all of them.
---
You might miss them. At certain times: a time of evening, a smell, a song in the supermarket. You might remember something kind and feel a rush of warmth, alongside a clear knowledge that leaving was right. You might feel relief and grief in the same hour.

## Why do I miss my abuser?
Because the relationship wasn't only harm. There were real moments of closeness, real plans, maybe real love on your side. And because the bond was built by cycles of hurt and warmth, which make attachment stronger. [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond) explains that part.

Missing someone isn't the same as wanting them back. You can hold both without either one cancelling the other.

> Human attachment doesn't switch off because the person turned out to be harmful. That isn't a flaw in you.

## What feelings are normal after leaving?
All of these are common, alone or together:

- missing them, sometimes intensely
- relief, even joy
- grief for the relationship you hoped for. See [The grief nobody will let you have](ch:the-grief-nobody-will-let-you-have).
- anger, sometimes much later. See [Anger, arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).
- guilt and shame. See [Guilt and shame](ch:guilt-and-shame).
- fear
- numbness
- tenderness, or pity for them
- loneliness, even in a full room

None of these feelings is evidence that the relationship wasn't harmful, or that you secretly want to go back.

## Why does this need saying?
Because the people around you often expect a simpler story: relief, a bit of sadness, then fine. When your feelings don't match, it's easy to decide something's wrong with you, when the story was simply too simple.

## When will these feelings settle?
This book won't give you a date. Any number would be more comforting than true. Feelings tend to soften in waves, with harder days around anniversaries, contact and change. However long it takes, and however contradictory it feels, it isn't a sign you're doing it wrong.

## What helps?
- **Let the feelings be there** without acting on them. Missing them doesn't mean you have to text.
- **Talk to someone** who can hear the complicated version without telling you what to feel.
- **Write the full story** down, good and bad, so neither side takes over.
- **Be wary of people who rush you.** Pressure to "move on" usually comes from their discomfort, not from your progress.


## What if I feel nothing at all?
Numbness is common. It's often the nervous system protecting you while it's overloaded. Feelings usually return as things settle. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## What if I feel happy, then guilty for feeling happy?
Happiness after leaving isn't a betrayal of anyone, including your children. You're allowed good days in the middle of a hard year.

## What if I want to reach out to them?
Notice the urge, and wait. Tell someone first. Read [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) and [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond). The urge usually passes, and it passes faster each time you don't act on it.

## What do I do with the good memories?
You may remember holidays, jokes, kindness, the early days. Those memories are real. They don't cancel out the harm, and the harm doesn't cancel them.

You don't have to throw them away. You can put them in their place: one part of a story that also includes things that hurt you. When a good memory comes, you can let it be there, then remind yourself of the rest. Some people find it helps to write both columns side by side.

## Missing them, or missing who you hoped they were?
Often what people miss is the person they believed in: the version that appeared at the start, or in the good spells, or in their hopes. That version was part real and part promise.

Asking yourself "Which one am I missing?" can make the grief clearer. You can grieve the hope without going back to the person.

## Feeling sorry for them
You may feel pity: for their childhood, their loneliness, their struggles. Compassion is a good quality. It may also have been used to keep you there.

You can feel sorry for someone and still keep your distance. Their pain is real, and it's not yours to fix.

## Love that lingers
Some people still love the person they left, months or years later. That doesn't mean you should go back, or that you've failed to move on. Love can fade slowly, and it can sit alongside the knowledge that the relationship wasn't safe. Feeling love isn't the same as acting on it.

## Telling people about mixed feelings
People around you may want you to only feel anger. If you say you miss them, they may worry, or get frustrated. Choose who you tell. A therapist, a helpline or a support group can hear mixed feelings without needing them to be simple.

## Both columns
Take a piece of paper and draw a line down the middle. On one side, write what you miss. On the other, what you don't. Keep both. On hard days, read both. Holding the whole picture is how mixed feelings start to settle.

## Sources
- Dutton, D. G., and Painter, S. (1993). Emotional attachments in abusive relationships: A test of traumatic bonding theory. *Violence and Victims*, 8(2), 105–120.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.
- Sbarra, D. A. (2006). Predicting the onset of emotional recovery following nonmarital relationship dissolution. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 32(3), 298–312.

=== the-grief-nobody-will-let-you-have
title: The grief nobody will let you have
question: Is grief after leaving an abusive relationship normal?
seo: Grief After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: Grief after leaving an abusive relationship is real grief. You may be mourning the person you thought they were, the future you planned, and years of your life. Others often only see the harm and expect relief, which can leave you grieving alone. You're allowed to mourn something that also hurt you.
---
There's a grief in this that almost nobody around you will let you have. Everyone who knows what happened sees the harm, and expects relief.

## What am I grieving?
Not always the person, though sometimes that too. More often:

- the relationship you hoped it would be
- the future you planned: the house, the children, growing old
- the version of them you fell for, who may have been real some of the time
- the years you gave it
- the family as it was, for your children
- the person you were before

## Why do people dismiss this grief?
Usually with good intentions: "But they were awful to you," as if that settles it. It's as if grief needs its object to have been good. It doesn't. People grieve difficult parents and painful marriages all the time.

Grief that other people don't recognise has a name: disenfranchised grief. It's harder to carry because you carry it alone.

> You can grieve the loss of something that was also harming you. Mourning one doesn't mean you're confused about the other.

## Is grief after leaving an abusive relationship normal?
Yes. It's one of the most common experiences after leaving, and one of the least talked about. It can arrive straight away, or months later, once the practical crisis has passed and there's room to feel it. It's often tangled with relief, anger and longing. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).

## What do I do with it?
- **Let it be grief.** You don't need anyone's permission for it to be real.
- **Find one person who can hold it.** If friends can't, a therapist or support group can. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).
- **Mark the losses.** Some people write a letter they never send, or mark the day the future they planned would have happened.
- **Watch for hard dates.** Anniversaries can bring grief back with force. See [The anniversary effect](ch:the-anniversary-effect).
- **Don't confuse grief with a reason to return.** Missing the hoped-for future is not the same as that future being available. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).

## When should I get more help?
If grief stops you functioning for a long time, or turns into hopelessness that won't lift, talk to a doctor or therapist. If you're thinking about harming yourself, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).


## Can I grieve while I'm still fighting them in court?
Yes. Many people grieve and fight at the same time. The grief may surface in odd moments: in the car after a hearing, or at night. It's part of the same experience, not a contradiction.

## How long does grief like this last?
It doesn't follow a timetable. It comes in waves that tend to get further apart. Some losses, like the family you hoped your children would have, may be felt again at milestones for years. That's grief doing its job.

## What about grieving for my children's family?
Many parents grieve the family their children didn't get. Your children can still have a loving home, or two homes, even if it isn't the one you pictured.

## The losses, named
Leaving abuse can mean losing many things at once:

- the person you loved, or thought you loved
- the future you'd planned together
- your home, your neighbourhood, your belongings
- in-laws, friends and a community
- money, security, a career
- years of your life
- the version of you from before
- the family your children had

Naming each one helps. Grief is easier to carry when you know what you're grieving.

## Grief in waves
Grief rarely moves in neat stages. It comes in waves: a song in a shop, a photo, a smell, a date. Some days are fine; some days you can't get out of bed. Over time, the waves usually come less often, and they're easier to ride. They may never stop completely, and that's okay.

## Rituals that help
- Write a letter to what you've lost, not to the person. Keep it, or burn it, or bury it.
- Box up reminders and put them away, to decide about later.
- Mark a date with something gentle: a walk, a meal, a candle.
- Make a list of what you've gained, alongside what you've lost.

## Your children's grief
Children grieve too: for the family they had, the house, the other parent, the routines. Let them be sad without having to make you feel better. Keep some familiar things the same. See [How children react](ch:how-children-react).

## When grief and anger mix
Grief and anger often arrive together. One minute you miss them; the next you're furious. Both are part of losing something that was also harming you. See [Anger arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late).

## How Helen marked the loss
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Helen's friends couldn't understand why she was grieving a man who had hurt her for twelve years. She stopped talking about it, and the grief got heavier. A counsellor suggested she write a letter to the life she'd hoped for: the house, the children growing up with both parents, growing old together. She read it aloud once, cried for an hour, then put it in a drawer.

The grief didn't vanish. It became something she could carry, instead of something she had to hide.

## Name one loss
Write down one thing you've lost that nobody has acknowledged. Let yourself be sad about it for ten minutes, on purpose.

## Sources
- Doka, K. J. (Ed.) (1989). *Disenfranchised Grief: Recognizing Hidden Sorrow.* Lexington Books.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.
- Boss, P. (1999). *Ambiguous Loss: Learning to Live with Unresolved Grief.* Harvard University Press.

=== guilt-and-shame
title: Guilt and shame
question: Why do I feel guilty after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: Guilt After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: Guilt after leaving an abusive relationship is very common: guilt for leaving, for staying so long, for the children, for not seeing it sooner. Much of it was planted by the relationship itself. Guilt says you did something wrong; usually you didn't. Shame says you are something wrong; you aren't.
---
Guilt after leaving an abusive relationship is one of the most common feelings there is. You left, and you feel guilty. For breaking up the family. For staying so long. For the times you went along with it. For not seeing it sooner. For feeling relieved.

## Why do I feel guilty after leaving?
Because guilt was often part of how the relationship worked. Controlling people often teach their partners that everything is their fault: the moods, the arguments, the children's behaviour. That training doesn't stop when you leave.

The Decoder describes the planting of shame in [shame installation](/playbook/shame-installation), and the steady pressure of guilt in [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping).

> Guilt was one of the tools. It's still working, even though the person using it is gone.

## Was it my fault?
No. Abuse is the responsibility of the person who chose to do it. You may have made choices you'd make differently now. Everyone does. But choices made under pressure, fear and confusion aren't the cause of someone else's abuse.

## Guilt or shame: what's the difference?
**Guilt** says: "I did something wrong." It can be useful when it's accurate, because it points to something to repair.

**Shame** says: "I am something wrong." It's never useful. It makes you hide, isolate and believe you deserve bad treatment.

Most of what people feel after abuse is shame wearing guilt's clothes.

## What about the guilt of staying so long?
People stay for many reasons: love, hope, fear, money, children, nowhere to go, a bond built by the abuse itself. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond). Staying wasn't a failure of intelligence or courage. And many people leave more than once before it holds. See [If you go back](ch:if-you-go-back).

## What about guilt about the children?
You may feel guilty that they saw things, or that they now have two homes. Children do better in one calm home than in one tense one. Leaving to protect them, or yourself, is a choice made for them, not against them. [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react) covers what helps them now.

## Guilt as a tool they used
In many controlling relationships, guilt was used on purpose. "After everything I've done for you." "You're tearing this family apart." "Look what you made me do."

Guilt that was trained into you over years doesn't switch off when you leave. It can keep firing at things that aren't your fault. When guilt arrives, ask: "Is this my voice, or theirs?"

## The common guilts, answered
- **"I broke up the family."** The abuse broke it. Leaving was a response to that.
- **"The children saw too much."** Many parents feel this. Leaving was one of the ways you protected them from seeing more.
- **"I stayed too long."** You stayed as long as you needed to be able to leave safely. That is how leaving works for most people.
- **"I left the pets, the house, their family."** You left what you couldn't take, to get out safely.
- **"I'm costing everyone money and stress."** Their behaviour is causing the cost.

## Shame about what you did to survive
People in abusive relationships often do things they'd never normally do: lie to protect themselves, go along with things they disagreed with, cut off friends, snap at their children, use alcohol to cope. Afterwards, they feel deep shame.

These were survival responses in an impossible situation. Understanding that doesn't mean pretending they didn't happen. It means placing them in context, and treating yourself with the understanding you'd give a friend.

## Shame in a community or faith
In some communities, separation and divorce carry stigma, or there's pressure to keep the family together "for the children" or because of religious teaching. You may feel judged by people you've known all your life.

Find people inside or outside your community who support you. Many faith traditions have leaders and groups that recognise abuse. You don't have to choose between your faith and your safety.

## Self-compassion that isn't fluffy
Self-compassion isn't telling yourself everything's fine. It's talking to yourself the way a fair, kind friend would:

- Acknowledge what's hard: "This is painful."
- Remember you're not alone: "Lots of people feel this after leaving."
- Offer one kind thing: "What would help a bit right now?"

Researchers have found that self-compassion is linked with lower anxiety and depression, and isn't the same as letting yourself off the hook.

## When guilt points to something real
Sometimes guilt is pointing at something you did that hurt someone. If so, you can take responsibility for that thing, make amends where it's safe and possible, and learn from it. That's different from carrying blame for the whole relationship. Owning your part doesn't mean owning theirs.

## How do I loosen guilt and shame?
- **Check the facts.** Write the guilty thought down. Then ask: what did I know at the time? What choices did I have? What would I say to a friend who wrote this?
- **Say it out loud** to someone safe. Shame shrinks when it's spoken and met with understanding.
- **Notice whose voice it is.** Often the critical voice in your head sounds like them.
- **Make repairs where they're due,** and only there. If you snapped at your children, apologise to them. That's guilt doing its job.
- **Get support** if shame is heavy or constant. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## Whose voice is it?
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Every time Aisha's children cried at handover, she heard her ex's voice: "You did this to them." She felt it as truth. Her therapist asked her to write the sentence down, then write who first said it. It was him, many times, for years. Then she wrote what she'd say to a friend in her position: "You got your children out of a frightening home. Their tears are about the change, not your failure."

The guilt still came. She started recognising it as an old recording, not a verdict.

## Write down the sentence
Write the guilty thought exactly as it sounds in your head. Then write whose words they first were.

- Tangney, J. P., and Dearing, R. L. (2002). *Shame and Guilt.* Guilford Press.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.

=== anger-arriving-late
title: Anger, arriving late
question: Why am I so angry now, long after the narcissistic abuse ended?
seo: Anger After Narcissistic Abuse: Why It Arrives Late
short: Anger after narcissistic abuse or any coercive relationship often arrives late, months or years afterwards, when you finally feel safe enough to feel it. It's a common, healthy sign that you've seen what happened clearly. It needs somewhere to go that doesn't hurt you or the people around you.
---
In the early months there was fear, relief, grief, exhaustion. Then, a year or more later, something small happens, and you're furious. Shaking-hands furious.

## Why does anger arrive so late?
Because survival came first. In the early period, staying safe and getting through each day took everything you had. Anger needs room, and room needs safety.

Anger also needs a settled sense that what happened was wrong. If self-doubt was part of the abuse, that certainty can take a long time to arrive. When it does, anger often comes with it.

> Late anger often means you can finally see what happened clearly.

## Is anger after narcissistic abuse normal?
Yes. Anger is common after any kind of trauma, and research links it with post-traumatic stress. Many people describe it as a turning point: the moment they stopped blaming themselves.

## What do I do with it?
Anger is a physical state as much as a feeling, so it helps to move it through your body:

- **Move:** walking fast, running, swimming, hitting a pillow, anything that uses big muscles.
- **Write:** an angry letter you never send. Say everything.
- **Say it:** to a friend or therapist who can hear it without flinching.
- **Make something:** art, music, a garden, anything that turns energy into form.

What doesn't help:

- aiming it at people who weren't responsible, including your children
- using it as fuel to contact or confront the person you left. That usually restarts the cycle. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) and [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- drinking or using drugs to quieten it. See [Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through](ch:drinking-drugs-and-getting-through).

## What if my anger frightens me?
If you're worried you might hurt someone, or you're lashing out in ways you regret, get support. A therapist can help you understand where it's coming from and what to do with it. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this). If you're being accused of being the angry one, see [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## Will it pass?
For most people, it settles over time into something quieter. It may come back briefly, years later, on an anniversary or when something reminds you. That doesn't mean anything has gone wrong.


## Is it normal to be angry with myself?
Very. Many people are angry that they stayed, that they didn't see it, or that they went back. That anger usually belongs to the person who did the harm. See [Guilt and shame](ch:guilt-and-shame).

## What if I'm angry at the people who didn't help?
Friends who didn't believe you, family who stayed neutral, services that let you down. That anger makes sense too. You get to decide what to do with those relationships. See [The friends who didn't believe you](ch:the-friends-who-didnt-believe-you).

## Letting anger out safely
Anger needs somewhere to go. Some safe places:

- **Your body:** fast walking, running, swimming, hitting a pillow, shouting into a cushion, physical work.
- **Paper:** write it all, uncensored, then destroy it if you like.
- **A person:** a friend who can hear it without being frightened, a therapist, a support group.

What tends not to help: sending angry messages, venting online, or replaying arguments in your head for hours.

## Anger in the body
Anger shows up physically: a tight jaw, clenched fists, a hot face, a racing heart. Noticing these signs early gives you a moment to choose what to do next. Slow breathing out, cold water on your face or stepping outside can take the edge off before it spills.

## Anger and the children
Your children may see your anger. That's okay, as long as it's not aimed at them and doesn't frighten them. You can name it: "I'm feeling cross about something grown-up. You haven't done anything wrong. I'm going to take a few minutes." Avoid venting about the other parent in front of them.

## Anger at the system
Many people feel furious at courts, police, social workers or family members who didn't help. That anger is often justified. You can use it to complain, to ask for reviews, or to support others later. Try not to let it make you give up on all help; some people and services will get it right.

## When anger gets stuck
Sometimes anger becomes a constant hum, and stays for years. It can keep you tied to the person you left. If you find you're thinking about them angrily most days, long after leaving, it may help to talk to a therapist about what the anger is protecting. Often it's grief or fear underneath.

## Can anger be useful?
Yes. Anger can power a boundary, a complaint, a court application, or help for someone else. Used that way, it becomes energy for your life, not fuel for theirs.

## When the anger came
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

For months after leaving, Pete felt nothing but tired. Then, about a year later, he was suddenly furious: at his ex, at himself, at the friends who'd laughed off what he told them. It frightened him. He started running three times a week, wrote angry letters he never sent and talked about it in a men's support group.

The anger didn't disappear, but it changed shape. He used some of it to write a clear statement for court, and some of it to set limits with people who'd let him down.

## Somewhere for it to go
Pick one safe outlet for anger this week, like a run, a notebook or a call, and use it the next time anger comes.

- Orth, U., and Wieland, E. (2006). Anger, hostility, and posttraumatic stress disorder in trauma-exposed adults: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 74(4), 698–706.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.

=== checking-their-profile
title: Checking their profile
question: Why do I keep checking my ex's social media, and how do I stop?
seo: Checking Your Ex's Social Media? Why It Hurts and How to Stop
short: Checking your ex's social media after an abusive relationship is common and hard to stop, because it feeds the same craving as contact. It also slows recovery. Muting, blocking, asking a friend to tell you anything you need to know, and replacing the habit with something else all help.
---
It's late. You tell yourself you'll just have a quick look. Forty minutes later you've seen their new partner's holiday photos, read their cryptic post, and feel worse than you have all week.

## Why do I keep checking my ex's social media?
For the same reasons you miss them. The bond is still there, and checking is a way of getting a little contact without making contact. Each check gives a small hit: relief, anger, curiosity, hurt. Unpredictable rewards are the hardest habits to break. The Decoder explains this pull in [push-pull](/playbook/intermittent-reinforcement-push-pull).

There's also fear. You want to know what they're doing, who they're telling about you, whether they're coming for you. And some people check hoping for a sign they'll come back, which is the opening [hoovering](ch:hoovering) relies on.

> Each check restarts the pull, and often costs you the rest of the evening.

## Does checking slow recovery?
Research says yes. A study of people after a break-up found that those who kept looking at an ex's profile had more distress, more longing and less personal growth than those who didn't.

## Why can't I stop thinking about my ex?
Obsessive thoughts are common after a trauma bond, especially in the early months. They're part of the withdrawal described in [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond). They do ease with distance.

## How do I stop checking?
- **Mute, unfollow or block** them, their new partner and the friends who post about them. It's the online version of [no contact](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- **Remove the shortcut.** Delete the app for a while, log out, or use an app blocker at night.
- **Ask a friend to watch for you.** If you need to know something for safety or court, a friend can check and tell you only what matters.
- **Notice the trigger.** Late at night? After a hard day? When you're lonely? Plan something else for that moment: a call, a show, a bath, a walk.
- **Delay.** When you want to check, wait 20 minutes. The urge usually passes.
- **Count the cost.** After a check, write down how you feel. Read it next time you want to look.

## What if I need to monitor them for safety?
If they're threatening you or posting about you, ask someone else to monitor and screenshot for you. That keeps the evidence without keeping you hooked. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## Practical blocks
Make checking take more effort than it's worth:

- Block or mute them, and their new partner, on every platform.
- Log out of social apps on your phone, or delete them for a month.
- Use your phone's screen-time settings to limit those apps after 9pm.
- Remove their name from search suggestions and your browser history.
- Mute keywords like their name, where the platform allows it.

Each extra step gives the urge a few seconds to pass.

## Checking through other people
Sometimes the checking moves sideways: asking a friend what they've posted, looking at their sister's profile, reading mutual friends' comments. It's the same habit with an extra step.

> **Say (to a friend):** "Please don't tell me what they're up to, even if I ask. Remind me I asked you not to."

## The story you tell yourself when you look
When you look, you usually find a scene that hurts: a smiling photo, a new partner, a holiday. Your mind fills in the rest: "They're happy. They've changed. It was me."

Profiles show the parts people choose to show. They tell you nothing about what that person's days are like, or how they treat the person beside them. The story you tell after checking is usually the story the bond wants you to believe.

## The night-time urge
Most checking happens late, in bed, alone. Charge your phone outside the bedroom. If you wake and want to look, have something else within reach: a book, a podcast, a written list of reasons not to check.

## A slip plan
Decide now what you'll do after a slip:

1. Close the app.
2. Say, "That was the habit, not a choice about my life."
3. Write down what you saw and how it made you feel, in two lines.
4. Do one thing for yourself straight away: a drink of water, a message to a friend, five slow breaths.
5. Start counting again tomorrow.

## Replacing the habit
Checking fills a gap: boredom, loneliness, worry. Choose a replacement for the same moments: a game, a walk, a message to a friend, a short video you enjoy. It works best when it's easy and ready.

## What if I slip?
You will, probably. Everyone does. Notice it, don't punish yourself, and start again. The aim is fewer checks, not a perfect record.

## How Sophie stopped checking
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Sophie checked her ex's profile and his new partner's every night in bed. Each time she felt worse. She deleted the app for a month, charged her phone in the kitchen and asked her sister to message her at 10pm each night. When she slipped, she wrote two lines in a notebook and went back to day one.

By the end of the month she was checking once a week. By the end of three, she'd forgotten to check for a fortnight and only noticed afterwards.

## Tonight's barrier
Add one obstacle between you and their profile tonight: log out, move the app, or charge your phone in another room.

- Marshall, T. C. (2012). Facebook surveillance of former romantic partners: Associations with postbreakup recovery and personal growth. *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*, 15(10), 521–526.
- Fisher, H. E., et al. (2010). Reward, addiction, and emotion regulation systems associated with rejection in love. *Journal of Neurophysiology*, 104(1), 51–60.
- Ferster, C. B., and Skinner, B. F. (1957). *Schedules of Reinforcement.* Appleton-Century-Crofts.

=== closure-and-forgiveness
title: Closure you give yourself, and whether you have to forgive
question: How do I find closure after narcissistic abuse? Do I have to forgive?
seo: Closure After Narcissistic Abuse, and Whether to Forgive
short: Closure after narcissistic abuse rarely comes from the person who hurt you: the apology, explanation or final conversation usually never arrives, or makes things worse. Closure is something you build yourself. Forgiveness is a choice, not a duty, and you can heal without it.
---
You want them to understand what they did. To say sorry and mean it. To explain why. You imagine the conversation that would finally let you put it down.

## Will I get closure from them?
Probably not. People who control others rarely give honest accounts of what they did. The longing for that conversation is often [the trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond) talking. A "closure conversation" often becomes another round: blame, denial, tears, a fresh hook. Even a real apology may not give you what you need.

The Decoder describes apologies that change nothing in [apologies without repair](/playbook/apologies-without-repair).

> If you wait for them to give you closure, you may wait for years.

## How do I find closure after narcissistic abuse?
Closure is something you make, not something you're given. Things that help:

- **Write the true story,** start to finish, in plain words. Not for anyone else. For you.
- **Write the letter you'd want to send,** then don't send it. Burn it, bury it, keep it in a drawer.
- **Name what you learned,** about patterns, about yourself, about what you'll never accept again.
- **Mark an ending.** Some people choose a date, a walk, a small ritual.
- **Let the questions stay open.** "Why did they do it?" may never be answered. You can live with an open question.

## Should I send a closure letter to my ex?
Usually not. It reopens contact and hands them a reaction. If you need to say something, write it and keep it. If you must send something, keep it short and final, and don't expect a reply. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).

## Do I have to forgive my abuser?
No. Forgiveness is a choice, not a requirement for healing. Some people find forgiveness brings peace. Some never forgive and heal fully. Both are legitimate.

Forgiveness also doesn't mean:

- excusing what happened
- reconciling, or letting them back in
- trusting them again
- saying it was okay
- stopping legal action or keeping quiet

Be wary of anyone who pushes you to forgive, especially the person who hurt you. Pressure to forgive can be another form of control; the Decoder calls this [forced forgiveness](/playbook/forced-forgiveness-weaponized-forgiveness).

## What if I want to forgive?
Then do it on your terms, in your time, privately if you like. You can forgive someone and still keep no contact. *The Repair*, in this library, looks at forgiveness from the other side in [If they never forgive you](/repair/if-they-never-forgive-you).

## What about forgiving myself?
Often that's the forgiveness that matters most. See [Guilt and shame](ch:guilt-and-shame).

## What closure looks like in practice
Closure is rarely one moment. For most people it looks like:

- thinking about them less often, and for less time
- being able to tell the story without it taking over your day
- making plans that have nothing to do with them
- feeling more curious about your future than about their life

It usually arrives quietly. You notice it when you look back.

## Endings you make yourself
You can mark the end without them:

- Write a letter you never send, saying everything.
- Pack away reminders and decide later what to keep.
- Go somewhere you used to go together, with a friend, and make a new memory there.
- Plant something, light a candle, or choose a small object that stands for starting again.

## Forgiveness myths
- **"You can't heal without forgiving."** Many people recover fully without forgiving.
- **"Forgiving means trusting them again."** It doesn't. Forgiveness and reconciliation are different things.
- **"Forgiving means it was okay."** It doesn't. You can forgive and still say clearly that it was wrong.
- **"Not forgiving means you're bitter."** You can refuse to forgive and still be at peace.

## When people push you to forgive
Family, friends or faith leaders may urge you to forgive, sometimes so they can stay comfortable or keep the peace. You can say:

> **Say:** "I'll decide about forgiveness in my own time. Right now, I'm focusing on being safe and well."

## Acceptance without forgiveness
Some people find a middle path: accepting that it happened, that they can't change it, and that they won't let it run their life, without forgiving. Acceptance is about your peace. It doesn't require anything from them.

## The letter you don't send
Write a letter to the person you left, saying everything you'll never get to say. Don't send it. Keep it, burn it or bury it. The point is to finish the conversation in your own head, on your own terms.

## Sources
- Worthington, E. L., Jr. (2006). *Forgiveness and Reconciliation: Theory and Application.* Routledge.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books (on the fantasy of revenge, forgiveness and compensation).
- Boss, P. (1999). *Ambiguous Loss.* Harvard University Press.

=== the-anniversary-effect
title: The anniversary effect
question: Why do certain dates bring it all back?
seo: Anniversary Reaction to Trauma: Why Dates Bring It Back
short: An anniversary reaction to trauma is when a date, a season or a reminder brings old feelings back with surprising force, sometimes years later. It's common across many kinds of loss and trauma. It doesn't mean you haven't moved on. Planning for hard dates softens them.
---
A date arrives: the anniversary of the day you left, a wedding anniversary, a birthday, the day something happened. And the past comes back with a force that surprises you, sometimes years after you thought it had settled.

## What is an anniversary reaction?
It's the return of feelings, memories or body reactions around a date, a season or a reminder linked to a loss or trauma. Grief and trauma researchers have recorded it across many kinds of loss. Less obvious triggers count too: a season, a song, a smell, a quality of light.

> This isn't a sign you haven't moved on. It happens to people who are otherwise doing well.

## What might I notice?
- low mood, tearfulness or irritability in the days before the date
- poor sleep or nightmares
- feeling on edge, or numb
- old memories arriving uninvited
- a strong urge to contact them, or to check on them. See [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile).

## Why does it happen?
Memory links feelings to time and place. Your body remembers the season it was hardest, even before your mind notices the date. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

Hard dates can also bring contact from the person you left. Hoovering often clusters around birthdays and holidays. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## How do I plan for a hard date?
- **Put it in your calendar** a week ahead, so it doesn't ambush you.
- **Plan something gentle** for the day: time with someone safe, a walk, a favourite meal.
- **Lower your expectations** of yourself that week.
- **Tell one person,** so someone knows why you're quiet.
- **Decide in advance** what you'll do if they make contact. Usually: nothing.
- **Make a new tradition** for that date, if you'd like it to mean something else.

## Does it get easier?
For most people, yes. The intensity usually softens over the years, even if it never disappears. [Holidays and the first year's calendar](ch:holidays-and-the-first-year) covers the first round of dates in more detail.


## Which dates might catch me off guard?
- the day you met, married or moved in together
- the day you left
- the date of a serious incident
- a court date, a year later
- their birthday, and yours
- holidays you spent together
- the season when it was worst

## What if the date is also a happy one?
A child's birthday can also be the anniversary of something terrible. You can hold both: be fully present for your child, and give yourself room for the rest later.

## What about the first anniversary of leaving?
Many people mark it. Some celebrate. Some grieve. Many do both. There's no right way.

## A year of hard dates
Make a list of dates that may be hard in the first year or two:

- the day you left, and the day you met
- your wedding or relationship anniversary
- birthdays: yours, theirs, the children's
- holidays and festivals
- court dates, police dates, the day an order was made
- dates of incidents your body remembers

Put them in your calendar, with a note a week before. Knowing they're coming takes away some of the ambush.

## A plan for a hard day
For each hard date, decide:

- who you'll be with, or talk to
- what you'll do in the morning, afternoon and evening
- what you'll avoid, like social media, old photos or alcohol
- one small thing that's just for you

Keep the plan simple. The aim is to get through the day gently.

## Telling people in advance
> **Say:** "Next Thursday is a year since I left. I might be a bit low. Could you check in with me that day?"

Most people are glad to be told. It lets them help without guessing.

## Court and police dates
The date of a hearing, an arrest or a statement can become an anniversary too. Your body may react days before you remember why. If you feel anxious for no clear reason, check the calendar.

## Children and anniversaries
Children may have their own hard dates: the day you moved, a birthday spent apart, the first holiday in two homes. Ask them what they'd like to do. Some want things the same; some want something new.

## Making new dates
Over time, you can add dates that belong to your new life: the day you moved into your own place, the day you started a job, a trip you took with friends. These become anniversaries worth marking.

## How Mark got through the first anniversary
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Mark knew the anniversary of leaving was coming in October. He felt anxious for a week beforehand without knowing why, then saw the date on the calendar. He made a plan: a morning walk with a friend, lunch with his sister, no social media and an early night. He told his manager he might be quiet that day.

The day was sad in places, and fine in others. In the evening he wrote down three things he'd done in the year since leaving that he couldn't have done before.

## Put the dates in now
Add the hard dates for the next year to your calendar, each with a reminder a week before.

## Sources
- US Department of Veterans Affairs, National Center for PTSD: Anniversary reactions to a traumatic event.
- Rando, T. A. (1993). *Treatment of Complicated Mourning.* Research Press.

=== memory
title: Memory, and doubting yourself
question: Am I making it up? Why is my memory so patchy?
seo: Am I Making It Up? Memory and Self-Doubt After Abuse
short: If you keep asking "am I making it up?", you're not alone. Stress makes memory patchy, and gaslighting teaches you to distrust your own account. Gaps don't mean it didn't happen. A written record, made at the time, protects you from both.
---
Some parts are vivid. Others are fog. The order of events won't line up when you try to tell it. And a voice keeps asking: maybe it wasn't that bad. Maybe you're exaggerating. Maybe you made it up.

## Why is my memory so patchy?
Stress and trauma affect how memories are stored and recalled. Intense moments can be remembered in sharp detail, sometimes too sharp. Long stretches can feel blurred or empty. Sequences get jumbled. This is a common, well-studied effect of stress on memory, not a sign that you're unreliable.

> Gaps in memory don't cancel what you do remember. Both can be true about the same years.

## Am I making it up?
The doubt itself is often a clue. Many people who were controlled were told for years that their memory was wrong: "That never happened." "You're too sensitive." "You're crazy." That's gaslighting, and it leaves a habit of second-guessing that outlasts the relationship.

The Decoder describes [gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting) and the self-doubt it trains, [self-gaslighting induction](/playbook/self-gaslighting-induction).

People who invent abuse don't usually lie awake wondering if they did.

## How do I trust my own account again?
- **Write things down,** from now on. A note made at the time beats memory made later. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **Collect anchors.** Old messages, emails, photos, diary entries, things you told friends at the time. They show what happened when your memory wobbles.
- **Talk to people who were there,** or who you told back then.
- **Notice the voice.** When doubt arrives, ask whose voice it sounds like.
- **Accept some gaps.** You don't need a perfect timeline to know what happened.

## Will patchy memory hurt me in court?
It can feel that way. Inconsistent dates can be used to question your credibility. That's exactly why a record made at the time matters so much: it doesn't depend on memory. Tell your lawyer about any gaps early, so they're not a surprise. Courts and trained professionals increasingly understand that trauma affects memory. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## When should I get help?
If memories intrude often, if you have flashbacks or nightmares, or if large parts of your life feel missing, talk to a therapist who understands trauma. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).


## What if I remember new things later?
Memories can surface months or years afterwards, often set off by a smell, a place or a conversation. Write them down with the date you remembered them. Tell your lawyer if there's a case. Remembering late doesn't make a memory less true.

## How do I tell my story if parts are missing?
Use what you know. Give approximate times ("spring 2022"), tie events to anchors ("just before her fifth birthday"), and say clearly what you don't remember. A clear "I don't remember that part" is more credible than a guess.

## How fear changes memory
When you're frightened, the brain focuses on survival. Some details are stored very sharply, like a sound, a smell or the look on someone's face. Others, like the order of events or the date, may be missing or jumbled. This is a well-known effect of stress on memory, and it doesn't mean you're lying or mistaken about what matters.

## What gaslighting does to memory
If you were told for years that things didn't happen, or happened differently, you may have learned to doubt yourself automatically. Even after leaving, you may hear their voice: "You're imagining it. That's not how it was."

Writing things down as they happen now, and rebuilding a timeline of the past, can slowly restore trust in your own mind.

## Building a timeline from fragments
1. Write down every memory you have, in any order, one per line or card.
2. Add anything that can anchor it in time: a season, a house, a job, a child's age, a holiday.
3. Look for outside records: texts, emails, photos, bank statements, medical notes, police reports.
4. Put the anchored memories in order, and leave gaps where you don't know.

A timeline with gaps is still a strong account. Honest gaps are more believable than invented detail.

## Triggers
Memories can return suddenly, set off by a smell, a song, a place or a phrase. You may feel the emotion before you know what memory it belongs to. Grounding helps in the moment. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing). If a new memory comes back, write it down without forcing more.

## "I don't remember" in interviews
If police, a lawyer or a court asks about something you don't remember, it's fine to say so.

> **Say:** "I don't remember that part clearly. What I do remember is..."

Never guess to fill a gap. Saying what you remember, and what you don't, makes your account more credible, not less.

## Building the timeline with gaps
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Before a court statement, Leila tried to write her history and froze. She remembered fragments: a smashed phone, a winter night locked out, the time he followed her to work. She wrote each fragment on a card. Then she matched them to anchors: which house they lived in, how old her son was, a holiday she had photos of. Her bank statements and texts filled some gaps.

Her final timeline had gaps marked "I don't remember exactly when." Her lawyer said those honest gaps made it stronger.

## One card today
Write one memory on a card or a line, with any anchor you have. That's the start of a timeline.

- Sweet, P. L. (2019). The sociology of gaslighting. *American Sociological Review*, 84(5), 851–875.
- Stern, R. (2007). *The Gaslight Effect.* Morgan Road Books.
