=== no-contact-low-contact
title: No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid
question: Should I go no contact with my ex, and what if I can't?
seo: No Contact With an Ex: When It Works and When You Can't
short: No contact with an ex means no messages, calls, meetings or news passed through others. It's often the fastest way to let things settle. When children, a court order or a shared workplace make that impossible, low contact keeps it to the minimum, in writing, on your terms.
---
Every book and forum says the same thing: go no contact. For some people that's the best advice there is. For others it's impossible. Most people end up somewhere in between.

## What does no contact with an ex mean?
No contact means you don't communicate with them at all:

- no calls, messages or emails, in either direction
- no meetings, "just to talk"
- no checking their social media
- no news passed through friends or family
- no replies when they reach out

It's a decision about your behaviour, not theirs. They may keep trying. No contact means you don't answer.

## Why does no contact work?
Because every reply keeps the channel open, and silence closes it. It also gives your nervous system a break from constant alert, and gives the trauma bond room to loosen. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).

> No contact is a rule you keep, especially on the days you don't want to. Waiting to feel ready for it rarely works.

## When is no contact not possible?
- **You share children.** Courts usually expect parents to communicate about the children.
- **There's a court case.** You may have to exchange documents or attend hearings.
- **You share a workplace, a business, a home or a community.**
- **There are financial ties** that take time to untangle.

In these cases, aim for low contact.

## What is low contact?
Low contact means the least contact the situation needs, on terms that protect you:

- **One channel only.** A co-parenting app or one email address. Block the rest. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).
- **Writing, not calls or visits.** Writing gives you time to think and a record. See [Grey rock and written-only contact](ch:grey-rock-and-written-only-contact).
- **Business only.** The children's logistics, the house sale, the court process. Nothing about the relationship, your new life or your feelings.
- **Short replies, at a time you choose.** See [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).
- **Third parties where helpful.** A lawyer, a mediator, a supervised contact centre.

The Decoder's page on [controlled communication](/playbook/controlled-communication) shows how the other side can use contact rules against you. Low contact turns that around: you set the channel.

## How do I go no contact with kids involved?
You can't go fully no contact with a co-parent, but you can get close:

- use a co-parenting app for everything
- set handovers at school, or through a third person
- keep messages to dates, times and the children's needs
- don't respond to anything else

Many people call this parallel parenting. [Co-parenting or parallel parenting?](ch:co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting) explains how it works and how to ask a court for it.

## How do I tell them?
You don't have to announce it. If you want to say something once:

> **Say:** "I won't be in contact from now on. If there's something about the children, please use the app. I won't reply to anything else."

Then stop. Don't explain, defend or repeat it.

## What about blocking?
Blocking is fine. If you might need evidence later, screenshot messages before blocking, or use a filter that sends their messages to a folder you don't read every day. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

If they get round a block with new numbers or accounts, that can be harassment or stalking. Keep a record and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## No contact and social media
No contact includes online. Each look at their profile, and each look they get at yours, keeps a thread open.

- Block or mute them on every platform, and their closest allies if they pass things on.
- Make your accounts private, or take a break from them.
- Remove shared photos from places they can see.
- Turn off "people you may know" suggestions where you can.

If blocking would make them escalate, muting is a quieter option: you stop seeing them, and they don't get a notification.

## No contact with their family
Their parents, siblings and friends may keep reaching out. Some mean well. Some are passing messages.

You can be polite and firm:

> **Say:** "I care about you, and I'm not able to talk about [name] or our relationship. If you want to stay in touch with me, I'd need that to stay separate."

If they keep passing things on, you can treat them the same way as your ex: limited, written, or none. When there are children, grandparents' time with them can be arranged through the other parent's time, without you in the middle.

## Cards, gifts and letters
Birthday cards, a parcel at the door, a letter "for when you're ready." These can be harder to ignore than a text.

- You don't have to open them. You can give them to a friend to check for anything important.
- Keep them, unopened if you like, as part of your record.
- Don't send anything back with a message; that's contact.
- If they keep coming after you've asked them to stop, that can count as harassment.

## The urge to make contact: what to do in the moment
The urge often arrives late at night, after a memory, a song, or a bad day. It passes faster than it feels like it will.

1. **Delay.** Tell yourself you'll decide in 20 minutes. Set a timer.
2. **Move.** Stand up, change rooms, step outside.
3. **Write it instead.** Write the message on paper, not in your phone. Don't send it.
4. **Call someone else.** A friend, a helpline, anyone.
5. **Read your record.** Remind yourself why you chose no contact.

If you still want to contact them the next day, talk it through with someone first.

## Family events
No contact gets complicated at funerals, weddings and christenings. Decide in advance how you'll handle being in the same room: a short "hello," then distance. Bring someone with you. Leave early if you need to. Being polite in public isn't breaking no contact.

## What if I break no contact?
Most people do at least once. It doesn't mean you've failed or have to start from zero. Notice what happened when you made contact, and decide what you'll do next time.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* puts it this way: "No contact is not a feeling. No contact is a system." See its [quick-start](/feminine/post-separation-control#quick-start) and [The Sovereign Playbook](/feminine/the-sovereign-playbook#core-concept).

## Ninety days of no contact
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Rosa went no contact, she blocked her ex on every app, asked friends not to pass on news and put his gifts in a box at her mum's. The first two weeks were the hardest: she wanted to unblock him every night. She called a friend instead, or wrote the message on paper and tore it up.

At day thirty she noticed whole hours without thinking about him. At day ninety she went to a party and didn't check whether he'd be there. She still had hard days. She also had her evenings back.

## Count the days
Mark each day without contact on a calendar. On the hard nights, look at how many there are.

=== grey-rock-and-written-only-contact
title: Grey rock and written-only contact
question: What is the grey rock method, and does it work?
seo: Grey Rock Method: How It Works, When to Use It, When Not
short: The grey rock method means being as dull as a grey rock when you have to deal with an abusive ex: short, flat, factual replies that give them nothing to react to. It can work well in writing and at handovers. It can also make some people escalate, so use it with a safety plan.
---
You can't go no contact. So you look for the next best thing: a way to deal with them that gives them nothing.

## What is the grey rock method?
The grey rock method means making yourself uninteresting to someone who feeds on reaction. Like a grey rock on a path, you're there, but there's nothing to pick up.

In practice:

- short answers: "Yes." "No." "Noted." "5pm Friday."
- a flat, polite tone, without emotion
- no personal news, opinions or feelings
- no arguing, defending or explaining
- no questions that open a conversation

The idea is that if pressure gets no reaction, the person gets bored and moves their attention elsewhere.

## When should I not use grey rock?
> Read this part first. Grey rock can make some people try harder to get a reaction. If the person has been violent, has threatened you, or escalates when ignored, grey rock alone is not a safety plan.

Avoid relying on grey rock:

- in person with someone who may become violent
- when you're living together. See [Still under the same roof](ch:still-under-the-same-roof).
- when a sudden change in your behaviour could alert them that you're planning to leave
- where the flatness could look like coldness to a court, without context

If any of these apply, plan with a domestic abuse service and use [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

## Does grey rock work?
There's very little research on grey rock itself. It comes from survivors and the people who support them. Many people find it reduces conflict and protects their peace, especially combined with written-only contact. Some find the other person escalates at first, then gives up. Some find it doesn't help at all. Watch what happens and adjust.

The Decoder's page on [stonewalling](/playbook/stonewalling) describes refusing to engage as a control tactic. Grey rock is different. Its purpose is to answer what's needed and nothing more, never to punish someone with silence.

## How do I grey rock someone?
In writing, it looks like this:

> They send: "I can't believe you'd do this to the kids. You've always been selfish. Are you even going to answer about Saturday?"
>
> **Say:** "Saturday pickup is 10am at school as agreed."

> They send: "I heard you've got a new boyfriend. Hope he knows what he's getting."
>
> No reply needed.

At a handover:

> They say: "So you're just going to ignore me now?"
>
> **Say:** "Here's her bag. Her medicine's in the front pocket. Bye."

## What is written-only contact?
Written-only contact means all communication happens in writing: a co-parenting app, email or text. No phone calls, no conversations at the door.

Why it helps:

- **A record.** Everything is dated and kept. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **Time.** You can read, wait, and reply when you're calm.
- **Less pressure.** It's harder to be talked round in writing.
- **Better behaviour.** People often write more carefully when they know it could be read by a judge.

Courts often support written-only arrangements in high-conflict cases, especially through co-parenting apps. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).

## How do I set it up?
> **Say:** "From now on I'll only communicate in writing, by email or through the app. I won't answer calls or discuss things at handovers."

Then hold to it. If they call, let it go to voicemail and reply in writing if a reply is needed. More replies are in [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).

## Five grey rock exchanges
**They send:** "You've ruined everything. The kids hate being with you."

**Reply:** "Pickup is at 5 on Friday."

**They send:** "Who was that man at your house?"

**Reply:** No reply needed. If there's a children's question in the message, answer only that.

**They send:** "I've been thinking about us. Can we talk properly?"

**Reply:** "I'll keep our messages to the children's arrangements."

**They send:** "Fine, I'll take them to my mum's this weekend instead. You can't stop me."

**Reply:** "The order says they're with me this weekend. Pickup is at 10 on Saturday."

**They send:** "You never answer me. What kind of parent are you?"

**Reply:** "I answer messages about the children. Is there something about them you need?"

Each reply is short, polite and about the arrangement. It gives nothing to argue with.

## Grey rock in person
At handovers and school events:

- Keep your face neutral and your voice even.
- Say less than feels natural: "Hi. Here's the bag. Bye."
- Don't react to comments about your appearance, new partner or home.
- Stand where you can leave easily, and have somewhere to go afterwards.
- If they start an argument, say: "I'm not discussing this now. Message me on the app." Then leave.

## Grey rock with the children watching
Children notice tension even when nothing is said. Grey rock can look cold to them. Soften it for their sake: warm with the children, neutral with the other parent.

> **Say (to your child, cheerfully):** "Have a great weekend. I'll see you Sunday."

If your child asks why you don't talk to their other parent, keep it simple: "Grown-ups sometimes talk less after they separate. We both love you."

## Grey rock is not ignoring
Grey rock means answering what needs answering, briefly, without emotion. It doesn't mean ignoring messages about the children, missing deadlines or refusing all contact. Courts expect parents to communicate about their children. Grey rock lets you do that without being pulled in.

## How do I keep grey rock from wearing me down?
Being a grey rock is tiring. You're holding back things you want to say. Say them somewhere else: to a friend, a therapist, a journal. And remember it's a tool for this one relationship, not a new personality.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers grey rock in [The Sovereign Playbook](/feminine/the-sovereign-playbook#core-concept), and the same "respond, don't react" rule in [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#r-respond-dont-react).

## The questions to ask before you reply
- Does this message ask something about the children, safety or money with a deadline?
- If yes, what's the shortest polite answer?
- If no, can I leave it unanswered?

Three questions, every time. They take ten seconds and stop most arguments before they start.

=== words-you-can-use
title: Words you can use
question: What phrases can I use when co-parenting with a narcissist or an abusive ex?
seo: Phrases to Use When Co-Parenting With a Narcissist
short: These are phrases to use when co-parenting with a narcissist or any controlling ex: short replies to accusations, schedule games, guilt, emergencies, questions about your life and threats of court. Keep every reply brief, factual, polite and final. Then stop.
---
You know what you want to say. You also know it would start a war. This chapter gives you replies you can copy, adjust and send.

## How should I reply to an abusive ex?
Bill Eddy, a lawyer and therapist who works with high-conflict cases, created the BIFF method. A good reply is:

- **Brief:** a few sentences at most.
- **Informative:** facts only.
- **Friendly:** polite, even if you don't feel it.
- **Firm:** it closes the topic and doesn't invite more.

Before you send anything, read it as if a judge will see it. Take out anything defensive, sarcastic or emotional. Then wait an hour if you can.

> The aim is to end the exchange, in as few words as you can.

## Phrases to use when co-parenting with a narcissist
These are grouped by the message you've received. Swap in your details.

**When they accuse you of something untrue**
> **Say:** "I don't agree with that. Pickup is still 5pm Friday."

**When they rant, insult or bring up the past**
> **Say:** "I'll respond to anything about the children's arrangements."

Or don't reply at all if nothing needs an answer.

**When they change the schedule at the last minute**
> **Say:** "The agreed time is 5pm. If you can't make that, let me know by Thursday and I'll keep the children with me this weekend."

**When they demand an answer now**
> **Say:** "I've received your message. I'll reply by Wednesday."

**When they ask about your new partner, your home or your life**
> **Say:** "That's private. Is there anything about the children you need?"

**When they try guilt**
The Decoder calls this [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping).
> **Say:** "I understand you're unhappy with the arrangement. It stays as agreed."

**When they threaten: "Do this or else"**
The Decoder calls this [emotional blackmail](/playbook/emotional-blackmail).
> **Say:** "I won't be changing the arrangement. If you want to change it, please go through the court or the mediator."

Keep any threat. If it's about your safety, see [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

**When they threaten court**
> **Say:** "That's your choice. I'll follow the current order."

**When they announce an "emergency"**
> **Say:** "If it's an emergency, please call 999 [or your local number]. If it's about the children, I'm happy to discuss it here."

**When they want to "just talk"**
> **Say:** "I'll only communicate in writing about the children."

**When they apologise or say they've changed**
> **Say:** "Thank you. I'll keep our contact to the children's arrangements."

See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) for more on this one.

**When they send messages through the children**
> **Say (to your ex, in writing):** "Please message me directly about arrangements, not through the children."

> **Say (to your child):** "You don't need to pass on messages. That's a grown-up job. Thanks for telling me."

**When they contact your family or friends**
> **Say (to the friend):** "Please don't pass on messages. If it matters, they can contact me in writing."

**When money is owed**
> **Say:** "The child support payment for [month] hasn't arrived. Please pay it by [date]." If it doesn't come, let the official service deal with it.

**When they apologise or ask to get back together**
> **Say:** "Thank you for your message. I'm not going to reopen our relationship. I'll keep contact to the children's arrangements."

**When someone else asks on their behalf**
> **Say:** "I know you mean well. I'm not discussing [name] with anyone."

**When they ask to meet in person**
> **Say:** "I'd rather keep things in writing. Please send me what you'd like to discuss."

**When they say you're not cooperating**
> **Say:** "I've answered each message about the children. I'm happy to keep doing that in writing."

## What should I never put in writing?
- insults, swearing or sarcasm, however deserved
- threats, including "or I'll call my lawyer," unless you mean to act
- details about your new home, routine or relationships
- anything you'd be embarrassed for a judge to read
- long explanations of your feelings

## What if they reply with more?
You don't have to answer a reply to your reply. If the follow-up doesn't need an answer, let it sit. [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer) helps you decide.

## Writing for a future judge
Imagine every message you send being read aloud in court. That doesn't mean being stiff. It means being the calmest person in the conversation.

- Short and polite, every time.
- Facts, dates and times, not feelings.
- No sarcasm, no capital letters, no "as I've told you before."
- Focus on the children's needs.
- Never reply in the first heat; wait an hour.

Your messages then become evidence of who you are, sitting next to theirs.

## Can I keep these somewhere?
The [pocket card](ch:pocket-card-words-you-can-use) has the most useful replies on one page. Keep it somewhere private. Write your own too: you know their patterns better than anyone.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* has soft, firm and final scripts for digital contact in [The Digital Leash](/feminine/the-digital-leash#r-respond-scripts-soft-firm-final).

## Make the replies yours
Copy the five replies you're most likely to need into your phone's notes, under a plain title like "Shopping list." Change the words until they sound like you. When a hard message arrives, you'll have something calm to start from.

=== messages-you-dont-have-to-answer
title: Messages you don't have to answer
question: Should I respond to my ex? Which messages need a reply?
seo: Should I Respond to My Ex? Which Messages Need a Reply
short: Should you respond to your ex? Only when a message needs an answer: a court deadline, the children's arrangements, a legal or financial matter. Insults, guilt, nostalgia, questions about your life and "we need to talk" don't. Waiting, then replying briefly or not at all, protects you.
---
Your phone buzzes. Your stomach drops. You read it three times and start drafting a reply in your head.

Stop. Not every message needs an answer.

## Should I respond to my ex?
Sort the message first. Ask: **does this need an answer to keep me, my children or my legal position safe?**

**Messages that usually need an answer:**
- arrangements for the children: times, places, health, school
- a court deadline, a document, or a question from a lawyer or the court
- a financial matter with a deadline: the house sale, a joint bill
- a real emergency involving the children

**Messages that usually don't:**
- insults, accusations, rants
- guilt: "after everything I did for you"
- nostalgia, love, apologies, "I miss you"
- questions about your new partner, home or life
- "we need to talk," "call me," "why are you ignoring me"
- threats that are meant to provoke a reaction (keep them; report them if they're about safety)

The Decoder's page on [false dilemmas](/playbook/false-dilemma) describes messages that offer only two bad options: "Either you answer me tonight or I'll tell the kids why." You don't have to pick either one.

> You decide what's urgent. A deadline in their message doesn't set yours.

## How quickly do I have to reply?
Unless there's a real deadline, you choose. Waiting a few hours, or a day, does three things: it lets the first rush of fear pass, it lets you write calmly, and it teaches them that pressure doesn't speed you up. The Decoder covers the pressure itself in [time control](/playbook/time-control).

If you're worried a slow reply could look bad in court, reply to the parts that matter within a reasonable time, and ignore the rest.

## How do I answer only part of a message?
Pull out the one thing that needs a reply, and answer just that:

> They send: "You're a terrible mother and everyone knows it. Also I'm taking them to my mum's on Sunday so I'll drop them back at 7 not 5. Don't bother arguing."
>
> **Say:** "Return time is 5pm as agreed. If you'd like to change it, please ask by Friday."

More replies are in [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).

## How do I read messages without being pulled in?
- Set times to check messages: once in the morning and once in the evening, for example.
- Turn off notifications for their messages, or for the app you use with them.
- Read with someone beside you, or forward to a trusted friend to read first.
- Some people ask a friend or family member to screen all messages and pass on only what needs action.

## Should I keep messages I don't answer?
Yes, if there's any chance you'll need them. Export or screenshot them, then file them away. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## Three piles
Sort every message into one of three piles before you do anything:

1. **Answer:** questions about the children, safety, money with a deadline, or court.
2. **Record:** insults, threats, accusations, anything you might need to show someone. Save it, don't answer it.
3. **Ignore:** everything else: opinions, history, guilt, attempts to start a conversation.

Most messages land in piles two and three. Pile one gets a short, calm answer.

## Filters and a separate inbox
You don't have to see their messages the moment they arrive.

- Create a separate email address used only for them, and check it at set times.
- Use filters to send their messages straight to a folder.
- Turn off notifications for their number or the co-parenting app, and check it once a day.
- On most phones you can silence a contact without blocking them.

## Having someone else read them first
Some people ask a trusted friend or family member to read messages first and tell them only what needs an answer. It can take a lot of weight off. Choose someone calm, who won't argue with your ex or share the messages with anyone.

## When a message contains a threat
Don't reply. Save it straight away: screenshot it with the date and sender showing, and back it up somewhere they can't reach. If it threatens your safety, the children or anyone else, report it to police. If there's a protective order, a threat may break it. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

## Reading once a day or once a week
If the messages are constant and none of them needs an urgent answer, you can choose a set time to read them: once a day, or once a week if a court order or lawyer allows it. Tell them once, in writing:

> **Say:** "I'll check messages about the children each evening. For emergencies, please call [number or service]."

Then stick to it. The pressure to answer instantly is part of the pattern. Reading on your schedule takes that back.

## What if not answering makes them angrier?
It might, at first. If silence leads to threats, turning up, or contact through others, that's a safety matter, not a reason to answer. Keep a record and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment) and [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* has ready if-then plans for this kind of contact in [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#if-then-plans).

## A week of messages, sorted
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

In one week, Priyanka's ex sent twenty-three messages. She sorted them each evening into three piles. Pile one, needing an answer: two, one about pickup time, one about a school form. Pile two, to record: five, including two insults and one veiled threat. Pile three, to ignore: sixteen, about the past, her new haircut and how she was "destroying the family."

She sent two short replies that week. She spent ten minutes a day on messages instead of two hours. The threat went into her record and, later, to her lawyer.

## Tonight's sort
Open the last ten messages from them, sort them into the three piles, and answer only pile one.
