=== a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left
title: A safety plan for after you've left
question: How do I stay safe after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: Safety Plan After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: A safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship covers your home, your routines, work, school, handovers, your phone and car, and who to call. It matters most in the first months and around court dates. Write it on paper and keep it where they can't find it.
---
Everyone talks about a safety plan for the day you leave. Hardly anyone mentions the months after. You're back to school runs and supermarket trips, and the person you left knows far more about your life than you'd like. That's what a safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship is for.

If you're in danger right now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) first.

> Keep this plan somewhere they can't reach: in your head, with a friend, or on paper hidden away from home. Don't leave it on a shared device.

## What is a safety plan after leaving?
A safety plan after leaving is a short, private set of decisions you make ahead of time about staying safe once you've gone. It covers your home, your routines, work, the children, your phone and car, and who you'll call if something happens. You think it through once, calmly, so you never have to work it out in a panic.

## Why do I need one if I've already left?
Because separation is a time of raised risk. The person you left may know your routines, your workplace, your children's school and your friends. [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first) explains the risk. Making a plan doesn't mean you expect the worst. It means you won't be making it up at 2am with your heart pounding.

The [safety plan worksheet](ch:safety-plan-worksheet) puts all of this on one page, if you'd rather fill in boxes than read lists.

## Home
- Change the locks if they ever had a key. Ask your landlord, or a domestic abuse service, which may help with the cost.
- Add a door chain, a peephole or a video doorbell that only you control.
- Light the outside of your home at night.
- Keep your phone charged and near you at night.
- Know how you'd get out of each room you spend time in, and where you'd go.
- Tell a neighbour you trust what the person and their car look like, and ask them to call the police if they see them.
- If they turn up, don't open the door. Call the police if they won't leave or you feel threatened. Write it down afterwards.
- Once the practical side is done, [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours) is about making it feel like yours again.

## Routines and routes
Anyone trying to find you will watch for patterns. For the first months especially, make yourself a little harder to predict.

- Vary when you leave home and which way you go.
- Change your gym, supermarket or coffee shop if they knew you went there.
- Park in well-lit places near entrances.
- Know where the nearest police station and busy public places are on your usual routes.

## Work
- Tell someone you trust at work: a manager, HR or security.
- Give reception or security a photo of the person. Ask them not to let them in, and not to confirm you're there.
- Ask for your calls to be screened and your name taken off public staff lists.
- Ask about changing your hours or work location for a while.
- In many places employers must take reasonable steps to keep staff safe, and some countries give paid leave for domestic abuse. [Work again](ch:work-again) has more.

## School and childcare
- Tell the school, nursery or childcare who may collect your children and who may not.
- Give them a copy of any court order, with a photo of the person if collection is restricted.
- Agree a password that anyone collecting your child must give.
- Ask the school not to give out your new address or phone number.

## Handovers
If you have to hand children over, plan each exchange. Use a public place with cameras, a school handover, a trusted third person, or a supervised contact centre. Avoid handing over at your home if you can. [Handovers](ch:handovers) goes through it in detail.

## Your phone, accounts and car
- Change passwords from a device they never touched, starting with your email.
- Turn off location sharing, and check for tracking apps and trackers. [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology) has the steps.
- Check your car for trackers, especially if they had access to it.

## People
- Pick two people you trust and tell them the plan.
- Agree a code word or emoji that means "call the police" or "come now."
- Ask one of them to check on you on court days and other high-risk days.

## What to keep ready
Some people keep a small bag at a friend's house or at work, even after they've left:

- copies of documents and court orders
- some cash and a bank card
- medicines and spare glasses
- phone charger, spare keys
- a written list of important numbers, in case your phone is taken

## Making the plan with a service
You can make a safety plan on your own, but it's better with someone trained. A domestic abuse service has seen hundreds of situations like yours. They'll think of things you won't, like which routes are hardest to follow or what to do about a shared car.

The conversation usually takes under an hour. They'll ask about the person you left, what's happened since, your home, your work and the children.

Then you build the plan together, one part at a time. You keep it. Nothing is decided for you.

> **Say:** "I've left my partner and I'd like help making a safety plan for after leaving."

## A plan for the children
Children need a plan they can manage at their age.

- **Under 7:** teach them your phone number and how to call the emergency number. Practise with a toy phone. Pick a safe person they can go to at school.
- **7 to 12:** agree a code word that means "go to your room and lock the door" or "go to the neighbour's." Show them how to call for help. Tell them it's never their job to protect you.
- **Teenagers:** talk honestly about what to do if their other parent turns up, asks questions or asks them to keep secrets. Check their phone settings with them. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).

Tell the school and anyone who collects them who can and can't take them, and give them a copy of any court order.

Keep the children's part about staying safe, never about what their other parent is like. If it's ever quoted back at you, you can say it plainly: this is a safety plan, and it's about keeping them safe.

## Your online life in the plan
- Don't post where you are while you're there. Post later, or not at all.
- Ask friends not to tag you or post photos showing your home, your street, your car or your children's school.
- Turn off location tags on photos.
- Check who can see your profiles, and remove mutual friends who pass things on.
- Think about pausing accounts for a while. You can come back.

## Pets in the plan
Abusers sometimes threaten or harm pets to control a former partner. If you have a pet:

- Keep it inside or supervised where you can.
- Make sure its microchip and vet records are in your name.
- Ask domestic abuse services about pet fostering schemes; many places have them for people leaving abuse.

See [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

## If you're disabled or have health needs
- Keep a supply of medicine and any equipment you need somewhere you can reach.
- If a carer or support worker is involved, decide who can know your plans.
- Ask for an accessible refuge or a service experienced with disability.
- If you rely on the person you left for care, read the disability section in [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

## Keeping the plan current
Update it whenever something changes: a new address, a new job, a court date, a new relationship, or new behaviour from them. Read it through every few months, even when nothing seems different. A plan that fitted last year may not fit now.

## When is the risk usually higher?
Plan ahead for:

- court hearings and decisions
- your new relationship becoming known
- birthdays, anniversaries and holidays
- the day they're served with papers
- the day you stop replying
- any time they've just been refused something

On those days, tell someone where you'll be, avoid being alone at places they know, and keep your phone charged.

## If they break a court order
Write down what happened, when and where. Keep messages. Report the breach to the police. In most places, a pattern of recorded breaches is what gets a stronger response, but the rules depend on where you live. [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders) and [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) have the details.

## If anything on this list feels dangerous to do
Some steps, like changing every password at once, can alert someone who is watching. If you're worried about how they'll react, plan the order with a domestic abuse service. [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) helps you judge how careful to be.

## Kemi's plan, one part at a time
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Kemi made her plan with a support worker over two phone calls. Home: a door chain and a camera doorbell set up under her own account. Routes: she varied her drive to work and parked near the entrance.

Work: reception had a photo of her ex. School: the office knew who could collect her son, and had the order. Phone: location sharing off, new passwords. People: her neighbour had her sister's number.

The plan sat in a notebook at her friend's house. When her ex turned up at the school gate in October, the office called her, then the police. Nobody had to work out what to do.

## Start with one room
If a whole plan feels like too much, start with home. Grab a pen and write down the one change that would make you feel safest there: new locks, a door chain, or a neighbour who knows their car. Put the note somewhere they'd never look.

=== warning-signs-to-take-seriously
title: Warning signs to take seriously
question: What are the domestic violence risk factors I shouldn't ignore?
seo: Domestic Violence Risk Factors and Warning Signs
short: Some domestic violence risk factors are linked to serious harm: past strangling or choking, threats to kill, access to weapons, stalking, violence that's getting worse, and separation itself. If any apply to you, talk to a domestic abuse service or the police now. If none apply, that doesn't prove you're safe.
---
Can a list tell you whether you're safe? No, and I won't pretend it can. This chapter is short and plain on purpose. It lists the signs services treat as most serious. It isn't a score. Only you, with help, can judge your own risk.

If you're in danger now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## Which warning signs matter most?
Research on serious and fatal domestic violence has found these risk factors again and again:

- **Past strangling or choking.** Even once. Even if it left no mark.
- **Threats to kill you, the children, or themselves.**
- **Access to a gun or other weapon**, or threats with one.
- **Violence that's getting more frequent or more severe.**
- **Stalking:** following, watching, turning up, tracking.
- **Extreme jealousy or control**, and saying things like "if I can't have you, no one can."
- **Separation**, especially recent, especially from someone very controlling.
- **Violence during pregnancy.** See [Pregnant, or with a new baby](ch:pregnant-or-with-a-new-baby).
- **Forced sex.**
- **Threats of suicide used to control you.** The Decoder covers this in [Emotional Blackmail via Self-Harm Threats](/playbook/emotional-blackmail-via-self-harm-threats).
- **Unemployment, heavy drinking or drug use** alongside the above.
- **Breaking court orders.**
- **Threats to hurt or kill a pet.** See [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

The Decoder's page on [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes the threats and shows of force that often sit around these signs.

## Why does strangulation matter so much?
Because it's one of the strongest warning signs there is. A study of women attacked by partners found that a past attempt to strangle them was linked to a many-times-higher risk of later attempted or completed murder.

Strangling can also cause injuries you can't see: brain injury from lack of oxygen, damage to the throat and neck, and strokes days or weeks later. If you were ever strangled or choked, even long ago, tell a doctor. Mention a changed voice, trouble swallowing, headaches, memory problems or dizziness.

It's also evidence, so tell the police or your lawyer that it happened. [Getting sick after leaving](ch:getting-sick-after-leaving) covers what to tell a doctor.

## Signs in how they talk about you
Words can tell you a lot about risk:

- **Ownership:** "You're mine." "If I can't have you, no one can."
- **Threats to kill,** even if said as a joke or in anger.
- **Fixation:** they talk about you constantly, to anyone, months after the break.
- **Blame for everything:** their job, their health, their losses are all your fault.
- **Talk of having nothing left to lose.**

If you hear these, write them down with the date and who heard them.

## Signs around children and pets
- Threats to take the children and disappear
- Threats to harm the children, or to harm you in front of them
- Threats to harm or kill a pet, or harming one
- Using the children to find out where you live

Any of these is a reason to talk to the police or a domestic abuse service about risk, and to tell the school.

## Signs it's escalating
Escalation means things getting more frequent, more frightening or more dangerous over time. Look through your record for:

- contact that's getting more frequent or more threatening
- turning up more often, or closer to home
- breaking a court order
- new behaviour, like following you or contacting your new partner
- violence that's getting worse

Escalation matters more than any single incident. If you see it, update your safety plan and ask for a risk assessment.

## Why your fear counts
Researchers who follow people after domestic violence have found that a victim's own sense of danger is one of the better predictors of what happens next. You know this person. You notice the change in tone, the look, the silence. If you're frightened, take that seriously, even if you can't explain why.

## Saying it without sounding dramatic
Some people hold back because they don't want to "overreact." Stick to facts and let them speak for themselves:

> **Say:** "Since I left, he has turned up at my work twice and messaged my sister asking where I live. In the past he strangled me once. I'm frightened that it's getting worse."

That isn't drama. It's the kind of detail professionals are trained to act on.

## What should I do if some of these apply?
1. **Talk to a domestic abuse service** today. They use risk checklists like these every day and can help you plan. [Find help](ch:find-help).
2. **Tell the police**, especially about threats, weapons or strangling.
3. **Ask about a protective order.** See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
4. **Make or update your safety plan.** See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
5. **Tell someone you trust** what's happening, so you're not carrying it alone.

When you call, use plain words that get attention: "He strangled me last year." "She has said she'll kill me." "He has a gun." "He's been following me." Professionals are trained to act on those sentences.

## What if none of these apply?
That's good news, but it isn't a guarantee. These lists come from patterns across many cases. They can't see your situation the way you can. If you're frightened, trust that. Your fear is information.

## What if they're threatening to harm themselves?
Threats of suicide can be real distress, a way of controlling you, or both. You aren't responsible for keeping them alive, and you don't have to go back to prevent it.

If the threat sounds immediate, call emergency services and tell them. Then step back. If thoughts of harming yourself are coming up for you too, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

=== surveillance-and-technology
title: Surveillance and technology
question: Is my ex tracking me, and how do I check?
seo: Is My Ex Tracking Me? How to Check Your Phone and Car
short: If you're asking "is my ex tracking me," check four places: shared accounts, location sharing, hidden apps on your phone, and trackers on your car or belongings. Do it from a device they never touched, and in a calm order, because sudden changes can alert someone who is watching.
---
They seem to know where you've been. They bring up a conversation you had with a friend. They turn up at the café you go to on Saturdays. And you start wondering if you're losing the plot.

You may not be. Tracking after a separation is common, and it's usually done with boring, everyday tools: shared accounts, location sharing and cheap trackers. Nobody needs a spy kit when they still know your iCloud password.

## Is my ex tracking me?
Signs that someone may be tracking you:

- they know where you are, or were, without being told
- they know what you said in private messages or emails
- your phone's battery drains fast or it runs hot when idle
- settings change that you didn't change
- you get alerts that an unknown tracker is moving with you
- your accounts show logins from devices you don't recognise

None of these proves tracking on its own. Several together are worth checking.

The Decoder calls this [Monitoring & Surveillance](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance) (being watched), with a separate page on [Location Tracking](/playbook/location-tracking).

## How do I check safely?
> Before you start: if you think they're watching closely, sudden changes can tell them you've noticed. Think about the order. If there's any risk of violence, plan it with a domestic abuse service first.

Work from a device they have never had access to: a new phone, a friend's, or a library computer.

**1. Shared accounts.** These are the most common way in. The Decoder covers this in [Account Control](/playbook/account-control).
- Start with your email. Change the password, turn on two-step login, and save the backup codes on paper.
- Then cloud storage (Apple ID or iCloud, Google), social media, banking, shopping, streaming and any shared calendars.
- In each, look for "devices" or "where you're logged in" and remove anything you don't recognise.
- Remove them as a "family" member, trusted contact or recovery contact.
- Phone family plans can share location, purchases and photos. Leave the family group, or ask the provider to move your number to your own account.
- Shared streaming and shopping accounts show viewing history, addresses and orders. Make your own.

**2. Location sharing.**
- On iPhone: Settings, your name, Find My. Check who can see your location, and check Family Sharing.
- On Android: Google Maps, Location sharing; and Settings, Google, Location.
- Check apps like Life360, Snapchat (Snap Map) and fitness apps.
- Check the car's own app if it has one. Many cars connect to an app that shows where the car is. If they have the login, change it or remove their access.

**3. Your phone.** Stalkerware is software hidden on a phone to report what you do.
- Look for apps you don't recognise, especially ones with access to location, messages or the microphone.
- Check whether the phone allows apps from outside the official store when you didn't set that up.
- Run a reputable security app that detects stalkerware.
- If you find something, think before deleting it. Removing it alerts the person and destroys evidence. Ask a local domestic abuse service, or the police, to help you decide first.
- The cleanest fix is a new phone with a new account they never had access to.

**4. Trackers on your car and things.**
- Small Bluetooth trackers can be hidden in cars, bags, coats or children's belongings.
- iPhones warn you about an unknown AirTag travelling with you. Android phones can scan for unknown trackers in Settings, under Safety and emergency. Don't ignore these alerts.
- Check the car: under bumpers, inside wheel arches, under seats, in the boot and glovebox, and around the OBD port under the dashboard.
- Check gifts and toys the children bring back from visits.

**5. Home devices.** Smart speakers, cameras, doorbells and thermostats they set up may still report to their account.
- Check each app for other users and remove them.
- Reset devices to factory settings if you can't get into the account, or remove them.
- If the devices are in a home you've left, assume the person can see and hear what they pick up.

If the heating keeps changing itself at odd hours, now you know who to suspect.

**6. Social media.**
- Make accounts private and review who follows you.
- Turn off location tags, and ask friends not to tag you or post where you are.
- Check old posts that show your routines.

## What should I do if I find something?
If it's safe to, keep evidence before you change anything. Photograph a device where you found it before you touch it. Screenshot settings. Write down the date and place, using the format in [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

Then talk to the police or a domestic abuse service before you remove it, because removing it tells them you know. Tracking someone without consent is a crime in many places, often as stalking or harassment, and the evidence matters for protective orders. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## Children's devices and gifts
A new phone, tablet, watch or games console from the other parent may come with location sharing or monitoring turned on. Check the settings with your child, without making them feel they've done something wrong. If you're unsure, a domestic abuse service or a tech safety service can help.

Change the settings, but don't bin the present. If the other parent ever says you "tampered" with your child's phone, you can say calmly that you checked the privacy settings together.

## A private phone
If you're not sure your phone is safe, a cheap pay-as-you-go phone bought with cash, used only for private calls, is a simple answer. Don't sign it into your old accounts, and keep it out of sight.

## Do I have to stay suspicious forever?
No. Do the check once, properly, and fold what you learn into your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left). Repeat it after handovers or visits if devices go back and forth, or if something new makes you wonder. Then let it go. You're closing doors so you can stop living on guard.

The [device and account audit](ch:device-and-account-audit) turns this chapter into a checklist.

=== stalking-and-harassment
title: Stalking and harassment after a breakup
question: What counts as stalking after a breakup, and what can I do?
seo: Stalking After a Breakup: Signs, Records and Reporting
short: Stalking after a breakup is a pattern of unwanted contact or attention that makes you afraid or distressed: following, watching, turning up, repeated messages, contact through others, or online harassment. It is a crime in many places. Keep a record of every incident and report it.
---
It starts small. A message after you asked them to stop. A car that looks like theirs outside your work. Your friend mentions they asked where you live now.

One of these is a worry. A pattern of them is stalking.

If you feel in danger now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What counts as stalking after a breakup?
Stalking after a breakup is a pattern of unwanted behaviour from a former partner that causes you fear, alarm or distress. It can mean following you, watching your home, turning up where you'll be, sending messages you've asked them to stop, contacting people around you, or tracking you online. Repeated over time, small things add up to stalking.

## What does it look like?
Stalking by a former partner usually mixes several of these:

- following you, or waiting where you'll be
- turning up at your home, work, gym or children's school
- driving past, or parking outside
- repeated calls, messages or emails after you've asked them to stop
- new numbers or accounts after you block them
- contacting your friends, family, employer or new partner to find out about you
- sending gifts or leaving things for you
- tracking your phone, car or accounts
- posting about you online, or making fake accounts to watch or contact you
- making complaints or reports about you to authorities

Former partners are the most common kind of stalker in many countries' figures, and they're more likely to be violent than strangers who stalk. That's why stalking by an ex needs a plan.

The Decoder covers the watching in [Monitoring & Surveillance](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance) and the threats in [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation).

If the contact is all charm, apologies and sudden emergencies, that may be [hoovering](ch:hoovering) instead. The two can overlap. If it frightens you, or keeps coming after you've said no, treat it as stalking.

## Is stalking a crime?
In many places, yes:

- **United States:** stalking is a crime in every state, and some forms are federal crimes.
- **England and Wales:** stalking and harassment are offences under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, and courts can make stalking protection orders.
- **Scotland:** stalking is an offence under the Criminal Justice and Licensing (Scotland) Act 2010.
- **Ireland:** stalking became a separate offence on 1 November 2023, with civil stalking orders added later.
- **Canada:** criminal harassment is an offence under the Criminal Code.
- **Australia:** every state and territory has a stalking offence.

Search "[your country or state] stalking law" for the details.

## How do I document stalking?
Stalking is a pattern, so the record is what proves it. For each incident, write down:

- the date and time
- what happened, in plain words
- where you were
- who saw it
- how it affected you (for example, you left work early, or changed your route)

Keep screenshots of messages and calls, with the date and the number or account. Save voicemails. Put gifts or notes in a bag, and handle them as little as you can. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) has a four-line format.

## Should I report it?
If you feel unsafe, yes. Report early, even if each incident seems small. In most places, police respond more strongly to a recorded pattern than to a single event. Ask for a crime reference number every time. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).

You can also ask about a protective or stalking order, which makes contact itself a breach. What's on offer depends on where you live. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## Orders that cover stalking
- **England and Wales:** police can apply for a Stalking Protection Order, which can ban contact and require the person to do things like attend a programme. You can also ask about non-molestation orders.
- **Scotland:** non-harassment orders, often made when a case reaches court.
- **United States:** many states have stalking protective orders, separate from domestic violence orders.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country or state] stalking protection order."

## Telling neighbours and work
You don't need to give details. A photo and a sentence can be enough:

> **Say:** "My ex-partner has been turning up uninvited. If you see this person near my home, please don't give them any information, and call me or the police."

At work, ask reception not to confirm you work there, and to call security if the person arrives.

## If police don't take it seriously
Stalking can look minor one incident at a time. If you feel dismissed:

- Use the word "stalking," and ask for it to be recorded as stalking.
- Bring your record, showing the pattern over time.
- Ask for the officer's name and a reference number.
- Ask for the case to be reviewed by a specialist, or contact a stalking advocacy service. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline can advise.

## What helps day to day?
- Don't engage. Replies, even angry ones, teach them that the channel works.
- Block and keep a record at the same time: screenshot first, then block.
- Tell your workplace, school and neighbours, and give them a photo. If you share a workplace or a small town, see [When you share a town, a workplace or a community](ch:sharing-a-town-a-workplace-or-a-community).
- Vary your routes and routines for a while.
- Check your phone and car for tracking: [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- Lean on a stalking helpline if one exists where you live. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline is run by the Suzy Lamplugh Trust.

If you share children and have to stay in touch, keep one calm channel for arrangements only. [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact) shows how. Then if they ever claim you won't co-parent, your messages show otherwise.

## Living with it
Stalking is exhausting because it keeps going. Vary routes and times where you can. Tell a few people your plans each day. Keep your record going even when nothing happens; the quiet gaps are part of the pattern too.

And find one place, person or activity that feels free of it, like a swim, or a friend who never asks about it. You're allowed rest from it, even while it carries on.

## Why does stalking matter so much for safety?
Stalking by a former partner is one of the warning signs services take most seriously. [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) explains why. If it's escalating, update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists stalking first among the forms of [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept).

::: scene
## A month of stalking, recorded
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Over four weeks, Grace's ex messaged her from six new numbers. He left flowers on her car, appeared twice outside her gym and called her mother "just to see how she was." Each time, Grace wrote it down: date, time, what happened, who saw. She took photos of the flowers and screenshots of the messages.

When she reported it, she didn't describe "a bad few weeks." She handed over a list of nineteen incidents. The officer recorded it as stalking and passed it to a specialist. Grace lived in England, where a Stalking Protection Order followed.
:::

::: action
## The habit that makes the difference
Open a notebook, or a note on a device they've never touched. Write today's date and the last thing that happened: when, where, what, who saw. Small ones count. Stalking is proved by the pattern.
:::

=== intimate-images-and-online-threats
title: Intimate images and online threats
question: My ex is threatening to post pictures of me. What can I do?
seo: Ex Threatening to Post Pictures? What to Do Now
short: If your ex is threatening to post pictures or videos of you, you are not powerless. Sharing intimate images without consent, and in many places threatening to, is a crime. Free tools can block images from being posted on major platforms, and helplines can get images taken down.
---
They have photos or videos of you from the relationship. Now they're threatening to send them to your family or your boss, or post them online. Or they already have.

This is abuse. Trusting someone you loved was not a mistake, and it isn't your fault. There's more you can do than you might think.

If you feel in danger, or you're thinking about harming yourself because of this, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) or [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath) first.

## Is it illegal for my ex to post pictures of me?
In many places, yes. Sharing intimate images without consent, sometimes called image-based abuse or "revenge porn," is a crime in:

- **United States:** almost every state, and since 2025 a federal law, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, which also requires platforms to remove reported images quickly.
- **England and Wales:** sharing intimate images without consent is an offence, and so is threatening to share them.
- **Scotland:** an offence under the Abusive Behaviour and Sexual Harm (Scotland) Act 2016, including threats.
- **Ireland:** an offence under the Harassment, Harmful Communications and Related Offences Act 2020, known as Coco's Law.
- **Canada:** an offence under the Criminal Code.
- **Australia:** offences in every state, plus a national removal scheme run by the eSafety Commissioner.

Fake images made with editing or AI tools are covered by some of these laws too.

## What do I do right now about the images?
1. **Don't pay, don't send more images, and don't negotiate.** Giving in rarely ends it.
2. **Save the evidence.** Screenshot the threats, with dates, usernames and links. Save links to anywhere an image has been posted. Do this before blocking.
3. **Block images before they spread.** StopNCII.org lets adults create a digital fingerprint of an image on their own device. The image never leaves your phone. Partner platforms then block matching images. For images taken when you were under 18, use Take It Down, run by the US National Center for Missing and Exploited Children.
4. **Report to the platform.** Every major platform has a reporting route for intimate images shared without consent. In the US, platforms must now remove reported images within 48 hours; if one doesn't, you can report it to the Federal Trade Commission at TakeItDown.ftc.gov.
5. **Get help with removal.** In the UK, the Revenge Porn Helpline helps adults get images removed. In Australia, report to the eSafety Commissioner. In the US, the Cyber Civil Rights Initiative runs a crisis line.
6. **Report to the police**, with your evidence.

## What if they're threatening to tell people things, not share images?
Threats to "expose" you, to post your address, or to contact your employer are also abuse. Posting someone's private details is often called doxxing, and the Decoder covers it in [Doxxing](/playbook/doxxing).

Using your name or reputation against you is covered in [Reputation Hijacking](/playbook/reputation-hijacking) and in [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign). Many of these threats are crimes too, as harassment or malicious communications. Keep the evidence and report it.

## What about fake accounts and online harassment?
Report fake accounts to the platform as impersonation. Keep a record of every account and post. A pattern of online harassment can be stalking. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## Fake and AI-made images
Someone can now make realistic fake intimate images from ordinary photos. If this happens, it's still abuse, and in many places it's now a crime.

In England and Wales, sharing or threatening to share intimate images, including faked ones, is a criminal offence. The US federal law described above covers them too. Report them the same way as real images, using the steps above.

## If you're under 18 and images are involved
If someone shares or threatens to share sexual images of you, it's a serious crime, whoever took the picture, even if you sent it yourself. You're not in trouble. Tell an adult you trust and use Take It Down. In the UK, you can also use the Report Remove tool from Childline.

## How do I cope with this?
Image-based abuse can bring intense shame and fear. Both are the intended effect. The shame belongs to the person who shared or threatened.

Silence is what the person threatening you is counting on, so tell one person you trust. A helpline worker will have heard it many times and won't judge you.

If people you know have seen the images, you don't owe anyone more than one sentence: "Someone shared private images of me without my consent, and it's being dealt with."

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers faked and manipulated evidence, including images, in its chapter on [synthetic evidence](/feminine/synthetic-evidence#core-concept).

## Save it before you block
Screenshot the most recent threat now, with the date, username and any link showing. Save it somewhere only you can get into. That one screenshot is enough for tonight.
:::

If they're also threatening to post where you live, [Your address, your records, your mail](ch:your-address-your-records-your-mail) shows you how to lock it down.

=== your-address-your-records-your-mail
title: Your address, your records, your mail
question: How do I keep my address from my ex? Is there an address confidentiality program?
seo: Address Confidentiality Program: Hiding Your Address From an Ex
short: An address confidentiality program gives you a substitute address to use on public records, so an abusive ex can't find you through them. Most US states run one, and other countries have similar protections. You can also hide your address on voter rolls, at school and with your doctor, and remove it from people-search websites.
---
You moved. Now you'd like to keep it that way. Trouble is, your address is in more places than you think: voter rolls, court papers, school records, delivery apps, and websites that collect public data. The pizza app probably knows where you live before your mum does.

## What is an address confidentiality program?
In most US states, an address confidentiality program gives survivors of domestic abuse, stalking or sexual assault a substitute address. You use it for state and local government records, including voter registration, and your mail is forwarded to your real address.

Search "[your state] address confidentiality program." A domestic abuse service can help you apply.

Other places have their own protections:

- **United Kingdom:** you can register to vote anonymously if your safety is at risk. You'll need a court order or a letter from a qualifying professional.
- **Australia:** you can apply to be a silent elector, so your address doesn't appear on the public roll.
- **Canada and Ireland:** ask your electoral office about keeping your address off public lists, and ask a domestic abuse service what exists locally.

The Decoder's page on [Location Tracking](/playbook/location-tracking) shows how a location gets found and used.

## Where else does my address appear?
Work through these, starting with the most public:

- **Voter rolls.** See above. In the US, many states' address confidentiality programs also keep your voter registration private.
- **Court papers.** Ask the court or your lawyer whether your address can be kept off documents the other side will see. Many courts allow this where there's a risk, and many have a form for it.
- **School and childcare.** Ask them not to release your address or phone number to the other parent, and give them any court order.
- **Doctor, dentist and hospital.** Ask them to flag your record so your details aren't shared.
- **Your employer and bank.** Ask them to keep your address private and to check who's asking before they say anything about you.
- **Company and property records.** If you own a business or property, your address may be public. Ask the registry about protecting it.
- **Social media.** Remove location tags, check photos that show your street or front door, and ask friends not to post your address or check you in.

If you keep your address off court papers and they claim you're hiding the children, keep it calm. You used the court's own process for safety, and you can say exactly that.

## How do I remove my address from people-search sites?
In the US especially, data broker websites list addresses, phone numbers and relatives. Most have an opt-out form. Search your own name to see what comes up, then opt out site by site, or pay a removal service to do it.

Some address confidentiality programs give extra protection here. In the UK and the EU, you can ask companies to delete your data under data protection law.

## What about my mail?
- Set up mail forwarding from any old shared address, so letters about you don't land with them.
- Think about a PO box or a mailbox service for your public address.
- Change your address with banks, pensions, insurers and government agencies directly, rather than relying on forwarding.

## Deliveries, online orders and apps
- Use a different delivery address, a locker or a pickup point.
- Check shared shopping, food delivery and taxi accounts. Old saved addresses and new orders can show up there, so delete them and make your own accounts.
- Turn off location on fitness and running apps. Some show your routes publicly.
- Be careful with neighbourhood apps and local groups, which can reveal your street.

## Your car and its registration
Your car's registration links to your address. If the person you left knows your car, they may find you by it. Ask the vehicle licensing agency whether you can protect your address, and think about whether your car stands out on your new street.

## The children's records
Tell the school, nursery, doctor, dentist and clubs that your address must not be given to the other parent without your agreement. Give them a copy of any court order and ask them to flag the record. Then check what's posted on school apps and in class group chats, where a birthday party invite can give away a whole street.

## Should I change my name?
Some people change their name for safety. It can help, but it doesn't erase records that link the old and new names, and the process often creates public records of its own. In some places courts can seal a name change where there's a safety risk. Get advice from a domestic abuse service or a lawyer first.

## A moving checklist
☐ Address confidentiality program applied for, where available
☐ Bank, employer, doctor and school told the new address privately
☐ Mail redirected, using a confidential address if you have one
☐ Electoral or voter registration made anonymous
☐ Delivery and app addresses updated or deleted
☐ Neighbours told who not to give information to
☐ Children's records flagged

## What if they find me anyway?
It isn't your fault, and it doesn't mean you did this wrong. Update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left), tell the police if there's any threat, and look at how they might have found you: shared contacts, the children's belongings, tracking. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology). If they turned up, write it down and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
