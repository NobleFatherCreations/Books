=== a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left
title: A safety plan for after you've left
question: How do I stay safe after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: Safety Plan After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: A safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship covers your home, your routines, work, school, handovers, your phone and car, and who to call. It is most useful in the first months and around court dates. Write it on paper and keep it where they can't find it.
---
Most safety plans are written for the day you leave. A safety plan after leaving an abusive relationship is different. This one is for the months after, when you're living your life and the person you left knows more about that life than you'd like.

If you're in danger right now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) first.

> Keep this plan somewhere they can't reach: in your head, with a friend, or on paper hidden away from home. Don't leave it on a shared device.

## Why do I need a safety plan after leaving?
Because separation is a time of raised risk, and because the person you left may know your routines, your workplace, your children's school and your friends. [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first) explains the risk. A plan doesn't mean you expect the worst. It means you've thought about it once, calmly, so you don't have to think about it in a panic.

The [safety plan worksheet](ch:safety-plan-worksheet) puts all of this on one page.

## Home
- Change the locks if they ever had a key. Ask your landlord, or a domestic abuse service, which may help with the cost.
- Add a door chain, a peephole or a video doorbell that only you control.
- Light the outside of your home at night.
- Keep your phone charged and near you at night.
- Plan an exit from each room you spend time in, and a place to go.
- Tell a neighbour you trust what the person and their car look like, and ask them to call the police if they see them.
- If they turn up, don't open the door. Call the police if they won't leave or if you feel threatened, and write it down afterwards.
- When the practical side is done, [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours) covers making it feel safe.

## Routines and routes
People who want to find you watch for patterns.

- Vary when you leave home and which way you go, especially for the first months.
- Change your gym, supermarket or coffee shop if they knew you went there.
- Park in well-lit places near entrances.
- Know where the nearest police station and busy public places are on your usual routes.

## Work
- Tell someone you trust at work: a manager, HR or security.
- Give reception or security a photo of the person and ask them not to let them in or confirm you're there.
- Ask to have your calls screened and your name removed from public staff lists.
- Ask about changing your hours or work location for a while.
- In many places employers must take reasonable steps to keep staff safe, and some countries give paid leave for domestic abuse. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

## School and childcare
- Tell the school, nursery or childcare who may collect your children and who may not.
- Give them a copy of any court order, with a photo of the person if collection is restricted.
- Agree a password that anyone collecting your child must give.
- Ask the school not to give out your new address or phone number.

## Handovers
If you have to hand children over, plan each exchange: a public place with cameras, a school handover, a trusted third person, or a supervised contact centre. Don't hand over at your home if you can avoid it. [Handovers](ch:handovers) covers this in detail.

## Your phone, accounts and car
- Change passwords from a device they never touched, starting with your email.
- Turn off location sharing, and check for tracking apps and trackers. [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology) has the steps.
- Check your car for trackers, especially if they had access to it.

## People
- Choose two people you trust and tell them the plan.
- Agree a code word or emoji that means "call the police" or "come now."
- Ask one person to check on you on court days and other high-risk days.

## What to keep ready
Some people keep a small bag at a friend's house or at work, even after they've left:

- copies of documents and court orders
- some cash and a bank card
- medicines and spare glasses
- phone charger, spare keys
- a written list of important numbers, in case your phone is taken

## When is the risk usually higher?
Plan ahead for:

- court hearings and decisions
- your new relationship becoming known
- birthdays, anniversaries and holidays
- the day they're served with papers
- the day you stop replying
- any time they've just been refused something

On those days, tell someone where you'll be, avoid being alone at places they know, and keep your phone charged.

## If they break a court order
Write down what happened, when and where. Keep messages. Report the breach to the police. A pattern of recorded breaches is what usually gets a stronger response. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## If anything on this list feels dangerous to do
Some steps, like changing every password at once, can alert someone who is watching. If you're worried about how they'll react, plan the order with a domestic abuse service. [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) helps you judge how careful to be.

=== warning-signs-to-take-seriously
title: Warning signs to take seriously
question: What are the domestic violence risk factors I shouldn't ignore?
seo: Domestic Violence Risk Factors and Warning Signs
short: Some domestic violence risk factors are linked to serious harm: past strangling or choking, threats to kill, access to weapons, stalking, violence that's getting worse, and separation itself. If any apply to you, talk to a domestic abuse service or the police now. If none apply, that doesn't prove you're safe.
---
This chapter is short and plain on purpose. It lists the signs that services treat as most serious. It isn't a score, and it can't tell you you're safe. Only you, with help, can judge that.

If you're in danger now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## Which warning signs matter most?
Research on serious and fatal domestic violence has found these risk factors again and again:

- **Past strangling or choking.** Even once. Even if it left no mark.
- **Threats to kill you, the children, or themselves.**
- **Access to a gun or other weapon**, or threats with one.
- **Violence that is getting more frequent or more severe.**
- **Stalking:** following, watching, turning up, tracking.
- **Extreme jealousy or control**, and saying things like "if I can't have you, no one can."
- **Separation**, especially recent, especially from someone very controlling.
- **Violence during pregnancy.** See [Pregnant, or with a new baby](ch:pregnant-or-with-a-new-baby).
- **Forced sex.**
- **Threats of suicide used to control you.** The Decoder describes this in [emotional blackmail through self-harm threats](/playbook/emotional-blackmail-via-self-harm-threats).
- **Unemployment, heavy drinking or drug use** alongside the above.
- **Breaking court orders.**
- **Threats to hurt or kill a pet.** See [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

The Decoder's page on [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes the threats and shows of force that often sit around these signs.

## Why does strangulation matter so much?
Because it is one of the strongest warning signs there is. A study of women attacked by partners found that a past attempt to strangle them was linked to a many-times-higher risk of later attempted or completed murder.

Strangling can also cause injuries you can't see: brain injury from lack of oxygen, damage to the throat and neck, strokes days or weeks later. If you were ever strangled or choked, even long ago, tell a doctor. Signs to mention include a changed voice, trouble swallowing, headaches, memory problems or dizziness. It is also evidence: tell the police or your lawyer that it happened. [Getting sick after leaving](ch:getting-sick-after-leaving) covers what to tell a doctor.

## What should I do if some of these apply?
1. **Talk to a domestic abuse service** today. They use risk checklists like these every day and can help you plan. [Find help](ch:find-help).
2. **Tell the police**, especially about threats, weapons or strangling.
3. **Ask about a protective order.** See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
4. **Make or update your safety plan.** See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
5. **Tell someone you trust** what's happening, so you're not holding it alone.

When you call, use plain words that get attention: "He strangled me last year." "She has said she'll kill me." "He has a gun." "He's been following me." Professionals are trained to act on those sentences.

## What if none of these apply?
That's good news, but it isn't a guarantee. These lists come from patterns across many cases. They can't see your situation the way you can. If you're frightened, trust that. Fear is information.

## What if they're threatening to harm themselves?
Threats of suicide can be real distress, a way of controlling you, or both. You are not responsible for keeping them alive, and you don't have to go back to prevent it. If the threat sounds immediate, call emergency services and tell them. Then step back. If thoughts of harming yourself are coming up for you too, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists the same high-risk signs in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

=== surveillance-and-technology
title: Surveillance and technology
question: Is my ex tracking me, and how do I check?
seo: Is My Ex Tracking Me? How to Check Your Phone and Car
short: If you're asking "is my ex tracking me," check four places: shared accounts, location sharing, hidden apps on your phone, and trackers on your car or belongings. Do it from a device they never touched, and in a calm order, because sudden changes can alert someone who is watching.
---
They seem to know where you've been. They mention a conversation you had with a friend. They turn up at the café you go to on Saturdays. You start to wonder if you're imagining it.

You may not be. Tracking after a separation is common, and it's usually done with ordinary tools: shared accounts, location sharing, and cheap trackers.

## Is my ex tracking me?
Signs that someone may be tracking you:

- they know where you are, or were, without being told
- they know what you said in private messages or emails
- your phone's battery drains fast or it runs hot when idle
- settings change that you didn't change
- you get alerts that an unknown tracker is moving with you
- your accounts show logins from devices you don't recognise

None of these proves tracking on its own. Several together are worth checking.

The Decoder describes this as [monitoring and surveillance](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance), with a separate page on [location tracking](/playbook/location-tracking).

## How do I check safely?
> Before you start: if you think they're watching closely, sudden changes can tell them you've noticed. Think about the order, and if there's any risk of violence, plan it with a domestic abuse service first.

Work from a device they have never had access to: a new phone, a friend's, a library computer.

**1. Shared accounts.** These are the most common route in. The Decoder covers this in [account control](/playbook/account-control).
- Start with your email. Change the password, turn on two-step login, and save the backup codes on paper.
- Then cloud storage (Apple ID or iCloud, Google), social media, banking, shopping, streaming and any shared calendars.
- In each, look for "devices" or "where you're logged in" and remove anything you don't recognise.
- Remove them as a "family" member, trusted contact or recovery contact.

**2. Location sharing.**
- On iPhone: Settings, your name, Find My. Check who can see your location, and check Family Sharing.
- On Android: Google Maps, Location sharing; and Settings, Google, Location.
- Check apps like Life360, Snapchat (Snap Map) and fitness apps.
- Check the car's own app if it has one.

**3. Your phone.** Stalkerware is software hidden on a phone to report what you do.
- Look for apps you don't recognise, especially ones with access to location, messages or the microphone.
- Check whether the phone allows apps from outside the official store when you didn't set that up.
- Run a reputable security app that detects stalkerware.
- If you find something, think before deleting it. Removing it alerts the person and destroys evidence. A domestic abuse service or the police can help you decide.
- The cleanest fix is a new phone with a new account that they never had access to.

**4. Trackers on your car and things.**
- Small Bluetooth trackers can be hidden in cars, bags, coats or children's belongings.
- iPhones warn you about an unknown AirTag travelling with you. Android phones can scan for unknown trackers in Settings, under Safety and emergency.
- Check the car: under bumpers, inside wheel arches, under seats, in the boot and glovebox, and around the OBD port under the dashboard.
- Check gifts and toys the children bring back from visits.

**5. Home devices.** Smart speakers, cameras, doorbells and thermostats they set up may still report to their account. Reset them to factory settings, or remove them.

**6. Social media.**
- Make accounts private and review who follows you.
- Turn off location tags, and ask friends not to tag you or post where you are.
- Check old posts that show your routines.

## What should I do if I find something?
Photograph it where you found it before you touch it. Write down the date and place, using the format in [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how). Then talk to the police or a domestic abuse service. Tracking someone without consent is a crime in many places, often as stalking or harassment. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## Do I have to stay suspicious forever?
No. Do the check once, properly, and fold what you learn into your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left). Repeat it after handovers or visits if devices go back and forth, or if something new makes you wonder. Then let it go. The aim is to close the doors, not to live on guard.

The [device and account audit](ch:device-and-account-audit) turns this chapter into a checklist.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers how phones become a leash inside the relationship in [The Digital Leash](/feminine/the-digital-leash#core-concept).

- Refuge (UK): tech safety guidance.
- Coalition Against Stalkerware: guidance on detecting stalkerware.

=== stalking-and-harassment
title: Stalking and harassment after a breakup
question: What counts as stalking after a breakup, and what can I do?
seo: Stalking After a Breakup: Signs, Records and Reporting
short: Stalking after a breakup is a pattern of unwanted contact or attention that makes you afraid or distressed: following, watching, turning up, repeated messages, contact through others, or online harassment. It is a crime in many places. Keep a record of every incident and report it.
---
It starts small. A message after you asked them to stop. A car that looks like theirs outside your work. Your friend says they asked where you live now.

One of these is a worry. A pattern of them is stalking.

If you feel in danger now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What counts as stalking after a breakup?
Stalking is a pattern of unwanted behaviour aimed at you that causes fear, alarm or distress. Common forms:

- following you, or waiting where you'll be
- turning up at your home, work, gym or children's school
- driving past, or parking outside
- repeated calls, messages or emails after you've asked them to stop
- new numbers or accounts after you block them
- contacting your friends, family or employer to find out about you
- sending gifts or leaving things for you
- tracking your phone or car
- posting about you online, or making fake accounts to watch or contact you

The Decoder describes the watching in [monitoring and surveillance](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance) and the threats in [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation).

## How is stalking different from hoovering?
Hoovering is an attempt to pull you back, usually with charm, apology or an emergency. Stalking is about watching and pursuing. It may not want you back at all. The two can overlap, and both can be part of post-separation abuse. If the contact makes you afraid, or follows you after you've said no, treat it as stalking. [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) covers the pull-back.

## Is stalking a crime?
In many places, yes:

- **United States:** stalking is a crime in every state, and some forms are federal crimes.
- **England and Wales:** stalking and harassment are offences under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, and courts can make stalking protection orders.
- **Scotland:** stalking is an offence under the Criminal Justice and Licensing (Scotland) Act 2010.
- **Ireland:** stalking became a separate offence in 2024.
- **Canada:** criminal harassment is an offence under the Criminal Code.
- **Australia:** every state and territory has a stalking offence.

Search "[your country or state] stalking law" for the details.

## How do I document stalking?
Stalking is a pattern, so the record is what proves it. For each incident, write down:

- the date and time
- what happened, in plain words
- where you were
- who saw it
- how it affected you (for example, you left work early, or changed your route)

Keep screenshots of messages and calls, with the date and the number or account. Save voicemails. Keep gifts or notes in a bag without handling them more than you need to. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) has a four-line format.

## Should I report it?
If you feel unsafe, yes. Report early, even if each incident seems small. Police respond more strongly to a recorded pattern than to a single event. Ask for a crime reference number every time. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).

You can also ask about a protective or stalking order, which makes contact itself a breach. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## What helps day to day?
- Don't engage. Replies, even angry ones, teach them that the channel works.
- Block and keep a record at the same time: screenshot first, then block.
- Tell your workplace, school and neighbours, and give them a photo. If you share a workplace or a small town, see [When you share a town, a workplace or a community](ch:sharing-a-town-a-workplace-or-a-community).
- Vary your routes and routines for a while.
- Check your phone and car for tracking: [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- Lean on a stalking helpline if one exists where you live. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline is run by the Suzy Lamplugh Trust.

## Why does stalking matter so much for safety?
Stalking by a former partner is one of the warning signs services take most seriously. [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) explains why. If it's escalating, update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists stalking first among the forms of [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept).

=== intimate-images-and-online-threats
title: Intimate images and online threats
question: My ex is threatening to post pictures of me. What can I do?
seo: Ex Threatening to Post Pictures? What to Do Now
short: If your ex is threatening to post pictures or videos of you, you are not powerless. Sharing intimate images without consent, and in many places threatening to, is a crime. Free tools can block images from being posted on major platforms, and helplines can get images taken down.
---
They have photos or videos of you from the relationship. Now they're threatening to send them to your family, your boss, or post them online. Or they already have.

This is abuse. It is not your fault for having trusted them. And there is more you can do than you might think.

If you feel in danger, or you're thinking about harming yourself because of this, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) or [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath) first.

## Is it illegal for my ex to post pictures of me?
In many places, yes. Sharing intimate images without consent, sometimes called image-based abuse or "revenge porn," is a crime in:

- **United States:** almost every state, and since 2025 a federal law, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, which also requires platforms to remove reported images quickly.
- **England and Wales:** sharing intimate images without consent is an offence, and so is threatening to share them.
- **Scotland:** an offence under the Abusive Behaviour and Sexual Harm (Scotland) Act 2016, including threats.
- **Ireland:** an offence under the Harassment, Harmful Communications and Related Offences Act 2020, known as Coco's Law.
- **Canada:** an offence under the Criminal Code.
- **Australia:** offences in every state, plus a national removal scheme run by the eSafety Commissioner.

Fake images made with editing or AI tools are covered by some of these laws too.

## What do I do right now?
1. **Don't pay, don't send more images, and don't negotiate.** Giving in rarely ends it.
2. **Save the evidence.** Screenshot the threats, with dates, usernames and links. Save links to anywhere an image has been posted. Do this before blocking.
3. **Block images before they spread.** StopNCII.org lets adults create a digital fingerprint of an image on their own device. The image never leaves your phone. Partner platforms then block matching images. For images taken when you were under 18, use Take It Down, run by the US National Center for Missing and Exploited Children.
4. **Report to the platform.** Every major platform has a reporting route for intimate images shared without consent.
5. **Get help with removal.** In the UK, the Revenge Porn Helpline helps adults get images removed. In Australia, report to the eSafety Commissioner. In the US, the Cyber Civil Rights Initiative runs a crisis line.
6. **Report to the police**, with your evidence.

## What if they're threatening to tell people things, not share images?
Threats to "expose" you, to post your address, or to contact your employer are also abuse. Posting someone's private details is often called doxxing; the Decoder describes it in [doxxing](/playbook/doxxing). Using your name or reputation against you is covered in [reputation hijacking](/playbook/reputation-hijacking) and in [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign). Many of these threats are crimes too, as harassment or malicious communications. Keep the evidence and report it.

## What about fake accounts and online harassment?
Report fake accounts to the platform as impersonation. Keep a record of every account and post. A pattern of online harassment can be stalking. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

## How do I cope with this?
Image-based abuse can bring intense shame and fear. Both are the intended effect. The shame belongs to the person who shared or threatened. Tell one person you trust. If the images have been seen by people you know, you don't owe anyone an explanation beyond "someone shared private images of me without my consent, and it's being dealt with."

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers faked and manipulated evidence, including images, in its chapter on [synthetic evidence](/feminine/synthetic-evidence#core-concept).

- Online Safety Act 2023 (England and Wales): intimate image offences, including threats.

=== your-address-your-records-your-mail
title: Your address, your records, your mail
question: How do I keep my address from my ex? Is there an address confidentiality program?
seo: Address Confidentiality Program: Hiding Your Address From an Ex
short: An address confidentiality program gives you a substitute address to use on public records, so an abusive ex can't find you through them. Most US states run one, and other countries have similar protections. You can also hide your address on voter rolls, at school and with your doctor, and remove it from people-search websites.
---
You moved. Now you want to keep it that way. But your address is in more places than you think: voter rolls, court papers, school records, delivery apps, and websites that collect public data.

## What is an address confidentiality program?
In most US states, an address confidentiality program gives survivors of domestic abuse, stalking or sexual assault a substitute address. You use it for state and local government records, including voter registration, and your mail is forwarded to your real address. Search "[your state] address confidentiality program." A domestic abuse service can usually help you apply.

Other places have their own protections:

- **United Kingdom:** you can register to vote anonymously if your safety is at risk. You'll need a court order or a letter from a qualifying professional.
- **Australia:** you can apply to be a silent elector, so your address doesn't appear on the public roll.
- **Canada and Ireland:** ask your electoral office about keeping your address off public lists, and ask a domestic abuse service what exists locally.

The Decoder's page on [location tracking](/playbook/location-tracking) shows how a location gets found and used.

## Where else does my address appear?
Work through these, starting with the most public:

- **Voter rolls:** see above.
- **Court papers.** Ask the court or your lawyer whether your address can be withheld from the other side. Many courts allow this where there's a risk.
- **School and childcare.** Ask them not to release your address or phone number to the other parent, and give them any court order.
- **Doctor, dentist and hospital.** Ask them to flag your record so your details aren't shared.
- **Your employer and bank.** Ask them to keep your address private and to verify anyone who calls about you.
- **Deliveries.** Check shared shopping and food delivery accounts; old addresses and new orders can show up there.
- **Social media.** Remove location tags, check photos that show your street or front door, and ask friends not to post your address or check you in.

## How do I remove my address from people-search sites?
In the US especially, data broker websites list addresses, phone numbers and relatives. Most have an opt-out form. You can search your own name to see what comes up, then opt out site by site, or use a paid removal service. Some address confidentiality programs give extra protection here. In the UK and the EU, you can ask companies to delete your data under data protection law.

## What about my mail?
- Set up mail forwarding from any old shared address, so letters about you don't land with them.
- Think about a PO box or a mailbox service for your public address.
- Change your address with banks, pensions, insurers and government agencies directly, rather than relying on forwarding.

## Should I change my name?
Some people change their name for safety. It can help, but it doesn't erase records that link the old and new names, and the process often creates public records of its own. In some places courts can seal a name change where there's a safety risk. Get advice from a domestic abuse service or a lawyer first.

## What if they find me anyway?
It isn't your fault, and it doesn't mean you did this wrong. Update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left), tell the police if there's any threat, and look at how they might have found you: shared contacts, the children's belongings, tracking. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology). If they turned up, write it down and see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers protecting your records while leaving in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).
