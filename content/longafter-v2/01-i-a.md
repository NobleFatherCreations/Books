=== leaving-was-the-middle
title: Leaving was the middle
question: Why is it still so hard after I left?
seo: After Leaving an Abusive Relationship: Why It's Still Hard
short: After leaving an abusive relationship, a lot of people find the hardest part isn't over. The control moves to new channels, your body stays on high alert, and everyone around you expects relief. None of that means leaving was a mistake.
---
Everyone says leaving is the end. The door closes, the music swells, the credits roll, and the healing starts on Monday.

If only. For a lot of people, leaving turns out to be the middle. If that's where you are, you haven't done it wrong. You've found the part the films skip.

## Why does it still feel this hard?
Three things usually hit at once after leaving an abusive relationship.

**The control changes shape.** The person you left loses most of their reach in a single day. Often they go looking for it somewhere else: the children, money, what they tell your friends, the courts.

It looks different from what happened at home, so it can take a while to see it's the same thing in a new coat. It has a name, [post-separation abuse](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse), and [The shape it takes when they can't reach you directly](ch:the-shape-it-takes-when-they-cant-reach-you-directly) shows you how to spot it.

**Your body hasn't caught up.** For months or years, staying alert kept you safe, and your nervous system learned that lesson well. It doesn't unlearn it because you changed address. The jolt when your phone buzzes, the 3am ceiling-staring, the flinch at a raised voice: [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) explains why these hang on.

**The people around you expected a different story.** They pictured relief, maybe a little sadness, then a steady climb to fine. When you miss the person, or fall apart months later, or go quiet, they don't know what to do with it. Some days, neither do you.

## Was leaving a mistake, then?
It wasn't. When someone who relied on control loses it, things often get harder for a while. That says nothing about whether you should have stayed. For many people the first months are the roughest stretch, and [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first) explains what drives that and how to stay safe through it.

Leaving was a real achievement, and it still wasn't the end. Both of those are true at once.

## What should I expect after leaving an emotionally abusive relationship?
Whether the abuse was physical, emotional or both, people describe a lot of the same things:

- doubting your own memory, or whether it was "that bad"
- missing them, sometimes badly
- jumping at a message tone, or at a car the same colour as theirs
- tiredness, illness, or lying awake replaying old arguments
- anger that turns up late, sometimes months late
- them trying harder to reach you, not less

None of this means you got it wrong. Emotional abuse is harder to point at than a bruise, so the doubt can hang around longer. The chapters on [the trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond), [your nervous system](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) and [anger arriving late](ch:anger-arriving-late) take these one at a time.

## What changes in the first months, and what doesn't?
Some things change the day you leave. You choose when the lights go off. Nobody goes through your phone. You can buy the cheap shampoo or the fancy one, and nobody asks you to justify it.

Other things take longer. Your body may still brace when a key turns in a lock. You may still rehearse what you'd say to them, in the shower, for the ninth time this week. You may still feel guilty for things that were never your fault. The habits that kept you safe haven't had the memo that you've left.

And some things only start after you leave: the court letters, the messages passed through other people, the grief. Expect a mix of all three for a while. The first months can feel harder than you thought, and some days can feel lighter than you thought you'd get.

## Why do people around you think it's over?
From the outside, leaving looks like the end. Your friends saw the bags packed. Your family heard the news. They're relieved, and they'd like you to be relieved too, ideally by Friday.

So you say the messages haven't stopped, or that you miss them, or that the court case is wearing you down. Back comes a blank look and a "But you left." That gap between what people expect and what you're living is a lonely place to stand.

You don't owe anyone a neat story. If it helps, send the people closest to you one page, [Helping someone in year one](ch:helping-someone-in-year-one). It does the explaining so you don't have to.

## What can you stop expecting of yourself?
- To feel only relief.
- To know exactly how you feel about them.
- To be "over it" by any date.
- To handle the court, the children, work and your own feelings all at full strength.
- To never wonder whether you did the right thing.

What you can expect instead is smaller and steadier: get through today, write down what happens, and ask for help with one thing at a time.

## How do I know I'm moving, when it doesn't feel like it?
Progress after leaving rarely feels like progress. It looks more like this:

- You saw a message and decided not to answer it straight away.
- You slept through one whole night.
- You laughed at something and didn't feel guilty for a minute.
- You made a small choice without picturing their reaction first.
- You caught yourself thinking "that wasn't my fault," and half believed it.

None of these feels big on the day. Added up, they're how the long after gets shorter.

## What does "the long after" cover?
This book stays with you after the door closes. It covers:

- **What's happening:** the ways control carries on once direct contact stops.
- **Staying safe:** a safety plan for life after leaving, technology, stalking, your address.
- **How it continues:** children, money, the story they tell, friends and family, the new partner, the return.
- **Contact:** how much, how, and what to say.
- **Courts and the systems:** records, legal help, family court, protective orders, the police and housing. The rules for all of these depend on where you live, and [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) points you to yours.
- **Children:** co-parenting, handovers, what to tell them, how they react.
- **Feelings:** the trauma bond, grief, guilt, anger, closure.
- **Body, health and help:** sleep, your nervous system, therapy, and what to do when it's more than a hard week.
- **People and rebuilding:** friends, new people, money, work, home, trust, dating, and who you are now.

## Who is this for?
Anyone who has left, however long ago. A husband or wife, a girlfriend or boyfriend, a partner of any kind, or a parent or a family that ran your life. The examples often say "your ex," because that's the most common case. If your story is different, [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one) is for you.

If you're still deciding whether to leave, some of this will give you an honest preview. *The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers the leaving itself, and its chapter on [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept) describes, from the inside, what can start once you go.

## Where should I start?
Wherever today is. [Where are you right now?](ch:where-are-you-right-now) sorts the book by situation, and [How to use this book](ch:how-to-use-this-book) explains what it will and won't do. If you left in the last few days, go straight to [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

=== what-is-post-separation-abuse
title: What post-separation abuse is
question: What is post-separation abuse, and what does it look like?
seo: What Is Post-Separation Abuse? Signs, Examples and Help
short: Post-separation abuse is when someone keeps trying to control, frighten or punish a former partner after the relationship ends. It usually runs through the children, money, the courts, technology and other people. It's a recognised form of domestic abuse, and in some places parts of it are crimes.
---
You left, and the control didn't stop. It moved house. Once you can see where it went, it gets much harder for it to make you doubt yourself.

## What is post-separation abuse?
Post-separation abuse is when a former partner keeps trying to control, frighten or punish you after the relationship has ended. It's a pattern of behaviour that runs through whatever roads are still open to them: the children, money, the courts, technology, other people. The aim, conscious or not, is to keep you reachable, unsettled and answering to them.

Researchers describe it as coercive control carried on by other means. Once they can't walk into your kitchen, they use the roads that are left. Whether or not they want you back, they often want you reachable. [The shape it takes when they can't reach you directly](ch:the-shape-it-takes-when-they-cant-reach-you-directly) maps those roads.

## What is coercive control?
Coercive control is a pattern of behaviour that takes away someone's freedom bit by bit. It mixes things like threats, monitoring, cutting you off from friends, control of money and rules about daily life, until you shape your whole day around the other person's reactions. It can happen with or without physical violence.

Post-separation abuse is what that pattern looks like once you've left and the old routes are closed.

## What does post-separation abuse look like?
Here are the common forms. Each one has its own chapter.

- **Through the children.** Arguments at every handover. Questions to the children about your life. Running you down to them. Schedule changes at the last minute, again and again. See [Children as the channel](ch:children-as-the-channel) and the Decoder's page on [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage) (using the children to get at you).
- **Through money.** Child support paid late, short or not at all. Debts left in your name. A house sale dragged out for years. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel) and the Decoder's [Financial Control](/playbook/financial-control).
- **Through the courts.** Filing after filing, each one eating your time and your savings. See [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse) and the Decoder's [Legal Abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse).
- **Through the story.** Telling friends, family, your boss or the school that you're unstable, dangerous or lying. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign) and the Decoder's [Smear Campaigns](/playbook/smear-campaigns).
- **Through other people.** Messages passed through your sister. Mutual friends who suddenly "check in" more often than your own mother does. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).
- **Through technology.** Tracking apps, shared accounts, the smart doorbell they still have the login for. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- **By turning up.** Following, watching, appearing wherever you happen to be. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
- **By coming back.** The warm message on your birthday, the apology, the sudden emergency. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## Is post-separation abuse domestic violence?
It is. Post-separation abuse is domestic abuse that carries on after the relationship has ended. Most definitions of domestic abuse include former partners, and many cover psychological, emotional and economic abuse as well as physical violence.

In England and Wales, for example, the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 covers people who are, or have been, partners. Its definition of domestic abuse includes controlling or coercive behaviour and economic abuse.

## Is post-separation abuse a crime?
Some of it is, depending on where you live.

Threats, assault, stalking, harassment, criminal damage and breaking a court order are crimes almost everywhere. A pattern of controlling behaviour is a crime in a growing number of places:

- **England and Wales:** controlling or coercive behaviour became a crime in 2015. Since 2023 the law also covers former partners who no longer live together.
- **Scotland:** the Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018 covers partners and ex-partners.
- **Ireland:** coercive control became a crime under the Domestic Violence Act 2018.
- **New South Wales, Australia:** coercive control in current and former relationships became a crime in July 2024.
- **United States:** there's no federal coercive control crime. Several states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, now allow courts to consider coercive control in protective order or custody decisions.
- **Canada:** the Divorce Act has included coercive and controlling behaviour in its definition of family violence since 2021.

Laws change. Search "[your country or state] coercive control law" to see where things stand now, and see [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).

## How is this different from a high-conflict divorce?
Plenty of separations are hard. People argue about money and the children, say things they regret, and grieve. That's conflict. It usually runs both ways, and it eases with time.

Post-separation abuse runs one way, and it has a purpose. One person keeps creating problems, and the problems keep landing at the moments that hurt you most. Calling it a "high-conflict divorce" can hide that, because it makes two people sound equally responsible. [Abuse, or a hard breakup?](ch:abuse-or-a-hard-breakup) gives you a fair test.

## Is verbal and emotional abuse after separation still abuse?
It still counts. A lot of post-separation abuse is words: insults in messages, threats dressed up as concern, a running commentary on your parenting. Leaving an emotionally abusive relationship doesn't always end the emotional abuse. It can move into texts, emails and handovers.

You don't need bruises for it to count. Most legal definitions of domestic abuse include psychological and emotional abuse. Keep the messages, even the ones that turn your stomach. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) explains what to save.

## What is the post-separation power and control wheel?
The Power and Control Wheel is a diagram made in 1984 by the Domestic Abuse Intervention Programs in Duluth, Minnesota. It shows physical violence on the rim and the everyday tactics of control as spokes: intimidation, isolation, using the children, money, and more.

Services have since adapted it for life after separation. Those versions add spokes such as using the courts, using the children's schedule, and using other people to watch you. Search "post-separation power and control wheel" to see one. Many people find that seeing it all on one page makes the pattern much easier to explain.

## Why do they keep going?
People ask this more than almost anything else. If it's over, why won't they stop?

There's rarely one answer, and you can't know what's in their head. These are the patterns researchers and advocates describe most:

- **Control.** For someone used to running another person's life, losing that is intolerable. The behaviour is an attempt to get it back.
- **Entitlement.** A belief that they're owed your time, your body, the children, the house or your silence.
- **Punishment.** Leaving is treated as a betrayal that has to cost you something.
- **Image.** If you tell the truth, people might believe you. Getting their version in first protects how they look.

None of these is about something you did or could fix. Plenty of people spend years hunting for the perfect reply, the right tone, the compromise that will finally make it stop. Often there isn't one, because the thing being argued about was never the point.

## A checklist: does this sound like post-separation abuse?
Tick any that fit since you separated:

- Contact keeps coming after you've asked for less, or for none.
- Arrangements about the children change at the last minute, again and again.
- Money you're owed arrives late, short or not at all, often around important dates.
- You've had more court applications, letters or complaints than the situation seems to need.
- People you know have been told things about you that aren't true.
- They seem to know where you've been, or who you've seen.
- You feel watched, even when you can't say how.
- You change your plans, your route or your words to avoid a reaction.

One or two can happen in any hard separation. Several, together and over months, look like a pattern. Take the list to a domestic abuse service and ask, "Does this sound like post-separation abuse to you?"

If they ever turn a list like this around on you, the same test applies: who keeps creating the problems, and who keeps having to react to them?

## What does it do to you?
People often describe post-separation abuse as a slow drain on everything at once.

- **Tiredness.** Being on alert all the time wears the body out. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- **Money.** Legal costs, lost work and unpaid support add up over years.
- **Health.** Many people get ill more often after leaving. See [Getting sick after leaving](ch:getting-sick-after-leaving).
- **Work.** Court dates, messages in work hours and poor sleep make a job harder to hold.
- **The children.** They feel the strain in both homes. See [How children react](ch:how-children-react).
- **Your sense of yourself.** Being doubted by courts or friends can make you doubt yourself.

Once you can name the drain, you can plan for it and ask for the right help, without blaming yourself for being tired.

## Why do systems miss it?
Courts, police and schools are often set up to look at single incidents. Post-separation abuse rarely has one incident that looks serious on its own. A late handover, a sharp email and a complaint to the school can each seem small.

Many systems also start from the idea that both parents are equally responsible for "conflict." That framing can hide one person controlling the other.

What helps is showing the pattern: the dates, what happened, and what it changed for you or the children. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) and [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) cover how.

## How common is post-separation abuse?
There's no single reliable figure, because studies use different definitions and different groups of people. Research does keep finding two things. Separation is a time of raised risk, and many people who lived with coercive control say it carried on after they left. [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first) covers the risk.

## Does it end?
For many people it fades or stops. For some it carries on for years in a smaller form. [When it stops](ch:when-it-stops) is honest about the range, and about what tends to help.

## What can I do about it?
- Write things down as they happen: [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- Make a safety plan for life after leaving: [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- Decide how much contact you'll have, and keep it in writing: [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- Get legal advice from someone who understands this pattern: [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- Look up a word or a question: [A to Z](ch:a-to-z) and [Questions people ask](ch:questions-people-ask).

After Daniel left, his ex-wife texted constantly at first, then stopped when he moved contact to an app. Three months later she applied to court to change the children's arrangements. No single event looked like abuse to his friends. When Daniel laid his record out on one page, he could see that each time one road closed, another opened. That page was what finally made his solicitor and the court understand.

=== why-it-often-gets-worse-first
title: Why it often gets worse first
question: Is leaving an abusive relationship the most dangerous time?
seo: Is Leaving an Abusive Relationship the Most Dangerous Time?
short: Leaving an abusive relationship can be the most dangerous time, especially when the partner was very controlling. Their loss of control is what drives it, and nothing you did caused it. That's a reason to plan for safety now, because staying doesn't make you safer.
---
People say the hard part is leaving, and after that things calm down. If you're reading this, you may have found the opposite. The messages doubled, the threats got sharper, or the court papers arrived. Many people find the first months after leaving are the hardest of all.

> Read this as a reason to plan, and never as a reason to go back. If you're in danger now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).

## Is leaving an abusive relationship the most dangerous time?
For many people, it is. Leaving an abusive relationship is linked with a higher risk of serious harm, especially in the weeks and months straight after, and especially when the partner was very controlling.

A large US study of women killed by partners found that separating from a highly controlling partner after living together was one of the strongest risk factors. Other studies of separated women report the same pattern: harassment and threats often rise around the time of leaving.

None of this means harm is likely for you. Most people who leave aren't seriously hurt. It means this stretch deserves a plan.

## Why does it get worse after leaving?
For the person you left, control was the point of the relationship. When you go, they lose most of it at once. Some people respond by trying harder to get it back. The more their sense of themselves depended on controlling you, the harder they push.

You may notice:

- more messages, calls and contact through other people
- threats, sometimes about the children, money or your reputation
- "emergencies" that need your attention right now
- turning up at your work, your home or the school gate
- court applications that arrive in a cluster
- a sudden switch to charm and apology, which is covered in [Hoovering](ch:hoovering)

The Decoder's page on [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes the threats and shows of force that often come with this.

## When is the risk usually highest?
Risk tends to rise at moments when the person you left feels their control slipping further:

- the first weeks and months after you leave
- when they find out where you live
- handovers of the children
- court dates and decisions about the children
- the day an order is served, or a decision goes against them
- when you start a new relationship, or they hear about one
- anniversaries, birthdays and holidays
- when you stop replying

For each of these moments, ask yourself three things. Who knows where I'll be? How will I get there and back? What will I do if they turn up?

Knowing the moments in advance lets you plan around them. [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) covers how.

## How do services assess risk?
Domestic abuse services and police often use a set list of questions to judge risk. You may be asked them, and it helps to know why.

- **United Kingdom:** many services use the DASH checklist. Police in England and Wales increasingly use a newer tool called DARA. A high score can lead to a MARAC, a meeting where police, health, housing and support services plan together to keep you safe.
- **United States and Canada:** many services use the Danger Assessment, developed by Jacquelyn Campbell, or a short version used by police called the Lethality Assessment Program.
- **Elsewhere:** ask, "Do you use a risk assessment? Can we do one together?"

The questions cover things like strangulation, threats to kill, weapons, stalking, jealousy and whether the violence is getting worse. Answer as honestly as you can. A higher score isn't a judgment on you. It opens the door to more help.

## What if they seem calm?
Sometimes the person you left goes quiet, or turns friendly. That can be real. It can also be a pause, or a way to get you to drop your guard.

Calm on its own isn't a reason to drop your plan. Watch what they do over weeks, and give less weight to what they say in one message. If the calm comes with questions about where you live, who you're seeing, or a sudden wish to "talk properly, just once," keep your safety steps in place. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## How do I tell police or a service about the risk?
Say exactly what happened, in plain words. Professionals are trained to act on facts, and adjectives don't help them.

> **Say:** "I left my partner on [date]. Since then they have [threatened to kill me / followed me / turned up at my home]. In the past they [strangled me / used a weapon / hit me while I was pregnant]. I'm frightened for my safety. I'd like a risk assessment."

If anything in the past included strangulation or choking, say so, even if it happened once and long ago. It's one of the strongest warning signs there is.

## What signs mean I should get help quickly?
Some signs call for help now: threats to kill you or themselves, past strangling or choking, access to a weapon, stalking, and violence that's getting worse. If any of these are part of your story, call a domestic abuse service or the police. [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) lists them and explains what to do.

## Does this mean I left badly?
You didn't. The escalation comes from their loss of control, and nothing you did wrong caused it. No way of leaving guarantees a calm response from someone who relied on controlling you.

Knowing this won't make it easier. It can lift one weight off you, though, the idea that you caused it.

## What helps this week?
1. **Make a plan.** Use [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
2. **Keep a record.** Write down each incident the same day. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows a four-line format.
3. **Talk to a domestic abuse service.** They know the local options, from refuges to protective orders. [Find help](ch:find-help) has numbers.
4. **Narrow the contact.** Fewer channels means fewer openings. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).

## What does planning for a risky moment look like?
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Priya's court hearing was on a Thursday. Her ex had been calm for weeks, but she knew the hearing could change that. She told her domestic abuse worker, who arranged a separate waiting room.

Her brother drove her there and back. She told her workplace she'd be out, and asked reception not to confirm her schedule to anyone. She left the children with a friend whose address her ex didn't know.

The hearing went badly for him. That night there were thirty messages. Because of the plan, she was at her brother's, the children were safe, and she screenshotted the messages for the record instead of answering them.

=== the-shape-it-takes-when-they-cant-reach-you-directly
title: The shape it takes when they can't reach you directly
question: Why is my ex still controlling me after the breakup?
seo: Post-Separation Control: Why Your Ex Still Has a Hold
short: Post-separation control is what happens when one way of reaching you closes and the person you left finds another. It can look nothing like what happened at home, which is why it's so easy to miss. The test is what it does to you, whatever it looks like on the surface.
---
You blocked the number, and the messages stopped. A week later your mum rang to say they'd popped round "just to talk." The school forwarded an email from them. A solicitor's letter landed on the mat.

One door closed, and three others swung open.

## Why is my ex still controlling me?
In a coercive relationship, the method always mattered less than the hold it gave them. When the obvious route closes, the need behind it doesn't go away. It goes looking for the next route.

Sometimes that's a cold, deliberate plan. Often it's habit, a sense of being owed, or a flat refusal to accept that you get to decide. Either way, it lands on you the same.

People will tell you to "just go no contact," as if your ex were a newsletter you could unsubscribe from. Sometimes that's complete protection. Sometimes it closes one door while several others stay open.

## What are the channels?
Post-separation control usually travels along one or more of these roads.

## Why is it so easy to miss?
It looks nothing like the old arguments. A court application about the children doesn't feel like being shouted at. A late payment doesn't feel like being watched. A friendly email to your child's teacher doesn't feel like anything at all. Even professionals who are meant to help can treat each incident as a separate, ordinary dispute.

The pattern only shows when you line the incidents up. That's one big reason to write them down, and [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows you the way.

## How can I tell control from ordinary friction?
Ask one question about each incident: **does this work to keep control over me, or to unsettle me, whatever it looks like on the surface?**

A late payment during a real job loss is a problem. A late payment that lands the week of your exams, every time you set a boundary, is a message.

Not every hard moment after a separation is abuse. Separations involve real disagreements. [Abuse, or a hard breakup?](ch:abuse-or-a-hard-breakup) helps you sort one from the other honestly, which also helps you get believed.

## What does one month of it look like?
Here's how it can go when no single thing seems serious.

- **Week 1:** a polite email about swapping a weekend. You say no. Two days later, a message to your sister: "Is she okay? I'm worried about her."
- **Week 2:** the child support payment arrives four days late. A comment on a photo you're tagged in.
- **Week 3:** a letter from their solicitor raising your parenting. The school calls to "check in" after a conversation with your ex.
- **Week 4:** a warm message on your birthday. When you don't reply, a long one about how you're hurting the children.

One at a time, each could be explained away. On one page, they make a shape. Someone is keeping you busy, doubted and answering to them. That page is the most useful thing you can make.

## How do the channels feed each other?
The channels rarely stay separate. A late payment leads to a message about money, which turns into an argument at the handover. That argument becomes a story told to friends, and before long the story turns up in a court statement.

That's why tackling each problem alone feels endless. Once you see the links, you can close the doors that matter most: move contact to writing, put money arrangements through an official service, and write things down. [Grey rock and written-only contact](ch:grey-rock-and-written-only-contact) and [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel) cover two of the biggest.

## What if a professional becomes the channel?
Sometimes the reach comes through people whose job is to help. A solicitor sends letters that seem designed to provoke. Their therapist contacts you. A teacher repeats something your ex told them.

They may not know what's going on. Stay calm and factual:

> **Say:** "Thank you for getting in touch. There's a history of domestic abuse, and I'm keeping contact with [name] to arrangements about the children, in writing. I'm happy to answer questions about [child]'s schooling."

It's calm and helpful, and fine for a judge to read, even if your ex ever quotes it back as you being "difficult."

You don't have to argue your whole case. You can ask them to put their questions in writing. If a professional keeps taking sides without knowing the facts, you can complain to their organisation.

## What should I write down when I spot a new channel?
Add it to your record in four lines:

1. **When:** the date and time.
2. **What:** what happened, in plain words, or a screenshot.
3. **Who:** who saw it or passed it on.
4. **What it changed:** what you had to do, miss or explain because of it.

Over time, a list of channels with dates is far more persuasive than any single incident. [The record](ch:the-record) has a template.

## What do I do once I see the pattern?
- **Name it, privately.** Seeing the shape takes some of its power away.
- **Close the channels you can.** Some you can shut completely. Others, like contact about the children, you can narrow to writing. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- **Write it down.** Dates and facts, kept somewhere safe.
- **Respond less.** Each reply teaches them which channel still works. See [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).

## Which door would you close first?
Grab any scrap of paper. Write the seven channels down the side: children, money, the story, other people, the courts, technology, the return. Tick the ones they've used this month, then circle the one costing you the most sleep. That's your first door.

Closing one channel won't end the pattern. It gives you back some ground, and shows you which way they'll try next.

If you've typed "narcissist" into a search bar at 3am, you're in very large company. The algorithm noticed too, and now your feed is wall-to-wall people in ring lights explaining your ex to you. The word has become shorthand for a certain kind of person: charming, then cruel; sorry, then cruel again; never, ever wrong.

It's a useful word. It's also a limited one.

## Is my ex a narcissist?
Maybe, maybe not. Narcissistic personality disorder is a diagnosis that a qualified clinician makes after assessing someone. Most people who behave badly after a breakup will never be assessed, and many controlling people wouldn't meet the full criteria anyway.

What you can know is what they did, how often, and what it did to you. That's enough to act on. You don't need to know what's wrong with them to protect yourself from what they do.

## Why does the word help so much?
Because it names something. Before you had it, each awful moment might have felt like a one-off, or like your fault. The word told you it was a pattern, that other people have seen it too, and that it has a shape. That's a big relief, and you're allowed to feel it.

## What are the limits of the label?
- **Courts and services work with behaviour.** "He's a narcissist" will get you less traction than "he has sent 140 messages since the order, including 12 threats." See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **It can make them sound more powerful than they are.** Online, narcissists come across as all-knowing chess masters. They aren't.
- **It can keep your attention on them.** Months of reading about narcissism can turn into one more way your life revolves around them.

## What does a narcissist do after a breakup?
Whatever the diagnosis, the patterns people describe after leaving a controlling partner are consistent. Here they are in plain language, with where to read more.

**They go silent, or they block you.** The silent treatment is a punishment and a test: will you chase? See [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).

**They tell a story about you.** You're unstable, you cheated, you're keeping the children from them. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign) and the Decoder's page on [Smear Campaigns](/playbook/smear-campaigns).

**They turn it around.** Suddenly you're the abuser and they're the one who got hurt. This move has a name, DARVO, explained just below.

**They move on fast.** A new partner appears within weeks, sometimes days. It can feel like proof you meant nothing. Usually it means they needed someone, and you weren't available any more. See [If they left you](ch:if-they-left-you).

**They come back.** The warm message, the apology, the emergency, usually just as you've started to feel steady. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) and the Decoder's [Hoovering / Relief Bait](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait).

## What is DARVO?
DARVO stands for deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. It's what happens when someone called out on their behaviour denies it, attacks the person who raised it, then casts themselves as the real victim. The psychologist Jennifer Freyd named it in 1997. After a breakup, it often sounds like "you're the abusive one."

It works by putting you on the defence, so the conversation is about you and never gets to what they did. The Decoder's [DARVO](/playbook/darvo) page shows it in action, and [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser) helps if it has reached professionals.

## Do they come back?
Often, yes. A return after silence is so common it has its own name. The timing tends to follow your recovery more than their growth, and they often turn up just as you've started sleeping again. [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) covers the forms it takes, and how to answer, or not.

## Why did they block me?
Blocking can be a way of having the last word, of punishing you, or of making you chase. It can also mean they've moved their attention elsewhere. Whatever the reason, a block does you one favour. It closes a channel, and you don't have to reopen it.

## What is the clinical diagnosis?
Picture a psychiatrist's office, an assessment that takes hours, and a manual open on the desk. That's what a diagnosis involves, and only a trained clinician who assesses the person can make it.

Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) is a mental health diagnosis in the DSM-5, the main psychiatric manual used in the US and many other countries. To be diagnosed, a person shows a long-standing pattern of grandiosity, a need for admiration and a lack of empathy, across most areas of life, starting by early adulthood.

The diagnosable disorder is uncommon. Estimates vary a lot between studies, from under 1 in 100 people to around 6 in 100. Plenty more people have some narcissistic traits without having the disorder.

So when you search "narcissist," you're mostly reading about a pattern of behaviour, not a diagnosis. That's fine. The pattern is what affects you.

## Why do narcissist, psychopath, sociopath and borderline all blur together?
Type any of these words into your phone at 2am and the same videos come up, often with the same shocked-face thumbnails. Online, the words get used almost interchangeably. Clinically, they're different:

- **Narcissistic personality disorder:** grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy.
- **"Psychopath" and "sociopath":** not diagnoses in the DSM. They usually refer to antisocial personality disorder, which involves disregard for others' rights, deceit and lack of remorse.
- **Borderline personality disorder:** intense fear of abandonment, unstable relationships and emotions. Many people with this diagnosis are far more often harmed than harmful.

For your safety and your case, the behaviour matters more than the label. "She sent 60 messages in a day after I said no" is clearer to everyone than any diagnosis.

## What if they have a real diagnosis or mental illness?
Some abusive people do have mental health conditions. Most people with mental health conditions aren't abusive.

A condition can explain some things, like mood or impulsivity. It rarely explains a pattern of control: the planning, the choice of targets, the way the behaviour changes when other people are watching.

Writers who work with abusive men, such as Lundy Bancroft in *Why Does He Do That?*, describe abuse as coming from beliefs and entitlement more than from illness. You can feel compassion for someone's struggles and still keep yourself safe from what they do. Their diagnosis doesn't make their behaviour your job to manage.

## What if they call you the narcissist?
It might arrive as a text, a comment to a mutual friend, or a line in a court statement. It's common. After a break-up, the person who controlled the relationship sometimes borrows the language of abuse and aims it at the person who left. It can be a deliberate tactic, or it can be how they see it.

If it shakes you, read [Abuse, or a hard breakup?](ch:abuse-or-a-hard-breakup) and its section "Am I the abuser?" People who worry they might be abusive, and look at it honestly, are usually not the person running the pattern. If you're being accused to professionals, read [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## What does "narcissistic abuse recovery" mean?
It's a popular phrase for recovering from a relationship with someone who controlled, belittled and manipulated you. You'll find books, videos and groups built around it.

Some of that material helps. Some sells quick fixes, or keeps you studying the other person's diagnosis for years. A good sign is anything that moves your attention back to your own life: your safety, your sleep, your people, your choices. That's what this book tries to do. Start with [What "recovered" means](ch:what-recovered-actually-means).

## Can a narcissist change?
People can change, but deep change in long-standing patterns is slow and rare. It shows up as steady behaviour over a long time, and promises don't count. If you're wondering whether someone has changed, look for accountability that doesn't put any pressure on you. [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice) offers a careful way to judge.

## So what should I call it?
Call it whatever helps you. In court, with a lawyer or with a domestic abuse service, describe the behaviour. In your own head, use the word that makes it make sense. *The Weighing*, another book in this library, covers how to judge people fairly when you're not sure.

## When the label starts doing your thinking
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

- Do I now read everything they do as proof, even the ordinary things?
- Am I spending more time studying them than looking after myself?

- Is the label helping me act, or keeping me stuck in the past?

## When the label takes over
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

For a year after leaving, Jo watched videos about narcissists every night. Each one explained something, and each one left her feeling worse. She spent hours trying to work out whether her ex was "covert" or "malignant," and whether he'd ever change.
