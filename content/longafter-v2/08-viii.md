=== what-your-nervous-system-is-doing
title: What your nervous system is doing
question: Why am I still on edge? What is hypervigilance after abuse?
seo: Hypervigilance After Abuse: Why You're Still on Edge
short: Hypervigilance after abuse is your nervous system staying on high alert after the danger has passed. A jumpy startle, scanning every room, a racing heart when your phone buzzes: these come from a body that learned alertness kept you safe. It eases with safety, time, small daily practices and, when needed, trauma-focused help.
---
Why are you still this jumpy when you're the one who got out?

A door slams down the street and your whole body jolts. Your phone buzzes and your heart is off before you've even looked. You sit facing the door in every café, like a mob boss in a film. Your mind knows you're safer now. Your body hasn't had the memo.

## What is hypervigilance after abuse?
Hypervigilance is a state of constant alert: scanning for danger, and reacting fast and hard to small things. After abuse it's very common. You might notice:

- a strong startle at noises, raised voices or sudden movement
- watching moods and faces closely, ready for a shift
- trouble relaxing, even in safe places
- a racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing
- trouble sleeping. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).
- feeling numb or shut down, which is the other side of the same system
- going along with people to keep the peace, sometimes called fawning

If you nodded at most of those, yep. You're in very ordinary company.

## Why doesn't it switch off now that I'm safe?
For a long time, being alert was useful. Reading their mood kept you safe. Your nervous system learned that lesson thoroughly, and it doesn't unlearn it because you moved out. As far as your body is concerned, vigilance worked.

It retrains slowly, through lots of small, repeated experiences of real safety. One big realisation won't do it on its own.

> Your body did its job well for a long time, and it's still doing it.

## Why do I feel spaced out or detached?
That's often dissociation. Many people who lived with coercive control describe it: feeling far away, numb, as if you're watching yourself, losing chunks of time, or the world looking flat and unreal.

It's a survival response. When you couldn't fight or leave, your mind found a way to step back. After leaving, it can keep happening, especially when something reminds you of them.

Grounding can help in the moment, and there are techniques below that work. If it happens a lot, or you lose time, tell a doctor or a therapist who understands trauma. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What helps my nervous system settle?
**Two practices you can do anywhere**

*A longer breath out.* Breathe in through your nose for a count of four. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of six or seven, as if blowing through a straw. Repeat five times. A longer out-breath tells your body to slow down.

*Five things.* Look around and name five things you can see. Then four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell (the bin counts), one you can taste. Say them out loud if you can. It pulls your attention back into the room you're in.

**Things that help over weeks and months**
- Regular sleep and meal times, so your body has predictable rhythms.
- Movement you like: walking, swimming, dancing, stretching.
- Time with people who feel safe.
- Less contact with the person you left, and fewer reminders. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- Small, safe experiences of calm, repeated often.

*The Root*, a guided practice in this library, has breathing and body exercises built for this. You can find it at [The Root](/shadowroot).

## Could this be PTSD or complex PTSD?
Maybe. Post-traumatic stress disorder includes reliving what happened (flashbacks, nightmares), avoiding reminders, and feeling constantly on alert.

Complex PTSD is recognised in the World Health Organization's diagnostic system. It adds long-lasting problems with emotions, self-worth and relationships, and it's linked to prolonged trauma such as ongoing abuse.

Only a qualified professional can diagnose either. If these sound familiar and they're getting in the way of your life, there are effective treatments. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## Triggers, and mapping yours
A trigger is anything that sets off the alarm before you've had time to think: a ringtone, a car like theirs, a smell, a tone of voice, a type of envelope. Your body reacts as if the danger is back.

Mapping your triggers takes some of their power away. For a week, note each time you suddenly feel panicky, frozen or furious, and what was happening just before. Patterns usually show up. Once you know them, you can plan: change the ringtone, take a different route, have a grounding step ready.

## Fight, flight, freeze, fawn
These are the body's automatic responses to threat:

- **Fight:** anger, arguing back, tension.
- **Flight:** wanting to get away, restlessness, keeping busy.
- **Freeze:** going blank, numb or unable to move or speak.
- **Fawn:** trying to please or calm the other person to stay safe.

You didn't choose any of these, and none of them is a weakness. Many people in abusive relationships learned to freeze or fawn because fighting or leaving felt too dangerous. Those responses may still switch on now, even with people who are safe.

## Grounding that works in the moment
When the alarm goes off, try one of these:

- **5-4-3-2-1:** name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can touch, two you can smell, one you can taste.
- **Long breath out:** breathe in for four, out for six or eight, for a minute.
- **Temperature:** hold something cold, splash your face, or wrap your hands round a hot drink.
- **Press down:** push your feet into the floor or your back into a wall, and feel the support.
- **Say where you are:** "It's Tuesday. I'm in my kitchen. I'm safe right now."

## Body-based help
Movement helps the nervous system settle: walking, swimming, dancing, stretching. Some people find trauma-sensitive yoga helpful, where you're always in control of what you do.

Regular routines, enough food, daylight and time with safe people all tell your body, over and over, that the danger has passed. Boring, yes. Boring is the point.

## Reacting around the children
You may snap, freeze or panic in front of your children when something triggers you. Afterwards, repair it simply: "I got a big fright just now and I was sharp with you. That wasn't your fault. I'm okay now."

Children cope better with a parent who repairs than with one who never shows feelings.

## Treatments for PTSD
If you have PTSD, there are treatments with good evidence. Clinical guidelines, such as the UK's NICE guidance, mainly recommend two: trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy and EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing).

Both are usually delivered over a set number of sessions by a trained therapist. Ask your doctor about them. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## When should I get help quickly?
If you feel unable to cope, can't function day to day, or have thoughts of harming yourself, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

::: scene
## When the doorbell rang
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Months after leaving, Jas froze every time the doorbell rang, heart pounding, even when she was expecting a parcel she'd ordered herself. She mapped her triggers for a week: the doorbell, a certain car, raised voices on TV.

She changed the doorbell chime, put a camera on the door and practised a breathing pattern each time it rang. The freeze still came, but shorter. After a few months, a delivery was just a delivery most days.
:::

::: action
## One grounding step, ready
Pick one grounding step from this chapter. Write it on a sticky note and put it on the fridge or the back of your phone, then try it once now, for one minute.
:::

A body on high alert rarely sleeps well. When you've got a minute, [Sleep](ch:sleep) is the natural next stop.

=== sleep
title: Sleep
question: Why can't I sleep after the breakup, and what helps?
seo: Can't Sleep After a Breakup or Abuse? What Helps
short: If you can't sleep after a breakup with an abusive partner, your body is likely still on alert, and nights are when grief, fear and stress have room to surface. Steady sleep and wake times, a wind-down routine and a plan for 3am help. If it lasts for weeks, see a doctor: there are effective treatments.
---
You're exhausted all day. Then you lie down, and your brain, which was useless at 3pm, suddenly wants to replay every conversation, rehearse court and listen for the car. Or you drop off fine and wake at 3am, wide awake and afraid.

## Why can't I sleep after the breakup?
- **Your nervous system is still on watch.** A quiet, dark room can be when alertness is strongest, because there's nothing else to focus on. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- **Grief and fear surface at night,** when the day's distractions are gone.
- **Practical stress lands hardest in the dark:** money, court, the children.
- **Your body may be worn down,** which [Getting sick after leaving](ch:getting-sick-after-leaving) explains.
- **The relationship may have trained you not to sleep deeply,** if nights were when things happened. The Decoder describes [Sleep Deprivation](/playbook/sleep-deprivation) (being kept tired on purpose) used as control.

Disrupted sleep after abuse is so common it's almost expected. It's part of the same aftermath, and the same things help.

## What helps me sleep?
- **Keep the same wake time** every day, even after a bad night. It anchors your body clock more than bedtime does.
- **Go to bed when sleepy,** not just tired.
- **Wind down for 30 to 60 minutes:** dim the lights, and leave the news and their social media alone. See [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile).
- **Make the room feel safe:** a lock you trust, a light you can reach, your phone charged nearby. See [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours).
- **Use the bed for sleep.** If you're awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up and do something dull in low light. Fold socks. Read the back of a cereal box. Go back when sleepy.
- **Cut caffeine after midday,** and be careful with alcohol: it helps you drop off but wrecks the second half of the night. See [Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through](ch:drinking-drugs-and-getting-through).

## What do I do at 3am?
- Don't check the clock or your phone.
- Try the longer out-breath from [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- Keep a notebook by the bed. Write the worry down and tell yourself you'll deal with it in the morning. It'll still be there. It always is.
- If fear is high, check the doors once, then go back to bed. Checking again and again feeds the fear.

## A wind-down routine
A predictable routine tells your body it's safe to sleep:

- Get up at the same time each day, even after a bad night.
- Dim the lights an hour before bed.
- Put screens away, especially messages from them.
- Do the same few calm things each night: a shower, a book, a stretch, a warm drink without caffeine.

## Making the bedroom feel safe
If you're on alert at night, small changes can help:

- Check the doors and windows once, then stop.
- A door chain or a simple door alarm can let your mind switch off.
- A soft light, white noise or a fan can mask sounds that startle you.
- If you shared a bed with them, change something: the bedding, the side you sleep on, where the bed stands.

## Nights when the children are away
Empty nights can be the hardest. The house is quiet, and your mind fills it. Plan for them: a friend on the phone, an audiobook, a later bedtime with a good film.

Some people sleep better on the sofa or in a different room on those nights. That's fine. Nobody's handing out marks for where you sleep.

## The phone at night
Messages at night can wreck sleep. Charge your phone outside the bedroom, use "do not disturb" with only emergency contacts allowed, or get a separate alarm clock. If you need the phone for safety, keep it nearby but face down, with their notifications silenced.

## Caffeine, alcohol and sleep
Caffeine can stay in your body for many hours, so cut it after early afternoon if you're struggling. Alcohol may help you fall asleep, but it tends to wake you in the night and makes sleep lighter. See [Drinking, drugs and getting through](ch:drinking-drugs-and-getting-through).

## When should I see a doctor?
If poor sleep lasts more than a few weeks, or affects your work, driving or parenting, talk to a doctor.

The best-tested treatment for long-term insomnia is a short course of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. It works without medication and is often available online or through health services. Nightmares can be treated too.

## What about nightmares?
Nightmares are common after abuse, and they can be treated. One method, imagery rehearsal therapy, has you rewrite a recurring nightmare while awake and rehearse the new version. It has good evidence for trauma-related nightmares. Ask your doctor or a therapist.

## Should I take sleeping pills?
Talk to a doctor first. Some sleep medicines help for a short time, but they're not usually a long-term answer, and some don't mix with alcohol or other medicines. The talking treatment for insomnia often works better in the long run.

::: action
## A week of wind-downs
Before you put your phone down tonight, set one daily alarm for the same wake-up time all week. Then put a pen and notebook by the bed. That's the week set up.
:::

If you're sleeping badly and catching every cold going, the two are often linked. [Getting sick after leaving](ch:getting-sick-after-leaving) is the next chapter, and it's a short one.

## Sources
- Krakow, B., et al. (2001). Imagery rehearsal therapy for chronic nightmares in sexual assault survivors with posttraumatic stress disorder. *JAMA*, 286(5), 537–545.
- Riemann, D., et al. (2017). European guideline for the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia. *Journal of Sleep Research*, 26(6), 675–700.
- National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. *Insomnia: Clinical Knowledge Summary* (revised regularly).
- Ebrahim, I. O., et al. (2013). Alcohol and sleep I: Effects on normal sleep. *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research*, 37(4), 539–549.

=== getting-sick-after-leaving
title: Getting sick after leaving
question: Why am I getting sick after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: Getting Sick After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: Getting sick after leaving an abusive relationship is common. Long-term stress affects the immune system, sleep, digestion and pain, and some people only fall ill once the crisis eases. Abuse can also leave injuries you didn't know about. See a doctor, and tell them what you've been through.
---
You finally got out. And then you got sick. Colds that won't shift, headaches, stomach trouble, exhaustion, pain that wanders around your body like it's looking for somewhere to sit. It feels deeply unfair, as if your body waited for the ceasefire to file all its complaints at once.

## Why do people get sick after leaving?
- **Long-term stress wears the body down.** Months or years of stress hormones affect the immune system, digestion, blood pressure, sleep and pain. Research links prolonged stress to weaker immune function.
- **The crash after the crisis.** Many people hold together through the worst of it, then fall ill once things calm down.
- **Poor sleep, poor food, no time to rest.** All common after leaving.
- **Injuries you didn't know about.** Head injuries and strangulation can have effects that show up later, such as headaches, dizziness, memory problems, or changes to your voice or swallowing.

Studies of people who've experienced partner abuse find higher rates of chronic pain, stomach problems, headaches and other health conditions, often for years afterwards.

> Your body kept going while it had to. Now it's asking for care.

## What should I tell my doctor?
You don't have to give every detail. Something like this is enough:

> **Say:** "I've recently left an abusive relationship. I've been getting ill a lot and I'm exhausted. I also want to mention that I was [strangled / hit on the head] at some point, in case that matters."

Doctors are increasingly trained to ask about abuse. What you tell them can also become a record if you ever need one. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

If you were ever strangled or choked, mention it even if it was long ago. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

## What helps the body recover?
- Sleep first. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).
- Regular, simple meals. Toast counts on a bad day.
- Gentle movement.
- Fewer stressors where you can, including less contact. See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- Follow up on anything that doesn't settle.

## Which symptoms should I never ignore?
Get urgent care for:

- a sudden, severe headache
- confusion, or trouble speaking or seeing
- weakness or numbness on one side of the body
- fainting or seizures
- trouble breathing or swallowing
- chest pain

After strangling, some injuries show up days later. Tell the doctor it happened.

## What about check-ups I've missed?
Abuse often means missed appointments and injuries that were never treated. Book the dentist, an eye test, any screening you've missed, and a check on any old injury that never healed right. Yes, all the boring ones. They add up to you being looked after.

## What about my medication?
If the person you left controlled your medicine or your appointments, ask your doctor to review everything you take. Ask the pharmacy to keep your details private.

## Can stress cause physical illness?
It can raise the risk and make many conditions worse: headaches, gut problems, high blood pressure, skin flare-ups, pain.

That doesn't mean it's "all in your head." Your body carried what happened, and it deserves treatment like any other illness. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## Common patterns
People often notice these in the months after leaving:

- more colds and infections
- headaches and migraines
- stomach and bowel problems
- back, neck and jaw pain
- skin flare-ups
- flare-ups of long-term conditions
- exhaustion that sleep doesn't fix

Tell your doctor about the abuse and the stress. It helps them see the pattern and choose the right tests.

## After strangulation: delayed signs
Strangulation can cause injuries that don't show at first. Seek medical help, even days or weeks later, if you have:

- a changed or hoarse voice, or trouble swallowing
- pain in your neck or throat
- trouble breathing
- tiny red spots on your face, neck or eyes
- headaches, dizziness, confusion or memory problems
- weakness or numbness on one side

Some of these can signal a serious problem, like damage to blood vessels in the neck that can lead to a stroke. Say clearly: "I was strangled on [date]."

## Head injuries
Many people who were hit, shaken or pushed have had head injuries without knowing it. Signs of a lasting brain injury can include headaches, memory and concentration problems, dizziness, irritability, and sensitivity to light or noise.

If this sounds familiar, tell your doctor about any blows to the head, even old ones. Support and rehabilitation can help.

## Dental care
Abuse often damages teeth: from injuries, from clenching and grinding, or from not being allowed or able to see a dentist. Book a check-up when you can. Dentists also see signs of abuse and can be a source of support.

## Sexual and reproductive health
If you couldn't control contraception, sex or health care, a check-up may help: screening tests you've missed, sexual health tests, and a review of contraception. Sexual health clinics are usually free and confidential. See [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse).

## Moving gently
Exercise helps both mood and sleep, but after a long stretch of stress your body may be tired. Start small: a short walk each day, stretching, gentle swimming. Build up slowly. Rest is part of recovery too, so the sofa is allowed.

::: action
## Book the appointment
Put your doctor's number or booking page on your screen now and book the check-up you've been putting off. When you go, say: "I've recently left an abusive relationship and I'd like a general health check." Mention any injuries, especially to the head or neck, even old ones.
:::

If any of what happened to your body involved sex, contraception or pregnancy, [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse) is next. Read it when you're ready, not before.

## Sources
- Campbell, J. C. (2002). Health consequences of intimate partner violence. *The Lancet*, 359(9314), 1331–1336.
- Segerstrom, S. C., and Miller, G. E. (2004). Psychological stress and the human immune system: A meta-analytic study of 30 years of inquiry. *Psychological Bulletin*, 130(4), 601–630.
- Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention (US): medical guidance on non-fatal strangulation.


=== if-there-was-sexual-abuse
title: If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion
question: Is sexual abuse in a relationship still abuse? What do I do now?
seo: Sexual Abuse in a Relationship and Reproductive Coercion
short: Sexual abuse in a relationship is abuse, and in most places sex without consent is rape or sexual assault even between partners or spouses. Reproductive coercion, such as interfering with contraception or pressuring a pregnancy, is abuse too. Health care, specialist support and reporting options exist, and the choices are yours.
---
This chapter is about sex that wasn't freely chosen, and about control over your body and fertility. It may be hard to read. Take breaks. Skip parts if you need to. You can come back any time.

If you're in danger, or you've just been assaulted, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now). If you're having thoughts of harming yourself, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

## Is sexual abuse in a relationship still abuse?
Yes. Being in a relationship, or married, doesn't mean consent to sex.

Sex you didn't want, were pressured into, or felt you couldn't refuse is sexual abuse. So is sex when you couldn't consent because you were asleep, drunk or afraid. In most countries it's also a crime, including within marriage.

It can include:

- sex after you said no, or when you couldn't say no
- pressure, guilt or threats until you gave in
- sex as a way to "make up" after violence
- being made to do things you didn't want to do
- being filmed or photographed without consent. See [Intimate images and online threats](ch:intimate-images-and-online-threats).

Many people only name it as abuse after they've left. That's common, and it doesn't make it any less real.

## What is reproductive coercion?
It's control over your fertility and reproductive choices, such as:

- hiding, damaging or refusing contraception, or removing a condom without agreement
- pressuring you to get pregnant, or to stay pregnant
- pressuring you to end a pregnancy
- controlling access to doctors or sexual health care

Researchers recognise it as a form of abuse, and health services increasingly ask about it.

## What health care might I need?
If the assault was recent:

- **Emergency contraception,** ideally as soon as possible.
- **Medicine to prevent HIV (PEP),** which needs to start within 72 hours.
- **A forensic exam,** if you might want to report now or later. Specialist services can collect and store evidence even if you haven't decided. In the US, ask a hospital for a sexual assault nurse examiner. In the UK, contact a sexual assault referral centre.

At any time:

- **Sexual health testing** for infections.
- **Contraception you control,** if that matters to you.
- **Support for your mental health.** See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## Where can I get specialist support?
- **United States:** RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline, 1-800-656-4673.
- **England and Wales:** Rape Crisis 24/7 Support Line, 0808 500 2222.
- **Scotland:** Rape Crisis Scotland helpline, 08088 01 03 02.
- **Ireland:** Rape Crisis Centre helpline, 1800 77 8888.
- **Australia:** 1800RESPECT, 1800 737 732.
- **Canada:** search "sexual assault centre [your city]."

## Do I have to report it?
No. Reporting is your choice. You can report now, later, or never. You can get medical care and support without reporting.

If you want to report, a specialist service can explain what happens and support you through it. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).

## It is a crime, including in marriage
Sex without consent is rape or sexual assault, whoever does it, including a husband, wife or long-term partner. In the UK it has been clear in law since 1991 that rape within marriage is a crime. Every US state now treats it as a crime.

Being in a relationship, having had sex before, or not fighting back doesn't mean you consented. Consent can't be given when someone is afraid, asleep, too drunk or high to decide, or pressured with threats. Saying yes once doesn't mean yes every time.

## Common reactions
After sexual abuse, people often feel:

- numb, or as if it happened to someone else
- ashamed, dirty or to blame
- confused, especially if they loved the person
- frightened of touch, or of particular places, smells or sounds
- unsure whether "it counts"

You may also have frozen or gone along with it to stay safe. That's one of the body's most common responses to threat, and it doesn't mean you agreed.

## Urgent care and evidence
If it happened recently, you can get medical care and, if you want, have evidence collected, even if you haven't decided whether to report.

- **United Kingdom:** Sexual Assault Referral Centres (SARCs) offer medical care, support and forensic examinations. Evidence can be collected up to around a week afterwards, and stored while you decide.
- **United States:** a sexual assault nurse examiner can do a forensic exam. Federal law means you can have the exam without reporting to police and without paying for it.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] sexual assault centre."

If you think you might want evidence, try not to wash or change clothes. If you already have, go anyway.

## Emergency contraception and HIV prevention
- **Emergency contraception:** pills work best as soon as possible, within three to five days depending on the type. A copper coil fitted within five days is the most effective option.
- **HIV prevention (PEP):** medicine that can prevent HIV must be started within 72 hours. Ask for it at a sexual health clinic or emergency department.
- **Sexual health tests:** a clinic can test for infections and arrange follow-up.

## Specialist services
- **United Kingdom:** Rape Crisis England and Wales runs a free 24/7 support line on 0808 500 2222, for anyone aged 16 or over, with online chat as well. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own services.
- **United States:** the RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline is 1-800-656-4673.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] rape crisis."

They support people whatever happened and however long ago.

## What about sex and intimacy later?
It may take time before sex or touch feels safe again. That's common. Go at your own pace. A new partner who respects your pace is showing you something important. [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not) covers this.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* includes sexual coercion among the signs to act on fast in [The 30-Minute Rescue](/feminine/the-30-minute-rescue#top).

::: scene
## Getting care, later
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Months after she left, Kim realised that what had happened in the relationship was sexual abuse. She hadn't reported it, and she wasn't sure she wanted to.

A rape crisis line listened and explained her options without pushing her. Then she booked a sexual health check, and started seeing a counsellor who specialised in sexual violence. Whether to report was still undecided, and it could stay that way. Getting care came first.
:::

::: action
## There's no deadline on support
Save the number of the specialist line for your country in your phone now. If it feels safer, save it under a name only you'll recognise. You don't have to call. It's there whenever you want it, even if you're not sure what to call what happened.
:::

When you want someone to work through this with, [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this) explains what to look for and what to ask.

=== drinking-drugs-and-getting-through
title: Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through
question: I'm drinking more since the breakup. Is that a problem?
seo: Drinking More After a Breakup or Abuse? What Helps
short: Drinking after a breakup from an abusive relationship, or using drugs, food, work or anything else to get through, is common. It's a way of coping, not a moral failing. If it's growing, or you can't cut back, help is free and confidential, and there are safer steps you can take today.
---
A glass to sleep. Then two. Something to take the edge off before a handover. Scrolling until 3am. Working until you drop. Not eating, or eating everything in the cupboard standing up.

You're getting through. The question is whether the way you're getting through is starting to cost you.

If you're in crisis, or you've had thoughts of harming yourself, go to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath) now.

## Why do people drink more after a breakup or abuse?
Because it works, for a while. Alcohol and drugs quieten an alert nervous system, blunt grief and fear, and help you sleep.

Research finds higher rates of drinking and drug use among people who've experienced partner abuse. Some people were also pressured to drink or use by the person they left.

There's no shame in having reached for something. What matters now is whether it's helping or starting to hurt.

## How do I know if it's becoming a problem?
Some questions to ask yourself, privately:

- Am I drinking or using more than I used to, or more often?
- Do I need it to sleep, to face a handover, or to get through the evening?
- Have I tried to cut back and found it hard?
- Is it affecting my work, my children, my health or my money?
- Am I hiding how much?
- Could the person I left use it against me in court?

If several are yes, it's worth getting support now, before it grows.

> You don't have to be in crisis to ask for help.

## What can I do today?
- **Count it.** Write down what you drink or use for a week. Seeing it clearly is a first step.
- **Have alcohol-free days.** Two or three a week, to start.
- **Don't drink to sleep.** It breaks up the second half of the night. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).
- **Plan the hard moments.** Before a handover or court date, decide what you'll do instead: a walk, a call, the breathing in [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- **Don't drink or use before handovers, court, or any contact with the person you left.**
- **Keep it out of the house** for a while, if that helps.
- **Never drive** after drinking or using.

If you've been drinking heavily every day, don't stop suddenly without medical advice. Talk to a doctor first.

## Where can I get help?
These services are free and confidential:

- **United States:** SAMHSA National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357.
- **United Kingdom:** Drinkline, 0300 123 1110 (Scotland: 0800 7314 314); FRANK (drugs), 0300 123 6600.
- **Australia:** National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline, 1800 250 015.
- **Ireland:** HSE Drugs and Alcohol Helpline, 1800 459 459.
- **Canada:** search "[your province] addiction help line."

A doctor can also help, and so can a therapist who understands trauma. Treating the trauma often makes it easier to cut back. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What it's doing for you
Drinking or using usually does a job: helping you sleep, quieting the fear, numbing grief, giving you courage for a hard conversation. Naming the job helps you find other ways to do it.

Ask yourself: "When do I most want a drink? What am I hoping it will do?" Then choose one alternative for that moment.

## If they pushed substances on you
Some abusers push their partner into drinking or using drugs, then use it against them: to discredit them, to control them, or in court. If that happened, it's part of the abuse.

Tell your lawyer or advocate, and a doctor or substance service, so it's understood in context.

## Fear about the children
Many parents are afraid to ask for help with drinking or drugs because they think their children will be taken. In most places, asking for help is seen as a protective step, and services want parents to get support.

A domestic abuse service can help you find substance support that understands both.

## Support groups
- **Mutual help groups:** Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous and SMART Recovery meet in person and online in many countries.
- **For families:** Al-Anon supports people affected by someone else's drinking.
- **Specialist services:** your doctor can refer you, or you can search "[your area] alcohol and drug service."

## What about other ways of getting through?
Overworking, not eating, overeating, gambling, shopping, scrolling, risky sex. The same questions apply: is it helping, or is it starting to cost you? The same help applies too.

::: scene
## Noticing the pattern
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

After he left his marriage, Gareth started having a few drinks every night to sleep. Within months it was most of a bottle. When he noticed he was drinking before handovers to calm his nerves, he talked to his doctor.

The doctor didn't judge him, and referred him to a local service. Limits came next: no drinking the night before seeing his children, and a weekly group. It wasn't a straight line. But he found other ways to get to sleep, and his mornings got clearer.
:::

::: action
## One honest question
Tonight, before the first drink, ask yourself: "What is the drink doing for me tonight?" And then: "Is there one other way I could do that job, just for tonight?"
:::

If the answer keeps coming back to the same old hurt, [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this) can help you find someone to work on that part.

=== finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this
title: Finding a therapist who understands this
question: How do I find a therapist for trauma bonding and abuse?
seo: Therapist for Trauma Bond and Abuse Recovery: What to Look For
short: A good therapist for trauma bond recovery and abuse understands coercive control, works in a trauma-informed way, and can offer approaches with evidence behind them. Ask about their experience before you start. Low-cost options exist in most places, and couples counselling with your ex is the wrong tool.
---
You finally book the appointment, sit down, and try to explain. Then the therapist asks what you did to make them so angry. Cue the long walk to the bus stop, feeling worse than when you arrived.

Not every therapist understands coercive control. A good one can help you loosen the trauma bond, settle your nervous system and rebuild trust in yourself. A poor fit can make you feel worse, or even take your ex's side.

## What should I look for in a therapist?
- **Experience with domestic abuse and coercive control,** not just relationship problems.
- **Trauma-informed practice:** they understand how trauma affects the body, memory and trust, and they go at your pace.
- **Comfort with mixed feelings,** including missing the person. See [What you're allowed to feel about someone you left](ch:what-youre-allowed-to-feel-about-someone-you-left).
- **No pressure to forgive,** reconcile or "see both sides" of the abuse.
- **You feel safe with them** after a few sessions.

## Which therapies help after abuse?
For post-traumatic stress, health guidelines recommend trauma-focused therapies, including:

- **Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy,** including cognitive processing therapy.
- **EMDR** (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing).
- **Prolonged exposure,** in some guidelines.

Other approaches can help with the wider effects: compassion-focused therapy for shame, and therapies that focus on relationships and boundaries. You don't have to pick the method yourself. A good therapist will explain their approach and why.

If you're looking for a therapist for trauma bonding in particular, ask how they work with attachment to an abusive partner. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).

> **Questions to ask:** "How much of your work involves domestic abuse or coercive control?" "How do you work with someone who still misses the person who hurt them?" "What approach would you use, and why?"

## Why is couples counselling the wrong tool?
Couples counselling assumes two people with roughly equal power, both acting in good faith. With coercive control, that isn't the case. Joint sessions can be unsafe, and can make the abuse look like a shared problem.

Many domestic abuse experts advise against couples counselling where there's been abuse.

Watch out, too, for therapy language used against you: "you're triggering me," "I need you to respect my boundaries," said as a way to control you. The Decoder calls this [Weaponized Therapy-Speak](/playbook/weaponized-therapy-speak) (therapy words used as a weapon).

## How do I find low-cost therapy?
- **Domestic abuse services** often offer free counselling or support groups.
- **Public health services:** in England, NHS Talking Therapies accept self-referral. In Australia, a GP can make a Mental Health Treatment Plan for subsidised sessions. In Ireland and Canada, ask your doctor about public mental health services.
- **Sliding-scale fees:** many private therapists reduce fees on request. In the US, Open Path Collective lists low-cost therapists.
- **Training clinics:** university clinics where supervised trainees offer low-cost therapy.
- **Support groups:** free, and often run by people who've been through it.

## What if the first therapist isn't right?
Try another. Fit matters more than anything else in how well therapy works. Therapy is one of the few places where walking away after one date is completely normal, and nobody will chase you with a 47-message essay about it.

You can say, "I don't think this is the right fit for me," and leave. You don't owe an explanation.

Signs it isn't right: they suggest you see things from the abuser's side, push couples work, rush you into the worst memories, or seem uncomfortable with what you tell them. When you look for the next one, ask about their experience with abuse and coercive control before you book.

## The main therapies in plain words
- **Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT):** helps you process memories and change frightening beliefs about yourself and the world.
- **EMDR:** uses guided eye movements or taps while you recall memories, to help the brain process them. Recommended for PTSD in guidelines such as NICE's.
- **Cognitive processing therapy (CPT):** focuses on the stuck beliefs trauma leaves, like "it was my fault."
- **Counselling and talking therapies:** regular time with someone to make sense of what happened, often helpful for grief, confidence and relationships.
- **Body-based therapies:** focus on calming the body. Some are well studied; others have less evidence. Ask what the therapist's training is.

## Group support
Groups for people who've left abuse can be powerful. Hearing someone else describe exactly what you lived through can lift a lot of shame.

In the UK, many services run the Freedom Programme or similar courses. In the US and elsewhere, domestic violence programs often have support groups. Groups are usually free.

## Therapy notes and court
In some family or criminal cases, the other side can ask for therapy records. It doesn't happen often, and courts usually protect them, but it's worth knowing.

Ask your therapist at the start: "Who can see my notes, and what would happen if a court asked for them?" Tell your lawyer you're in therapy. Don't let this stop you getting help.

If your ex ever tries to paint therapy as a sign something's wrong with you, your lawyer can point out that getting support is a responsible step.

## The first sessions
Early sessions are often about safety, getting to know each other and planning. Going straight into the worst memories isn't usually where it starts. You may feel worse for a little while before you feel better. That's common. If you feel unsafe, unheard or judged, say so, or try someone else.

## Therapy for children
Children may need their own support. Look for a therapist trained in working with children after domestic abuse or trauma. Their doctor or school can refer them. See [How children react](ch:how-children-react).

## What if I need help now?
If you're in crisis, don't wait for the right therapist. Any doctor, crisis line or mental health service is the right first step. See [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

Her second asked, in the first session, what she needed to feel safe, and explained how she worked with people after coercive control. After a few weeks of building trust, they started trauma-focused work.

=== when-its-more-than-aftermath
title: When it's more than aftermath
question: I'm not okay. I've had thoughts of ending my life. What do I do?
seo: Suicidal After Leaving an Abusive Relationship? Help Now
short: If you're suicidal after leaving an abusive relationship, please reach out now: call your local crisis line or emergency number. You don't need to be sure it's serious enough. Feeling this way is a sign of how much you've been carrying, not a sign you've failed, and help works.
---
**If you're thinking about ending your life, please call for help now.**

- **United States and Canada:** call or text 988.
- **United Kingdom and Ireland:** Samaritans, 116 123.
- **Australia:** Lifeline, 13 11 14.
- **Anywhere:** your emergency number, or search "[your country] crisis line."

You don't need to be at a certain point to call. The people on these lines have heard it before. They're there for exactly this call.

> Read this chapter whenever you need it, in any order. You don't have to read anything else first.

## Is it normal to feel this way after leaving?
Feeling suicidal after leaving an abusive relationship is more common than people say. Many people go through very dark times after leaving abuse.

Exhaustion, grief, fear, isolation and the weight of everything that happened can pile up until it feels like too much. Research finds that people who've experienced partner abuse are at higher risk of thoughts of suicide.

Feeling this way says nothing bad about you. It shows you're carrying more than one person should carry alone. It's treatable, and people do come through it.

## What's ordinary aftermath, and what's more?
Much of what this book describes is hard but expected: hypervigilance, poor sleep, late anger, patchy memory, hard anniversaries. That's the range this book calls ordinary aftermath.

These signs suggest you need more support than a book, friends or family can give:

- hopelessness that doesn't lift, day after day
- not being able to manage daily life (work, eating, looking after yourself or your children) for weeks
- thoughts of harming yourself, in any form
- drinking or using drugs much more than before to cope. See [Drinking, drugs and other ways of getting through](ch:drinking-drugs-and-getting-through).
- feeling unable to cope in a way that feels different from the hard days you've had before

None of these means you've failed at recovering. They mean you need, and deserve, more help.

## What do I do right now?
1. **Call a crisis line or your emergency number** if you might act on thoughts of harming yourself.
2. **Make your surroundings safer** for the next few hours: be with someone, go somewhere public, or ask someone to come to you.
3. **Tell one person.** A friend, a family member, a doctor. Say it plainly: "I'm having thoughts of ending my life and I need help."
4. **Contact a doctor today,** or a mental health service. You don't need to wait for the right therapist. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this) for later.

## What if the person I left is part of why I feel this way?
They may be. Ongoing abuse after separation, threats, court cases and being disbelieved can all wear you down.

Getting safer from them is part of getting well. A domestic abuse service can help with both. See [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) and [Find help](ch:find-help).

## What if I'm worried about someone else?
Ask them directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Asking doesn't put the idea in their head. It often brings relief. Listen, stay with them if you can, and help them contact a crisis line or emergency services.

## Signs it's more than aftermath
Talk to a doctor soon if, for more than two weeks, you notice:

- feeling low, empty or hopeless most of the day
- losing interest in everything, even things you used to love
- not being able to sleep, or sleeping most of the day
- not being able to eat, or eating far more than usual
- panic attacks that keep coming
- flashbacks or nightmares that stop you functioning
- thoughts that you'd be better off dead, or of harming yourself

If you're having thoughts of ending your life, don't wait two weeks. Call a crisis line or your emergency number now.

## What happens when you tell a doctor
A doctor will ask about your mood, sleep, appetite and safety. They may ask directly about suicidal thoughts. That's normal, and answering honestly helps them help you.

They might suggest talking therapy, medicine, more support, or a referral to a mental health team. Telling a doctor you're struggling won't mean your children are taken away. It shows you're looking after yourself and them.

## A crisis plan
Make a plan while you're feeling steadier, and keep it on paper and in your phone:

1. **My warning signs:** the thoughts, feelings or situations that mean a crisis may be coming.
2. **Things I can do alone to get through:** a walk, music, a shower, a grounding exercise.
3. **People and places that help:** friends, family, a café, a place of worship.
4. **People I can ask for help:** names and numbers.
5. **Professionals and crisis lines:** your doctor, a mental health team, crisis numbers.
6. **Making my space safer:** asking someone to help put distance between me and anything I might use to hurt myself.
7. **My reasons for living:** people, pets, plans, anything.

This kind of plan is widely used by mental health services, and it can help on the worst nights.

## If you've hurt yourself
If you've harmed yourself, get medical help straight away: call your emergency number or go to an emergency department. You won't be judged. After you're safe, tell your doctor or a crisis service, so you get support with what led up to it.

## After a crisis
After a crisis, people often feel exhausted, embarrassed or flat. Be gentle with yourself. Tell one person what happened. Go over your crisis plan and add what helped. Book a follow-up with your doctor. Getting through a crisis took strength.

## What about medication?
Medication can help some people, especially with depression, anxiety or severe sleep problems. It isn't a sign of weakness, and it doesn't replace other help.

- Talk to a doctor about the options, how long they take to work and possible side effects.
- For PTSD, guidelines usually recommend trauma-focused talking therapies first, with medication as an option for some people.
- Don't stop a medicine suddenly without advice; some need to be reduced slowly.
- Tell the doctor if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, or drinking more than usual.
- If you're worried the person you left could use your medication against you in court, tell your lawyer. Getting treatment is usually seen as a responsible step.

## You are not a burden
That voice telling you everyone would be better off without you is lying. It's one of the things deep pain says. You've already done one of the hardest things a person can do: you left. Getting through this is possible, and you don't have to do it alone.

## A card to keep
Write these on a card and keep it in your wallet or phone case:

- a crisis line number for your country
- the name and number of one person you can call at any hour
- three things that help you get through a bad hour
- one reason to get to tomorrow

On the worst nights, you won't have to remember. You'll only have to read.
