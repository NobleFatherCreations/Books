=== co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting
title: Co-parenting or parallel parenting?
question: Can you co-parent with a narcissist, or is parallel parenting better?
seo: Co-Parenting With a Narcissist? Try Parallel Parenting
short: Co-parenting with a narcissist or any controlling ex often fails, because co-parenting needs trust and good-faith talk. Parallel parenting is the alternative: each parent runs their own home, contact between you is minimal and written, and the plan spells out the details so there's little to argue about.
---
Every parenting guide says the same thing: work together for the children. That's good advice when both parents act in good faith. When one parent uses every conversation to control or punish, "working together" can become another channel for abuse.

## Can you co-parent with a narcissist?
Usually not in the way the guides mean. Co-parenting assumes you can talk, compromise, swap weekends and make decisions together. With a controlling ex, those conversations become openings: for criticism, for pressure, for using the children. The Decoder calls this [children used as leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage).

That doesn't mean your children lose. It means you need a different model.

## What is parallel parenting?
Parallel parenting is a way of raising children in two homes with as little contact between the parents as possible. Psychologists who work with high-conflict families developed it. In practice:

- **Each home has its own rules.** You don't try to agree on bedtimes, screens or food. You run your home; they run theirs.
- **Contact is minimal and written.** One channel, usually a co-parenting app or email, used for logistics only.
- **The plan is detailed.** Exchange times and places, holidays, who decides what, how to handle illness and school events. The more the plan settles in advance, the less there is to fight about.
- **Decisions are split.** One parent may handle schooling and the other medical appointments, or decisions follow a set process.
- **Handovers are neutral.** At school, in a public place, or through a third person. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).

> Parallel parenting takes the fight out of your children's lives.

## Co-parenting vs parallel parenting

| | Co-parenting | Parallel parenting |
|---|---|---|
| Contact between parents | regular, flexible | minimal, written only |
| Decisions | made together | split, or set out in the plan |
| House rules | agreed and shared | each home decides |
| Handovers | face to face, often at home | neutral place or third person |
| Works best when | both parents cooperate | one or both use conflict |

## Is parallel parenting bad for kids?
Research on children and divorce consistently finds that ongoing conflict between parents harms children. Parallel parenting reduces that conflict. Children adapt well to two homes with different rules, as long as each home is warm and predictable. What hurts them is being in the middle.

## Can parallel parenting be court ordered?
In many places courts can order arrangements that work like parallel parenting: communication only through an app, exchanges at school or a contact centre, and decision-making split between parents. Some US states use the term in their parenting plans. Ask your lawyer how to request it, and explain why with your record. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## How do I start parallel parenting?
1. **Move all contact to one written channel.** See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).
2. **Write down the current arrangements in detail,** even if they're informal.
3. **Stop discussing anything that isn't logistics.** The Decoder's page on [controlled communication](/playbook/controlled-communication) shows how talk gets used; parallel parenting removes the talk.
4. **Let go of their home.** You can't control what happens there, unless it's unsafe. Focus on making yours steady.
5. **Ask a court to formalise it** if they won't stick to it.

## What do I say to my children?
Keep it simple: "In Dad's house there are Dad's rules, and in my house there are my rules. Both houses love you." See [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* looks at the pressure parenting puts on mothers in [The Motherhood Matrix](/feminine/the-motherhood-matrix#core-concept).

## Sources
- Stahl, P. M. (2007). *Parenting After Divorce: Resolving Conflicts and Meeting Your Children's Needs* (2nd ed.). Impact Publishers.
- Kelly, J. B. (2007). Children's living arrangements following separation and divorce: Insights from empirical and clinical research. *Family Process*, 46(1), 35–52.
- Amato, P. R. (2001). Children of divorce in the 1990s: An update of the Amato and Keith (1991) meta-analysis. *Journal of Family Psychology*, 15(3), 355–370.

=== handovers
title: Handovers
question: How do I make custody exchanges safe with an abusive ex?
seo: Safe Custody Exchange With an Abusive Ex: Options That Work
short: A safe custody exchange keeps contact between you and your ex to a minimum. Options include handovers at school, in a busy public place with cameras, through a trusted third person, or at a supervised contact centre. Keep exchanges short, and write down anything that goes wrong.
---
Handovers are where many co-parents meet. They're also where much post-separation abuse happens: the long conversation at the door, the comments in front of the children, the "accidental" lateness.

## How do I make custody exchanges safe?
Choose the option that gives the least contact you can arrange.

**School or nursery handovers.** One parent drops off in the morning; the other collects in the afternoon. You never meet. This is often the simplest option for school-age children.

**A busy public place.** A supermarket car park, a café, a library. Choose somewhere with cameras and other people. Some police stations have designated exchange areas or cameras in their car parks.

**A trusted third person.** A grandparent, friend or relative collects and returns the children. Choose someone calm, who won't carry messages.

**A supervised exchange or contact centre.** Staff manage the handover so parents arrive and leave at different times and never meet. In the UK, search the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search "supervised exchange" plus your area, or the Supervised Visitation Network. Elsewhere, ask your court or a domestic abuse service.

Avoid handovers at your home if you can. If you must, hand over at the door, not inside.

> A handover should take a minute. Hello, the bag, the medicine, goodbye.

## What should the handover look like?
- Arrive on time, and leave promptly.
- Keep the children's bags packed and ready.
- Pass on only what's needed: medicine, a school letter.
- Don't discuss anything else. If they start, say "I'll reply in writing" and leave.
- Keep your voice calm, for the children's sake.

The Decoder describes the looks, comments and shows of force that often happen at handovers in [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation), and the use of children as messengers in [children used as leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage).

## What do I say when they start something at the door?
> **Say:** "I'm not discussing this now. Message me on the app."

Then turn to the children: "Have a lovely time. See you Sunday." And go.

## What should I write down?
- the agreed time and the time they arrived
- anything said in front of the children
- the children's state when they returned
- any change to arrangements made at the door

See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if my child doesn't want to go?
Children resist handovers for many reasons: tiredness, missing you, not wanting to stop playing, or something more serious. Stay calm and neutral. Don't promise they can stay. Listen afterwards, without leading questions. If the resistance is strong or ongoing, read [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) and get advice before refusing contact, because refusing without a court order can count against you in some places.

## What if handovers become dangerous?
Change the arrangement now: use a contact centre, involve the police, or ask the court to vary the order. Update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left). Handovers and court dates are among the times when risk can rise.

## Sources
- National Association of Child Contact Centres (UK).
- Supervised Visitation Network (US): standards for supervised exchange.
- Office on Violence Against Women (US): Safe Havens supervised visitation and safe exchange guiding principles.

=== your-children-and-what-to-say
title: Your children, and what to say
question: How do I explain the divorce, or the abuse, to my child?
seo: How to Explain Divorce to a Child After Abuse
short: How to explain divorce to a child after abuse: give simple, true facts that fit their age, tell them it isn't their fault and that they're loved, and keep adult details out. Don't use them as messengers, spies or confidants. Answer the questions they ask, not the ones you're afraid of.
---
Children notice everything. Saying nothing leaves them to fill the gaps, often with the idea that it's their fault. Saying too much burdens them with adult problems. The goal is the honest middle.

## How do I explain divorce to a child?
Three messages matter at every age:

1. **This is a grown-up decision.** "Mum and Dad decided to live in different houses."
2. **It isn't your fault.** Say it plainly, and say it more than once.
3. **You are loved, and you'll be looked after.** Tell them what will stay the same: their school, their friends, their pets, their bedtime story.

Then fit the rest to their age.

## What do I say to young children (under 5)?
Short, concrete, repeated. Young children need to know what happens to them, not why.

> **Say:** "Daddy is going to live in a different house. You'll sleep at my house on school nights and see Daddy at the weekend. We both love you. It's not because of anything you did."

Keep routines steady. Expect clinginess, tantrums or sleep changes. See [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react).

## What do I say to children aged 6 to 11?
They'll ask why. Give a true answer without blaming:

> **Say:** "Mum and I weren't able to keep our home calm and safe. Grown-ups sometimes can't fix that together, so we decided to live apart. That's our job to sort out, not yours."

If they saw or heard abuse, you can name it without detail:

> **Say:** "You saw some scary things happen. That wasn't okay, and it wasn't your fault. I'm keeping us safe now."

## What do I say to teenagers?
Teenagers often know more than you think. Be honest, answer their questions, and respect that they may have their own view of the other parent:

> **Say:** "You've probably noticed things weren't okay. I'm not going to ask you to take sides, and I won't badmouth your dad. If you want to talk about anything you saw or felt, I'm here."

Don't make them your confidant. Their job is to be a teenager, not your support.

## What should I never do?
- **Don't use them as messengers.** "Tell your mother she's late with the money."
- **Don't ask them to report back.** "Who was at Dad's house?"
- **Don't share adult details:** court, money, what the other parent did to you.
- **Don't run down the other parent,** even when they run you down. It forces children to defend half of themselves.
- **Don't make them choose.**

The Decoder's page on [parentification](/playbook/parentification) describes what happens when children are made to carry adult worries. This applies whatever the other parent is doing. Matching a harmful tactic costs the children, not the ex. See [Children as the channel](ch:children-as-the-channel).

## What if they ask questions I can't answer?
> **Say:** "That's a good question. I don't know yet, but I'll tell you when I do."

Or: "That's a grown-up thing I'm sorting out. You don't need to worry about it."

## What if they want to talk about the other parent?
Let them. Listen without reacting. If they love the other parent, let them. That isn't disloyalty to you, and it isn't a sign they didn't see what happened. [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) covers what to do when what they say worries you.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* looks at motherhood under pressure in [The Motherhood Matrix](/feminine/the-motherhood-matrix#core-concept). *Playground Protectors* is written for children themselves, and can help you start conversations about safety.

## Sources
- American Academy of Pediatrics: talking with children about divorce.
- Kelly, J. B., and Emery, R. E. (2003). Children's adjustment following divorce: Risk and resilience perspectives. *Family Relations*, 52(4), 352–362.
- Katz, E. (2022). *Coercive Control in Children's and Mothers' Lives.* Oxford University Press.

=== how-children-react
title: How children react, and what helps them
question: What are the effects of domestic abuse on children, and how do I help?
seo: Effects of Domestic Abuse on Children: Signs and Help
short: The effects of domestic abuse on children can include anxiety, sleep problems, anger, regression, trouble at school, and taking care of a parent. Many children recover well with a safe, steady home, a parent who listens, and help when they need it. Children who still love the other parent aren't confused; they're children.
---
You left partly for them. Now you're watching them, worried about every tantrum and every quiet day. This chapter helps you know what's common, what's worth more attention, and what helps.

If a child is in danger, or talks about wanting to hurt themselves, contact emergency services or a crisis line now. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What are the effects of domestic abuse on children?
Children don't have to see violence to be affected by it. Research on coercive control finds children are harmed by living inside it: the fear and the tension count as much as anything they witness. In England and Wales, the law now recognises children as victims of domestic abuse in their own right.

Common reactions by age:

**Babies and toddlers:** sleep and feeding problems, clinginess, distress when separated, startling easily.

**Young children (3 to 6):** going back to earlier behaviour (bedwetting, baby talk), tantrums, fears, worry about you, playing out what they saw.

**School-age children (6 to 11):** stomach aches and headaches, trouble concentrating, anger or sadness, blaming themselves, trying to be "perfect," worrying about the parent who isn't with them.

**Teenagers:** withdrawal, anger, risk-taking, trouble at school, protecting a parent, taking on adult roles, mistrust in their own relationships.

The Decoder describes two patterns that can carry on after separation: [parentification](/playbook/parentification), when a child becomes a parent's carer or confidant, and [golden child elevation](/playbook/golden-child-elevation), when one child is favoured and another blamed.

> Many children recover well. What helps most is a safe, steady home and at least one adult who listens.

## What helps children recover?
- **Safety and routine.** Regular meals, bedtimes and school. Predictability tells a child's body the danger has passed.
- **Your calm, as much as you can manage.** Children read your mood. You don't have to be perfect; you can say "I'm sad today, but I'm okay, and it's not because of you."
- **Permission to feel.** Let them be angry, sad, or happy to see the other parent.
- **Listening without leading.** Ask open questions: "How was the weekend?" Not "Did Dad shout?"
- **Telling school.** A teacher who knows what's happening can support them and notice changes.
- **Fun.** Ordinary play and laughter are part of healing, not a distraction from it.

## When should I get professional help?
Talk to your child's doctor or school counsellor if:

- reactions are getting worse, not easing, after a few months
- they talk about hurting themselves, or about not wanting to be alive
- they can't go to school, or can't sleep, for long stretches
- they're hurting others or animals
- they tell you about being hurt, threatened or asked to keep secrets

Therapies with good evidence for children after abuse include trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, and child-parent psychotherapy for young children. Ask for a therapist with experience of domestic abuse. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What if my child still loves the other parent?
Most do. That isn't a sign they didn't notice, or that you were wrong to leave. Children can love a parent and be hurt by them at the same time. Let them love both. Don't ask them to see the other parent as you do.

## What if my child is angry with me?
Children often direct anger at the safe parent, because it's safe to. It can feel deeply unfair. Hold steady, name the feeling ("You're so angry that we moved"), and keep the rules. It usually eases as they feel more secure.

## What about me?
Your recovery helps theirs. Looking after yourself isn't selfish. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing), and [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) for problems that come from the other home.

- Callaghan, J. E. M., et al. (2018). Beyond "witnessing": Children's experiences of coercive control in domestic violence and abuse. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*, 33(10), 1551–1581.
- Domestic Abuse Act 2021, section 3 (children as victims) (England and Wales).

=== when-your-child-is-pulled-in
title: When your child is pulled into it
question: What do I do when my child repeats what the other parent says about me?
seo: Child Repeating What the Other Parent Says? What to Do
short: When a child is repeating what the other parent says about you, stay calm, correct the fact gently, and tell them it's not their job to sort out grown-up problems. Don't question them about the other home. If they're asked to spy, keep secrets or refuse visits, keep a record and get advice.
---
Your seven-year-old says, "Daddy says you broke up our family because you're selfish." Your teenager won't come back after a weekend away. Your child asks why you "don't let" them see their other parent more.

A child repeating what the other parent says about you is one of the most common ways children get pulled into the conflict. This chapter helps you respond without making it worse for them.

If your child talks about being hurt or threatened, or about harming themselves, contact the police, child protection services or a crisis line now. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What do I say when my child repeats something cruel about me?
Take a breath first. Your reaction teaches them whether it's safe to tell you things.

> **Say:** "I can see that's confusing. That isn't true, and you don't need to worry about it. Grown-ups sort out grown-up things. I love you, and that won't change."

Then move on. Don't ask what else was said. Don't attack the other parent, even though you may want to. Write it down later, in your child's words, with the date. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if my child is being asked to spy or keep secrets?
Children are often asked "Who's at Mum's house?" or "What did she buy?" Tell your child, without blaming:

> **Say:** "You don't have to answer questions about my house. You can just say 'I don't know' or 'ask Mum.' And you never have to keep secrets from me that make you feel worried."

The Decoder describes children put in the middle in [triangulation](/playbook/triangulation) and [children used as leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage).

## What about gifts and promises?
Big gifts, special treats, or promises like "you can live with me full time" are common, especially around court dates. Don't compete. Don't criticise the gift. Keep your home steady and your promises small and kept.

## What if my child refuses to see the other parent?
Children refuse visits for many reasons: fear, something that happened, loyalty pressure, wanting to stay with friends, or not wanting to leave you alone. Find out gently, without leading questions.

- If you believe the child is unsafe there, get legal advice urgently, and contact child protection services if there's immediate risk.
- If there's a court order, don't stop contact on your own without advice. In many places that can count against you. Ask your lawyer about asking the court to review it.
- Keep a record of what the child says and does around visits.

## What if my child refuses to see me?
This is one of the most painful things a parent can go through. Keep the door open: send short, warm messages, turn up when you're supposed to, don't blame the child. Get legal advice, and keep a record. If you're accused of being the cause, see [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser) and the parental alienation section of [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## When should I get help?
If your child seems frightened of the other parent, talks about being hurt, shows sudden changes in behaviour, or is struggling for weeks, talk to their doctor, school or a child therapist. See [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react).

=== apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan
title: Apps, calendars and the parenting plan
question: Which co-parenting app is court approved, and what goes in a parenting plan?
seo: Court-Approved Co-Parenting Apps and Parenting Plans
short: A court-approved co-parenting app keeps every message timestamped and undeletable, which protects you and calms things down. Courts often order them in high-conflict cases. Pair the app with a detailed parenting plan that settles schedules, holidays and decisions in advance, so there's less to argue about.
---
The less you have to negotiate, the less there is to fight about. Two tools do most of that work: a co-parenting app and a detailed parenting plan.

## Which co-parenting app is court approved?
Courts don't usually "approve" a single brand, but many order parents to use a dedicated co-parenting app in high-conflict cases. Well-known options include OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents and AppClose. Look for:

- **messages that can't be edited or deleted,** with timestamps
- **a record you can export** for your lawyer or the court
- **a shared calendar** for the schedule
- **an expense tracker** for shared costs
- **tone-checking features,** in some apps, that flag hostile wording before it's sent

Some apps charge a fee; some courts can waive it. Ask your lawyer which apps your local court is used to.

The Decoder's page on [controlled communication](/playbook/controlled-communication) shows how the other side can use contact to control you. A dedicated app puts the rules in writing for both of you.

## How do I use the app well?
- Use it for everything. Block other channels if you can.
- Keep messages short and factual. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).
- Check it at set times, not all day.
- Don't respond to anything that isn't about the children. See [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).

## What goes in a parenting plan for a high-conflict ex?
The more detailed, the better. Vague plans ("reasonable contact," "to be agreed") give a controlling parent room to argue. Settle:

- **The regular schedule:** days, times, who collects and drops off, and where.
- **Handovers:** place and method. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).
- **Holidays, birthdays and special days,** year by year, alternating if needed.
- **Lateness:** how long to wait before the visit is treated as missed.
- **Communication:** the app only, and response times.
- **Decisions:** who decides about school, health and activities, or how disagreements are settled.
- **Travel:** notice needed for trips, and passport arrangements. See [Moving, passports and travel with children](ch:moving-passports-and-travel-with-children).
- **Phone or video calls** with the children while they're at the other home: when and how long.
- **Changes:** how to request them, with how much notice.

## Should the plan be a court order?
If your ex won't stick to an informal plan, ask the court to make it an order. An order can be enforced; an informal agreement usually can't. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## What about a shared calendar?
Use the app's calendar, or one shared calendar for the children only. Don't share your personal calendar. It shows your routine.

=== moving-passports-and-travel-with-children
title: Moving, passports and travel with children
question: Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child, and how do I stop them taking the children abroad?
seo: Moving Away From an Abusive Ex With Your Child: The Rules
short: Moving away from an abusive ex with your child usually needs the other parent's agreement or a court's permission, especially across borders, even when you're the one escaping abuse. If you fear your ex will take the children abroad, passport alerts and court orders can help. Get legal advice before you move.
---
You want distance. A fresh start near family, or simply further away. But moving with children after separation has legal rules, and getting them wrong can hurt your case.

If you or your children are in immediate danger, your safety comes first. Go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now), then get legal advice as soon as you can.

## Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child?
Often yes, but usually not on your own say-so once the other parent has rights:

- **Within the same country:** many places need the other parent's agreement, or a court order, for a move that affects contact. Courts look at the reasons for the move, the effect on the child, and how contact would work.
- **To another country:** almost always needs the other parent's written consent or a court order. Taking a child abroad without it can be child abduction, even for a parent fleeing abuse.

Courts can and do allow moves where abuse is part of the reason. Explain the abuse, the reasons for the move, and how contact with the other parent will work, if it's safe. Your record helps. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

The Decoder's page on [mobility control](/playbook/mobility-control) describes how controlling people restrict where you can go. Courts can weigh that too.

## What if I've already moved to escape?
If you moved to a refuge or another area for safety, tell your lawyer straight away. Leaving for safety is very different from moving to cut the other parent out, and courts generally understand that, especially with evidence.

## How do I stop my ex taking the children abroad?
If you fear abduction:

- **United States:** enrol your children in the State Department's Children's Passport Issuance Alert Program, so you're told if anyone applies for a US passport for them. Both parents usually have to consent to a passport for a child under 16.
- **United Kingdom:** apply for a prohibited steps order to stop a child being removed from the country. If removal is imminent, contact the police, who can issue an all-ports alert. You can also ask HM Passport Office not to issue a passport.
- **Elsewhere:** ask a family lawyer about court orders that stop travel, and whether passports can be held by the court.

Keep copies of the children's passports and recent photos somewhere safe.

## What if the children are taken abroad?
Act fast. Contact the police and a family lawyer. If the country is part of the 1980 Hague Convention on child abduction, there's a process to ask for the children's return. In the US, contact the State Department's Office of Children's Issues; in the UK, the International Child Abduction and Contact Unit; in other countries, your government's central authority.

## What about holidays abroad?
Your parenting plan should say how much notice is needed for a trip, and how passports are handled. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan). Get written consent for any trip abroad and carry it with you.

- Children Act 1989, section 8 (prohibited steps orders) and section 13 (removal from the UK) (England and Wales).
- Child Abduction Act 1984 (UK).
- Reunite International (UK): parental child abduction guidance.

=== pregnant-or-with-a-new-baby
title: Pregnant, or with a new baby
question: What should I know about leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant or with a newborn?
seo: Leaving an Abusive Relationship While Pregnant or With a Baby
short: Leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant or with a newborn is common, and abuse often starts or gets worse in pregnancy. Tell your midwife or doctor, plan the birth and the first weeks for safety, and get advice about the baby's legal arrangements. You can protect yourself and your baby.
---
You're pregnant, or you've just had a baby, and you've left or are about to. Leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant is more common than people think. You may be exhausted, frightened, and told by everyone that a baby needs both parents.

Your safety and your baby's come first. If you're in danger now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).

## Why does abuse often get worse in pregnancy?
Pregnancy can be a time when abuse starts or escalates. Researchers think the reasons include a partner's jealousy, loss of control and attention, and the extra vulnerability pregnancy brings. Violence during pregnancy is one of the warning signs services take most seriously. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

The Decoder's page on [vulnerability weaponization](/playbook/vulnerability-weaponization) describes how controlling people use moments of weakness. Pregnancy and a newborn are exactly those moments.

## Who should I tell?
**Your midwife, obstetrician or doctor.** In many countries midwives ask about domestic abuse routinely, often when you're on your own. They can flag your notes, refer you to specialist support, and help you plan. You can ask for appointments without your ex present.

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I'm worried about my safety and the baby's. Can we plan for that?"

## How do I plan the birth safely?
- Ask the hospital to keep your details private and to restrict who can visit or get information about you.
- Choose your birth partner carefully. You don't have to have the other parent there.
- Plan how you'll get home and who will be with you in the first weeks.
- Make sure your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) covers the birth and the newborn weeks.

## What about the baby's legal arrangements?
Rules differ a lot. In some places an unmarried father gets legal rights by being named on the birth certificate; in others he has to apply. A court may need to decide on contact with a newborn, often with short, frequent visits at first.

Get legal advice before registering the birth if you're worried. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money) and [Co-parenting or parallel parenting?](ch:co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting).

## How do I look after myself with a newborn?
- Accept practical help: meals, laundry, someone holding the baby while you sleep.
- Watch your mood. Postnatal depression and anxiety are common, and more common after abuse. Tell your midwife, health visitor or doctor if you feel low, hopeless or frightened most days. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, get help right away: see [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).
- Keep a record of anything the other parent does that worries you. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
