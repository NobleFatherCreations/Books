=== co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting
title: Co-parenting or parallel parenting?
question: Can you co-parent with a narcissist, or is parallel parenting better?
seo: Co-Parenting With a Narcissist? Try Parallel Parenting
short: Co-parenting with a narcissist or any controlling ex often fails, because co-parenting needs trust and good-faith talk. Parallel parenting is the alternative: each parent runs their own home, contact between you is minimal and written, and the plan spells out the details so there's little to argue about.
---
Every parenting guide says the same thing: work together for the children. Lovely advice, when both parents act in good faith. When one parent uses every conversation to control or punish, "working together" turns into one more channel for abuse. And somehow you're the one being told to be more cooperative.

## Can you co-parent with a narcissist?
Usually not in the way the guides mean. Co-parenting assumes you can talk, compromise, swap weekends and make decisions together. With a controlling ex, each of those conversations is an opening: for criticism, for pressure, for using the children. The Decoder calls this [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage) (using the kids to get to you).

That doesn't mean your children lose out. It means you need a different model.

::: decoder
**They say:** "If you cared about the kids, you'd pick up the phone instead of hiding behind an app."
**What it's doing:** Making your boundary sound like the thing that hurts the children, so you'll reopen the channel they use for pressure. See [Controlled Communication](/playbook/controlled-communication).
**A reply you could send:** "I want what's best for the children too. I'll keep using the app for everything about them, so we both have a clear record. I'll answer about Saturday there by tomorrow."
:::

## What if they're a covert narcissist?
"Covert narcissist" is a popular term for someone who comes across as shy, sensitive or hard done by, but still needs control and admiration. Researchers call this "vulnerable" narcissism, as opposed to the loud, grandiose kind.

In co-parenting, it can look like this:

- playing the victim to the school, the court or your family
- agreeing to everything in person, then not doing it
- small, deniable digs that sound like nothing when you repeat them
- sulking or going silent when you hold a boundary

You don't need to prove a diagnosis. Courts and schools respond to behaviour, written down. Parallel parenting works on the quiet version too, because it cuts the number of moments where those tactics can land. [Is my ex a narcissist?](ch:is-my-ex-a-narcissist) covers the label and its limits.

## What is parallel parenting?
Parallel parenting is a way of raising children across two homes when the parents can't work together safely. Each parent runs their own home their own way. Contact between you is kept to a minimum, in writing and only about the children, and a detailed plan settles the rest in advance, so there's little to argue about.

Psychologists who work with high-conflict families developed it. In practice:

- **Each home has its own rules.** You stop trying to agree on bedtimes, screens or whether cereal counts as dinner. You run your home; they run theirs.
- **Contact is minimal and written.** One channel, usually a co-parenting app or email, used for logistics only.
- **The plan is detailed.** Exchange times and places, holidays, who decides what, what happens when someone's ill, school events.
- **Decisions are split.** One parent may handle schooling and the other medical appointments, or decisions follow a set process.
- **Handovers are neutral.** At school, in a public place, or through a third person. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).

> Parallel parenting leaves far fewer places in your children's week where a fight can start.

## Co-parenting vs parallel parenting

| | Co-parenting | Parallel parenting |
|---|---|---|
| Contact between parents | regular, flexible | minimal, written only |
| Decisions | made together | split, or set out in the plan |
| House rules | agreed and shared | each home decides |
| Handovers | face to face, often at home | neutral place or third person |
| Works best when | both parents cooperate | one or both use conflict |

## Is parallel parenting bad for kids?
Research on children and divorce consistently finds that ongoing conflict between parents harms children. Parallel parenting cuts that conflict down. Children adapt well to two homes with different rules, as long as each home is warm and predictable. Being stuck in the middle is what hurts them.

If they ever call this refusing to co-parent, let the record answer. You follow the plan, reply to every message about the children, and support their time with the other parent.

## Can parallel parenting be court ordered?
In many places courts can order arrangements that work like parallel parenting: communication only through an app, exchanges at school or a contact centre, and decision-making split between parents. Some US states use the term in their parenting plans. Ask your lawyer how to request it, and back up the request with your record. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## Do I have to take a co-parenting class?
Sometimes. Many courts send separating parents on a parenting course, and some require one before a final order.

- **United States:** many states or counties require a parenting class for divorcing parents with children. They're often online. Search "[your state] court approved parenting class."
- **England and Wales:** the court may refer you to an online course called Planning Together for Children. Cafcass is meant to consider domestic abuse before a referral.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] parenting after separation course court."

If there has been abuse, say so to the court or the officer who makes the referral. Ask to attend separately or online, and never in a joint session with your ex.

A course aimed at "reducing conflict" can assume the two of you share the blame. You can take the class and still keep your record of what happened. Nodding through the slide about communication styles doesn't cancel a single dated entry. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## How do I start parallel parenting?
1. **Move all contact to one written channel.** See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).
2. **Write down the current arrangements in detail,** even if they're informal.
3. **Stop discussing anything that isn't logistics.** The Decoder's page on [Controlled Communication](/playbook/controlled-communication) shows how talk gets used; parallel parenting takes the talk away.
4. **Let go of their home.** You can't control what happens there, unless it's unsafe. Put your energy into making your home steady.
5. **Ask a court to formalise it** if they won't stick to it.

## What does a parallel parenting plan contain?
A good parallel parenting plan is detailed, so there's less to negotiate. It usually covers:

- **The regular schedule:** which days and nights the children spend where, with exact times.
- **Handovers:** where, when and how, ideally through school or a neutral place.
- **Holidays and special days:** birthdays, religious holidays, school breaks, Mother's and Father's Day, alternating years.
- **Communication:** one method, usually an app or email, and what it's for.
- **Decisions:** who makes which decisions, and how disagreements are settled.
- **Travel:** notice periods and consent for trips away.
- **Changes:** how to request a swap, and how far in advance.

The more the plan answers in advance, the fewer chances there are for a fight.

## Communication rules
Write them into the plan:

- Messages only through the agreed app or email.
- Only about the children: schedule, health, school, activities.
- Replies within an agreed time, like 48 hours, except emergencies.
- Emergencies by phone call, followed by a written note.
- No messages through the children.

## Who decides what?
Each parent usually decides day-to-day matters in their own home: bedtimes, meals, screen time, chores. Bigger decisions need a plan:

- **Education:** choice of school, special needs support.
- **Health:** non-urgent medical treatment, therapy, vaccinations.
- **Religion and major activities.**

Some plans give each parent one area. Others need both parents to agree, with a mediator or the court as a backstop. Where you have a court order, follow what it says.

## Medical and school information
In most places both parents can get information from schools and doctors directly, unless a court order says otherwise. So you don't have to pass on every report card and appointment. Ask the school to send letters to both homes.

If there's a reason to limit the other parent's access, you'll need a court order; tell the school and doctor if you have one.

## When the plan is broken
- Record each breach: the date, what was agreed, what happened.
- Don't argue about it at the handover.
- Raise it once, in writing: "The plan says pickup at 5. Today you arrived at 7.30. Please keep to the agreed times."
- If breaches keep happening, take your record to your lawyer, a mediator or the court.

## Moving towards cooperation later
Sometimes, after a long stretch of calm, parallel parenting can loosen into something more cooperative. Go slowly, and only if the other parent has been consistent for a long time. Loosen one thing at a time. If the old patterns come back, go back to the plan.

## What do I say to my children?
Keep it simple: "In Dad's house there are Dad's rules, and in my house there are my rules. Both houses love you." See [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say).

::: scene
## How parallel parenting calmed things
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

For a year, every handover between Amira and her ex turned into an argument, and every message became a fight about the past.

With a mediator, they agreed a detailed plan. Handovers moved to school, and all messages went through a co-parenting app. Holidays were written out for two years ahead, and each parent set the day-to-day rules in their own home.

The first months were bumpy. Then the messages shrank to times and dates. Her son stopped having stomach aches on Sundays. Her ex hadn't changed at all. The plan just gave him far fewer places to start a fight.
:::

## One clause at a time
Open your notes app and write one line: the clause that causes the most trouble right now (usually handovers or messages) and how you'd want it to read. That's the first thing to ask for in writing.

=== handovers
title: Handovers
question: How do I make custody exchanges safe with an abusive ex?
seo: Safe Custody Exchange With an Abusive Ex: Options That Work
short: A safe custody exchange keeps contact between you and your ex to a minimum. Options include handovers at school, in a busy public place with cameras, through a trusted third person, or at a supervised contact centre. Keep exchanges short, and write down anything that goes wrong.
---
Handovers are where a lot of separated parents still meet face to face. They're also where a lot of post-separation abuse happens: the long conversation at the door, the comments in front of the children, the lateness that's somehow always "accidental".

## How do I make custody exchanges safe?
Pick the option that gives you the least contact you can arrange.

**School or nursery handovers.** One parent drops off in the morning; the other collects in the afternoon. You never meet. For school-age children this is often the simplest option.

**A busy public place.** A supermarket car park, a café, a library. Choose somewhere with cameras and other people around. Some police stations have exchange areas or cameras in their car parks.

**A trusted third person.** A grandparent, friend or relative collects and returns the children. Choose someone calm, who won't carry messages.

**A supervised exchange or contact centre.** Staff manage the handover so parents arrive and leave at different times and never meet.

To find one in the UK, search the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search "supervised exchange" plus your area, or the Supervised Visitation Network. Elsewhere, ask your court or a domestic abuse service.

Avoid handovers at your home if you can. If you must, hand over at the door, not inside.

> A handover should take a minute. Hello, the bag, the medicine, goodbye.

## What should the handover look like?
- Arrive on time, and leave promptly.
- Have the bags packed and by the door, including the one trainer that always goes missing.
- Pass on only what's needed: medicine, a school letter.
- Don't discuss anything else. If they start, say "I'll reply in writing" and go.
- Keep your voice calm, for the children's sake.

The Decoder describes the looks, comments and shows of force that often turn up at handovers in [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation).

## What do I say when they start something at the door?
> **Say:** "I'm not discussing this now. Message me on the app and I'll reply there."

Then turn to the children: "Have a lovely time. See you Sunday." And go.

::: decoder
**They say:** "Come in for a coffee. It's better for the kids if they see us being friendly."
**What it's doing:** Using the children's feelings to stretch a one-minute handover back into contact. See [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage).
**A reply you could send:** "Thanks, but I'll keep handovers short and at the agreed place, as the plan says. I'm happy to sort anything else on the app."
:::

If they ever call you cold at handovers, your record shows you were on time, polite and brief.

## What should I record about handovers?
- the agreed time and the time they arrived
- anything said in front of the children
- the children's state when they came back
- any change to arrangements made at the door

See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if my child doesn't want to go?
Children resist handovers for all sorts of reasons: tiredness, missing you, not wanting to stop playing, or something more serious. Stay calm and neutral, and don't promise they can stay. Listen afterwards, without leading questions.

If the resistance is strong or keeps happening, read [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) and get advice before refusing contact. In some places, refusing without a court order can count against you.

## Handover options compared

| Option | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Through school or nursery | One parent drops off, the other picks up | Avoiding contact completely on school days |
| A public place | Meet at a busy, well-lit spot with cameras | Short, calm exchanges |
| A third person | A trusted friend or relative does the handover | When seeing the other parent is too difficult |
| A contact centre | Staff manage arrival and departure times | Higher risk, or when ordered by a court |
| Kerbside | Child walks from one car to the door | Older children, lower risk |

## Contact centres
Contact centres are run by charities or local services. Some offer supervised time, with staff present, as well as handovers. Ask your local family court, a domestic abuse service or the national association in your country.

## Handovers through school
If one parent drops the children at school in the morning and the other collects them in the afternoon, you never need to meet. Tell the school the schedule in writing, and give them a copy of any order. For weekends and holidays, pick another neutral option.

## Preparing children for handovers
- Tell them in advance when they're going, and when they'll be back.
- Pack their bag together, with something from home to take.
- Keep goodbyes short and warm: "Have a lovely time. See you on Sunday."
- Keep your worry off your face if you can. You can fall apart in the car afterwards.

## Late or no-shows
If the other parent is often late or doesn't turn up:

- Agree a waiting time in the plan, like 30 minutes, after which the visit is off for that day.
- Record each time.
- Don't let your child wait for hours. Have a backup plan for the day.
- Explain the no-show without criticising the other parent: "Dad couldn't make it today. Let's do something nice."

## What if handovers become dangerous?
Change the arrangement now: use a contact centre, involve the police, or ask the court to vary the order. Update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left). Handovers and court dates are among the times when risk can rise.

::: scene
## How the handovers moved to school
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Handovers at Tom's door had turned into shouting matches, with his daughter watching from the stairs. He asked the court to change the arrangement so his ex collected her from school on Fridays and dropped her back at school on Mondays. The school agreed to the schedule in writing.

After the change, Tom and his ex didn't see each other for months. His daughter said she liked that "nobody shouts on Fridays any more."
:::

::: action
## One small change
Look at your current handover and pick one change that would make it shorter or calmer. Write the request in one sentence and send it through your usual written channel now.
:::

Once handovers are calmer, [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say) helps with the questions that come out in the car on the way home.

=== your-children-and-what-to-say
title: Your children, and what to say
question: How do I explain the divorce, or the abuse, to my child?
seo: How to Explain Divorce to a Child After Abuse
short: How to explain divorce to a child after abuse: give simple, true facts that fit their age, tell them it isn't their fault and that they're loved, and keep adult details out. Don't use them as messengers, spies or confidants. Answer the questions they ask, not the ones you're afraid of.
---
Children notice everything, usually at the worst possible moment, from the back seat. Saying nothing leaves them to fill the gaps, often with the idea that it's their fault. Saying too much hands them adult problems. You're aiming for the honest middle.

## How do I explain divorce to a child?
Three messages matter at every age:

1. **This is a grown-up decision.** "Mum and Dad decided to live in different houses."
2. **It isn't your fault.** Say it plainly, and say it more than once.
3. **You are loved, and you'll be looked after.** Tell them what will stay the same: their school, their friends, their pets, their bedtime story.

Then fit the rest to their age.

A good test for every script here: could your child repeat it word for word at the other house, or to a court officer, without it causing trouble? They're written to pass that test.

## What do I say to young children (under 5)?
Short, concrete, repeated. Young children need to know what happens to them, not why.

> **Say:** "Daddy is going to live in a different house. You'll sleep at my house on school nights and see Daddy at the weekend. We both love you. It's not because of anything you did."

Keep routines steady. Expect clinginess, tantrums or sleep changes. See [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react).

## What do I say to children aged 6 to 11?
They'll ask why, probably more than once. Give a true answer without blaming:

> **Say:** "Mum and I weren't able to keep our home calm and safe. Grown-ups sometimes can't fix that together, so we decided to live apart. That's our job to sort out, not yours."

If they saw or heard abuse, you can name it without detail:

> **Say:** "You saw some scary things happen. That wasn't okay, and it wasn't your fault. I'm keeping us safe now."

## What do I say to teenagers?
Teenagers often know more than you think. Be honest, answer their questions, and respect that they may have their own view of the other parent:

> **Say:** "You've probably noticed things weren't okay. I'm not going to ask you to take sides, and I won't badmouth your dad. If you want to talk about anything you saw or felt, I'm here."

Don't make them your confidant. Their job is to be a teenager, eye-rolls and all, not your support.

## What should I never do?
- **Don't use them as messengers.** "Tell your mother she's late with the money."
- **Don't ask them to report back.** "Who was at Dad's house?"
- **Don't share adult details:** court, money, what the other parent did to you.
- **Don't run down the other parent,** even when they run you down. It forces children to defend half of themselves.
- **Don't make them choose.**

The Decoder's page on [Parentification](/playbook/parentification) describes what happens when children are made to carry adult worries. This holds whatever the other parent is doing. Matching a harmful tactic costs the children, not the ex. See [Children as the channel](ch:children-as-the-channel).

## What if they ask questions I can't answer?
> **Say:** "That's a good question. I don't know yet, but I'll tell you when I do."

Or: "That's a grown-up thing I'm sorting out. You don't need to worry about it."

## Explaining the abuse they saw or heard
Children who saw or heard abuse usually know more than adults think. Silence can leave them confused, or blaming themselves.

> **Say (to a young child):** "You heard shouting and saw some scary things. That wasn't okay. It wasn't your fault. My job is to keep us safe."

> **Say (to an older child):** "What you saw wasn't how people should treat each other. It's not your job to fix it or to choose. You can talk to me about it any time, and you can still love your dad."

Answer what they ask, honestly and simply, without frightening details.

## Explaining the police, a court or a protective order
> **Say:** "The court has made some rules to help everyone stay safe and calm. It means Mum won't come to our house for now. You haven't done anything wrong."

Keep it short and calm. Children take their cue from how you sound. Your child only needs the rules part. For the grown-up version, and how orders work where you live, see [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## Explaining a move or a new school
> **Say:** "We're moving to a new home so we can be safe and settled. It's okay to feel sad about leaving. We'll take your things, and you can help choose where they go."

If the address needs to stay secret, explain it in a way they can manage: "Our address is a special family secret for now, to help us stay safe. You can tell teachers, but not other people."

## Questions children ask, with answers
- **"Is it my fault?"** "No. Never. Grown-up problems are never a child's fault."
- **"Will you get back together?"** "No, we won't. But we will both always be your parents."
- **"Why can't I see Dad?"** "The court is deciding what's safest. When I know more, I'll tell you."
- **"Does Mum still love me?"** "What I know is that I love you, always."
- **"Are we going to be okay?"** "Yes. We're working it out, and you're safe with me."

## Talking about safety without scaring them
Make safety plans feel like ordinary family habits, like a fire drill. Practise calling for help, learn your phone number, agree a code word. Keep your tone everyday. Children feel safer when the adults sound calm and in charge.

## What if they want to talk about the other parent?
Let them. Listen without reacting, even when your face wants to. If they love the other parent, let them. That isn't disloyalty to you, and it isn't a sign they didn't see what happened. [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) covers what to do when what they say worries you.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* looks at motherhood under pressure in [The Motherhood Matrix](/feminine/the-motherhood-matrix#core-concept). *Playground Protectors* is written for children themselves, and can help you start conversations about safety.

::: honest
## Whose need is the conversation meeting?
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

Before a big conversation with your child, ask yourself:

- Am I telling them this because they need to know, or because I need them on my side?
- Is it something a child their age can carry?
- Would I be comfortable if they repeated it word for word to their other parent?

If the honest answer points to your own need, find an adult to tell instead. Your child needs the simple, true version.
:::

::: action
## Practise out loud
Pick one hard question your child might ask. Say your answer out loud three times, in the car or while you wash up, until it comes out calm. Children notice your tone more than your words.
:::

If their behaviour has changed since you left, [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react) covers what's common and when to get more help.

=== how-children-react
title: How children react, and what helps them
question: What are the effects of domestic abuse on children, and how do I help?
seo: Effects of Domestic Abuse on Children: Signs and Help
short: The effects of domestic abuse on children can include anxiety, sleep problems, anger, regression, trouble at school, and taking care of a parent. Many children recover well with a safe, steady home, a parent who listens, and help when they need it. Children who still love the other parent aren't confused; they're children.
---
You left partly for them. Now you're watching them closely, worried about every tantrum and every quiet day. Here's what's common, what needs more attention, and what helps.

If a child is in danger, or talks about wanting to hurt themselves, contact emergency services or a crisis line now. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What are the effects of domestic abuse on children?
Children don't have to see violence to be affected by it. Research on coercive control finds children are harmed by living inside it. The fear and the tension count as much as anything they witness. In England and Wales, the law now recognises children as victims of domestic abuse in their own right.

Common reactions by age:

**Babies and toddlers:** sleep and feeding problems, clinginess, distress when separated, startling easily.

**Young children (3 to 6):** going back to earlier behaviour (bedwetting, baby talk), tantrums, fears, worry about you, playing out what they saw.

**School-age children (6 to 11):** stomach aches and headaches, trouble concentrating, anger or sadness, blaming themselves, trying to be "perfect," worrying about the parent who isn't with them.

**Teenagers:** withdrawal, anger, risk-taking, trouble at school, protecting a parent, taking on adult roles, mistrust in their own relationships.

The Decoder describes two patterns that can carry on after separation. [Parentification](/playbook/parentification) is when a child becomes a parent's carer or confidant. [Golden Child Elevation](/playbook/golden-child-elevation) is when one child is favoured and another blamed.

> Many children recover well. What helps most is a safe, steady home and at least one adult who listens.

## What helps children recover?
- **Safety and routine.** Regular meals, bedtimes and school. Predictability tells a child's body the danger has passed.
- **Your calm, as much as you can manage.** Children read your mood. You don't have to be perfect. You can say "I'm sad today, but I'm okay, and it's not because of you."
- **Permission to feel.** Let them be angry, sad, or happy to see the other parent.
- **Listening without leading.** Ask open questions: "How was the weekend?" Not "Did Dad shout?"
- **Telling school.** A teacher who knows what's happening can support them and notice changes.
- **Fun.** Ordinary play and laughter are part of healing. Losing at a card game to a seven-year-old who makes up the rules as they go counts.

## When should I get professional help?
Talk to your child's doctor or school counsellor if:

- reactions are getting worse, not easing, after a few months
- they talk about hurting themselves, or about not wanting to be alive
- they can't go to school, or can't sleep, for long stretches
- they're hurting others or animals
- they tell you about being hurt, threatened or asked to keep secrets

Ask for a therapist with experience of domestic abuse. The types of therapy with good evidence are under "Therapy that helps children" below. See also [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What if my child still loves the other parent?
Most do. That isn't a sign they didn't notice, or that you were wrong to leave. Children can love a parent and be hurt by them at the same time. Let them love both. Don't ask them to see the other parent the way you do.

## What if my child is angry with me?
Children often aim their anger at the safe parent, because it's safe to. It can feel deeply unfair. Hold steady, name the feeling ("You're so angry that we moved"), and keep the rules. It usually eases as they feel more secure.

## Signs by age
- **Babies and toddlers:** trouble sleeping, clinginess, crying a lot, feeding problems, startling easily.
- **Young children:** bedwetting, nightmares, tantrums, fears of separation, acting out what they saw in play.
- **Primary school age:** stomach aches and headaches, worry, trouble concentrating, aggression or withdrawal, blaming themselves.
- **Teenagers:** anger, withdrawal, risk-taking, school problems, taking a parent's side strongly, or trying to protect you.

Many children show some of these for a while after separation. They usually ease with safety and steady routines.

## Going backwards
Some children slip back to younger behaviours: wetting the bed, baby talk, needing help with things they could do before. It's a common response to stress. Stay calm and supportive, without shaming it or making a fuss of it. It usually passes as they feel safer.

## Anger
Children who lived with abuse may lash out, often at the safe parent, because it's safe to. Set calm limits on behaviour while you name the feeling: "You're angry. You can be angry. You can't hit." Look for patterns, like anger after handovers, and note them.

## Teenagers: self-harm and their own relationships
Teenagers who've grown up with abuse can be at higher risk of self-harm, low mood and controlling relationships of their own. Watch for withdrawal, secrecy about injuries, or a new partner who checks their phone and cuts them off from friends. Talk openly about healthy relationships. If you're worried about self-harm, contact their doctor or a young people's crisis service.

## Getting school on side
Tell the school what's happening, briefly. Ask for a named person your child can go to. Teachers can watch for changes, give a little slack on homework, and support your child at hard times like handovers or court dates. Ask them to tell you if they notice anything.

## Therapy that helps children
If your child is struggling for more than a few weeks, ask their doctor about therapy. Approaches with good evidence for children after trauma include trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT). For younger children there's child-parent psychotherapy, where you and your child take part together. A good therapist will include you and explain what they're doing.

## What about me?
Your recovery helps theirs. Looking after yourself is part of looking after them. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

::: scene
## How Lily's son settled
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

After they left, Lily's six-year-old started wetting the bed, crying at school drop-off and hitting his sister. Lily kept bedtimes the same and told the school.

Every evening she spent fifteen minutes playing whatever he chose, which was usually the same dinosaur game. When the bedwetting didn't ease after two months, his doctor referred them to a children's therapist who worked with them together.

By the end of the year he was sleeping through, and drop-offs were calm. He still had wobbles before visits with his dad. Lily learned to expect them and plan a quiet evening afterwards.
:::

## Fifteen minutes a day
Pick a time today for fifteen minutes with each child, doing something they choose, with your phone in another room. Put it in your calendar now. A short, regular time with your full attention is one of the simplest things that helps children feel safe.

=== when-your-child-is-pulled-in
title: When your child is pulled into it
question: What do I do when my child repeats what the other parent says about me?
seo: Child Repeating What the Other Parent Says? What to Do
short: When a child is repeating what the other parent says about you, stay calm, correct the fact gently, and tell them it's not their job to sort out grown-up problems. Don't question them about the other home. If they're asked to spy, keep secrets or refuse visits, keep a record and get advice.
---
Your seven-year-old says, "Daddy says you broke up our family because you're selfish." Your teenager won't come back after a weekend away. Your child asks why you "don't let" them see their other parent more.

A child repeating what the other parent says about you is one of the most common ways children get pulled into the conflict. It lands like a punch. You can still answer in a way that doesn't make it worse for them.

If your child talks about being hurt or threatened, or about harming themselves, contact the police, child protection services or a crisis line now. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## What do I say when my child repeats something cruel about me?
Take a breath first. Your reaction teaches them whether it's safe to tell you things.

> **Say:** "I can see that's confusing. That's not what happened, but it's a grown-up thing and you don't need to worry about it. Grown-ups sort out grown-up things. I love you, and that won't change."

Then move on. Don't ask what else was said. Don't attack the other parent, however much you want to. Later, write it down in your child's words, with the date. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if my child is being asked to spy or keep secrets?
Children often get asked "Who's at Mum's house?" or "What did she buy?" Tell your child, without blaming anyone:

> **Say:** "You don't have to answer questions about my house. You can just say 'I don't know' or 'ask Mum.' And you never have to keep a secret that makes you feel worried. You can always tell me or a teacher."

The Decoder describes children put in the middle in [Triangulation](/playbook/triangulation) and [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage).

## What about gifts and promises?
Big gifts, special treats, or promises like "you can live with me full time" are common, especially around court dates. If the other house suddenly has a trampoline, a new games console and no bedtime, don't try to match it. Don't criticise the gift either. Keep your home steady and your promises small and kept.

## What if my child refuses to see the other parent?
Children refuse visits for many reasons: fear, something that happened, loyalty pressure, wanting to stay with friends, or not wanting to leave you alone. Find out gently, without leading questions.

- If you believe your child is unsafe there, get legal advice urgently, and contact child protection services if there's immediate risk.
- If there's a court order, don't stop contact on your own without advice. In many places that can count against you. Ask your lawyer about asking the court to review it.
- Keep a record of what your child says and does around visits.

## What if my child refuses to see me?
This is one of the most painful things a parent can go through. Keep the door open: send short, warm messages, turn up when you're supposed to, and don't blame your child.

Get legal advice, and keep a record. If you're accused of being the cause, see [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser) and the parental alienation section of [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## Loyalty conflicts
Children love both parents, often fiercely, even a parent who hurt them. When one parent pulls them in, they can feel torn in two: guilty for enjoying time at your house, guilty for missing the other.

You can ease it by giving permission:

> **Say:** "You're allowed to love your mum and have fun with her. You don't need to worry about my feelings. I'll be fine, and I'll be here."

Children who know they're allowed to love both parents tend to cope better, even when one parent is causing harm.

## Claims of "alienation"
If you raise abuse, the other parent may say you're turning the children against them. This is a common response in family court, and it's hard to hear. Courts in many countries are now more cautious about these claims where abuse has been alleged.

What helps:

- Show that you support the children's relationship with the other parent where it's safe.
- Keep your record of the children's own words and reactions.
- Explain clearly why you're worried, focusing on what happened, not on labels.
- Ask for a professional assessment, not a quick judgment.

See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) for more.

## Keeping your bond strong
- Spend regular one-to-one time with each child, doing something they choose.
- Keep your home predictable: meals, bedtimes, rules.
- Notice and name what they do well.
- Say sorry when you get it wrong. It shows them repair is possible.
- Keep your promises small and keep them.

Steady, ordinary love does more than anything you could say about the other parent.

## If a teenager chooses the other home
Sometimes a teenager decides to live with the other parent, even an abusive one. It can feel like a terrible loss and a rejection. Often it's about freedom, fewer rules, loyalty or guilt, not about loving you less.

Keep the door open. Stay in touch without pressure, and keep inviting them. Don't criticise the other parent to them. If you're worried for their safety, talk to a lawyer or children's services. Many teenagers come back, or grow closer again, in time.

## Working with the school or a therapist
Professionals can help a pulled-in child, as long as they understand the situation. Give the school or therapist a brief, factual account, and any court order. Ask them to tell you if your child says things that worry them. Don't ask them to take sides; ask them to support your child.

## When should I get outside help for my child?
Talk to their doctor, school or a child therapist if your child seems frightened of the other parent or talks about being hurt. The same goes for sudden changes in behaviour, or a struggle that lasts for weeks. See [How children react, and what helps them](ch:how-children-react).

## Reconnecting with adult children
Sometimes children who were turned against you stay distant into adulthood. Reconnection is still possible, often years later, when they start to see things for themselves.

- Keep a door open without pressure: a birthday card, a short message once in a while.
- Don't use contact to argue your case. Let them lead.
- If they reach out, listen more than you explain.
- Look after yourself while you wait. Support groups for estranged parents exist in many countries.

::: honest
## Is my own hurt colouring what I see?
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

When your child comes back changed, it's natural to see the other parent behind it. Sometimes that's true. Sometimes your child is simply growing up, tired, or sad about having two homes. Ask yourself:

- Is this behaviour new, or has my child always reacted like this to change?
- What did my child say, in their own words, before I interpreted it?
- Could I be asking questions that pull them into the middle too?
- Would a teacher or another adult see it the way I do?

If you're unsure, ask a professional who knows your child. Getting it right protects them either way.
:::

::: scene
## The sentence that helped
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Kwame's twelve-year-old daughter came back from her mum's saying she didn't want to come to his house any more. He felt sick, but he didn't argue or criticise her mum.

Instead he said, "You're always welcome here, and I'm not going anywhere. You can love both of us." He kept sending her short messages about things she liked, and kept her room ready.

Three months later she asked to come for a weekend. She never explained why. He didn't ask.
:::

::: action
## Keep the door open
Send your child one short, warm message today about something they like, with no questions and nothing about the other home. A small, steady signal tells them they can come back.
:::

=== apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan
title: Apps, calendars and the parenting plan
question: Which co-parenting app is court approved, and what goes in a parenting plan?
seo: Court-Approved Co-Parenting Apps and Parenting Plans
short: A court-approved co-parenting app keeps every message timestamped and undeletable, which protects you and calms things down. Courts often order them in high-conflict cases. Pair the app with a detailed parenting plan that settles schedules, holidays and decisions in advance, so there's less to argue about.
---
The less you have to negotiate, the less there is to fight about. Two boring tools do most of that work: a co-parenting app and a detailed parenting plan. Boring is exactly what you want here.

## Which co-parenting app is court approved?
Courts don't usually "approve" one brand, but many order parents to use a co-parenting app in high-conflict cases. Well-known options include OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents and AppClose. Look for:

- **messages that can't be edited or deleted,** with timestamps
- **a record you can export** for your lawyer or the court
- **a shared calendar** for the schedule
- **an expense tracker** for shared costs
- **tone-checking features,** in some apps, that flag hostile wording before it's sent

Some apps charge a fee; some courts can waive it. Ask your lawyer which apps your local court is used to.

The Decoder's page on [Controlled Communication](/playbook/controlled-communication) shows how the other side can use contact to control you. A dedicated app puts the rules in writing for both of you.

## How do I use the app well?
- Use it for everything. Block other channels if you can.
- Keep messages short and factual. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).
- Check it at set times, not all day. A 47-message essay sent at midnight will still be there at nine, and it reads shorter with coffee.
- Don't respond to anything that isn't about the children. See [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).

## What goes in a parenting plan for a high-conflict ex?
The more detail, the better. Vague plans ("reasonable contact," "to be agreed") give a controlling parent room to argue. Settle:

- **The regular schedule:** days, times, who collects and drops off, and where.
- **Handovers:** place and method. See [Handovers](ch:handovers).
- **Holidays, birthdays and special days,** year by year, alternating if needed.
- **Lateness:** how long to wait before the visit counts as missed.
- **Communication:** the app only, and response times.
- **Decisions:** who decides about school, health and activities, or how disagreements are settled.
- **Travel:** notice needed for trips, and passport arrangements. See [Moving, passports and travel with children](ch:moving-passports-and-travel-with-children).
- **Phone or video calls** with the children while they're at the other home: when and how long.
- **Changes:** how to request them, with how much notice.

## How do we split children's expenses without arguing?
Money for the children is one of the easiest places for control to carry on. A few things help:

- **Put the split in the plan.** Who pays for school trips, clothes, phones and clubs, and in what share.
- **Use the app's expense log.** Most co-parenting apps let you upload a receipt and request payment. That leaves a record, and you never need to discuss it.
- **Set a limit.** Agree that costs under an amount are each parent's own, and costs over it need written agreement first.
- **Don't chase in messages.** Log it, request it, and let the record build. If it goes unpaid, the record goes to the child support service or the court.

See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel) for when money becomes the weapon.

## Should the plan be a court order?
If your ex won't stick to an informal plan, ask the court to make it an order. An order can be enforced; an informal agreement usually can't. See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

## What about a shared calendar?
Use the app's calendar, or one shared calendar just for the children. Don't share your personal calendar. It shows your routine.

## Setting up the app
- Agree which app to use, or ask the court to order one.
- Set it up on your own account and your own device.
- Turn off notifications if they spike your heart rate; check at set times instead.
- Use the app's built-in tone check, if it has one, before sending.

## Calendar rules
- Put the court order or agreed schedule into the calendar as the default.
- Requests to change must go through the app, with a reason and a deadline to reply.
- Don't treat silence as agreement. If they haven't confirmed, the original schedule stands.

Don't be surprised if someone who never knew when half-term was develops a sudden passion for the school calendar. Let the calendar and the plan do the answering.

## What to log
Most apps keep messages automatically. Also log:

- late or missed handovers, with times
- expenses, with receipts
- school and medical appointments
- anything the children report that worries you

## Exporting records for court
Most co-parenting apps can produce a report of messages, calendar changes and payments, with timestamps that can't be edited. Check how your app does it before you need it. Courts often accept these reports as evidence.

## If they refuse to use the app
Keep using it yourself, and send a short email saying all messages about the children will be answered through the app. Keep proof of your offer. If they refuse completely, you can ask the court to order its use.

If they ever say the app proves you're being difficult, the record shows the opposite: polite, prompt replies about the children, all in one place.

::: action
## Export once, now
Open your co-parenting app and find the export or report button. Save one test export somewhere safe, so you'll know how it works before you need it.
:::

=== moving-passports-and-travel-with-children
title: Moving, passports and travel with children
question: Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child, and how do I stop them taking the children abroad?
seo: Moving Away From an Abusive Ex With Your Child: The Rules
short: Moving away from an abusive ex with your child usually needs the other parent's agreement or a court's permission, especially across borders, even when you're the one escaping abuse. If you fear your ex will take the children abroad, passport alerts and court orders can help. Get legal advice before you move.
---
You want distance. A fresh start near family, or just more miles between you. It feels like it should be simple. But moving with children after separation has legal rules, and getting them wrong can hurt your case.

If you or your children are in immediate danger, your safety comes first. Go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now), then get legal advice as soon as you can.

## Can I move away from my abusive ex with my child?
Often yes, but usually not on your own say-so once the other parent has rights:

- **Within the same country:** many places need the other parent's agreement, or a court order, for a move that affects contact. Courts look at the reasons for the move, the effect on the child, and how contact would work.
- **To another country:** almost always needs the other parent's written consent or a court order. Taking a child abroad without it can be child abduction, even for a parent fleeing abuse.

In many places, courts can and do allow moves where abuse is part of the reason. Explain the abuse, the reasons for the move, and how contact with the other parent will work, if it's safe. Your record helps.

See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how), and [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) for the rules where you live.

The Decoder's page on [mobility control](/playbook/mobility-control) describes how controlling people restrict where you can go. Courts in many places can weigh that too.

## What if I've already moved to escape?
If you moved to a refuge or another area for safety, tell your lawyer straight away. Leaving for safety is very different from moving to cut the other parent out. Courts generally understand that, especially with evidence.

## How do I stop my ex taking the children abroad?
If you fear abduction:

- **United States:** enrol your children in the State Department's Children's Passport Issuance Alert Program, so you're told if anyone applies for a US passport for them. Both parents usually have to consent to a passport for a child under 16.
- **United Kingdom:** apply for a prohibited steps order to stop a child being taken out of the country. If removal is about to happen, contact the police, who can issue an all-ports alert. You can also ask HM Passport Office not to issue a passport.
- **Elsewhere:** ask a family lawyer about court orders that stop travel, and whether the court can hold passports.

Keep copies of the children's passports and recent photos somewhere safe.

## What if the children are taken abroad?
Act fast. Contact the police and a family lawyer. If the country is part of the 1980 Hague Convention on child abduction, there's a process to ask for the children's return.

In the US, contact the State Department's Office of Children's Issues. In the UK, contact the International Child Abduction and Contact Unit. In other countries, contact your government's central authority.

## What about holidays abroad?
Your parenting plan should say how much notice is needed for a trip, and how passports are handled. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan). Get written consent for any trip abroad and carry it with you.

## The Hague Convention in plain words
Many countries have signed the 1980 Hague Convention on child abduction. Say a child is taken from their home country to another signing country without consent. The courts there are meant to send the child back quickly, so the home country can decide where they should live.

It isn't a custody decision. There are exceptions, including where going back would put the child at grave risk of harm. If you fled abroad with your children because of abuse, get specialist legal advice urgently. In the UK, the charity Reunite International gives advice on international child abduction.

## Consent letters for travel
When taking children abroad, carry written consent from the other parent if you can get it, or a copy of a court order that allows travel. Include their contact details, travel dates and where you'll stay. Border staff in some countries ask for this when a child travels with one parent.

## Moving away: what courts weigh
If you want to move far away, or to another country, with your children and the other parent doesn't agree, a court usually decides. Courts tend to look at:

- the reasons for the move, including safety
- the effect on the children's relationship with the other parent
- the practical plan: home, school, support, contact arrangements
- the children's wishes, depending on age

Abuse is a relevant reason. Bring evidence and a detailed plan for how the children will keep in touch with the other parent where it's safe.

If they ever say a request to move shows you're trying to cut them out, your plan for contact answers that on paper. Keep it fair, and built around the children's week.

## Travelling with an order
If you have a protective order or child arrangements order, carry a copy when you travel. If you're moving within the US, protective orders are enforceable across states. If you're moving country, check whether your order will be recognised, and how to register it.

*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Her lawyer applied for a prohibited steps order, and the court ordered that the passport be held by her solicitor. In the US, she might have enrolled her son in the passport alert programme.

The trip didn't happen. Ana kept a copy of the order with her, and gave one to her son's school.

::: action
## Check the passports now
Go and look: find your children's passports, or write down who has them and where. If you're worried, get advice before a trip is planned, not after.

=== pregnant-or-with-a-new-baby
title: Pregnant, or with a new baby
question: What should I know about leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant or with a newborn?
seo: Leaving an Abusive Relationship While Pregnant or With a Baby
short: Leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant or with a newborn is common, and abuse often starts or gets worse in pregnancy. Tell your midwife or doctor, plan the birth and the first weeks for safety, and get advice about the baby's legal arrangements. You can protect yourself and your baby.
---
Everyone says a baby needs both parents. You may have heard it from your family, your friends, even yourself at 3am. What a baby needs most is a parent who's safe.

You're pregnant, or you've just had a baby, and you've left or are about to. Leaving an abusive relationship while pregnant is more common than people think. You may be exhausted and frightened, and still doing all the ordinary work of growing or feeding a small person.

Your safety and your baby's come first. If you're in danger now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).

## Why does abuse often get worse in pregnancy?
Pregnancy can be a time when abuse starts or gets worse. Researchers think the reasons include a partner's jealousy, a loss of control and attention, and the extra vulnerability pregnancy brings.

Violence during pregnancy is one of the warning signs services take most seriously. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

The Decoder's page on [vulnerability weaponization](/playbook/vulnerability-weaponization) describes how controlling people use moments of weakness. Pregnancy and a newborn are exactly those moments.

## Who should I tell?
**Your midwife, obstetrician or doctor.** In many countries midwives ask about domestic abuse routinely, often when you're on your own. They can flag your notes, refer you to specialist support, and help you plan. You can ask for appointments without your ex there.

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I'm worried about my safety and the baby's. Can we plan for that?"

## How do I plan the birth safely?
- Ask the hospital to keep your details private and to limit who can visit or get information about you.
- Choose your birth partner carefully. You don't have to have the other parent there.
- Plan how you'll get home and who will be with you in the first weeks.
- Make sure your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) covers the birth and the newborn weeks.

## What about the baby's legal arrangements?
Rules differ a lot. In some places an unmarried father gets legal rights by being named on the birth certificate; in others he has to apply. A court may need to decide on contact with a newborn, often with short, frequent visits at first.

Get legal advice before registering the birth if you're worried. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money) and [Co-parenting or parallel parenting?](ch:co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting).

## Antenatal care and being asked about abuse
In many countries, midwives and doctors are meant to ask every pregnant person about domestic abuse, when they're alone. If you're asked, you can tell them. They can help with a safety plan, a referral and extra appointments.

If the person you left comes to appointments, ask to be seen alone for part of it. Many clinics have a quiet way to do this, like a sign in the toilets you can respond to.

## Safety at the birth
- Tell your midwife or hospital about the risk in advance.
- Ask for your details not to be given out, and for a password system for visitors and phone calls.
- Agree who can be with you during labour.
- Ask for a copy of any protective order (whatever it's called where you live) to be kept in your notes.

## Registering the birth
- **England and Wales:** if you're not married, the father's name only goes on the birth certificate if you register together, or with certain documents. Being named gives an unmarried father parental responsibility for babies registered since December 2003.
- **United States:** rules for naming the father vary by state, and an unmarried father may need to sign an acknowledgement of paternity.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] registering a birth unmarried father."

Get advice before registering if you're unsure, because it can affect his legal rights.

## Feeding and contact arrangements
Courts and parents often have to think about how contact works with a very young baby, especially if you're breastfeeding. Short, frequent visits may suit a baby better than overnight stays. If there are safety concerns, contact may need to be supervised. Raise feeding and the baby's routine when arrangements are discussed.

## Postnatal depression and support
Having a baby while dealing with abuse puts you at higher risk of postnatal depression and anxiety. Signs include feeling low most days, not enjoying anything, intense worry, trouble bonding, or thoughts of harming yourself or the baby.

Tell your midwife, health visitor or doctor. It's common and treatable, and asking for help is very unlikely to mean your baby is taken away. Services want to keep babies safe with their parent. If you're in crisis, see [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

## How do I look after myself with a newborn?
- Accept practical help: meals, laundry, someone holding the baby while you sleep. If someone offers to bring a meal, the answer is yes.
- Watch your mood. Postnatal depression and anxiety are more common after abuse. If you feel low, hopeless or frightened most days, tell your midwife, health visitor or doctor. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, get help right away: see [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).
- Keep a record of anything the other parent does that worries you. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
