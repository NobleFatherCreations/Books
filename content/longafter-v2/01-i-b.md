=== abuse-or-a-hard-breakup
title: Abuse, or a hard breakup?
question: How do I tell a normal breakup from a narcissist breakup, or from abuse?
seo: Normal Breakup vs Narcissist Breakup: An Honest Test
short: The difference between a normal breakup and a narcissist breakup, or any abusive one, is pattern and purpose. Ordinary conflict runs both ways and eases with time. Abuse runs one way, keeps landing where it hurts most, and serves to keep control.
---
Every breakup hurts. People fight about money and the kids, say things they'd love to take back, and cry in the car outside the supermarket. So how do you know when yours is more than that?

You can test it fairly, and you don't need anyone's permission to do it. Fair matters for two reasons. It gives you peace of mind, and it's what makes other people, a court included, take you seriously.

## What does a normal breakup look like?
Normal breakups can still be ugly. Expect some of this:

- rows about money, the house, the kids and who keeps the good saucepans
- anger, blame and bad days on both sides
- one person grieving harder, or longer, than the other
- slow, clumsy agreements that do eventually stick
- contact that cools off over the months

The giveaway is that the conflict runs both ways and runs down. You're both hurt. You both behave badly at times. Then, slowly, it settles.

## What does a narcissist breakup, or an abusive one, look like?
Whatever word you use for the person, an abusive breakup has a different shape:

- **One direction.** The trouble keeps coming from the same side.
- **Timing.** It lands when you're doing well, when you set a limit, or when you go quiet.
- **Purpose.** Each round leaves you more drained, more scared or more tied to them.
- **No settling.** Agreements get broken or reopened. The calm ends the moment you relax.
- **Fear.** You plan your life around how they'll react.

> In ordinary conflict, people argue about the issue. In control, the issue keeps changing and the target stays you.

## The three-question test
For any incident, ask:

1. **Pattern:** has this, or something like it, happened before? How often?
2. **Purpose:** what did it get them? Contact, control, punishment, an audience?
3. **Effect:** what did it do to you? Did it change what you did, where you went, what you said?

One late payment is a late payment. Everyone has a bad week with the bank.

Late payments that always land in the week of your job interview, right after every boundary you set, for two years, are a pattern with a purpose. Write the answers down. Give it time and the record will show you which one you're dealing with. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if it's both?
It often is. A real disagreement about schooling can sit right next to a pattern of control. You can take the disagreement seriously and still call the control what it is.

Watch out for one word that gets thrown around here: "high-conflict." It makes two people sound equally to blame. When one person is driving it, the Decoder calls that framing a [false equivalence](/playbook/false-equivalence). You're allowed to say it plainly: "There are real disagreements, and there's also a pattern of control coming from one side."

## Am I the abuser?
If you're asking, you're in big company. Years of hearing that everything was your fault can leave you frisking yourself for blame. The Decoder shows how that self-doubt gets planted in [self-gaslighting induction](/playbook/self-gaslighting-induction).

Some straight questions:

- Were you afraid of them, or were they afraid of you?
- Did you bend your life around their reactions, or did they bend theirs around yours?
- When you shouted or snapped, was it after months of being pushed? (That's sometimes called reactive abuse. See [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).)
- Do you want to control what they do now, or do you just want them to leave you alone?

If reading those brings relief, notice that. If you recognise yourself on the other side, that's worth facing too, and it takes guts to look. *The Weighing* has a careful chapter, [Am I the one doing it?](/scale/am-i-the-one-doing-it)

::: bridge repair:index
If those questions left you thinking the harm might have come from you, [The Repair](/repair) is another book in this library, written for people who want to stop harming someone.
:::

## Side by side: a hard breakup and an abusive one

| In a hard breakup | In an abusive one |
|---|---|
| Arguments are about an issue, and the issue can be settled | The issue keeps changing; settling one starts another |
| Both people are upset, then gradually less so | One person's anger grows when the other moves on |
| Contact drops off over time | Contact rises when you set limits |
| Each person tells friends their side | One person works to turn friends against the other |
| Children's arrangements settle into a routine | Arrangements are used to unsettle you |
| Money is argued about, then agreed | Money is withheld, delayed or used to punish |
| You feel sad, angry, relieved | You feel watched, frightened, or that you can't win |

Nobody fits one column perfectly. Look at which column sounds like most of the last six months.

## A week of noticing
Still not sure? Keep a short note each day for a week. Two minutes, tops:

- What contact did we have, and who started it?
- Did anything happen that made me change my plans?
- Did I feel afraid, or careful about what I said?
- Did anything they said to other people get back to me?
- How did I feel after the contact: settled, or shaken for hours?

At the end of the week, read it back as if your best friend had written it. What would you tell them?

## What does a professional look at?
Domestic abuse workers and some family court professionals are trained to look past single arguments. They usually ask about:

- **Fear.** Is one person frightened of the other?
- **Control.** Does one person restrict the other's money, movement, friends or decisions?
- **Pattern.** Is this repeated over time, or a few bad weeks?
- **Escalation.** Is it getting worse after separation?
- **Children.** Are the children being used, or put at risk?

If most of your answers point one way, that's useful to know. Take it to a domestic abuse service. They can talk it through with you, and nobody else has to be involved.

## If you were both at your worst at the end
Plenty of separations end with both people behaving badly. Maybe you shouted, said something cruel, or did a thing you'd rather nobody ever mentioned again. That doesn't automatically make it mutual abuse, and it doesn't cancel out what happened to you.

What counts is the pattern across the whole relationship, not its worst week. Who was afraid? Who bent their life around the other's moods? Who held the money and the decisions? If you reacted to months of being pushed, read the section on reactive abuse in [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

Owning what you did at the end takes strength. It's also a separate thing from taking the blame for a pattern you didn't build.

## Why does getting this right matter?
Because it protects you. Professionals see plenty of separations where both people say they're the victim. A calm account of a pattern, with dates and details, stands out from a list of grievances. It also keeps your head clear on the days they switch the charm back on and you start doubting yourself.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* runs the same check, which it calls manipulation versus friction, in its [reality check](/feminine/post-separation-control#reality-check-manipulation-vs-friction).

## What next?
- If it's a pattern: [the post-separation abuse chapter](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse).
- If it's mostly ordinary conflict: [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact) still helps you keep things calm.
- If you're unsure: start [a record](ch:the-record) and look again in a month.

::: honest
## Looking at it straight, both ways
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

Pain bends the view in both directions. Some people shrink what happened so they don't have to face it. Others, hurt and exhausted, see control in every late reply. Ask yourself:

- If a friend told me this exact story, what would I call it?
- Am I leaving out the worst parts when I tell it, or adding to the hard parts?
- When I picture their behaviour, am I thinking of the pattern, or one bad night?
- Is there anything I did that I'd want to own, separate from what they did?

There's no score. If the answers surprise you, take them to a domestic abuse service or a counsellor and think them through out loud.
:::

::: scene
## A week of notes, read back
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Sam and Alex's breakup was messy. They'd both said cruel things in the last month, and Sam wondered if he was being dramatic to call it abuse. So he kept a week of notes.

Alex had messaged forty times, mostly at night. Three messages mentioned where Sam had been that day. Alex had told Sam's mother he was drinking again, which wasn't true. When Sam set a limit, the messages doubled.

Reading it back, Sam saw the pattern he hadn't let himself see. He called a helpline. He didn't want a label for Alex. He wanted to know what to do next.
:::

::: action
## Three questions that cut through the noise
Open your notes app and type these at the top of a new note:

- Who was afraid of whom, most of the time?
- When I set a limit, what happened next?
- Am I calmer or more frightened as time passes?

Answer the first one in a single line. The other two can wait until tomorrow.
:::

=== if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one
title: If your story doesn't look like the usual one
question: Does post-separation abuse happen to men, LGBTQ+ people, and people leaving their families?
seo: Post-Separation Abuse Against Men, LGBTQ+ People and Families
short: Post-separation abuse against men is real, and so is abuse after same-sex relationships, abuse by parents and families, and abuse that uses immigration status or disability. Most of this book applies to you. This chapter covers what's different and where to find help made for your situation.
---
Most writing about leaving abuse tells one story: a woman leaving a man she lived with. It's a common story. It isn't the only one, and if it isn't yours, you may have spent a while wondering whether any of this is meant for you.

It is. If the examples in this book don't look like your life, the patterns probably still will. Here's what changes when your story is different.

## What if you're a man?
Men are abused by partners too, by women and by men. In England and Wales, official estimates suggest around one in three adults who experience domestic abuse in a year are men.

Men often hit extra walls after leaving:

- being disbelieved, or laughed at
- being treated as the likely aggressor by default
- fewer services, and fewer refuges that take men
- a story from the ex that he's the dangerous one, which people find easy to believe

The patterns in this book apply in full, and [the post-separation abuse chapter](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) has the plain definition. In the UK, the Men's Advice Line is on 0808 801 0327. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline serves everyone.

::: bridge silence:nobody-asked-and-you-noticed
If you're a man and nobody thought to ask about your side of it, [The Silence](/silence/nobody-asked-and-you-noticed) is another book in this library, written for you. It covers recognition and help in depth.
:::

## What about after a same-sex or queer relationship?
Abuse happens in lesbian, gay, bi and trans relationships too. Rates of abuse in LGBTQ+ relationships are similar to, or higher than, rates in straight relationships. Some tactics are particular to these relationships:

- threatening to out you to family, work or your community
- using a small community against you, where everyone knows you both
- telling services it was "mutual," and services that expect a man and a woman and miss what happened
- for trans people, misgendering, attacks on transition, or threats about documents and healthcare

The Decoder's page on [identity-based isolation](/playbook/identity-based-isolation) covers tactics that turn who you are into a weapon. LGBTQ+ domestic abuse services exist in many places. In the UK, Galop runs a helpline on 0800 999 5428. Elsewhere, search "LGBTQ domestic abuse support [your area]."

## Leaving a controlling parent or family
Plenty of people reading this left a family, not a partner. A parent who controlled money, friends, faith or marriage. Siblings who report back.

The patterns are close cousins. There's guilt, contact through relatives, and returns dressed up as "we just miss you." There's a story about you told at every gathering, usually by the time the cake comes out. Read [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends) and [Your parents](ch:your-parents).

## Honour-based abuse and forced marriage
If your family or community controls you in the name of honour, or is pressuring you to marry, the danger after leaving can come from several people at once. Specialist services understand this.

In the UK, Karma Nirvana runs a helpline on 0800 5999 247, and the Forced Marriage Unit gives advice, including for people at risk of being taken abroad. Elsewhere, search "forced marriage help [your country]."

## When immigration status is used against you
"Leave and you'll be deported" is a common threat. It's often untrue, and many countries protect people whose status depended on an abusive partner:

- **United States:** the Violence Against Women Act lets some survivors apply for status without the abuser's help (a self-petition), and there are U visas for victims of certain crimes.
- **United Kingdom:** the Migrant Victims of Domestic Abuse Concession gives some people temporary access to benefits while they apply to stay.

Get advice from an immigration lawyer or a domestic abuse service before you act. The Decoder's page on [status threats](/playbook/status-threats) shows how this pressure works.

## If you're pregnant or have a new baby
See [Pregnant, or with a new baby](ch:pregnant-or-with-a-new-baby).

## If you're disabled, or you were their carer, or they were yours
Abuse can involve controlling medication, mobility aids, care or money. Leaving can mean losing care at the same time. Ask a domestic abuse service about accessible refuge, and ask your local social care team about emergency support.

## If you're under 18
Abuse in teenage relationships is real abuse, and you deserve help made for you. In the US, love is respect offers support by phone (1-866-331-9474) or text (LOVEIS to 22522). In the UK, Childline is on 0800 1111. Elsewhere, search "teen dating abuse help [your country]." If you're in danger now, see [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).

## Older readers, rural readers, readers of faith
- **Older readers:** *The Slow Take* covers coercion in later life, including money.
- **Rural readers:** distance and few services make everything harder. So does a small town where everyone knows your ex, their mum and their mum's hairdresser. Phone and online services can fill some of the gap. See [When you share a town, a workplace or a community](ch:sharing-a-town-a-workplace-or-a-community).
- **Readers of faith:** a congregation can be a support or a pressure. *The Sacred Divide* looks at control inside religious institutions.

## If you're neurodivergent
Autistic people and people with ADHD describe some particular difficulties:

- being told your memory or perception is wrong, when you already doubt how you read people
- routines being disrupted on purpose
- sensory overload used against you, or your reactions to it called "aggression"
- services that read a flat tone or direct speech as a sign you're lying

You're allowed to ask services for adjustments: written information, extra time, a quieter room, a support person. In court, you can ask the judge for adjustments to how you give evidence.

Write things down as they happen. It helps everyone, and it helps you trust your own record when someone tells you that you've got it wrong. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## If English isn't your first language
Most national domestic abuse helplines can arrange an interpreter. You don't need good English to call. Say the name of your language and wait.

- Ask for a professional interpreter, not a family member or friend, and never your children. The person you left may know your community's interpreters, so you can ask for someone from outside it.
- Many services have information translated into common languages. Ask for it.
- Courts usually provide interpreters for hearings. Ask early.

## If you or they are in the armed forces
Military life adds its own pressures: frequent moves, living on a base, a chain of command, and fear that reporting will end a career.

- **United States:** each installation has a Family Advocacy Program for domestic abuse. You can choose a restricted report, which gets you support without starting an investigation, or an unrestricted one.
- **United Kingdom and elsewhere:** service welfare teams and civilian domestic abuse services both help. You don't have to go through the chain of command to get support.

A civilian helpline can explain your options before you decide anything.

## If you have no money, no papers or a record
None of these stops you getting help.

- **No money:** helplines, refuges and many legal services are free. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- **No papers, or a visa tied to them:** read "When immigration status is used against you" above. In the UK, ask about the Migrant Victims of Domestic Abuse Concession. In the US, ask about VAWA and U visas. Talk to an immigration adviser before you tell an authority about your status.
- **A criminal record:** many refuges and services will still take you. Ask. Past convictions don't remove your right to protection.

## If the person was an adult child or another relative
Abuse doesn't only come from partners. An adult son or daughter, a sibling, an in-law or a grandchild can control you, frighten you or take your money. Cutting contact with family brings its own grief and guilt, and nobody sends a card for it.

Most of this book still applies: the safety planning, the record, contact in writing, the legal protections.

In many places, protective orders cover family members as well as partners. The details depend on where you live, so check with [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body). If you're older, read "Older readers" above, and see [The Slow Take](/slowtake), another book in this library, which covers coercion in later life.

## If you're a teenager and your parent has left
If one of your parents has left an abusive relationship, this is your story too. You might feel relieved, angry, guilty, protective or confused, sometimes all before breakfast.

- It isn't your job to protect your parent or fix the situation.
- You're allowed to love both parents, even if one of them hurt the family.
- You don't have to pass messages, report on either home or pick a side.
- If you feel unsafe at either home, tell a trusted adult: a teacher, a school counsellor, a relative.
- In the UK you can call Childline on 0800 1111. In the US you can text love is respect or call a youth crisis line.

You can read any chapter in this book. [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say) and [How children react](ch:how-children-react) are written for parents, but you might find yourself in them.

## The rest of this book is yours too
Whoever you are and whoever you left, the chapters on safety, contact, records, courts, your body and rebuilding are for you. Start at [Where are you right now?](ch:where-are-you-right-now)

## Ask anyway
Scroll back up, pick one helpline, and save the number in your phone now, under a plain name. When you're ready, ring it and ask: "Does this sound like abuse to you?" Most helplines would far rather hear from you than not.
:::

=== the-first-30-days
title: The first 30 days
question: What do I do in the first month after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: After Leaving an Abusive Relationship: A 30-Day Checklist
short: This is an after leaving an abusive relationship checklist for the first month: safety first, then your phone and accounts, documents, money, children, and your record. Do what you can. Nothing on this list has to be done in a single day.
---
The first weeks after leaving are a blur. You're tired, you might be sleeping somewhere new, and everything is shouting that it's urgent.

This after leaving an abusive relationship checklist is for that blur. It puts things in a sensible order. Tick what applies, skip what doesn't, and come back when you can. Nobody does all of it in a day, and nobody hands out medals for trying.

Keep the list somewhere the person you left can't see it.

## What do I do in the first three days?
- **Save emergency and helpline numbers** under plain names. See [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).
- **Tell one or two trusted people** where you are, and agree a code word that means "call for help."
- **Make a basic safety plan:** routes, routines, who collects the children. [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- **If there's any risk of violence,** talk to a domestic abuse service or the police about a protective order. [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## Days 1 to 7: your phone and accounts
Start with your email. It's the master key: whoever can get into it can reset almost everything else.

- Change your email password. Turn on two-step login. Write the backup codes on paper.
- Then change passwords for banking, social media, cloud storage, shopping and streaming.
- Check which devices are logged in to each account, and log out any you don't recognise.
- Turn off location sharing with them on your phone, in apps and on social media.
- Check for tracking: [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).

One warning. Sudden changes can alert someone who is watching. If you're worried about how they'll react, plan the order with a domestic abuse service. The Decoder's page on [account control](/playbook/account-control) explains how shared accounts become a lead.

## Days 1 to 14: documents
Gather the originals, or photograph them if you can't take them yet:

- passports, ID, birth certificates (yours and the children's)
- marriage and divorce papers, and any court orders about custody or protection (the names vary between countries)
- bank statements, pay slips, tax records, pension details
- tenancy or mortgage papers, car documents
- medical records and prescriptions
- any evidence of abuse you already have

Keep one copy somewhere safe away from home, and one encrypted copy online in an account only you can open.

## Days 1 to 30: money
- Open a bank account in your name only, at a bank you didn't share.
- Move your pay to it, and change any direct debits that matter.
- Get your credit report (free in most countries) and look for accounts you didn't open. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- List what you owe and what's in joint names. [Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork](ch:untangling-the-paperwork) covers the longer job.
- Ask about emergency funds and benefits. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).

## Days 1 to 30: children
- Tell the school or nursery who may and may not collect your children. Give them a copy of any court order.
- Decide how contact with the other parent will happen, ideally in writing. See [Co-parenting or parallel parenting?](ch:co-parenting-or-parallel-parenting)
- Give the children simple, honest reassurance. See [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say).

## Days 1 to 30: start your record
Start a record now, even if nothing has happened yet. Date, what happened, who saw it, what it changed. [The record](ch:the-record) has a template. A month of plain notes is worth more later than a long account written from memory.

## Days 7 to 30: people and paperwork
- Change your address with your doctor, bank and employer. If your address must stay hidden, see [Your address, your records, your mail](ch:your-address-your-records-your-mail).
- Update emergency contacts at work, at school and on medical records.
- Check who is named as beneficiary on any life insurance or pension.
- Ask your employer about leave or safety support. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

## The one-page version
Copy this, or print it, and tick as you go:

☐ Somewhere safe to sleep tonight
☐ Emergency number and one helpline saved under plain names
☐ Passwords changed on email, bank and phone account; location sharing off
☐ Copies of ID, birth certificates, bank and housing papers somewhere they can't reach
☐ A bank account only you can see

☐ The children's school and doctor told who can collect them
☐ Your record started: four lines, the same day
☐ One person who knows where you are
☐ A domestic abuse service called, even once
☐ One meal and one night's sleep, as best you can

## What can wait?
More than it feels like. These rarely need doing in the first month:

- Telling everyone you know
- Deciding about divorce, the house or long-term arrangements
- Replying to their long messages (yes, even the 2am one with numbered points)
- Collecting all your belongings
- Working out exactly what happened and why

Write them on a list called "later" so they stop circling. The first month is for safety, money, children and sleep.

## If you left with nothing
Plenty of people leave with only what they're wearing. You can rebuild from there.

- **ID:** most documents can be replaced. A domestic abuse service can help with fees and letters.
- **Belongings:** police in many places will go with you to collect essentials. Ask for this.
- **Money:** emergency grants, crisis payments and local charity funds exist for exactly this. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits) and [Money from zero](ch:money-from-zero).
- **Phone:** a cheap pay-as-you-go phone keeps you reachable and private.

What you left behind can be replaced. You can't.

## Telling work and school
You don't have to give details. Something short does the job.

> **Say (to your manager):** "I've left an abusive relationship and I'm dealing with some safety and legal matters. I may need some flexibility for appointments. Please don't give my details or location to anyone who calls asking for me."

> **Say (to the school):** "There's been domestic abuse and we've separated. Please don't release [child] to anyone except me and [names] without checking with me. I'll send you a copy of any court order."

Many employers have a domestic abuse policy, and some offer paid leave. Ask HR. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

## Your health in the first month
Your body has been under strain, and it may start showing it now. Expect to feel wired and wrecked at the same time. [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) explains why.

- **See a doctor** if you were hurt, even if it seems minor. Injuries to the head or neck, including strangulation, need checking even days later.
- **Keep taking any medicine.** If yours got left behind, a pharmacist can help with an emergency supply.
- **Eat something regular**, even if it's toast. Shock kills appetite.
- **Expect poor sleep.** It usually settles. Sleep when you can. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).

If you feel you can't cope, or you're thinking about harming yourself, go straight to [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* has a longer version of this list for the leaving itself in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

::: scene
## Leon's first month
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Leon left with two bags and his daughter. Week one: a friend's spare room, a new phone, a call to a helpline. Week two: a new bank account, copies of his daughter's birth certificate from the register office, a chat with her school.

Week three: a first appointment with a local legal aid solicitor, and his first full night's sleep. Week four: a small flat, furniture from a charity, and a record with eleven entries.

He didn't do everything on the list. He did enough. By the end of the month he had somewhere to live, a plan, and a sense that he could manage the next thing.
:::

=== if-they-left-you
title: If they left you
question: What happens after a narcissist breaks up with you?
seo: After a Narcissist Breaks Up With You: What Happens Next
short: After a narcissist breaks up with you, or any controlling partner does, it can feel like being thrown away. The fast replacement, the cold silence and the sudden return are common patterns. Being left doesn't mean you deserved it, and it doesn't mean it's over.
---
You didn't leave. They did. Maybe it was sudden. Maybe there was someone new within days. Maybe they told everyone it was your fault before you'd even changed the sheets.

Most writing about abuse assumes you were the one who walked out. If you're the one who got left, the pain has a different shape.

## Why does being left hurt this much?
Because control often ends with a discard. After months or years of hearing you were the problem, being dropped can feel like the final verdict. It isn't. It's often the last move in a pattern you were living inside.

The Decoder calls it [Blame Discard](/playbook/blame-discard): you're made responsible for everything, then dropped for it.

> Being left by someone who hurt you isn't proof that you were the problem.

## What happens after a narcissist breaks up with you?
The patterns people describe are strikingly consistent, whether or not the person would ever be diagnosed with anything.

**They replace you fast.** A new partner turns up almost at once, and the new relationship often looks perfect online. That usually says more about their need for an audience than about you. The Decoder calls the comparison game [triangulation](/playbook/triangulation).

**They go cold.** You might be blocked, ignored or spoken to like a stranger. The silence can feel unbearable, especially if they used to be your whole world.

**They rewrite the story.** You were unstable, you pushed them away, you were the abusive one. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).

**They come back.** Weeks or months later: a message, a "how are you," an apology, a crisis. Often just as you've started to feel better. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## Why did my ex move on so fast?
Maybe it was a new profile photo. Two people grinning on a beach, a few weeks after they walked out. Of course there was a sunset.

A very fast move often means the next person was already lined up, or that they can't stand being alone. It rarely means you were easy to replace. A relationship built that quickly is usually built on the same patterns, and the shine tends to wear off.

It's very hard not to compare. Try not to watch. [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile) explains why the urge is so strong and how to ease it.

## Is it still abuse if they ended it?
Yes, if the pattern carries on. Many people who were left find the control keeps going: through the children, money, shared friends, or by turning up again when it suits them. Everything in [the post-separation abuse chapter](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) applies to you.

## What about the grief?
Being left brings grief. Sometimes it's mixed with relief you feel guilty about. Sometimes it's mixed with longing you feel guilty about too.

You may grieve the person, the future you'd planned, and the version of them you loved. All of that's allowed. Read [The grief nobody will let you have](ch:the-grief-nobody-will-let-you-have) and [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond), which explains why you can miss someone who treated you badly.

## What do I do if they come back?
Decide now, while you're steady. Most people find that not replying, or one short closing reply, works better than a long conversation. [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) has wording for both. If you've already replied, you haven't failed. The same chapter covers that too.

## The questions that loop
*Why? What did I do? Was any of it real? What does the new person have that I didn't?*

These questions can run for months. They feel like they need answers before you can move on. Often there are no good answers, and the person who left won't give you true ones.

A few things that help people step out of the loop:

- Write the questions down, once, on paper. Then write: "I may never know."
- Notice when the loop starts, usually late at night or after you've seen something about them. Name it: "This is the loop."
- Answer the one question you can: "What do I need tonight?"

## Their version of the breakup
You hear it second-hand, over coffee, from someone who only heard their side. Apparently you were cold, unstable, impossible, or they'd been "unhappy for years." People who discard a partner often need a story that makes them the reasonable one.

You can't control their version. You can control how much energy you spend correcting it. Most people who matter will see the truth over time, from how you behave. The few who don't were rarely going to. [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign) covers what to do if the story spreads.

## When you were left with the children, the debts or the house
Being left can mean being left holding everything: the children full time, a mortgage you can't pay alone, debts in your name.

- **Children:** you can apply for child support even if they've gone quiet. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- **Debts:** you may not be responsible for debts they ran up in your name. See [Money from zero](ch:money-from-zero).
- **The house:** get advice before you agree to anything or stop paying. See [Divorce and separation proceedings](ch:divorce-and-separation-proceedings).

## If part of you was relieved
That's common, and it doesn't make the hurt any less real. You can be devastated and relieved in the same afternoon. The relief is often your body noticing what you couldn't say out loud: being with them was hard. Let both feelings be there.

## What helps in the first weeks after being left?
- **Stop the feed.** Mute or block their accounts and the new partner's.
- **Tell one person the real story.** Being left often comes with being misrepresented. One person who knows the truth is a lifeline.
- **Keep a record** if anything continues after the break: messages, contact through other people, problems with shared things. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **Read [Leaving was the middle](ch:leaving-was-the-middle).** It's written for people who left, but almost all of it applies to being left.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* describes discard as the "punishment" stage of [the manipulation cycle](/feminine/the-manipulation-cycle#core-concept).

## When the one who left comes back
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Ruth's husband left her for someone else, suddenly, after fifteen years. For months she blamed herself. Then, nine months later, he messaged: "I've made a terrible mistake."

Part of her wanted to believe it. So before replying, she showed the message to a friend. Together they looked at her record: the coldness, the cruelty in the last year, the way he'd turned their friends against her.

She replied once: "I hope you find what you're looking for. I'm not going to reopen our relationship." Then she blocked the number and went for a walk.

## Put the questions on paper
Grab a pen and any scrap of paper. Write down the questions that keep looping, then write "I may never know" underneath. Add one line about what you want now, for yourself. Fold it up and put it in a drawer.

=== still-under-the-same-roof
title: Still under the same roof
question: How do I cope with being separated but living together?
seo: Separated But Living Together With an Abusive Ex
short: Being separated but living together with someone controlling is one of the hardest places to be. You can't avoid them, and the risk can rise once they know you're leaving. Plan quietly, keep a record, protect your phone and money, and get advice before you announce anything.
---
How do you share a home with the person you're trying to get away from?

Being separated but living together is more common than people think. You've decided it's over, or they have. But the lease runs to spring, or the house hasn't sold, or you can't afford to move yet. So you share a kitchen with someone you're trying to leave.

If you're in danger in the home now, go to [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now).

## Is it safe to stay while separated?
For some people, staying for a while is manageable. For others it's dangerous. The weeks around a separation are a time of raised risk, especially if the person has been violent, threatened you, or controlled you closely. [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first) and [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously) can help you judge.

If any warning sign applies, talk to a domestic abuse service before you tell the person you're leaving. In many places a court can order an abusive partner out of a shared home. Ask about an occupation order, exclusion order or protective order.

## How do I cope day to day?
- **Keep plans quiet.** Don't announce dates or details until you're ready and safe.
- **Keep conversations short and practical.** Bills, children, logistics. Don't get pulled into debates about the relationship.
- **Move important talks to writing.** Text or email gives you a record and keeps things calmer. See [Grey rock and written-only contact](ch:grey-rock-and-written-only-contact).
- **Have a room with a lock**, if you can, and a charged phone in it.
- **Keep a go bag** at a friend's house or at work: documents, cash, medicine, chargers.
- **Expect pressure.** Controlling people often tighten their grip when they sense you leaving: checking where you go, tracking your time, limiting the car or money. The Decoder describes these in [time control](/playbook/time-control) and [mobility control](/playbook/mobility-control).

## What should I protect first?
**Your phone.** Assume a shared home means shared access. Change passwords on a device they haven't touched, and check for tracking. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).

**Your money.** Open an account in your name, at a different bank, with statements sent somewhere safe. Move what you can of your own income.

**Your documents.** Copy or remove passports, birth certificates and financial records now. Things go missing during separations.

**Your record.** Write down incidents the same day and store the notes away from the house. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What about the children?
Keep their routines steady. Don't argue in front of them, and don't ask them to take sides or keep secrets. If they ask what's happening, give them a short, true answer: "Mum and Dad are going to live in different houses. You haven't done anything wrong." [Your children, and what to say](ch:your-children-and-what-to-say) has more.

If you plan to move with the children, get legal advice first. Moving children without agreement can count against you in some places. See [Moving, passports and travel with children](ch:moving-passports-and-travel-with-children).

## What if it's my home, or our home?
Who can stay, and how a shared home is sold or transferred, depends on whose name is on it and where you live. Get legal advice before you leave a home you own or rent jointly, because leaving can affect your claim in some places. [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money) lists free and low-cost options.

## Sharing the space
A few habits can lower the temperature while you're stuck under one roof:

- **Separate where you can:** different rooms, different times in the kitchen, your own shelf in the fridge.
- **Avoid flashpoints:** late nights, drinking, and arguments in front of the children.
- **Put money talk in writing.** A text about who pays which bill is boring, and boring is what you want. It also gives you a record.

## What should you keep out of sight?
- Your plans to leave, until you're ready
- Search history and messages to helplines (use a private window or a friend's phone)
- Copies of documents, kept outside the home or with someone you trust
- Any money you're saving
- New passwords, and the fact that you've changed them

If they're checking your devices, see [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology). Sudden changes can tip off a controlling person, so plan the order with a domestic abuse service.

## Planning the move without announcing it
For many people, the safest move is one the other person doesn't know about in advance.

1. Talk it through with a domestic abuse service. They can help with timing, refuges and police support.
2. Choose a time when they'll be out.
3. Arrange transport and help in advance.
4. Take the essentials first: documents, medicine, children's things, anything irreplaceable.
5. Leave a note only if your advisers think it's safe, and keep it short.

## Asking a court to have them leave
In many places a court can order the other person out of the home, even if they own it or rent it with you.

- **England and Wales:** an occupation order, under the Family Law Act 1996.
- **Scotland:** an exclusion order.
- **United States:** many protective orders can give you exclusive use of the home, sometimes called a "kick-out" or "move-out" order.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country or state] court order remove abusive partner from home."

These orders usually need evidence of harm or risk, and the rules vary from place to place. A domestic abuse service or a legal aid lawyer near you can tell you whether you're likely to qualify. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## If they're the one moving out
If they leave, the danger doesn't always leave with them. Change the locks if you're allowed to, and tell neighbours not to let them in. Collect their keys, or assume they still have one.

Agree in writing how and when they'll collect their things, ideally with someone else there. Then start your record. The first weeks after they go are often when contact spikes.

## When it's time to go
Leave at a time they don't expect, with your documents, money, phone and medicine. If you need help removing belongings safely, many police services will attend while you collect them, so ask locally. Then start [The first 30 days](ch:the-first-30-days).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers planning an exit from a shared home in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## Three months under one roof
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

For three months, Ana and her partner lived in the same flat after agreeing to separate. She kept her documents at her friend's house, moved into the spare room and kept her phone charged. Conversations stayed short and about bills. Every two weeks she talked to a domestic abuse worker, by phone, from her car.

When he started going through her post, she knew it was time. With her worker's help, she arranged to move on a day he was at work. She left a short note, then called her worker to say she was safe. The plan didn't make it easy. It made it possible.
