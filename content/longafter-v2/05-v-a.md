=== what-to-document-and-how
title: What to document and how
question: How do I document post-separation abuse so it helps in court?
seo: How to Document Post-Separation Abuse: A Simple Method
short: To document post-separation abuse, write a short record of each incident the same day: when, what happened, who saw it, and what it changed. Keep messages, photos and reports alongside it, stored where your ex can't reach. A plain record kept over months is often what makes a court, the police or a service see the pattern.
---
One late handover means nothing to a judge. Forty of them, each written down the same day, tell a story. That's the whole case for keeping a record.

If you're still working out whether what's happening counts, [What is post-separation abuse?](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) sets it out. Here, we're getting it down on paper.

## What counts as documentation?
Documentation is a plain, dated record of what your ex does: what happened, when, who saw it and what it changed. It sits alongside the messages, photos and reports that back it up. Written the same day and stored where they can't reach it, it turns a blur of separate incidents into a pattern that other people can see.

It also protects you from your own memory. Stress makes memory patchy, and patchy memory can be used against you. If you've caught yourself checking your own memory like it's lying to you, see [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## How do I write an entry?
Four lines per incident. That's a complete record. It doesn't need to be longer.

1. **When.** The date, and the time if you have it.
2. **What happened.** The act, in plain words. Skip "he was difficult at handover" and write "arrived 40 minutes late, and said in front of the children that I had made them wait."
3. **Who saw it.** A name, or "nobody." Both are worth writing.
4. **What it changed.** The missed appointment, the money not paid, the child upset that evening, the route you changed.

Four lines written the same day are worth more than a beautiful essay written six months later. Your record should be gloriously dull. If a stranger would nod off reading it, you're doing it right.

[The record](ch:the-record) has a template you can copy.

> Write what you saw and heard, not what you think it meant. "She said she'd make sure I never see the kids" is evidence. "She's trying to destroy me" is a conclusion.

## What should I write down?
- **Handovers:** time agreed, time they arrived, what was said, who was there.
- **Missed or late payments:** due date, date paid, amount.
- **Messages and calls:** keep them all, including the ugly ones.
- **Contact that breaks an agreement or order,** however small.
- **Contact through other people:** who passed on what, and when.
- **Things your children say,** in their words, without prompting them. See [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in).
- **Turning up, following, watching.** See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
- **Reports you make:** police reference numbers, the officer's name, the date.
- **Effects on you:** time off work, doctor visits, therapy, costs.

## How do I keep messages as evidence?
Screenshot with context, so the date, the time and the sender's name or number are all in the shot. Better still, export whole conversations where you can. Most messaging apps have an export option, and a whole thread is harder to dismiss than one cropped image.

Keep the originals on your phone, even after screenshotting. Save voicemails as audio files. Print the most important messages and date the printouts.

Messages can be faked or edited, and you may be accused of doing it. Originals and full exports are your answer to that. The Decoder's page on [False Reports](/playbook/false-reports) covers what happens when the other side makes things up.

## Can I record calls or conversations?
It depends on where you live. In some places you can record a conversation you're part of without telling the other person. In others, everyone must agree.

Recording without the right consent can be a crime, and it can count against you in court. Search "[your country or state] recording conversation consent law," or ask a lawyer before you press record.

## How do I turn a record into a timeline?
When a lawyer, a court or the police needs it:

1. List incidents in date order, one line each.
2. Group them by type: handovers, money, messages, children.
3. Mark the ones with evidence attached.
4. Add a short summary at the top: the pattern, the period, and a count. "Since the order on 4 June, 63 messages outside the agreed app, 11 late handovers, 4 missed payments."

That summary is often the first thing a busy professional reads.

## Where should I keep it?
Somewhere completely separate from any device or account the person you left ever touched:

- a notebook kept at work, or with a friend
- an email account only you can open, with two-step login
- a document in a cloud account they never had access to
- a dedicated app, if a domestic abuse service recommends one

Check [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology) first, so nobody else is reading along.

## A sample entry, filled in
> **14 March, 5.40pm.** At the school gate handover, he stood in front of the car door and said, "You'll never keep them, I'll make sure of it." Our daughter heard and started crying. The teacher on duty, Ms Patel, saw him block the door. Afterwards I was too shaken to drive for twenty minutes and we were late for her swimming class.

Four lines: when, what, who saw it, what it changed. It took two minutes to write. It's exactly the kind of entry that helps a police officer, a lawyer or a judge see what happened.

## Photos, injuries and medical records
- Photograph injuries straight away and again over the next few days, as bruises often show more later. Include something that shows the date, or keep the original file, which stores it.
- See a doctor or go to an urgent care clinic, even for small injuries, and tell them how it happened. Medical notes made at the time are strong evidence.
- Photograph damage to property, messages written on things, and items left at your home.
- Keep receipts for anything you had to replace or repair.

## Witnesses
Write down who saw or heard things: neighbours, teachers, friends, the person at the shop till. Note their name and how to reach them. Don't ask them to take sides. Later, you can ask whether they'd be willing to say what they saw if it's ever needed.

## Your child's words
If your child says something worrying, write down their exact words as soon as you can, and what you asked, if anything. Don't question them again and again, or ask leading questions like "Did Daddy hurt you?" If you're worried about harm, tell a professional and let them do the asking.

## What police and courts find persuasive
- **Records made at the time,** not written up months later.
- **Patterns over time,** with dates.
- **Evidence from others:** messages, medical notes, police logs, school records.
- **Calm, factual language.** "She threatened to take the children abroad" lands better than "She's evil."
- **Consistency.** Small, honest details hold up better than big claims.

## If you lost old evidence
Lots of people do: a phone that was taken, messages that were deleted, years nobody wrote down. You can still write a summary now of what you remember, with rough dates. Ask your phone provider, email service or bank about old records. Police and doctors may have notes from past contacts. A partial record is still worth having.

## How do I stop this taking over my life?
Treat it as a habit, not a project. A few minutes when something happens is enough. Once a week, take ten minutes to check every entry has a date and the screenshots are filed. Then close it and do something you enjoy.

If you're writing for hours every evening, or rereading it to stoke the anger, step back. The record is there to hold the facts so your head doesn't have to.

If your ex ever says you're "keeping a file on them" as if that's sinister, stay calm. A dated, factual record is what domestic abuse services advise people to keep. Say that, once, and leave it there.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* sets out an evidence routine in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top), and covers faked or doctored evidence in [Synthetic "Evidence"](/feminine/synthetic-evidence#core-concept).

::: action
## Start it in two minutes
Open a notes app or a notebook that only you can get to. Give it a dull name, like "Garden". Write today's date and one line about the last thing that happened. That's your record started.
:::

=== systems-abuse
title: Systems abuse and litigation abuse
question: What is litigation abuse, and how do I prove it in family court?
seo: Litigation Abuse in Family Court: Signs and What to Do
short: Litigation abuse is using the legal system to keep control over a former partner: repeated filings, delays, false complaints and endless applications that drain your time, money and peace. Courts can limit it. You prove it by showing the pattern, with dates, outcomes and costs.
---
People say going to court settles things once and for all. That was the idea, anyway. Instead it's become the place where the fight carries on. Every few months there's a new application, a new allegation, a new hearing. You start recognising the court's envelopes from across the room. Each one costs you money, time off work and sleep.

## What is litigation abuse?
Litigation abuse is when someone uses the courts to harass, control or wear down a former partner. Think repeated applications, needless delays, false allegations and complaints that drain your money, time and sleep. Each step can look legal on its own. The harm is in the pattern, and in many places courts can act once they see it.

People also call it legal abuse, abusive litigation or paper abuse. When it spreads to other agencies as well, like the police, child services or your employer, it's sometimes called systems abuse.

> If the legal system itself feels like part of what's happening to you, you may be right.

## What does it look like?
- repeated applications about the same issue, often with little basis
- appeals of every decision
- applications timed around holidays, exams, your new job or your health
- delay: missed deadlines, last-minute adjournments, refusing to share documents
- piles of documents served at the last minute
- false or exaggerated allegations to police, child services or the court
- complaints to your employer, landlord, regulator or licensing body
- refusing to agree anything, so every small matter goes to a full hearing
- representing themselves so they can question you directly, where that's allowed

Any one of these can happen in a normal case. A run of them, aimed at you, is a pattern.

The Decoder covers this as [Legal Abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse), [Administrative Escalation](/playbook/administrative-escalation) when it spreads to other agencies, and [Legal / Administrative Threats](/playbook/legal-administrative-threats) (the "see you in court" messages).

## Does the law treat it as abuse?
It can. Many researchers and services see litigation abuse as one of the main ways [coercive control](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) carries on after separation.

Some laws now name it. In England and Wales, the official guidance on controlling or coercive behaviour lists using legal proceedings as a form of it. The criminal law rarely punishes it directly, but family courts can take it into account.

## How do I prove litigation abuse in family court?
By showing the pattern, plainly:

- **A timeline of every application,** complaint and report: date, what was claimed, the outcome.
- **The outcomes:** how many were dismissed, withdrawn or found to have no basis.
- **The costs:** legal fees, time off work, travel.
- **The timing:** what was happening in your life when each one landed.
- **The effect on the children,** if any: missed school, distress around hearings.

Present it calmly and with numbers: "Since the final order in March 2024, there have been seven applications. Six were dismissed. All seven were filed within two weeks of a school holiday." [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows how to build this.

## A one-page pattern summary for the court
Judges have little time. A one-page table can show what pages of statements can't:

| Date | Application or action | Outcome | Effect on the children or me |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | Application to change contact | Dismissed | Three days off work |
| Mar | Complaint to school | No action | Child questioned at school |
| May | Appeal | Refused | Legal costs |

Ask your lawyer or the court whether you can file it with your statement.

## Can a court stop repeated applications?
Yes, in many places:

- **England and Wales:** family courts can make a barring order under section 91(14) of the Children Act 1989, so a parent needs permission before making new applications. Since the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, the law makes clear these orders can be used to protect a child or a parent from harm caused by repeated applications. Civil courts can also make civil restraint orders.
- **United States:** many states let courts declare someone a vexatious litigant, so they need a judge's permission to file. Some states, including Georgia and Washington, have laws aimed at abusive litigation in family cases.
- **Australia:** family courts can restrict proceedings considered vexatious.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] vexatious litigant family court," or start with [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body).

Ask your lawyer whether it's worth applying for one. If you don't have a lawyer, you can raise it yourself at a hearing:

> **Say:** "I'm asking the court to consider an order stopping further applications without permission."

That's a calm request, and it's the court's decision to make. Nobody sensible could read it as you trying to shut the other parent out.

## What is legal abuse syndrome?
"Legal abuse syndrome" is a term made popular by Karin Huffer, a US family therapist, in a 1995 book. It describes the stress, anxiety and sense of injustice that long or abusive court cases can cause.

It isn't a medical diagnosis, and despite some claims online, it isn't in the DSM-5, the main psychiatric manual. That doesn't make what you feel any less real. Years of filings, hearings and waiting can cause symptoms a lot like trauma: dread when the post arrives, poor sleep, trouble thinking straight.

You can't usually sue someone for "legal abuse syndrome" itself. What you can do is ask the court to deal with the litigation, as set out above. That means costs orders, limits on new applications, or a finding that the case is vexatious. For the effects on your health, see [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## Complaints to your employer or regulator
If you work in a regulated job, like nursing, teaching or law, the person you left may complain about you to your regulator. Get ahead of it. Tell your employer or regulator early that there's a history of domestic abuse and an ongoing court case, and keep a copy of everything.

Most regulators have seen malicious complaints before and know how to handle them. A union or professional body can help.

## What helps while it's happening?
- **A lawyer who has seen this before,** and who treats each filing as part of a pattern rather than a fresh crisis. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- **Respond to every deadline.** Missing one can hand them a win.
- **Don't match them.** Counter-applications can make you both look high-conflict, and that label helps nobody but them.
- **Ask about protections in court**, so you don't have to face them alone. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).
- **Pace yourself.** This is often long. Plan your energy as if it will be.

## Keeping your costs and energy down
- One folder, physical or digital, for everything, in date order.
- One list of deadlines, checked every Monday.
- Answer what the court asks, and no more.
- Use free legal help lines between hearings.
- Protect some time each week that has nothing to do with the case. The case will happily eat every evening you give it.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* calls it "paper terrorism" in its chapter on [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept), and shows how paperwork becomes a trap in [The Administrative Cage](/feminine/the-administrative-cage#core-concept).

::: scene
## When the applications kept coming
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

In eighteen months, Fiona's ex made five applications about the children, appealed twice and complained to her professional body. Each one cost her time off work and nights of sleep. Her new solicitor asked her to make a one-page table: date, application, outcome, effect.

At the next hearing, the judge read the table and made an order stopping further applications without permission. It didn't undo the eighteen months. It did give her the next two years back.
:::

::: action
## Start the table now
Take a sheet of paper or open a blank document. Draw four columns: Date, Application or action, Outcome, Effect. Fill in one row for the most recent thing they did. You can add the rest another day.
:::

=== solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money
title: Legal help with little or no money
question: Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?
seo: Free Legal Help for Domestic Violence and Abuse
short: Free legal help for domestic violence exists in most countries: legal aid, domestic abuse legal services, law school clinics, free advice lines and court help desks. Many people who qualify never ask. Call one, say you've experienced domestic abuse, and ask what they can offer.
---
You look up a family lawyer's fees, see the hourly rate, and close the page quickly, like it might bite. Plenty of people stop right there and never ask for protections the system was built to give them. Wherever you live, you probably have more options than you think. They're just badly advertised.

## Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?
Work through these one at a time.

**Legal aid.** Government-funded legal help for people on lower incomes. Many countries give priority or extra eligibility to people who have experienced domestic abuse.
- **England and Wales:** legal aid for family cases is usually available if you pass the means test and can show evidence of domestic abuse.
- **Scotland:** apply through the Scottish Legal Aid Board.
- **United States:** legal aid organisations in every state help with protective orders and family law. LawHelp.org lists them by state.
- **Canada:** each province has a legal aid plan, and many prioritise family violence cases.
- **Australia:** Legal Aid commissions in each state, community legal centres, and specialist family violence legal services.
- **Ireland:** the Legal Aid Board provides family law services.

**Domestic abuse legal services.** Many domestic abuse organisations have their own lawyers or advocates, or refer people to lawyers who specialise in abuse.

**Law school clinics.** In many countries, supervised law students give free help, often with family law and protective orders.

**Pro bono schemes.** Some law firms take on cases for free. Ask your local bar association or law society.

**Court help desks and self-help centres.** Many courts have free help with forms and procedures, and some domestic abuse services provide court support workers.

**Free advice lines.** In the UK, Rights of Women runs a family law advice line for women. Many countries have similar lines. If you're not sure who covers your area, [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) helps you track them down.

> When you call, say "I've experienced domestic abuse." It can open doors that a general enquiry doesn't.

## What do I say on the first call?
> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I need help with [a protective order / custody / divorce / a court date on (date)]. My income is about [amount]. What can you offer, and what do I need to bring?"

Have ready:

- any court papers and deadlines
- your income and benefits details
- any evidence of abuse you already have, such as police reference numbers or your record. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## Legal aid when there's been domestic abuse
- **England and Wales:** legal aid for family cases, like child arrangements and finances, usually needs evidence of domestic abuse. Accepted evidence includes letters from a doctor, police, a domestic abuse service or a social worker, a protective order or a relevant conviction. You'll also need to pass a means test. For protective orders, the financial rules can be more flexible.
- **Scotland and Northern Ireland** have their own legal aid systems. Ask a solicitor or the legal aid board.
- **United States:** there's no general right to a lawyer in family cases. Legal aid organisations, law school clinics, bar association referral services and domestic violence legal advocates can help. Many domestic violence programs have lawyers or can refer you.
- **Australia and Canada:** legal aid commissions and community legal centres offer help, often with priority for family violence.

## What if I don't qualify for free help?
You still don't have to pay for a whole case up front.

- **Limited-scope or "unbundled" help.** Some lawyers will do one part of a case for a fixed fee: an hour of advice, checking your forms, drafting one document, or one hearing. You handle the rest. It puts expert help where it matters most.
- **A one-off consultation.** Often cheaper, and enough to understand your options.
- **Representing yourself,** with support from a court help desk or a domestic abuse advocate.

## What if I have to represent myself?
Many people do. Some things help:

- Read the court's guide for people without lawyers. Most courts publish one.
- Keep everything in one folder, in date order.
- Ask for protections if you're afraid of the other side. In England and Wales, an abuser can't cross-examine you in person in family court. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).
- Bring a supporter. Many courts allow a McKenzie friend or support person.

## How do I find the right lawyer?
Look for someone with experience of domestic abuse and coercive control, not just family law. Ask:

> **Say:** "How much of your work involves domestic abuse or coercive control? How would you approach repeated applications from the other side?"

Other questions worth asking before you hire anyone:

- How would you present a pattern, not just incidents?
- How do you charge, and roughly what will this cost?
- How quickly do you reply, and how will you keep me informed?
- What will you need from me?

If they talk over you, wave the abuse away, or push you to settle without hearing why you're worried, keep looking if you can. [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) explains why the right lawyer matters so much.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists legal help among the first steps in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## Keeping costs down
- Arrive organised: a timeline, copies of key documents, your questions in writing.
- Send one clear email with several questions, not six short ones. Lawyers often charge for each email they read, so the midnight trickle of "one more thing" adds up fast.
- Ask what you can do yourself, like collecting documents or drafting your timeline.
- Ask for an estimate before each stage.

## If your lawyer isn't listening
Tell them plainly, in writing:

> **Say:** "I'm worried that the history of abuse isn't being presented to the court. I'd like to discuss how we'll do that."

If nothing changes, you can change lawyers. In most places you can also complain to the lawyer's firm, then to a regulator.

=== family-court-honestly
title: Family court, honestly
question: Does family court recognise coercive control?
seo: Family Court and Coercive Control: An Honest Guide
short: Family courts vary a lot in how well they recognise coercive control, as opposed to physical violence. Some have made real progress; others still push for contact with both parents and miss non-physical abuse. Get advice from a lawyer who knows coercive control, and present a pattern with dates.
---
Somebody has probably told you the judge is bound to see straight through them. Somebody else has told you family courts never believe victims. Both people meant well, and neither is reliably right. What helps is an honest picture, so you can plan for the court you'll stand in.

## Does family court see abuse that isn't physical?
More than it used to, and less than it should. Many courts still focus mostly on physical violence, so [coercive control](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) without bruises can be missed. Some places have made real changes:

- **England and Wales:** a major government review in 2020 found that family courts often minimised domestic abuse, put too much weight on contact with both parents, and didn't hear victims well. Reforms followed, including the Domestic Abuse Act 2021.
- **Canada:** the Divorce Act now lists coercive and controlling behaviour as family violence that courts must consider.
- **United States:** several states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, have written coercive control into their family or protective order laws.
- **Australia:** family law changed in 2023 to put children's safety first.

Practice still varies by country, by court and by judge. The Decoder's pages on [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage) and [Legal Abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse) describe what courts often fail to see.

> Neither "the court will understand" nor "the court never believes victims" is safe to assume. Find out how your court works.

## Who will I meet?
Depending on where you are:

- **A judge or magistrates,** who make the decisions.
- **A child's representative:** a guardian, a children's lawyer or an independent children's lawyer, who speaks for the child's interests.
- **A family court adviser or evaluator,** who interviews parents and children and writes a report. In England and Wales this is often Cafcass; in the US, a custody evaluator; in Australia, a family consultant.
- **A mediator,** who tries to help parents agree.

Each of them is trying to work out what's best for the children. A calm, clear account of the pattern, with the effect on the children, helps them most.

## Should I go to mediation?
Not necessarily. Mediation assumes two people can negotiate fairly. Where there's been control, that often isn't true.

- **England and Wales:** you usually have to attend a mediation meeting before applying to court, but domestic abuse is an exemption.
- **United States:** many states let you opt out of mandatory mediation where there's been domestic violence.
- **Australia:** family dispute resolution is usually required, but there are exemptions for family violence.

If you do mediate, ask for separate rooms ("shuttle mediation"), and say you don't feel safe negotiating face to face.

> **Say:** "There's a history of domestic abuse and I don't feel able to negotiate safely in the same room. I'd like an exemption, or shuttle mediation."

## How do I present a pattern rather than incidents?
Courts hear a lot of competing claims. What stands out is a calm summary backed by records:

- **Start with the pattern in one or two sentences, numbers first.** "Since we separated in May 2023, there have been 23 late or missed handovers, 140 messages outside the agreed app, and five applications, four of them dismissed."
- **Then the effect on the children.** Courts decide for children, so tie each point to them. "Our daughter now has nightmares before handovers."
- **Then the evidence,** organised by type.
- **Leave out labels.** Describe behaviour, not diagnoses. See [Is my ex a narcissist?](ch:is-my-ex-a-narcissist).

Notice what that summary doesn't do. It doesn't call anyone a name or guess at motives. It gives a judge nothing to mistake for bitterness, because it's only dates and counts. [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows how to build the timeline behind it.

## What if they use the case to pressure me?
Expect messages that wave the court around. A calm reply on the record is worth more than a clever one.

::: decoder
**They say:** "Keep this up and I'll tell the judge you're keeping the kids from me."
**What it's doing:** Using the court as a threat, and the children as the lever, so you back down before anyone neutral looks at it. See [Legal / Administrative Threats](/playbook/legal-administrative-threats).
**A reply you could send:** "I want the children to spend time with you when it's safe for them. I'm happy for the court to look at the arrangements. Please keep messages to the children's plans."
:::

Every line of that reply is something you'd be glad for a judge to read. It supports their relationship with the children, welcomes the court, and sets one boundary.

## What about "parental alienation"?
You may be accused of turning the children against the other parent, especially if a child is reluctant to see them. This is one of the most disputed areas in family law.

What's agreed is that a parent can wrongly turn a child against the other parent, and that this harms children.

What's disputed is how often it happens, how it should be judged, and whether alienation claims are being used to discredit parents who raise abuse. Research in the US found that when mothers alleged abuse and fathers then claimed alienation, courts often disbelieved the abuse.

If you're accused:

- stay calm and keep the focus on the children
- show that you support the children's relationship with the other parent wherever it's safe
- explain the child's reluctance with facts, not your interpretation
- get a lawyer who knows this area well

See also [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) and [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## How often do narcissists win in court?
People ask this a lot. There's no reliable figure, and the word "narcissist" won't be used in court. What's true is that charming, confident people can do well in short hearings. Cue the ex's sudden, touching passion for the school calendar.

Calm, organised parents with clear records still do better than anxious ones with long lists of grievances. You can't control how they come across. You can control how prepared you are.

## How are decisions about children made?
Family courts decide based on the child's welfare or "best interests." The details differ by country, but courts usually consider:

- the child's needs, and their wishes depending on age and understanding
- any harm the child has suffered or is at risk of
- each parent's ability to meet the child's needs
- the effect of any change

Domestic abuse is meant to be central to that assessment. In England and Wales, courts follow guidance called Practice Direction 12J when abuse is raised. In many US states, a finding of domestic violence affects custody decisions, sometimes with a presumption against custody for the abusive parent. Ask your lawyer or advocate how this works where you live.

## Reports and evaluators
Before deciding, courts often ask a professional to report.

- **England and Wales:** a Cafcass officer may write a "section 7" report after speaking to both parents and, often, the children.
- **United States:** courts may appoint a custody evaluator or a guardian ad litem.
- **Elsewhere:** family consultants or social workers play a similar role.

When you speak to them, be calm and factual, bring your record, and keep the focus on the children's experience. Say clearly what you're worried about and why.

## Protections in court
You can ask for protections if you're frightened of the other person:

- separate waiting rooms and different arrival times
- a screen in the courtroom, or giving evidence by video
- in England and Wales, people accused of abuse can't cross-examine the person they're accused of abusing in person. The court appoints a lawyer to ask the questions instead.

Ask before the hearing. You don't need to be sure you'll need them.

## Contact centres and supervised contact
If there are concerns about a parent's behaviour, courts may order time with the children to be supervised, or to take place at a contact centre. In the UK, accredited centres are listed by the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search "supervised visitation center [your area]." Handovers through a centre can also keep parents apart.

## If the decision goes against you
- Ask your lawyer to explain the reasons, in writing if possible.
- Appeals usually need permission and have short time limits, often 21 days in England and Wales. Get advice quickly.
- If circumstances change later, like new incidents, you may be able to apply to change the order.
- Look after yourself first. A bad decision can feel like being abused all over again. Call a helpline that night, not a lawyer at midnight.

## What can I control?
- How well documented the pattern is.
- How calm and child-focused you are.
- Whether you have a lawyer who understands coercive control. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- Whether you ask for protections at court. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).
- Whether you meet every deadline.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* shows how systems and paperwork get turned into weapons in [The Administrative Cage](/feminine/the-administrative-cage#core-concept).

## What can I share about the case?
Be careful before you post, publish or pass on anything from family court. The rules are strict, and breaking them can be serious.

- **England and Wales:** cases about children are private. Publishing information from them, or sharing court documents with people outside a permitted list, can be contempt of court. You can usually share information with your lawyer, a McKenzie friend, a doctor or therapist, and a domestic abuse adviser, for advice and support. Posting about the case on social media, even without names, can break the rules. Identifying the children is never allowed.
- **United States:** rules vary by state. Family court records are sometimes sealed, and judges can make orders limiting what parents say publicly. Ask your lawyer before you post anything.
- **Elsewhere:** assume family court matters are private until a lawyer tells you otherwise.

You can still talk about your own experience of abuse in general terms with friends and support services. If you want to speak out publicly, get legal advice first.

::: honest
## Checking what's driving your case
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

A court case can turn a hard situation into a contest. Every so often, ask yourself:

- Is what I'm asking for about my children's safety and needs, or about winning?
- Am I opposing something because it's harmful, or because it came from them?
- What would my children say they need, if I asked in a way that didn't put them in the middle?
- Could I explain each request to a fair stranger in one calm sentence?

If one of these stings, talk it through with your lawyer or advocate before the next step. Courts notice which parent keeps the children at the centre. So do children, later.
:::

::: scene
## What helped in Carla's case
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Carla went into family court expecting not to be believed. She brought a two-page timeline with dates and references to evidence: police logs, school records, messages. She asked for a separate waiting room and a screen.

When the Cafcass officer interviewed her, she spoke about her daughter's experience: the nightmares, the fear before handovers. She didn't call her ex names. The court ordered contact at a supervised centre for six months, with a review. It wasn't everything she hoped for. It was safe, and it was based on what had happened.
:::

::: action
## Write your two sentences
Open your record and write the pattern in two sentences at the very top: since when, what kind of thing, and how many times. Numbers, not adjectives. That's the opening of every conversation you'll have with the court.

=== your-day-in-court
title: Your day in court
question: What should I expect at a family court hearing with an abusive ex?
seo: What to Expect at a Family Court Hearing With an Abusive Ex
short: What to expect at a family court hearing: a waiting area, often a short hearing, and a judge who has read some of the papers. Prepare a folder, ask in advance for protections like a separate waiting room or a screen, bring a supporter, and plan how you'll arrive and leave safely.
---
The date is set. You've been dreading it for weeks, and you've rehearsed what you'll say in the shower more times than you'd admit. Here's what to expect at a family court hearing, step by step, so at least the practical parts hold no surprises.

## What should I do before the hearing?
**Ask for protections early.** Contact the court, your lawyer, or a court support worker at least a week before, and ask what's available:

- a separate waiting room
- a separate entrance, or arriving and leaving at different times
- a screen so you can't see the other person, or giving evidence by video link
- in England and Wales, victims of domestic abuse are automatically eligible for these measures in family court, and the other person can't cross-examine you in person

> **Say:** "There's a history of domestic abuse in this case and I'm frightened of the other party. Which protections can I have at the hearing, and how do I request them?"

**Prepare a folder.**
- every court document, in date order
- your timeline summary on top. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- copies of key evidence, flagged
- a list of the points you want to make, in order
- a notebook and pen

**Plan the day.** Arrange childcare well ahead. Work out how you'll get there, where you'll park, who's coming with you, and how you'll leave without meeting them.

**Bring a supporter.** A friend, a family member, or a domestic abuse court advocate. Many domestic abuse services offer court support workers. Ask the court in advance whether your supporter can come into the hearing.

The [court day checklist](ch:court-day-checklist) puts all of this on one page.

## What should I wear and bring?
Something clean and comfortable that you'd wear to an important work meeting. Then pack:

- your court papers and any order
- your timeline and record
- a pen and paper for notes
- water, a snack, something to read, and your charger
- something calming: a photo, a smooth stone, a card with a breathing reminder
- your phone, charged, on silent

## What happens on the day?
- **Go straight in.** Don't wait outside where you might meet them. Tell security or the usher you've asked for a separate waiting room.
- **You may wait a long time.** Court lists run on a timetable known only to the court. This is what the snack is for.
- **Lawyers may negotiate outside the room.** You don't have to agree to anything you're not sure about. Ask for time.
- **The hearing itself may be short,** sometimes 20 or 30 minutes. The judge has usually read some of the papers.

The Decoder's page on [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes looks, gestures and comments meant to unsettle you. If anything happens at court, tell your lawyer or the usher straight away, and write it down afterwards.

## What if they message me before the hearing?
Some people use the night before to rattle you. Here's one version, and a way to handle it.

::: decoder
**They say:** "Hope you're ready for tomorrow. The judge is going to see exactly who you are."
**What it's doing:** Trying to shake you before you walk in, so you arrive tired and on edge. See [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation).
**A reply you could send:** None is needed. If you want one on record, keep it to: "Please keep messages to arrangements for the children."
:::

Screenshot it, send it to your lawyer or advocate, and put your phone away for the night. A one-line reply like that reads as calm and reasonable to anyone who sees it later.

## How do I speak in the hearing?
- Speak slowly, and address the judge as the court tells you to.
- Answer the question asked, then stop.
- Stick to facts, dates and the effect on the children.
- Don't respond to anything the other side says directly to you. Speak only to the judge.

> You don't have to be perfect. You have to be clear, calm enough, and focused on the children.

## What if I freeze, cry or don't understand?
That's all right. Judges see it often. You're allowed to ask for a moment.

> **Say:** "I'm sorry, could I have a moment?" or "Could I have some water, please?"

Breathe slowly, feet on the floor, and look at your notes. If you're giving evidence and you're very distressed, you can ask for a short break.

Legal language can be baffling, especially under stress. You're allowed to ask for it in plain words.

> **Say:** "I'm sorry, I didn't understand that. Could you explain it in a different way?"

Never agree to something you don't understand. If you're representing yourself, ask the court staff or a help desk to explain the next steps before you leave.

## What if they're representing themselves?
That can mean they're allowed to speak a lot, or try to question you. In England and Wales, they can't cross-examine you in person if there's evidence of domestic abuse; the court appoints a lawyer to ask their questions instead. Elsewhere, ask the court in advance what protections apply.

## What happens after?
- **Get a copy of any order** before you leave, or ask when it will arrive.
- **Read it carefully.** Check dates, times and conditions.
- **Give copies** to the school or anyone else who needs them.
- **Plan for the next few days.** Court dates are among the times when risk can rise. See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

Most people feel wrecked after a hearing, whatever the result. Plan the evening before you go: a friend, a meal, a phone call. Don't read their messages, and don't make big decisions. Write down what happened while you remember it, then put it away. Tomorrow you can ask for advice on what comes next.

If the case continues, [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) has the longer view.

## Your court-day card
Write these on a small card and keep it in your pocket:

- my case number and courtroom
- my support person's number
- three points I must say
- "Could you explain that again, please?"
- "Could I have a moment?"
- where I'm going straight afterwards
:::

If the date is close, print the [court day checklist](ch:court-day-checklist) tonight and tick it off as you pack.

=== divorce-and-separation-proceedings
title: Divorce and separation proceedings
question: What should I know about divorcing a narcissist or an abusive spouse?
seo: Divorcing a Narcissist or Abusive Spouse: What to Expect
short: Divorcing a narcissist or any controlling spouse often takes longer and costs more than an ordinary divorce, because delay, hidden money and conflict can be tactics. Get advice from a lawyer who understands abuse, gather financial records early, ask for exemptions from mediation if needed, and expect it to take time.
---
Any divorce is hard. A divorce from someone who wants to keep control can feel like it'll never end. Here's what tends to be different, and what helps.

## What is different about divorcing a narcissist?
Whatever word fits the person, controlling spouses often:

- **delay:** missing deadlines, refusing to sign, asking for adjournments, and suddenly being unable to find a pen
- **hide money:** moving it, undervaluing a business, "forgetting" accounts
- **fight everything:** refusing reasonable offers so every issue needs a hearing
- **use the children** as bargaining chips in the financial case. See [Children as the channel](ch:children-as-the-channel).
- **play for sympathy:** presenting as the calm, wronged party

The Decoder's pages on [Financial Control](/playbook/financial-control) and [Time Control](/playbook/time-control) describe the moves.

> Delay is often a tactic. Plan as if it will be.

## How does divorce work, broadly?
The details differ by country and state, but the main stages are similar:

1. **Starting the divorce.** Many places now allow no-fault divorce, so you don't have to prove what they did. England and Wales introduced no-fault divorce in 2022.
2. **Arrangements for the children.** Agreed between you, through mediation, or decided by a court.
3. **The financial settlement.** Both sides disclose their finances; then the money, property and pensions are divided by agreement or by a court.
4. **The final order,** which ends the marriage legally.

Separating from an unmarried partner usually has no divorce stage, but property and children may still need court orders.

## The steps in England and Wales
Since April 2022, divorce in England and Wales has been no-fault and mostly online:

1. **Application.** One of you, or both together, applies online. You don't have to give reasons or blame anyone.
2. **The other person is notified.** They can respond, but they can only dispute the divorce on very narrow grounds, such as the marriage not being valid.
3. **Wait 20 weeks,** then apply for a conditional order.
4. **Wait six weeks and a day,** then apply for the final order, which ends the marriage.

Money and the children are dealt with separately, by agreement or by court order. Get financial arrangements made into a court order, because a divorce alone doesn't end financial claims.

Elsewhere, search "[your country or state] how to file for divorce." Most court websites have step-by-step guides. [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) can point you to yours.

## Is abuse grounds for divorce?
In many places you don't need grounds at all. England and Wales, Scotland, Australia, Canada and every US state now allow some form of no-fault divorce. You can end the marriage without proving what they did.

That doesn't make the abuse irrelevant. [Coercive control](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse) can matter for arrangements for the children, protective orders, and sometimes money. In a few US states, fault grounds such as cruelty still exist and can be used. Ask a family lawyer where you live whether raising it helps your case.

## What about hidden money?
Both sides usually have to give full financial disclosure, under oath or with a statement of truth. In England and Wales this is usually done on a long form called Form E. Gather what you can early:

- bank, savings and credit card statements
- pay slips, tax returns, business accounts
- pension statements
- mortgage, property and loan documents
- photos of anything valuable in the home

If you think money is hidden, say so to your lawyer and ask for documents. In many places courts can order more disclosure, and some can penalise someone who hides assets. A forensic accountant may help in larger cases.

Agreeing a settlement without full disclosure can leave you worse off, and it's hard to undo. If they're rushing you to sign, that's a reason to slow down.

## Don't forget the pensions
Pensions are often one of the biggest assets in a marriage, and one of the most overlooked. In many places courts can split them, through pension sharing or similar orders. Ask for a valuation. If the person you left had control of the money, the pension may be bigger than you think.

## Do I have to go to mediation?
Not always. Where there's domestic abuse, many places offer exemptions, and mediation may not be safe. See the mediation section of [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

In England and Wales, you usually have to attend a mediation information meeting before applying to court about children or money, but domestic abuse is an exemption, with evidence. In many US states, judges can waive mediation where there's domestic violence.

## How do I negotiate safely?
- You don't have to be in the same room. Ask for shuttle mediation, where the mediator moves between rooms, or for lawyer-to-lawyer negotiation.
- Put offers and agreements in writing.
- Don't agree anything on the spot, especially under pressure.
- If they threaten you during negotiations, write it down and tell your lawyer.

When you're pushed for an answer, this line does the job:

> **Say:** "I'll consider it and reply in writing."

It's polite, it's reasonable, and it's exactly what a sensible person does with an offer. If they later call it stalling, the written reply you send is your answer.

## What if they won't respond?
Some people ignore divorce papers to delay or to keep control. In most places the process can go ahead without them.

Courts have ways to confirm the papers were sent, and to continue if the other person doesn't take part. Ask the court or a lawyer about the next step, and keep proof of every attempt to notify them.

## How long will it take?
Longer than an ordinary divorce, usually. Some take months; some take years. You can't control their delays. You can control your own deadlines, your records and your energy.

## How do I keep costs down?
- Use free and low-cost legal help where you can: [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- Prepare well, so your lawyer's time goes further: organised documents, a clear timeline, a list of questions.
- Lean on a friend, a domestic abuse advocate or a therapist for emotional support. They cost less per tear than your lawyer does.
- Ask whether parts of the case can be settled separately.
- If they keep filing, see [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse).

## Religious divorce
If you married in a religious ceremony, you may need a religious divorce as well as a civil one, such as a Jewish get or an Islamic divorce. Some people refuse to grant a religious divorce as a way to keep control.

In England and Wales, for some faiths, a court can delay the final civil divorce until the religious divorce is given. Government guidance on domestic abuse recognises withholding a religious divorce as a form of abuse, and it has formed part of at least one conviction for controlling behaviour.

Advice services within your faith community can help. Ask your lawyer early.
