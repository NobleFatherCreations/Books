=== what-to-document-and-how
title: What to document and how
question: How do I document post-separation abuse so it helps in court?
seo: How to Document Post-Separation Abuse: A Simple Method
short: To document post-separation abuse, write a short record of each incident the same day: when, what happened, who saw it, and what it changed. Keep messages, photos and reports alongside it, stored where your ex can't reach. A plain record kept over months is often what makes a court, the police or a service see the pattern.
---
Post-separation abuse often happens slowly, across months or years. One late handover means nothing to a judge. Forty of them, each written down the same day, tell a story.

A record also protects you from your own memory. Stress makes memory patchy, and patchy memory can be used against you. See [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## How do I document post-separation abuse?
Use four lines for each incident. That's a complete record. It doesn't need to be longer.

1. **When.** The date, and the time if you have it.
2. **What happened.** The act, in plain words. Not "he was difficult at handover" but "arrived 40 minutes late, and said in front of the children that I had made them wait."
3. **Who saw it.** A name, or "nobody." Both are worth writing.
4. **What it changed.** The missed appointment, the money not paid, the child upset that evening, the route you changed.

Written the same day, four lines is worth more than a detailed account written six months later. That's the whole reason to do it while it's boring.

[The record](ch:the-record) has a template you can copy.

> Write what you saw and heard, not what you think it meant. "She said she'd make sure I never see the kids" is evidence. "She's trying to destroy me" is a conclusion.

## What should I document?
- **Handovers:** time agreed, time they arrived, what was said, who was there.
- **Missed or late payments:** due date, date paid, amount.
- **Messages and calls:** keep them all, including the ugly ones.
- **Contact that breaks an agreement or order,** however small.
- **Contact through other people:** who passed on what, and when.
- **Things your children say,** in their words, without prompting them. See [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in).
- **Turning up, following, watching.** See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
- **Reports you make:** police reference numbers, the officer's name, the date.
- **Effects on you:** time off work, doctor visits, therapy, costs.

## How do I keep messages as evidence?
- **Screenshot with context:** the date, the time, and the sender's name or number in the shot.
- **Export whole conversations** where you can. Most messaging apps have an export option. A whole thread is harder to dismiss than a single cropped image.
- **Keep the originals.** Don't delete them from your phone, even after screenshotting.
- **Save voicemails** as audio files.
- **Print the most important ones**, and date the printouts.

Be aware that messages can be faked or edited, and you may be accused of it. Keeping originals and full exports protects you. The Decoder's page on [false reports](/playbook/false-reports) covers what happens when the other side fabricates claims.

## Can I record calls or conversations?
It depends on where you live. In some places you can record a conversation you're part of without telling the other person. In others, everyone must agree. Recording without the right consent can be a crime, and it can count against you in court. Search "[your country or state] recording conversation consent law," or ask a lawyer first.

## How do I turn a record into a timeline?
When you need it for a lawyer, a court or the police:

1. List incidents in date order: one line each.
2. Group them by type: handovers, money, messages, children.
3. Mark the ones with evidence attached.
4. Add a short summary at the top: what the pattern is, over what period, with a count. "Since the order on 4 June, 63 messages outside the agreed app, 11 late handovers, 4 missed payments."

That summary is often what a busy professional reads first.

## Where should I keep it?
Somewhere entirely separate from any device or account the person you left ever touched:

- a notebook kept at work, or with a friend
- an email account that only you can open, with two-step login
- a document in a cloud account they never had access to
- a dedicated app, if a domestic abuse service recommends one

Check [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology) first, so your record isn't being read.

## A sample entry, filled in
> **14 March, 5.40pm.** At the school gate handover, he stood in front of the car door and said, "You'll never keep them, I'll make sure of it." Our daughter heard and started crying. The teacher on duty, Ms Patel, saw him block the door. Afterwards I was too shaken to drive for twenty minutes and we were late for her swimming class.

That's four lines: when, what, who saw it, what it changed. It took two minutes, and it's the kind of entry that helps a police officer, a lawyer or a judge see what happened.

## Photos, injuries and medical records
- Photograph injuries straight away and again over the next few days, as bruises often show more later. Include something that shows the date, or keep the original file, which stores it.
- See a doctor or go to an urgent care clinic, even for small injuries. Tell them how it happened. Medical notes made at the time are strong evidence.
- Photograph damage to property, messages written on things, items left at your home.
- Keep receipts for anything you had to replace or repair.

## Witnesses
Write down who saw or heard things: neighbours, teachers, friends, a shop worker. Note their name and how to contact them. Don't ask them to take sides. Just ask, later, whether they'd be willing to say what they saw if it's ever needed.

## Recording your child's words
If your child says something worrying, write down their exact words as soon as you can, and what you asked if anything. Don't question them repeatedly or ask leading questions like "Did Daddy hurt you?" If you're worried about harm, tell a professional and let them ask.

## What police and courts find persuasive
- **Records made at the time,** not written up months later.
- **Patterns over time,** with dates.
- **Evidence from others:** messages, medical notes, police logs, school records.
- **Calm, factual language.** "She threatened to take the children abroad" works better than "She's evil."
- **Consistency.** Small, honest details hold up better than big claims.

## If you lost old evidence
Many people lose evidence: a phone that was taken, messages that were deleted, years that weren't written down. You can still write a summary now of what you remember, with rough dates. Ask your phone provider, email service or bank about old records. Police and doctors may have notes from past contacts. Even a partial record is worth having.

## How do I stop this taking over my life?
A record is a habit, not a project. A few minutes when something happens is enough. If you find yourself writing for hours every evening, or rereading it to feel angry, step back. You're documenting what happened to you, not running an investigation into them.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* sets out an evidence routine in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top), and covers faked or doctored evidence in [Synthetic "Evidence"](/feminine/synthetic-evidence#core-concept).

## Ten minutes on a Sunday
Once a week, spend ten minutes with your record. Check each entry has a date. Add any screenshots you saved during the week. Then close it and do something you enjoy. A record kept in small, regular pieces is easier to live with than one written in a rush before court.

=== systems-abuse
title: Systems abuse and litigation abuse
question: What is litigation abuse, and how do I prove it in family court?
seo: Litigation Abuse in Family Court: Signs and What to Do
short: Litigation abuse is using the legal system to keep control over a former partner: repeated filings, delays, false complaints and endless applications that drain your time, money and peace. Courts can limit it. You prove it by showing the pattern, with dates, outcomes and costs.
---
The court was supposed to settle things. Instead it became the place where the fight continues. Every few months there's a new application, a new allegation, a new hearing. Each one costs you money, time off work and sleep.

## What is litigation abuse?
Litigation abuse is when someone uses the courts to harass, control or wear down another person. People also call it legal abuse, abusive litigation or paper abuse. When it spreads to other agencies too, it's sometimes called systems abuse.

It can look like:

- repeated applications about the same issue, often with little basis
- applications timed around holidays, exams, your new job or your health
- delay: missing deadlines, asking for adjournments, refusing to share documents
- false or exaggerated allegations to police, child services or the court
- complaints to your employer, landlord, regulator or licensing body
- refusing to settle, so everything goes to a full hearing
- representing themselves so they can question you directly, where that's allowed

The Decoder covers this as [legal abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse), [administrative escalation](/playbook/administrative-escalation) when it spreads to other agencies, and [legal and administrative threats](/playbook/legal-administrative-threats).

> If the legal system itself feels like part of what's happening to you, you may be right.

## Is litigation abuse coercive control?
It can be. Many researchers and services see litigation abuse as one of the main ways coercive control continues after separation. Some laws now name it: in England and Wales, the official guidance on controlling or coercive behaviour lists using legal proceedings as a form of it. The criminal law rarely punishes it directly, but family courts can take it into account.

## How do I prove litigation abuse in family court?
By showing the pattern, plainly:

- **A timeline of every application,** complaint and report: date, what was claimed, the outcome.
- **The outcomes:** how many were dismissed, withdrawn or found to have no basis.
- **The costs:** legal fees, time off work, travel.
- **The timing:** what was happening in your life when each one landed.
- **The effect on the children,** if any: missed school, distress around hearings.

Present it calmly and with numbers: "Since the final order in March 2024, there have been seven applications. Six were dismissed. All seven were filed within two weeks of a school holiday." [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows how to build this.

## Can a court stop repeated applications?
Yes, in many places:

- **England and Wales:** family courts can make a barring order under section 91(14) of the Children Act 1989, so a parent needs permission before making new applications. The Domestic Abuse Act 2021 made clear these orders can be used to protect victims of abuse. Civil courts can also make civil restraint orders.
- **United States:** many states let courts declare someone a vexatious litigant, so they need permission to file. Some states, including Georgia and Washington, have laws aimed at abusive litigation in family cases.
- **Australia:** family courts can restrict proceedings considered vexatious.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] vexatious litigant family court."

Ask your lawyer whether it's worth applying for one.

## What is legal abuse syndrome?
"Legal abuse syndrome" is a term made popular by Karin Huffer, a US family therapist, in a 1995 book. It describes the stress, anxiety and sense of injustice that long or abusive court cases can cause.

It isn't a medical diagnosis, and it isn't in the DSM-5, the main psychiatric manual, despite some claims online. That doesn't mean what you feel isn't real. Years of filings, hearings and waiting can cause symptoms that look a lot like trauma: dread when the post arrives, poor sleep, trouble thinking straight.

You can't usually sue someone for "legal abuse syndrome" itself. What you can do is ask the court to deal with the litigation: costs orders, limits on new applications, or a finding that the case is vexatious. The section above on stopping repeated applications explains how. For the effects on your health, see [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## The common forms
Litigation and systems abuse can take many shapes:

- repeated applications to change arrangements for the children
- appeals of every decision
- refusing to agree anything, so every small matter goes to court
- deliberate delays, missed deadlines and last-minute adjournments
- complaints about you to police, social services, your employer or a professional regulator
- reports to child protection that turn out to have no basis
- piles of documents served at the last minute

Any one of these can happen in a normal case. A run of them, aimed at you, is a pattern.

## Complaints to your employer or regulator
If you work in a regulated job, like nursing, teaching or law, the person you left may complain about you to your regulator. Tell your employer or regulator early that there's a history of domestic abuse and an ongoing court case. Keep a copy of everything. Most regulators see malicious complaints and know how to handle them. A union or professional body can help.

## Orders that limit repeated applications
- **England and Wales:** courts can make a section 91(14) order under the Children Act 1989, which stops someone applying again without permission. Since the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, the law makes clear these orders can be used to protect a child or a parent from harm caused by repeated applications.
- **United States:** many states have vexatious litigant rules that require someone to get a judge's permission before filing new cases.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] restrict repeated family court applications."

Ask your lawyer, or raise it yourself at a hearing: "I'm asking the court to consider an order stopping further applications without permission."

## A one-page pattern summary for the court
Judges have little time. A one-page table can show what pages of statements can't:

| Date | Application or action | Outcome | Effect on the children or me |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | Application to change contact | Dismissed | Three days off work |
| Mar | Complaint to school | No action | Child questioned at school |
| May | Appeal | Refused | Legal costs |

Ask whether you can file it with your statement.

## Keeping your costs and energy down
- One folder, physical or digital, for everything, in date order.
- One list of deadlines, checked every Monday.
- Answer what the court asks, no more.
- Use free legal help lines between hearings.
- Protect some time each week that has nothing to do with the case.

## What helps while it's happening?
- **A lawyer who has seen this before,** and who treats each filing as part of a pattern rather than a fresh crisis. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- **Respond to every deadline.** Missing one can hand them a win.
- **Don't match them.** Counter-applications can make you both look high-conflict.
- **Pace yourself.** This is often long. Plan your energy as if it will be.
- **Ask about protections in court**, so you don't have to face them alone. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* calls it "paper terrorism" in its chapter on [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept), and shows how paperwork becomes a trap in [The Administrative Cage](/feminine/the-administrative-cage#core-concept).

## When the applications kept coming
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

In eighteen months, Fiona's ex made five applications about the children, appealed twice and complained to her professional body. Each one cost her time off work and nights of sleep. Her new solicitor asked her to make a one-page table: date, application, outcome, effect. At the next hearing, the judge read the table and made an order stopping further applications without permission.

It didn't undo the eighteen months. It did give her the next two years back.

## Make the table this week
Even if you have no hearing coming up, start the one-page table now. It's much easier to keep up than to build in a hurry.

=== solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money
title: Legal help with little or no money
question: Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?
seo: Free Legal Help for Domestic Violence and Abuse
short: Free legal help for domestic violence exists in most countries: legal aid, domestic abuse legal services, law school clinics, free advice lines and court help desks. Many people who qualify never ask. Call one, say you've experienced domestic abuse, and ask what they can offer.
---
You look up a family lawyer's fees, see the hourly rate, and close the page. The cost of a lawyer stops many people from asking for protections they're entitled to. You may have more options than you think, and none of them are well advertised.

## Where can I get free legal help for domestic violence?
Check each of these, one at a time.

**Legal aid.** Government-funded legal help for people on lower incomes. Many countries give priority or extra eligibility to people who have experienced domestic abuse.
- **England and Wales:** legal aid for family cases is usually available if you pass the means test and can show evidence of domestic abuse. Evidence can be a protective order, a police report, or a letter from a doctor or a domestic abuse service.
- **Scotland:** apply through the Scottish Legal Aid Board.
- **United States:** legal aid organisations in every state help with protective orders and family law. LawHelp.org lists them by state.
- **Canada:** each province has a legal aid plan, and many prioritise family violence cases.
- **Australia:** Legal Aid commissions in each state, community legal centres, and specialist family violence legal services.
- **Ireland:** the Legal Aid Board provides family law services.

**Domestic abuse legal services.** Many domestic abuse organisations have their own lawyers or advocates, or refer to lawyers who specialise in abuse.

**Law school clinics.** Supervised students give free help, often with family law and protective orders.

**Pro bono schemes.** Some law firms take on cases for free. Ask your local bar association or law society.

**Court help desks and self-help centres.** Many courts have free help with forms and procedures, and some domestic abuse services provide court support workers.

**Free advice lines.** In the UK, Rights of Women runs a family law advice line for women. Many countries have similar lines.

> When you call, say "I've experienced domestic abuse." It can open doors that a general enquiry doesn't.

## What do I say on the first call?
> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I need help with [a protective order / custody / divorce / a court date on (date)]. My income is about [amount]. What can you offer, and what do I need to bring?"

Have ready:

- any court papers and deadlines
- your income and benefits details
- any evidence of abuse you already have, such as police reference numbers or your record. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What if I don't qualify for free help?
- **Limited-scope help:** some lawyers will do one part of a case, like a single hearing or checking your forms, for a fixed fee.
- **A one-off consultation:** often cheaper, and enough to understand your options.
- **Representing yourself,** with support from a court help desk or a domestic abuse advocate.

## What if I have to represent myself?
Many people do. Some things help:

- Read the court's guide for people without lawyers. Most courts publish one.
- Keep everything in a folder, in date order.
- Ask for protections if you're afraid of the other side. In England and Wales, an abuser can't cross-examine you in person in family court. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).
- Bring a supporter. Many courts allow a McKenzie friend or support person.

## How do I find the right lawyer?
Look for someone with experience of domestic abuse and coercive control, not just family law. Ask:

> **Say:** "How much of your work involves domestic abuse or coercive control? How would you approach repeated applications from the other side?"

See [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) for why this matters, and [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) for other places that can help.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists legal help among the first steps in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

Other questions worth asking before you hire anyone:

- How would you present a pattern, not just incidents?
- How do you charge, and roughly what will this cost?
- How quickly do you reply, and how will you keep me informed?
- What will you need from me?

If they talk over you, dismiss the abuse, or push you to settle without hearing why you're worried, keep looking if you can.

## Legal aid when there's been domestic abuse
- **England and Wales:** legal aid for family cases, like child arrangements and finances, usually needs evidence of domestic abuse. Accepted evidence includes letters from a doctor, police, a domestic abuse service or a social worker, a protective order or a relevant conviction. You'll also need to pass a means test. For protective orders, the financial rules can be more flexible.
- **Scotland and Northern Ireland:** have their own legal aid systems. Ask a solicitor or the legal aid board.
- **United States:** there's no general right to a lawyer in family cases. Legal aid organisations, law school clinics, bar association referral services and domestic violence legal advocates can help. Many domestic violence programs have lawyers or can refer you.
- **Australia and Canada:** legal aid commissions and community legal centres offer help, often with priority for family violence.

## Keeping costs down
- Arrive organised: a timeline, copies of key documents, your questions in writing.
- Send one clear email with several questions, not many short ones.
- Ask what you can do yourself, like collecting documents or drafting your timeline.
- Ask for an estimate before each stage.

## Help with only part of a case
Some lawyers offer limited-scope or "unbundled" help: advice for an hour, drafting one document, or representing you at one hearing, while you handle the rest. It can make expert help affordable for the parts that matter most.

## If your lawyer isn't listening
Tell them plainly in writing: "I'm worried that the history of abuse isn't being presented to the court. I'd like to discuss how we'll do that." If nothing changes, you can change lawyers. In most places you can also complain to the lawyer's firm, then to a regulator.

## How Ahmed found help with no money
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Ahmed had no savings and a court date in three weeks. He called a domestic abuse helpline, which told him about a legal advice clinic at the local law school. The clinic helped him fill in the forms and explained what would happen at the hearing. His support worker wrote a letter confirming the abuse, which made him eligible for legal aid for the next stage.

He didn't have a lawyer for every hearing. He had help at the moments that mattered most.

## Your first call
Call one free legal help service this week, even if your hearing is months away. Ask, "What help can I get, and what do I need to bring?"

=== family-court-honestly
title: Family court, honestly
question: Does family court recognise coercive control?
seo: Family Court and Coercive Control: An Honest Guide
short: Family courts vary a lot in how well they recognise coercive control, as opposed to physical violence. Some have made real progress; others still push for contact with both parents and miss non-physical abuse. Get advice from a lawyer who knows coercive control, and present a pattern with dates.
---
You may have heard that family court will see through them. You may have heard that it never does. Neither is reliably true. What helps is an honest picture, so you can plan for the court you'll stand in.

## Does family court recognise coercive control?
More than it used to, and less than it should.

Some places have made real changes. In England and Wales, a major government review in 2020 looked at how family courts handle abuse. It found that courts often minimised domestic abuse, put too much weight on contact with both parents, and didn't hear victims well. Reforms followed, including the Domestic Abuse Act 2021. In Canada, the Divorce Act now lists coercive and controlling behaviour as family violence that courts must consider. Several US states, including California, Hawaii and Connecticut, have written coercive control into their family or protective order laws. Australia's family law changed in 2023 to put children's safety first.

But practice varies by country, by court and by judge. Many courts still focus mostly on physical violence, and patterns of control without violence can be missed. The Decoder's page on [children used as leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage) and [legal abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse) describe what courts often fail to see.

> Neither "the court will understand" nor "the court never believes victims" is safe to assume. Find out how your court works.

## Who will I meet?
Depending on where you are:

- **A judge or magistrates,** who make the decisions.
- **A child's representative:** a guardian, a children's lawyer or an independent children's lawyer, who speaks for the child's interests.
- **A family court adviser or evaluator,** who interviews parents and children and writes a report. In England and Wales this is often Cafcass; in the US, a custody evaluator; in Australia, a family consultant.
- **A mediator,** who tries to help parents agree.

Each of them is trying to work out what's best for the children. A calm, clear account of the pattern, with the effect on the children, helps them most.

## Should I go to mediation?
Not necessarily. Mediation assumes two people can negotiate fairly. Where there's coercive control, that often isn't true.

- **England and Wales:** you usually have to attend a mediation meeting before applying to court, but domestic abuse is an exemption.
- **United States:** many states let you opt out of mandatory mediation where there's been domestic violence.
- **Australia:** family dispute resolution is usually required, but there are exemptions for family violence.

If you do mediate, ask for separate rooms ("shuttle mediation"), and say you don't feel safe negotiating directly.

> **Say:** "There's a history of domestic abuse and I don't feel able to negotiate safely in the same room. I'd like an exemption, or shuttle mediation."

## How do I present a pattern rather than incidents?
Courts hear a lot of competing claims. What stands out is a calm summary backed by records:

- **Start with the pattern in one or two sentences.** "Since we separated in May 2023, my ex has used the children's handovers and the courts to keep control. There have been 23 late or missed handovers, 140 messages outside the agreed app, and five applications, four dismissed."
- **Then the effect on the children.** Courts decide for children, so tie each point to them.
- **Then the evidence,** organised by type.
- **Avoid labels.** Describe behaviour, not diagnoses. See [Is my ex a narcissist?](ch:is-my-ex-a-narcissist).

[What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how) shows how to build the timeline.

## What about "parental alienation"?
You may be accused of turning the children against the other parent, especially if a child is reluctant to see them. This is one of the most disputed areas in family law.

What's agreed: a parent can wrongly turn a child against the other parent, and that harms children. What's disputed: how often it happens, how it should be judged, and whether claims of alienation are being used to discredit parents who raise abuse. Research in the US has found that when mothers alleged abuse and fathers then claimed alienation, courts often disbelieved the abuse.

If you're accused:

- stay calm and focus on the children
- show that you support the children's relationship with the other parent wherever it's safe
- explain the child's reluctance with facts, not your interpretation
- get a lawyer who knows this area well

See also [When your child is pulled into it](ch:when-your-child-is-pulled-in) and [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## How often do narcissists win in court?
People ask this a lot. There's no reliable figure, and the word "narcissist" won't be used in court. What's true is that charming, confident people can do well in short hearings, and that calm, organised parents with clear records do better than anxious ones with long lists of grievances. You can't control how they come across. You can control how prepared you are.

## How decisions about children are made
Family courts make decisions based on the child's welfare or "best interests." The details differ by country, but courts usually consider:

- the child's needs, and their wishes depending on age and understanding
- any harm the child has suffered or is at risk of
- each parent's ability to meet the child's needs
- the effect of any change

Domestic abuse is meant to be central to that assessment. In England and Wales, courts follow guidance called Practice Direction 12J when abuse is raised. In many US states, a finding of domestic violence affects custody decisions, sometimes with a presumption against custody for the abusive parent. Ask your lawyer or advocate how this works where you live.

## Reports and evaluators
Before deciding, courts often ask a professional to report.

- **England and Wales:** a Cafcass officer may write a "section 7" report after speaking to both parents and, often, the children.
- **United States:** courts may appoint a custody evaluator or a guardian ad litem.
- **Elsewhere:** family consultants or social workers play a similar role.

When you speak to them, be calm and factual, bring your record, and focus on the children's experience. Say clearly what you're worried about and why.

## Protections in court
You can ask for protections if you're frightened of the other person:

- separate waiting rooms and different arrival times
- a screen in the courtroom, or giving evidence by video
- in England and Wales, people accused of abuse can't cross-examine the person they're accused of abusing in person. The court appoints a lawyer to ask the questions instead.

Ask before the hearing. You don't need to be sure you'll need them.

## Contact centres and supervised contact
If there are concerns about a parent's behaviour, courts may order time with the children to be supervised, or to take place at a contact centre. In the UK, accredited centres are listed by the National Association of Child Contact Centres. In the US, search "supervised visitation center [your area]." Handovers through a centre can also keep parents apart.

## If the decision goes against you
- Ask your lawyer to explain the reasons, in writing if possible.
- Appeals usually need permission and have short time limits, often 21 days in England and Wales. Get advice quickly.
- If circumstances change later, like new incidents, you may be able to apply to change the order.
- Look after yourself first. A bad decision can feel like being abused again. Call a helpline that night, not a lawyer at midnight.

## What can I control?
- How well documented the pattern is.
- How calm and child-focused you are.
- Whether you have a lawyer who understands coercive control. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- Whether you ask for protections at court. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).
- Whether you meet every deadline.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* shows how systems and paperwork get turned into weapons in [The Administrative Cage](/feminine/the-administrative-cage#core-concept).

## What can I share about the case?
Be careful before you post, publish or pass on anything from family court. The rules are strict, and breaking them can be serious.

- **England and Wales:** cases about children are private. Publishing information from them, or sharing court documents with people outside a permitted list, can be contempt of court. You can usually share information with your lawyer, a McKenzie friend, a doctor or therapist, and a domestic abuse adviser, for advice and support. Posting about the case on social media, even without names, can break the rules. Identifying the children is never allowed.
- **United States:** rules vary by state. Family court records are sometimes sealed, and judges can make orders limiting what parents say publicly. Ask your lawyer before you post anything.
- **Elsewhere:** assume family court matters are private until a lawyer tells you otherwise.

You can still talk about your own experience of abuse in general terms with friends and support services. If you want to speak out publicly, get legal advice first.

## What helped in Carla's case
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Carla went into family court expecting not to be believed. She brought a two-page timeline with dates and references to evidence: police logs, school records, messages. She asked for a separate waiting room and a screen. When the Cafcass officer interviewed her, she spoke about her daughter's experience: the nightmares, the fear before handovers. She didn't call her ex names.

The court ordered contact at a supervised centre for six months, with a review. It wasn't everything she hoped for. It was safe, and it was based on what had happened.

## What you can control, in one line
Your preparation, your calm and your focus on the children. Spend your energy there.

=== your-day-in-court
title: Your day in court
question: What should I expect at a family court hearing with an abusive ex?
seo: What to Expect at a Family Court Hearing With an Abusive Ex
short: What to expect at a family court hearing: a waiting area, often a short hearing, and a judge who has read some of the papers. Prepare a folder, ask in advance for protections like a separate waiting room or a screen, bring a supporter, and plan how you'll arrive and leave safely.
---
The date is set. You've been dreading it for weeks, and you want to know what to expect at a family court hearing. Here's the day, step by step, so at least the practical parts hold no surprises.

## What should I do before the hearing?
**Ask for protections early.** Contact the court, your lawyer, or a court support worker at least a week before, and ask what's available:

- a separate waiting room
- a separate entrance, or arriving and leaving at different times
- a screen so you can't see the other person, or giving evidence by video link
- in England and Wales, victims of domestic abuse are automatically eligible for these measures in family court, and the other person can't cross-examine you in person

> **Say:** "There's a history of domestic abuse in this case and I'm frightened of the other party. Which protections can I have at the hearing, and how do I request them?"

**Prepare a folder.**
- every court document, in date order
- your timeline summary on top. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- copies of key evidence, flagged
- a list of the points you want to make, in order
- a notebook and pen

**Plan the day.** How you'll get there, where you'll park, who's coming with you, and how you'll leave without meeting them.

**Bring a supporter.** A friend, a family member, or a domestic abuse court advocate. Ask the court in advance whether they can come into the hearing.

**Wear something comfortable that you'd wear to a work meeting.**

The [court day checklist](ch:court-day-checklist) puts this on one page.

## What happens on the day?
- **Arrive early.** Tell security or the usher you've asked for a separate waiting room.
- **You may wait a long time.** Bring water, a snack, something to read, and your charger.
- **Lawyers may negotiate outside the room.** You don't have to agree to anything you're not sure about. Ask for time.
- **The hearing itself may be short,** sometimes 20 or 30 minutes. The judge has usually read some of the papers.

The Decoder's page on [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes looks, gestures and comments meant to unsettle you. If anything happens at court, tell your lawyer or the usher straight away, and write it down afterwards.

## How do I speak in the hearing?
- Speak slowly, and address the judge as the court tells you to.
- Answer the question asked, and stop.
- Stick to facts, dates and the effect on the children.
- If you don't understand a question, say so.
- If you freeze or cry, that's all right. Ask for a moment, take a breath, look at your notes.
- Don't respond to anything the other side says directly to you. Speak only to the judge.

> You don't have to be perfect. You have to be clear, calm enough, and focused on the children.

## What if they're representing themselves?
That can mean they're allowed to speak a lot, or try to question you. In England and Wales, they can't cross-examine you in person if there's evidence of domestic abuse; the court appoints a lawyer to ask their questions instead. Elsewhere, ask the court in advance what protections apply.

## What to wear and bring
Wear something clean and comfortable that you'd wear to an important meeting. Bring:

- your court papers and any order
- your timeline and record
- a pen and paper for notes
- water and a snack
- something calming: a photo, a smooth stone, a card with a breathing reminder
- your phone, charged, on silent

## Getting there safely
- Arrange childcare well in advance.
- Plan your route, and if you're worried, ask the court about a separate entrance or waiting area.
- Bring someone with you if you can. Many domestic abuse services offer court support workers.
- Don't wait outside where you might meet them. Go straight in.

## If you're overwhelmed in the hearing
It's okay to ask for a moment.

> **Say:** "I'm sorry, could I have a moment?" or "Could I have some water, please?"

Breathe slowly, feet on the floor. If you're giving evidence and you're very distressed, you can ask for a short break. Judges see this often.

## Saying "I don't understand"
Legal language can be confusing, especially under stress. You're allowed to ask.

> **Say:** "I'm sorry, I didn't understand that. Could you explain it in a different way?"

Never agree to something you don't understand. If you're representing yourself, ask the court staff or a help desk to explain the next steps before you leave.

## The evening after a hard result
Plan something for that evening before you go: a friend, a meal, a phone call. Don't read their messages. Don't make big decisions. Write down what happened while you remember it, then put it away. Tomorrow you can ask for advice on what comes next.

## What happens after?
- **Get a copy of any order** before you leave, or ask when it will arrive.
- **Read it carefully.** Check dates, times and conditions.
- **Give copies** to the school or anyone else who needs them.
- **Plan for the next few days.** Court dates are among the times when risk can rise. See [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).
- **Look after yourself.** Most people feel wrecked after a hearing, whatever the result. Plan something gentle.

If the case continues, see [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly) for the longer view.

## Your court-day card
Write these on a small card and keep it in your pocket:

- my case number and courtroom
- my support person's number
- three points I must say
- "Could you explain that again, please?"
- "Could I have a moment?"
- where I'm going straight afterwards

=== divorce-and-separation-proceedings
title: Divorce and separation proceedings
question: What should I know about divorcing a narcissist or an abusive spouse?
seo: Divorcing a Narcissist or Abusive Spouse: What to Expect
short: Divorcing a narcissist or any controlling spouse often takes longer and costs more than an ordinary divorce, because delay, hidden money and conflict can be tactics. Get advice from a lawyer who understands abuse, gather financial records early, ask for exemptions from mediation if needed, and expect it to take time.
---
A divorce is hard. A divorce from someone who wants to keep control can feel endless. Here's what tends to be different, and what helps.

## What is different about divorcing a narcissist?
Whatever word fits the person, controlling spouses often:

- **delay:** missing deadlines, refusing to sign, asking for adjournments
- **hide money:** moving it, undervaluing a business, "forgetting" accounts
- **fight everything:** refusing reasonable offers so every issue needs a hearing
- **use the children** as bargaining chips in the financial case. See [Children as the channel](ch:children-as-the-channel).
- **play for sympathy:** presenting as the calm, wronged party

The Decoder's pages on [financial control](/playbook/financial-control) and [time control](/playbook/time-control) describe the moves.

> Delay is often a tactic. Plan as if it will be.

## How does divorce work, broadly?
The details differ by country and state, but the main stages are similar:

1. **Starting the divorce.** Many places now allow no-fault divorce, so you don't have to prove what they did. England and Wales introduced no-fault divorce in 2022.
2. **Arrangements for the children.** Agreed between you, through mediation, or decided by a court.
3. **The financial settlement.** Both sides disclose their finances; then the money, property and pensions are divided by agreement or by a court.
4. **The final order,** which ends the marriage legally.

Separating from an unmarried partner usually has no divorce stage, but property and children may still need court orders.

## What about hidden money?
Both sides usually have to give full financial disclosure, under oath or with a statement of truth. Gather what you can early:

- bank, savings and credit card statements
- pay slips, tax returns, business accounts
- pension statements
- mortgage, property and loan documents
- photos of anything valuable in the home

If you think money is hidden, tell your lawyer. Courts can order more disclosure, and some can penalise someone who hides assets. A forensic accountant may help in larger cases.

## Do I have to go to mediation?
Not always. Where there's domestic abuse, many places offer exemptions, and mediation may not be safe. See the mediation section of [Family court, honestly](ch:family-court-honestly).

In England and Wales, you usually have to attend a mediation information meeting before applying to court about children or money, but domestic abuse is an exemption, with evidence. In many US states, judges can waive mediation where there's domestic violence. If you do mediate, ask for separate rooms or shuttle mediation, and never agree to anything on the spot.

## How do I keep costs down?
- Use free and low-cost legal help where you can: [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).
- Prepare well, so your lawyer's time goes further: organised documents, a clear timeline, a list of questions.
- Don't use your lawyer for emotional support. A friend, a domestic abuse advocate or a therapist costs less.
- Ask whether parts of the case can be settled separately.
- If they keep filing, see [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse).

## Is coercive control grounds for divorce?
In many places you don't need grounds at all. England and Wales, Scotland, Australia, Canada and every US state now allow some form of no-fault divorce. You can end the marriage without proving what they did.

That doesn't make the abuse irrelevant. Coercive control can matter for arrangements for the children, protective orders, and sometimes money. In a few US states, fault grounds such as cruelty still exist and can be used. Ask a family lawyer where you live whether raising it helps your case. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

## How long will it take?
Longer than an ordinary divorce, usually. Some take months; some take years. You can't control their delays. You can control your own deadlines, your records and your energy.

## What about the house, the car and the accounts?
The divorce settles who gets what. You'll still need to change names, remove yourself from accounts and update beneficiaries. See [Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork](ch:untangling-the-paperwork).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* shows how money and paperwork become a cage in [The Administrative Cage](/feminine/the-administrative-cage#core-concept).

## The steps in England and Wales
Since April 2022, divorce in England and Wales has been no-fault and mostly online:

1. **Application.** One of you, or both together, applies online. You don't have to give reasons or blame anyone.
2. **The other person is notified.** They can respond, but they can only dispute the divorce on very narrow grounds, such as the marriage not being valid.
3. **Wait 20 weeks,** then apply for a conditional order.
4. **Wait six weeks and a day,** then apply for the final order, which ends the marriage.

Money and the children are dealt with separately, by agreement or by court order. Get financial arrangements made into a court order; a divorce alone doesn't end financial claims.

Elsewhere, search "[your country or state] how to file for divorce." Most court websites have step-by-step guides.

## Financial disclosure
Before agreeing money, both sides should share full details of income, savings, debts, property and pensions. In England and Wales this is usually done on a form called Form E. If you suspect hidden assets, say so and ask for documents. Agreeing without full disclosure can leave you worse off, and is hard to undo.

## Pensions
Pensions are often one of the biggest assets in a marriage, and one of the most overlooked. Courts can split them, through pension sharing or similar orders. Ask for a valuation. If the person you left had control of the money, the pension may be bigger than you think.

## If they won't respond
Some people ignore divorce papers to delay or to keep control. In most places the process can go ahead without them. Courts have ways to prove they were sent the papers, and to continue if they don't take part. Ask the court or a lawyer about the next step, and keep proof of every attempt to notify them.

## Negotiating safely
- You don't have to be in the same room. Ask for shuttle mediation, where the mediator moves between rooms, or for lawyer-to-lawyer negotiation.
- Put offers and agreements in writing.
- Don't agree anything on the spot, especially under pressure. Say: "I'll consider it and reply in writing."
- If they threaten you during negotiations, record it and tell your lawyer.

## How Mei kept the divorce from becoming the abuse
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Mei's husband refused to answer letters about the divorce, then sent long emails accusing her of ruining his life. Her solicitor handled all communication. Mei applied for the divorce on her own; the court confirmed he had received the papers. When it came to money, she asked for full financial disclosure, which showed a pension and an account she hadn't known about. Negotiation happened in separate rooms, with a mediator moving between them.

It took eleven months. She never had to sit across a table from him.

## Religious divorce
If you married in a religious ceremony, you may need a religious divorce as well as a civil one, such as a Jewish get or an Islamic divorce. Some people refuse to grant a religious divorce as a way to keep control. In England and Wales, for some faiths, a court can delay the final civil divorce until the religious divorce is given. Government guidance on domestic abuse recognises withholding a religious divorce as a form of abuse, and it has formed part of at least one conviction for controlling behaviour. Advice services within your faith community can help. Ask your lawyer early.
