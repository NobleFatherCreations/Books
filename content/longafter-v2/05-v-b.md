=== police-and-protective-orders
title: Protective orders
question: How does a protective order work, and is it worth getting?
seo: How Does a Protective Order Work? A Plain Guide
short: A protective order is a court order telling someone to stay away from you and stop contacting you. Breaking it can lead to arrest. How a protective order works depends on where you live, but most places offer emergency, temporary and longer-term orders. It's a real tool with real limits, so pair it with a safety plan.
---
If the person you left keeps messaging you, turning up or threatening you, a protective order can draw a legal line around you. In many places, crossing it becomes a matter for the police.

If you're in danger right now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## How does a protective order work?
A protective order is a court order that tells someone to stop contacting you and stay away from you. It can also cover your home, your work and your children's school. If they break it, the police can act, and in many places the breach is a crime in itself. The exact rules depend on where you live.

Depending on where you live, an order can:

- ban contact, including messages, calls and contact passed on through other people
- keep them a set distance from you, your home, your work or your children's school
- order them out of a home you shared
- in some places, deal with temporary custody, firearms, or a shared pet

The names differ from place to place:

| Where | Common names |
|---|---|
| United States | protective order, restraining order, order of protection |
| England and Wales | non-molestation order, occupation order; domestic abuse protection order (being introduced) |
| Scotland | interdict, non-harassment order |
| Ireland | safety order, protection order, barring order, emergency barring order |
| Canada | emergency protection order (by province), peace bond |
| Australia | AVO (NSW), IVO (Victoria), DVO (Queensland), FVRO (WA), and others by state |

Search "[your country or state] protective order domestic abuse" to find your local version.

## What types are there?
Most places have three levels:

1. **Emergency orders.** Made fast, sometimes on the same day by the police or a judge, often without the other person there. They last days.
2. **Temporary or interim orders.** These last until a full hearing, usually a few weeks.
3. **Longer-term or final orders.** Made after a hearing where both sides can be heard. They often last months or years, and can be renewed.

## How do I apply?
There are usually three ways in. The details vary by place.

- **Through a court.** Most courts have forms and a help desk for protective orders. A domestic abuse service can sit with you and help fill them in, and in many places there's no fee.
- **Through the police.** In some places the police can apply for you, or put an emergency order in place themselves.
- **Through a lawyer.** Help is often free for protective orders. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

## What do I write in the application?
Write it for a busy stranger who needs to see, fast, why you're scared:

- The most recent incident, in plain words, with the date.
- The pattern: earlier incidents, with dates. This is where your record earns its keep. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- Any threats, weapons, strangling or stalking. Say so plainly, because these carry weight. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).
- What you're afraid will happen next.
- What you're asking the court to order.

Stick to what happened. Leave out labels, and old history that doesn't touch your safety.

## What happens at the hearing?
It's usually short. The judge reads your application, may ask you a few questions, and hears from the other person if they turn up. Bring your evidence and a supporter. Ask for protections, such as a separate waiting room. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).

They may deny everything, or apply for an order against you. Applying doesn't make them right. Stay calm and stick to your dates. See [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## How long does a protective order last?
It depends on the type and on where you live. Emergency orders last days, temporary orders weeks, and longer orders anywhere from months to several years.

Write down the end date. Then set a reminder for a month before it, so you can ask for a renewal if you still need one.

## Do protective orders work?
They help many people. They also have limits.

**What an order can do:** give the police a legal basis to act, create a formal record, and put off many people, especially those who care about consequences.

**What it can't do:** physically stop someone who's determined to break it. Police responses also vary by place and by officer.

> An order is a real tool, and it can't physically stop anyone. Plan for the gap between a breach and the response in your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

## What do I do if they break it?
1. If you're in danger, call your emergency number and say there's an order.
2. Write down what happened, when and where, and who saw it.
3. Keep the messages, call logs and photos.
4. Report every breach, however small, and ask for a reference number each time.

A pattern of recorded breaches is often what gets a stronger response. The Decoder's page on [Intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes the borderline behaviour people use to test an order.

If they ever call your reports petty, or say you're using the police to harass them, stay calm. Reporting a breach is what the order is for, and each report is simply a dated fact.

## The main orders in the UK
- **Non-molestation order (England, Wales, Northern Ireland):** stops someone using or threatening violence, harassing or intimidating you. Breaking it is a crime.
- **Occupation order:** decides who can live in the home. See [Still under the same roof](ch:still-under-the-same-roof).
- **Domestic Violence Protection Notice and Order:** short-term protection that police can put in place quickly after an incident.
- **Domestic Abuse Protection Orders:** a newer, more flexible order introduced by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, being brought in across areas from late 2024. It can include requirements like tagging or attending a programme.
- **Scotland:** interdicts, non-harassment orders and exclusion orders.

## Serving the order
An order usually only takes effect once the other person knows about it. This is called service. Ask the court how and when it will be served. Don't serve it yourself. Until you know it's been served, keep following your safety plan as if the order isn't there yet.

## Telling the people who need to know
Give a copy to, or at least tell, the people who may need to act on it:

- the children's school or nursery
- your employer and building security
- neighbours you trust
- your local police station, if they don't already have it

Keep a copy with you, one at home somewhere safe, and a photo on your phone.

## Changing or renewing an order
Orders often last for a fixed time. If you still need protection as the end date gets close, apply to extend it before it runs out. If things change, like a move or new incidents, you can apply to vary it.

If they ask you to "just ignore" the order, don't agree. Only a court can change it. If you want it changed, a local domestic abuse service can help you apply.

## Orders across borders
- **United States:** under federal law, a protective order made in one state must be enforced in every other state, as well as tribal lands and territories.
- **European Union:** protection orders can be recognised in other EU countries.
- **Elsewhere:** if you're moving country, ask a lawyer before you go whether your order will be recognised, and how to register it.

## Where should I keep the order?
On you, at home, and on your phone, with copies for anyone who might need to call the police for you. *The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists protective orders, whatever they're called in your country, among your core documents in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## What happened when the order was broken
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Sara lives in England, where breaking a non-molestation order is a crime. Three weeks after her order was served, her ex sent a message through her cousin. Sara screenshotted the cousin's message, wrote down the time and called the police, giving the order's reference number. The officer recorded a breach. When it happened again, he was arrested.

Sara had worried that reporting a "small" breach would look petty. Her support worker told her that small breaches, reported every time, are how orders keep their strength.

=== police-and-reporting
title: Police and reporting
question: Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?
seo: Can I Report Post-Separation Abuse to the Police?
short: Yes, you can report post-separation abuse to the police. Threats, stalking, harassment, criminal damage, breaking an order and, in some places, coercive control are crimes. Report early, describe the pattern with dates, and ask for a reference number every time.
---
Does it have to be something dramatic before you can call the police? Plenty of people assume so, and wait for the big moment. But a lot of what happens after a breakup comes in smaller pieces: the twentieth message, the car parked across the road, the threat dressed up as a joke. Some of those smaller pieces are crimes.

If you're in danger now, call your emergency number.

## Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?
Yes. You can report:

- threats, including ones sent by message
- assault, of any kind
- stalking and harassment. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
- criminal damage to your home, car or belongings
- breaking a protective order. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
- sharing, or threatening to share, intimate images. See [Intimate images and online threats](ch:intimate-images-and-online-threats).
- in some places, controlling or coercive behaviour itself, including after you've separated. See [What post-separation abuse is](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse).

## What do I say so they hear the pattern?
Officers often see one incident at a time. Your job is to hand them the whole picture in a few sentences:

> **Say:** "I'm reporting [today's incident]. It's part of a pattern since we separated in [month and year]. I have a record of [number] incidents, including [threats / turning up / messages after the order]. I'd like this recorded as domestic abuse."

Bring your record and evidence, or offer it. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What should I ask for?
- **A reference or incident number.** Every time, even when it feels like overkill.
- **The officer's name and badge number.**
- **What happens next,** and when you'll hear.
- **A referral to a domestic abuse service,** if you don't already have one.
- **Whether a risk assessment will be done.** Many police services use one for domestic abuse.

## What is Clare's Law?
Some places let you ask the police whether someone has a history of abuse. In England and Wales this is the Domestic Violence Disclosure Scheme, known as Clare's Law. Scotland and parts of Australia have similar schemes. It can help if you're worried about a new partner, or about the person your ex is now seeing.

## What if they make a false report about me?
Stay calm, get legal advice before any interview, and bring your record. The Decoder's page on [False Reports](/playbook/false-reports) describes the tactic, and [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser) walks you through what to do.

## What if the police response is poor?
It happens. If you were brushed off, not believed, or the report never got recorded:

- ask to speak to a supervisor, or to a specialist domestic abuse officer
- ask a domestic abuse service to advocate for you
- make a formal complaint. Most police forces have a complaints process, and many countries have an independent police complaints body.

Keep reporting. Each report adds to the record, even when it feels like you're shouting into a void.

## What happens after you report
- An officer takes your account and records it. Ask for the crime or incident reference number.
- They may ask questions to assess risk. See [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first).
- They may speak to the person you reported, arrest them, or gather more evidence first.
- In many places, a specialist domestic abuse officer or advocate will contact you.
- You should be told about decisions, such as whether there will be a charge.

## Your rights as a victim
- **England and Wales:** the Victims' Code sets out your rights. They include being kept informed, being referred to support, and asking for a review if a decision is made not to charge.
- **United States:** prosecutors' offices often have victim advocates. Many states let you sign up for notifications when an offender is released.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] victims' rights domestic abuse."

## If you don't want a prosecution
You might want the abuse on record, and to be safe, without the person being prosecuted. Tell the police that's what you want.

In many places, police and prosecutors can still go ahead if there's enough evidence without you, because domestic abuse is treated as a crime against the public too. Your safety and your wishes should still be taken into account.

## Reporting online
Many police forces let you report non-emergency crimes online. That helps if talking on the phone isn't safe, or if you'd rather not spend forty minutes on hold. Never use online reporting for an emergency.

## Complaints
If the police response was poor, you can complain to the force. In England and Wales, serious complaints can go to the Independent Office for Police Conduct. In the US, contact the department's internal affairs or professional standards unit. A domestic abuse service can help you complain.

## Do I have to report?
No. Some people don't feel safe involving the police, or have had bad experiences with them. That's your call. You can still keep a record and get support from a domestic abuse service. In many places you can also apply to a court for a protective order yourself.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* sets out how to keep evidence for systems in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## If the case goes to criminal court
If the person is charged, you may be asked to give evidence as a witness. Usually a witness care service or a victim advocate supports you, explains each step and tells you the dates.

You can ask for protections, often called special measures: a screen so you don't see the defendant, giving evidence by video link from another room, or a supporter sitting nearby. Ask early. You may be able to visit the court beforehand to see the room.

## Bail conditions and orders after the case
- **Bail conditions:** before a trial, a court can set conditions, such as no contact with you and staying away from your home. If they're broken, tell the police straight away.
- **After the case:** in England and Wales, a court can make a restraining order when someone is convicted, and in some cases even when they're found not guilty. Other countries have similar orders.

## Telling the court how it affected you
You can usually make a statement about how the crime affected you. It's called a victim personal statement in England and Wales, or a victim impact statement in the US. You can read it out, have it read for you, or give it in writing.

It's your chance to say, in your own words, what this has done to your life.

::: scene
## What a good report sounded like
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Daniela's ex had turned up outside her flat for the third time in a week. When she called the police, she had her record open, and she said:

"I'm reporting stalking by my ex-partner. Since we separated in January there have been fourteen incidents, including three times outside my home this week. I'd like it recorded as domestic abuse and stalking, and I'd like a reference number."

The officer asked for her record, and passed the case to a domestic abuse team the next day.
:::

::: action
## Your three sentences
Open your notes app and write three sentences about the pattern: when it started, how many incidents there have been, and the worst recent one. Save it where you can find it fast if you ever need to report.
:::

=== housing-and-benefits
title: Housing and benefits
question: Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?
seo: Housing Help After Domestic Abuse: Options and Rights
short: Housing help after domestic abuse can include refuge, emergency housing, priority for social housing, the right to end a lease early, and help to stay safely in your own home. Benefits and child support can give you a floor. A local domestic abuse service usually knows exactly what's available where you live.
---
Maybe you're on a friend's sofa with your life in bin bags, or in a refuge, or in a home you can't afford on one income. Money and housing are often the biggest reasons people feel stuck. There's help for the months after you leave, as well as the first few days.

## Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?
The options usually include:

- **Refuge or shelter:** safe, confidential housing, usually short term, often with support workers.
- **Emergency housing through your local council or housing authority.**
- **Priority for social housing.** In England, people made homeless by domestic abuse automatically have priority need for housing help.
- **Help to stay in your own home safely:** better locks, alarms and other changes, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.
- **Transitional housing:** longer stays with support, in some places.

Your local domestic abuse service is the best first call. They know what's free near you and how long the waits are, which saves you ringing six numbers to find out.

The Decoder's page on [Financial Dependence by Design](/playbook/financial-dependence-by-design) explains how control often leaves people without money or housing of their own. If that's where you've landed, it's what the control was built to do. It says nothing bad about you.

## Can I end my lease early?
In many places, yes:

- **United States:** many states let survivors end a lease early with notice and documentation. In federally assisted housing, the Violence Against Women Act gives protections, including emergency transfers and removing an abuser from a lease.
- **England and Wales:** options depend on your tenancy. Councils and housing associations often have domestic abuse policies, and joint tenancies can sometimes be transferred into your name.
- **Australia:** most states let victims end a tenancy early with evidence of family violence.
- **Canada:** several provinces, including Ontario and British Columbia, let survivors end a tenancy early with notice.

Search "[your state or country] break lease domestic violence", or ask a tenancy advice service.

## What benefits might help?
The rules vary by country, but look at:

- **Benefits for single parents** and people on low incomes.
- **Child support.** Use the official service, so you don't have to deal with them directly. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- **Emergency funds** from domestic abuse charities.
- **Help with legal costs.** See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

If you apply for benefits, tell the agency you've left an abusive relationship. Some have rules that help, like a break from work rules for a while, or a way to keep your address private. You may have to explain it on more than one form. Forms, sadly, do not talk to each other.

## What if my immigration status depends on my ex?
Many countries have protections for this. See the immigration section in [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

In the UK, the Migrant Victims of Domestic Abuse Concession can give short-term access to benefits while you apply to settle. In the US, VAWA lets some people apply for status without their abuser, and U visas exist for victims of certain crimes.

Other countries have their own routes. Get advice from an immigration adviser or a domestic abuse service before you contact any authority about your status.

## What are refuges and shelters like?
A refuge or shelter is a safe house for people escaping abuse. Its address is kept secret. You usually have your own room and share the kitchen and living spaces. Staff help with safety, money, housing, court and children.

Stays range from a few weeks to several months. Some take children; some take older boys only in certain cases; some take pets. To find a space, call a national domestic abuse helpline. They can search for spaces across the country.

## Homelessness help and priority need
- **England and Wales:** if you're homeless because of domestic abuse, local councils must treat you as being in "priority need" for housing, under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021. Ask the council's housing team for a homelessness assessment.
- **Scotland:** councils must help people who are homeless, with guidance of its own on domestic abuse.
- **United States:** contact your local domestic violence program and the local homelessness system. Ask about rapid rehousing and transitional housing for survivors.

## Staying put safely
If you want to stay in your home, ask about "sanctuary schemes" (UK) or similar programmes. They add extra security, like stronger doors, locks, alarms and window guards, so you can stay where you are. You may also be able to get an occupation order. See [Police and protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## Moving social housing
If you're in social or council housing, you can ask for a management transfer, or a move to a different area, for safety. Explain the risk and give them any evidence you have.

## Renting with no references
Leaving abuse can leave you with no rental history, debt or poor credit. Ask domestic abuse services about deposit schemes, rent guarantors and landlords who accept a support worker's letter. With a landlord, keep it brief: "I've recently left an abusive relationship, which is why I don't have a previous landlord reference."

## US housing protections under VAWA
In housing covered by federal programmes, like public housing and Section 8, the Violence Against Women Act protects you. You can't be evicted or denied housing because you're a victim of domestic violence. It also provides for emergency transfers. Many states add protections, like ending a lease early.

## Where do I start with housing?
Call a domestic abuse service and say:

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I need help with housing and money. Can you tell me what's available locally and help me apply?"

Then see [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) for other agencies that can help.

## From refuge to a home
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Zainab arrived at a refuge with her two children and one suitcase. Over four months, staff helped her apply for benefits, register with a doctor and get the children into a new school.

The council accepted that she was homeless because of domestic abuse and offered her a flat. A furniture charity provided beds and a cooker.

The flat was small and far from her old friends. It was also the first place in years where she chose what went on the walls.

## Ask for the assessment
If you've lost your home because of abuse, call or email your local council or housing authority today and ask for a homelessness assessment. Say clearly that domestic abuse is the reason.

=== where-to-find-the-right-body
title: Where to find the right body
question: Where do I get help after domestic abuse, and who can make my ex stop?
seo: Where to Get Help After Domestic Abuse: Who Does What
short: Where to get help after domestic abuse depends on the problem: courts for protective orders and children, the police for crimes, child support services for money, housing authorities for a home, and domestic abuse services for almost everything. This chapter shows which body can do what, and what to search to find yours.
---
Every chapter in this part has said "it depends where you live." True, and maddening when you just want an answer. This one helps you find the answer for where you live.

## Where do I get help after domestic abuse?
Start with a domestic abuse service. They know your area better than any book, this one included. They can point you to everyone else. Then use this table.

| Problem | Who can act | What to search |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate danger | Police | your emergency number |
| Threats, stalking, breaking an order | Police | "[area] police report domestic abuse" |
| Contact, distance, staying away | Family or civil court | "[area] protective order" |
| Children's arrangements | Family court | "[area] family court child arrangements" |
| Repeated court applications | Family court (barring or vexatious orders) | "[area] vexatious litigant family court" |
| Child support | Child support agency | "[area] child support enforcement" |
| Housing | Council or housing authority; refuge | "[area] domestic abuse housing help" |
| Hidden or coerced debt | Lender, credit agency, financial ombudsman | "[area] coerced debt dispute" |
| Poor police response | Police complaints body | "[area] police complaint" |
| Poor response from a public agency | Ombudsman | "[area] ombudsman complaint" |
| Online abuse, images | Platform; online safety regulator | "[area] intimate image abuse report" |
| Legal advice | Legal aid, clinics, domestic abuse legal services | "[area] legal aid domestic abuse" |

The Decoder's page on [Administrative Escalation](/playbook/administrative-escalation) shows how controlling people use agencies against you. This table is the same system, pointed the other way.

## Who can make my ex stop?
No single body can. Between them, though:

- **Courts** can order them to stay away and stop contacting you, and limit repeat applications, under the local rules. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders) and [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse).
- **Police** can arrest and charge for crimes, including breaches of orders. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).
- **Child support agencies** can take payments from wages.
- **Platforms** can remove content and accounts.

## What do I search for?
Try "[your country or state] domestic abuse support" first. National charities usually come up at the top, and they know who does what locally. The [Find help](ch:find-help) page lists the main ones for the US, UK, Canada, Australia and Ireland.

## Problem → who handles it
A few more that the table above leaves out:

| Problem | Usually handled by |
|---|---|
| Arrangements for the children | Family court, or agreement through mediation where safe |
| Benefits | Benefits agency |
| Debt in your name | Lender, then a free debt advice service |
| Your health and sleep | Doctor |
| Children's distress | School, doctor, child mental health services |
| Planning, advice and support | Domestic abuse services |

## Who to call first
Stuck on where to start? Call a domestic abuse helpline. They know which agencies work where you live. They can refer you on, or tell you what to say.

## One sheet to keep track
Agencies multiply fast, and every one of them gives you a new reference number. Keep a single page with:

- each agency's name and phone number
- the name of the person you spoke to
- your reference number
- what they said they'd do, and by when

When you call again, you can say, "I spoke to [name] on [date], reference [number]." It saves telling the whole story from the top, again.

## When agencies disagree
Sometimes the police, the court and children's services see things differently. Ask each one to put its decision in writing. Share decisions between them where you're allowed to. An advocate can help the agencies talk to each other.

## Advocates who coordinate
- **United Kingdom:** an Independent Domestic Violence Adviser (IDVA) supports people at higher risk. They attend meetings and work with police, courts and housing on your behalf.
- **United States:** domestic violence programs have advocates who help with protective orders, court, housing and safety.
- **Elsewhere:** ask a domestic abuse service for "an advocate who can help me deal with the agencies."

## What if an agency won't help?
Ask for the decision in writing, and ask how to complain or appeal. Then ask a domestic abuse advocate to contact them for you. Agencies often respond differently once an advocate is involved.

## What advocates can and can't do
Domestic abuse advocates are among the most useful people you'll meet. Knowing their limits saves you from being let down:

- They can explain options, help you plan, come to meetings, and speak to agencies with your permission.
- They usually can't give legal advice, make the police or a court act, or guarantee housing.
- They can tell you who can do those things, and help you ask in the right way.

## When Sean didn't know where to start
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Sean had six problems at once: threatening messages, unpaid child support, a joint debt, his son's anxiety, no stable home and no sleep. He called a domestic abuse helpline, which put him in touch with a local advocate.

Six problems still felt like a lot. Six names and numbers on one sheet felt manageable.

=== untangling-the-paperwork
title: Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork

seo: What to Change After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: After leaving an abusive relationship, change more than your passwords: beneficiaries on insurance and pensions, your will, emergency contacts, medical consent, joint accounts, the phone plan, the car, and who can access your records. This chapter is the full list, in a sensible order.
---
The first month is about safety. After that comes the slow job of untangling your life from theirs. It's boring, and it matters. A forgotten joint account or an old insurance form can keep you tied to them for years. Nobody writes songs about changing a pension beneficiary. Do it anyway.

## What should I change after leaving an abusive relationship?
Work through this list over a few months. Tick what applies, and skip what doesn't.

**Safety and access first:**
- Email, phone and cloud passwords, changed from a device they never touched. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- Recovery phone numbers and emails on every account.
- Security questions they could answer ("first pet," "mother's maiden name").
- Remove them as a trusted contact, family member or authorised user anywhere.

**Money:**
- Open accounts in your name only. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- Close or freeze joint accounts and credit cards, or ask the bank to require both signatures.
- Remove them as an authorised user on your cards.
- Check your credit report for accounts you didn't open.

**The phone plan and subscriptions:**
- Move your number off a shared family plan. Many carriers will help survivors do this.
- Cancel or move streaming, shopping and delivery accounts they can see. Yes, including the one where they still get your takeaway notifications.

**The home:**
- **Rented:** ask your landlord or housing provider about taking over the tenancy, or ending a joint one. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Owned jointly:** you usually can't remove a name from a mortgage without the lender's agreement, or a court order. Get legal advice; it will often be part of the divorce settlement. See [Divorce and separation proceedings](ch:divorce-and-separation-proceedings).
- Change locks, alarm codes and garage door codes.
- Pets and belongings still with them: see [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

**The car:**
- Change the registration and insurance into your name if you're keeping it.
- Remove them from the car's app, and check for trackers.

**Insurance and pensions:**
- Change beneficiaries on life insurance, pensions and workplace death-in-service benefits.
- Check health insurance: who's covered, and who receives the paperwork.

**Your will and legal powers:**
- Make or update your will. In some places divorce cancels gifts to an ex in a will, but separation alone doesn't.
- Cancel any power of attorney or health care proxy that names them, and make new ones.

**Medical and school records:**
- Update emergency contacts with your doctor, dentist, the children's school and your workplace.
- Ask your doctor and the school to keep your address private.

**Public records:**
- Voter registration, driving licence and government records: see [Your address, your records, your mail](ch:your-address-your-records-your-mail).

**Social media and photos:**
- Remove them from shared albums and cloud photo libraries.
- Review tagged photos and old posts that show your routine.

The Decoder shows how tangled money and accounts keep people tied: [Financial Enmeshment](/playbook/financial-enmeshment) and [Account Control](/playbook/account-control).

## Does any of this need their agreement?
Some things do: joint mortgages, joint loans, some joint accounts, and anything already covered by a court order. Don't stop paying shared debts without advice, because you may both still be liable. Your lawyer or a debt advice service can help.

If they ever claim you emptied or hid joint money, your notes are your answer. Write down what you changed, when, and on whose advice.

## Insurance and pensions
Lots of people forget that the person they left is still named as the beneficiary on life insurance, pensions or workplace death-in-service benefits. Change these as soon as you're allowed to. During a divorce, some changes may have to wait until the end, so check with a lawyer first.

## Your will and next of kin
If you have a will naming the person you left, they may inherit if something happens to you. In some places marriage or divorce changes a will automatically; in others it doesn't. Make a new will, even a simple one. Change your next of kin with your doctor, your employer and any hospital records.

## Medical consent and emergency contacts
If you were hurt or ill, who would the hospital call? Make sure it's someone safe. If you have children, tell the school and doctor who can consent to treatment. Where a court order allows, also tell them who must not be given information about the children's health without your agreement.

## Vehicles and shared pets
- Transfer car ownership and insurance into your own name, or agree how the car will be dealt with.
- Make sure pet microchips and vet records list you.
- Keep receipts showing who paid for what, in case ownership is disputed later.

## How do I keep track?
Keep a simple list: what you changed, when, and the reference number. It's also useful evidence if they later claim you hid something.

## An afternoon a week
Open your calendar now and block out one afternoon a week for the next five weeks. Label them money, home, health, legal and digital. Work through one category per afternoon, and in five weeks most of the paperwork will be done, without it taking over your life.
:::
