=== police-and-protective-orders
title: Protective orders
question: How does a protective order work, and is it worth getting?
seo: How Does a Protective Order Work? A Plain Guide
short: A protective order is a court order that tells someone to stay away from you and stop contacting you, and breaking it can lead to arrest. How a protective order works depends on where you live, but most places offer emergency, temporary and longer-term orders. It's a real tool, not a guarantee, so pair it with a safety plan.
---
If the person you left keeps contacting you, turning up or threatening you, a protective order can put a legal line around you. Crossing it becomes a matter for the police.

If you're in danger right now, call your emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](ch:if-youre-not-safe-right-now) has the numbers.

## How does a protective order work?
A protective order is a court order that can:

- ban contact, including messages and contact through other people
- order them to stay a set distance from you, your home, your work or your children's school
- order them out of a shared home
- in some places, deal with temporary custody, firearms, or a shared pet

If they break it, the police can act. In many places a breach is a crime in itself.

The names differ:

| Where | Common names |
|---|---|
| United States | protective order, restraining order, order of protection |
| England and Wales | non-molestation order, occupation order; domestic abuse protection order (being introduced) |
| Scotland | interdict, non-harassment order |
| Ireland | safety order, protection order, barring order, emergency barring order |
| Canada | emergency protection order (by province), peace bond |
| Australia | AVO (NSW), IVO (Victoria), DVO (Queensland), FVRO (WA), and others by state |

Search "[your country or state] protective order domestic abuse" for your local version.

## What types are there?
Most places have three levels:

1. **Emergency orders.** Issued fast, sometimes by police or a judge on the same day, often without the other person present. They last days.
2. **Temporary or interim orders.** Last until a full hearing, usually weeks.
3. **Longer-term or final orders.** Made after a hearing where both sides can be heard. They often last months or years, and can usually be renewed.

## How do I apply?
- **Through a court:** most courts have forms and help desks for protective orders. Domestic abuse services can help you fill them in, and in many places there's no fee.
- **Through the police:** in some places the police can apply for you, or issue an emergency order themselves.
- **Through a lawyer,** often free for protective orders. See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

## What do I write in the application?
- The most recent incident, in plain words, with the date.
- The pattern: earlier incidents, with dates. Your record does this work. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- Any threats, weapons, strangling or stalking. These carry weight. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).
- What you're afraid will happen.
- What you're asking the court to order.

Stick to what happened. Leave out labels and long histories that don't relate to your safety.

## What happens at the hearing?
Usually short. The judge reads your application, may ask you questions, and hears from the other person if they attend. Bring your evidence and a supporter. Ask for protections such as a separate waiting room. See [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).

The other person may deny everything, or apply for an order against you. Stay calm. See [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser).

## How long does a protective order last?
It depends on the type and the place. Emergency orders last days, temporary orders weeks, and longer orders anywhere from months to several years. Note the end date, and diary a reminder a month before so you can ask for a renewal if you still need it.

## Do protective orders work?
They help many people. They also have limits.

**What an order can do:** create a legal basis for the police to act; make a formal record; deter many people, especially those who care about consequences.

**What it can't do:** physically stop someone determined to break it. Police responses also vary by place and by officer.

> A protective order is a real tool, and it can't physically stop anyone. Plan for the gap between a breach and the response.

## What do I do if they break it?
1. If you're in danger, call your emergency number and say there's an order.
2. Write down what happened, when and where, and who saw it.
3. Keep messages, call logs and photos.
4. Report every breach, however small, and ask for a reference number each time.

A pattern of recorded breaches is often what produces stronger action. The Decoder's page on [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation) describes the borderline behaviour people use to test an order.

## The main orders in the UK
- **Non-molestation order (England, Wales, Northern Ireland):** stops someone using or threatening violence, harassing or intimidating you. Breaking it is a crime.
- **Occupation order:** decides who can live in the home. See [Still under the same roof](ch:still-under-the-same-roof).
- **Domestic Violence Protection Notice and Order:** short-term protection that police can put in place quickly after an incident.
- **Domestic Abuse Protection Orders:** a newer, more flexible order introduced by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, being brought in across areas from late 2024. It can include requirements like tagging or attending a programme.
- **Scotland:** interdicts, non-harassment orders and exclusion orders.

## Serving the order
An order usually only takes effect once the other person knows about it. This is called service. Ask the court how it will be served and when. Don't serve it yourself. Until you know it's been served, keep your safety plan as if it hasn't.

## Telling the people who need to know
Give a copy, or tell, those who may need to act on it:

- the children's school or nursery
- your employer and building security
- neighbours you trust
- your local police station, if they don't already have it

Keep a copy with you, one at home somewhere safe, and a photo on your phone.

## Changing or renewing an order
Orders often last for a fixed time. If you still need protection as the end date approaches, apply to extend it before it runs out. If circumstances change, like a move or new incidents, you can usually apply to vary it. Don't agree informally to "ignore" an order because they've asked you to. Only a court can change it.

## Orders across borders
- **United States:** under federal law, a protective order made in one state must be enforced in every other state, as well as tribal lands and territories.
- **European Union:** protection orders can be recognised in other EU countries.
- **Elsewhere:** if you're moving country, ask a lawyer before you go whether your order will be recognised, and how to register it.

## Where should I keep the order?
Keep a copy on you, and give copies to your children's school, your workplace and anyone else who might need to call the police. Photograph it on your phone.

=== police-and-reporting
title: Police and reporting
question: Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?
seo: Can I Report Post-Separation Abuse to the Police?
short: Yes, you can report post-separation abuse to the police. Threats, stalking, harassment, criminal damage, breaking an order and, in some places, coercive control are crimes. Report early, describe the pattern with dates, and ask for a reference number every time.
---
Many people wait until something dramatic happens before calling the police. But much of post-separation abuse is made of smaller things, and some of those smaller things are crimes.

If you're in danger now, call your emergency number.

## Can I report post-separation abuse to the police?
Yes. You can report:

- threats, including threats sent by message
- assault, of any kind
- stalking and harassment. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).
- criminal damage to your home, car or belongings
- breaking a protective order. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
- sharing or threatening to share intimate images. See [Intimate images and online threats](ch:intimate-images-and-online-threats).
- in some places, controlling or coercive behaviour itself, including after separation. See [What post-separation abuse is](ch:what-is-post-separation-abuse).

## What do I say so they hear the pattern?
Officers often see one incident at a time. Help them see the whole picture:

> **Say:** "I'm reporting [today's incident]. It's part of a pattern since we separated in [month and year]. I have a record of [number] incidents, including [threats / turning up / messages after the order]. I'd like this recorded as domestic abuse."

Bring or offer your record and evidence. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

## What should I ask for?
- **A reference or incident number.** Every time.
- **The officer's name and badge number.**
- **What happens next,** and when you'll hear.
- **A referral to a domestic abuse service,** if you don't already have one.
- **Whether a risk assessment will be done.** Many police services use one for domestic abuse.

## What is Clare's Law?
Some places let you ask the police whether someone has a history of abuse. In England and Wales this is the Domestic Violence Disclosure Scheme, known as Clare's Law. Scotland and parts of Australia have similar schemes. It can help if you're worried about a new partner, or about someone your ex is now seeing.

## What if they make a false report about me?
Stay calm, get legal advice before an interview, and bring your record. The Decoder's page on [false reports](/playbook/false-reports) describes the tactic, and [When they say you're the abuser](ch:when-they-say-youre-the-abuser) covers what to do.

## What if the police response is poor?
It happens. If you were dismissed, not believed, or the report wasn't recorded:

- ask to speak to a supervisor, or to a specialist domestic abuse officer
- ask a domestic abuse service to advocate for you
- make a formal complaint. Most police forces have a complaints process, and many countries have an independent police complaints body.

Keep reporting. Each report adds to the record, even when nothing seems to happen.

## What happens after you report
- An officer takes your account and records it. Ask for the crime or incident reference number.
- They may ask questions to assess risk. See [Why it often gets worse first](ch:why-it-often-gets-worse-first).
- They may speak to the person you reported, arrest them, or gather more evidence first.
- In many places, a specialist domestic abuse officer or advocate will contact you.
- You should be told about decisions, such as whether there will be a charge.

## Your rights as a victim
- **England and Wales:** the Victims' Code sets out your rights, including being kept informed, being referred to support, and asking for a review if a decision is made not to charge.
- **United States:** prosecutors' offices often have victim advocates. Many states let you sign up for notifications when an offender is released.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] victims' rights domestic abuse."

## If you don't want a prosecution
You may want the abuse recorded and to be safe, without the person being prosecuted. Tell the police what you want. Police and prosecutors may still decide to go ahead if there's enough evidence without you, because domestic abuse is treated as a crime against the public too. Your safety and your wishes should still be considered.

## Reporting online
Many police forces let you report non-emergency crimes online, which can be easier if you can't talk on the phone safely. Never use online reporting for an emergency.

## Complaints
If the police response was poor, you can complain to the force. In England and Wales, serious complaints can go to the Independent Office for Police Conduct. In the US, contact the department's internal affairs or professional standards unit. A domestic abuse service can help you complain.

## Do I have to report?
No. Some people don't feel safe involving the police, or have had bad experiences with them. That's your decision. You can still keep a record, get a protective order through a court, and get support from a domestic abuse service.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* sets out how to keep evidence for systems in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## Sources
- Serious Crime Act 2015, section 76, as amended by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 (England and Wales).
- Home Office (2016). *Domestic Violence Disclosure Scheme guidance* (England and Wales).
- Police Scotland: Disclosure Scheme for Domestic Abuse Scotland.
- Independent Office for Police Conduct (England and Wales): making a complaint.

=== housing-and-benefits
title: Housing and benefits
question: Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?
seo: Housing Help After Domestic Abuse: Options and Rights
short: Housing help after domestic abuse can include refuge, emergency housing, priority for social housing, the right to end a lease early, and help to stay safely in your own home. Benefits and child support can provide a floor. A local domestic abuse service usually knows exactly what's available where you live.
---
Money and housing are often the biggest reasons people feel stuck. This chapter covers help for the months after you leave, as well as the first days.

## Where can I get housing help after domestic abuse?
The options usually include:

- **Refuge or shelter:** safe, confidential housing, usually short term, often with support workers.
- **Emergency housing through your local council or housing authority.**
- **Priority for social housing.** In England, people made homeless by domestic abuse automatically have priority need for housing help.
- **Help to stay in your own home safely:** better locks, alarms and other changes, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.
- **Transitional housing:** longer stays with support, in some places.

Your local domestic abuse service is the best first call. They know what's free near you, and how long the waits are.

The Decoder's page on [financial dependence by design](/playbook/financial-dependence-by-design) explains how control often leaves people without money or housing of their own. That's a common result of abuse, not a personal failing.

## Can I end my lease early?
In many places, yes:

- **United States:** many states let survivors end a lease early with notice and documentation. In federally assisted housing, the Violence Against Women Act gives protections, including emergency transfers and removing an abuser from a lease.
- **England and Wales:** options depend on your tenancy. Councils and housing associations often have domestic abuse policies, and joint tenancies can sometimes be transferred into your name.
- **Australia:** most states let victims end a tenancy early with evidence of family violence.
- **Canada:** several provinces, including Ontario and British Columbia, let survivors end a tenancy early with notice.

Search "[your state or country] break lease domestic violence," or ask a tenancy advice service.

## What benefits might help?
Rules vary, but look at:

- **Benefits for single parents** and people on low incomes.
- **Child support.** Use the official service so you don't deal with them directly. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- **Emergency funds** from domestic abuse charities.
- **Help with legal costs.** See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

If you apply for benefits, tell the agency you've left an abusive relationship. Some have rules that help. You may get a break from work rules for a while, or a way to keep your address private.

## What if my immigration status depends on my ex?
Many countries have protections for this. See the immigration section in [If your story doesn't look like the usual one](ch:if-your-story-doesnt-look-like-the-usual-one).

## What are refuges and shelters like?
A refuge or shelter is a safe house for people escaping abuse. Its address is kept secret. You usually have your own room, and you share kitchens and living spaces. There are staff who help with safety, money, housing, court and children.

Stays range from a few weeks to several months. Some take children; some take older boys only in certain cases; some take pets. To find a space, call a national domestic abuse helpline. They can search for spaces across the country.

## Homelessness help and priority need
- **England and Wales:** if you're homeless because of domestic abuse, local councils must treat you as being in "priority need" for housing, under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021. Ask the council's housing team for a homelessness assessment.
- **Scotland:** councils must help people who are homeless, with guidance of its own on domestic abuse.
- **United States:** contact your local domestic violence program and the local homelessness system. Ask about rapid rehousing and transitional housing for survivors.

## Staying put safely
If you want to stay in your home, ask about "sanctuary schemes" (UK) or similar programmes. These add extra security, like stronger doors, locks, alarms and window guards, so you can stay where you are. You may also be able to get an occupation order. See [Police and protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).

## Moving social housing
If you're in social or council housing, you can usually ask for a management transfer or a move to a different area for safety. Explain the risk and provide any evidence you have.

## Renting with no references
Leaving abuse can leave you with no rental history, debt or poor credit. Ask domestic abuse services about deposit schemes, rent guarantors and landlords who accept a support worker's letter. Explain briefly to a landlord: "I've recently left an abusive relationship, which is why I don't have a previous landlord reference."

## US housing protections under VAWA
In housing covered by federal programmes, like public housing and Section 8, the Violence Against Women Act means you can't be evicted or denied housing because you're a victim of domestic violence. It also provides for emergency transfers. Many states add protections, like ending a lease early.

## Where do I start?
Call a domestic abuse service and say:

> **Say:** "I've left an abusive relationship and I need help with housing and money. Can you tell me what's available locally and help me apply?"

Then see [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) for other agencies that can help.

## Sources
- Domestic Abuse Act 2021, section 78 (priority need for victims of domestic abuse) (England).
- Violence Against Women Act, housing protections (34 U.S.C. 12491).
- National Housing Law Project (US): state laws on lease termination for survivors.
- Residential Tenancies Act 2006, section 47.1 (Ontario); Residential Tenancy Act, section 45.1 (British Columbia).
- Shelter (UK) and Women's Aid (UK): housing guidance for survivors.

short: Where to get help after domestic abuse depends on the problem: courts for protective orders and children, the police for crimes, child support services for money, housing authorities for a home, and domestic abuse services for almost everything. This chapter shows which body can do what, and what to search to find yours.

Every chapter in this part has said "it depends where you live." This one helps you find the answer for where you live.

## Where do I get help after domestic abuse?
Start with a domestic abuse service. They know your area better than any book, and they can point you to the rest. Then use this table.

## Who can make my ex stop?
No single body can. But together:

- **Courts** can order them to stay away and stop contacting you, and limit repeat applications. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders) and [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse).
- **Police** can arrest and charge for crimes, including breaches of orders. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).
- **Child support agencies** can take payments from wages.
- **Platforms** can remove content and accounts.

## What do I search for?
Use "[your country or state] domestic abuse support" first. National charities usually come up at the top, and they know who does what locally. The [Find help](ch:find-help) page lists the main ones for the US, UK, Canada, Australia and Ireland.

## Problem → who handles it

| Problem | Usually handled by |
|---|---|
| Immediate danger | Emergency services |
| Threats, stalking, breaking an order | Police |
| A protective order | Court |
| Arrangements for the children | Family court, or agreement through mediation where safe |
| Child support | Child support service |
| Housing | Local council or housing authority, domestic abuse services |
| Benefits | Benefits agency |
| Debt in your name | Lender, then a free debt advice service |
| Your health and sleep | Doctor |
| Children's distress | School, doctor, child mental health services |
| Planning, advice and support | Domestic abuse services |

## Who to call first
If you don't know where to start, start with a domestic abuse helpline. They know which agencies work where you live, and can often refer you directly or tell you what to say.

## One sheet to keep track
Agencies multiply quickly. Keep a single page with:

- each agency's name and phone number
- the name of the person you spoke to
- your reference number
- what they said they'd do, and by when

When you call again, you can say, "I spoke to [name] on [date], reference [number]."

## When agencies disagree
Sometimes the police, the court and children's services see things differently. Ask each one to put its decision in writing, and share decisions between them where you're allowed to. An advocate can help agencies talk to each other.

## Advocates who coordinate
- **United Kingdom:** an Independent Domestic Violence Adviser (IDVA) supports people at higher risk, attends meetings and works with police, courts and housing on your behalf.
- **United States:** domestic violence programs have advocates who help with protective orders, court, housing and safety.
- **Elsewhere:** ask a domestic abuse service for "an advocate who can help me deal with the agencies."

## What if an agency won't help?
Ask for a decision in writing. Ask how to complain or appeal. Ask a domestic abuse advocate to contact them for you. Agencies often respond differently when an advocate is involved.

- National Domestic Violence Hotline (US); National Domestic Abuse Helpline (England); 1800RESPECT (Australia); Women's Aid Ireland; ShelterSafe (Canada).

=== untangling-the-paperwork
title: Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork
question: What do I need to change after leaving an abusive relationship?
seo: What to Change After Leaving an Abusive Relationship
short: After leaving an abusive relationship, change more than your passwords: beneficiaries on insurance and pensions, your will, emergency contacts, medical consent, joint accounts, the phone plan, the car, and who can access your records. This chapter is the full list, in a sensible order.
---
The first month is about safety. After that comes the slow job of untangling your life from theirs. It's boring, and it matters: a forgotten joint account or an old insurance form can keep you tied to them for years.

## What should I change after leaving an abusive relationship?
Work through this list over a few months. Tick what applies.

**Safety and access first**
- Email, phone and cloud passwords, from a device they never touched. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- Recovery phone numbers and emails on every account.
- Security questions they could answer ("first pet," "mother's maiden name").
- Remove them as a trusted contact, family member or authorised user anywhere.

**Money**
- Open accounts in your name only. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
- Close or freeze joint accounts and credit cards, or ask the bank to require both signatures.
- Remove them as an authorised user on your cards.
- Check your credit report for accounts you didn't open.

**The phone plan and subscriptions**
- Move your number off a shared family plan. Many carriers will help survivors do this.
- Cancel or move streaming, shopping and delivery accounts they can see.

**The home**
- **Rented:** ask your landlord or housing provider about taking over the tenancy, or ending a joint one. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Owned jointly:** you usually can't remove a name from a mortgage without the lender's agreement, or a court order. Get legal advice; it will often be part of the divorce settlement. See [Divorce and separation proceedings](ch:divorce-and-separation-proceedings).
- Change locks, alarm codes and garage door codes.
- Pets and belongings still with them: see [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

**The car**
- Change the registration and insurance into your name if you're keeping it.
- Remove them from the car's app, and check for trackers.

**Insurance and pensions**
- Change beneficiaries on life insurance, pensions and workplace death-in-service benefits.
- Check health insurance: who's covered, and who receives the paperwork.

**Your will and legal powers**
- Make or update your will. In some places divorce cancels gifts to an ex in a will, but separation alone doesn't.
- Cancel any power of attorney or health care proxy that names them, and make new ones.

**Medical and school records**
- Update emergency contacts with your doctor, dentist, the children's school and your workplace.
- Ask your doctor and the school to keep your address private.

**Public records**
- Voter registration, driving licence and government records: see [Your address, your records, your mail](ch:your-address-your-records-your-mail).

**Social media and photos**
- Remove them from shared albums and cloud photo libraries.
- Review tagged photos and old posts that show your routine.

The Decoder describes how tangled money and accounts keep people tied: [financial enmeshment](/playbook/financial-enmeshment) and [account control](/playbook/account-control).

## Does any of this need their agreement?
Some things do: joint mortgages, joint loans, some joint accounts, and anything already covered by a court order. Don't stop paying shared debts without advice, because you may both remain liable. Your lawyer or a debt advice service can help.

## A checklist by category
**Money**
☐ Bank accounts in your name only; joint accounts frozen or closed with advice
☐ New PINs and passwords
☐ Credit report checked
☐ Direct debits and standing orders reviewed

**Home**
☐ Lease, mortgage and utilities updated
☐ Insurance for home and contents in your name
☐ Locks changed where you're allowed to

**Health**
☐ Doctor, dentist and pharmacy updated, with a note not to share your address
☐ Emergency contact changed
☐ Medical consent and next of kin updated

**Legal**
☐ Will updated
☐ Power of attorney revoked or changed
☐ Life insurance and pension beneficiaries updated

**Digital**
☐ Email, phone, cloud and social accounts secured
☐ Shared subscriptions and streaming services separated
☐ Photos backed up to an account only you control

## Insurance and pensions
Many people forget that the person they left is still named as the beneficiary on life insurance, pensions or workplace death-in-service benefits. Change these as soon as you're allowed to. During a divorce, some changes may need to wait until the end, so check with a lawyer first.

## Your will and next of kin
If you have a will naming the person you left, they may inherit if something happens to you. In some places marriage or divorce changes a will automatically; in others it doesn't. Make a new will, even a simple one. Change your next of kin with your doctor, employer and any hospital records.

## Medical consent and emergency contacts
If you were hurt or ill, who would the hospital call? Make sure it's someone safe. If you have children, tell the school and doctor who can consent to treatment and who must not be told about the children's health without your agreement, where a court order allows.

## Vehicles and shared pets
- Transfer car ownership and insurance into your own name, or agree how the car will be dealt with.
- Make sure pet microchips and vet records list you.
- Keep receipts showing who paid for what, in case ownership is disputed later.

## How do I keep track?
Keep a simple list: what you changed, when, and the reference number. It's also useful evidence if they later claim you hid something.
