=== money-from-zero
title: Money from zero
question: How do I start rebuilding my finances after an abusive relationship?
seo: Rebuilding Finances After an Abusive Relationship
short: Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship starts with an honest list of what you have and owe, a budget built on today's numbers, and small steps on credit and savings. Coerced debt can be disputed. Starting from little is a common result of financial abuse, not a personal failing.
---
Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship often starts from very little. You may have left with far less than you had, or nothing at all. Maybe you'd never been allowed to see the bank statements. Maybe there are debts in your name you didn't choose. Starting over financially is unglamorous, and that's said on purpose: slow and boring is what works.

## Where do I start rebuilding my finances?
1. **List what you have:** income, benefits, savings, anything you own.
2. **List what you owe:** every debt, with the lender, the amount and the minimum payment.
3. **Get your credit report** from each credit agency (free in most countries) and check every entry.
4. **Build a budget on today's numbers,** not the life you hope to have next year. Essentials first: housing, food, utilities, transport, the children.
5. **Open accounts in your name only,** at a bank they never used. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
6. **Protect your income at work,** including any leave you're entitled to. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

> Boring works. A slow, steady rebuild beats a dramatic fix almost every time.

## What about debts I didn't agree to?
This is called coerced debt: loans, cards or bills run up in your name through pressure, deception or fraud. You can dispute them with the lender and the credit agency. A police report, a protective order or a letter from a domestic abuse service can help. In the UK, Surviving Economic Abuse has guidance and templates. In the US, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how to dispute errors. The Decoder describes the wider pattern in [financial control](/playbook/financial-control).

## How do I rebuild credit?
- Pay every bill on time, even the small ones.
- Keep any credit card balance low.
- Consider a credit-builder card or loan, if the fees are low.
- Make sure you're on the electoral roll or equivalent, where that affects credit.
- Give it time. Credit scores recover over months and years.

## How do I start saving?
Start tiny. A few pounds or dollars a week into a separate account builds a habit, and a small buffer against emergencies. Many domestic abuse services can point you to emergency grants and matched-savings schemes.

## Where can I get free money advice?
- **United Kingdom:** Citizens Advice, StepChange, National Debtline.
- **United States:** non-profit credit counselling agencies (look for ones accredited by the National Foundation for Credit Counseling).
- **Australia:** the National Debt Helpline, 1800 007 007.
- **Ireland:** MABS, the Money Advice and Budgeting Service.
- **Canada:** non-profit credit counselling agencies.

## Is there funding to help me leave or start again?
Often, yes, though it varies a lot by place. Things to ask about:

- **Emergency grants** from domestic abuse charities, for deposits, travel or replacing essentials.
- **Benefits and housing help.** Many countries have faster routes for people fleeing abuse. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Victims' funds** where a crime was reported, in some states and countries.
- **Charity funds** for furniture, school uniforms or white goods.

A domestic abuse helpline knows what exists where you live. Ask them: "What funding is there for someone who has left an abusive relationship?" [Find help](ch:find-help) lists the numbers.

## What are the steps to financial independence?
Small and in order:

1. A bank account only you can see, with paperless statements to an email they don't know.
2. A list of every debt and every bill, even the frightening ones.
3. Your credit report, checked for anything opened in your name.
4. One small regular saving, even a tiny amount, so the habit exists.
5. A plan for income, whether that's benefits, child support, work or training. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

Financial independence after leaving an abusive relationship rarely arrives in one go. Each step makes the next one less frightening.

## Why does starting from zero feel so shameful?
Because it can feel like proof you failed. It isn't. Financial abuse leaves people with less money, damaged credit and debts they didn't choose. Starting again from very little is one of the most common results of a controlling relationship. It's evidence of what was done to you, not of who you are.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers building a "freedom fund" and separate accounts in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top). [Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork](ch:untangling-the-paperwork) covers separating what's left.

## A first budget
A budget after leaving doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be real.

1. Write down everything coming in each month: wages, benefits, child support.
2. Write down the fixed costs: rent, bills, phone, travel, childcare, debt payments.
3. Write down a realistic amount for food and essentials.
4. Look at what's left. If it's negative, that's information, not failure. It tells you what help to ask for.

Review it every month. It will change as your situation settles.

## Bank accounts and identity safety
- Use an account at a different bank from the person you left, if you can.
- Choose new security answers they couldn't guess. They know your mother's maiden name and first pet.
- Turn on alerts for every transaction.
- Ask your bank whether it has a domestic abuse support team. Many do, and they can help with joint accounts and coerced debt.
- Check your credit report every few months for anything opened in your name.

## Benefits and tax credits to check
People leaving abuse are often entitled to help they don't know about: child benefit, housing help, income top-ups, council tax or local tax reductions, free school meals, help with childcare costs.

- **United Kingdom:** free online calculators from charities like Turn2us or entitledto show what you might get.
- **United States:** use a government benefits finder and ask your local domestic violence program about state programmes.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] benefits calculator" or ask a domestic abuse service.

## Income from child support
Child support is your children's money, not a favour. Use an official service to set and collect it, so it doesn't depend on contact. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).

## Scams and predatory lenders
People rebuilding after abuse are often targeted by high-cost lenders, "credit repair" firms and scams. Be wary of:

- loans that are easy to get but expensive to repay
- anyone charging to fix your credit record
- offers that need payment up front
- pressure to decide quickly

Free debt advice services can do what paid firms promise, without the fees.

## A small cushion
An emergency fund, even a tiny one, makes the next crisis less frightening. Start with a small amount each week into a separate account. Round up purchases, or save any unexpected money. The habit matters more than the amount.

## How Bea rebuilt from debt
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Bea left with £4,000 of debt her ex had run up in her name and no savings. A free debt adviser contacted the lenders, explained the coerced debt and arranged affordable payments. Two lenders wrote off part of the debt. She opened a new account at a different bank, set up a small weekly saving and checked her credit report every three months.

Two years later, the debt was nearly gone and she had a few hundred in savings. It didn't feel rich. It felt like hers.

## Write the budget tonight
Write your first budget on one sheet: what comes in, what goes out, what's left. Take it to a free money adviser if the numbers don't work.

## Sources
- Littwin, A. (2012). Coerced debt: The role of consumer credit in domestic violence. *California Law Review*, 100(4), 951–1026.
- Postmus, J. L., et al. (2012). Understanding economic abuse in the lives of survivors. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*, 27(3), 411–430.
- Surviving Economic Abuse (UK); Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (US); National Debt Helpline (Australia); MABS (Ireland).

=== work-again
title: Work again
question: Can I take domestic violence leave, and how do I explain a gap?
seo: Domestic Violence Leave and Going Back to Work
short: Domestic violence leave exists in a growing number of places, sometimes paid: Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, Northern Ireland and parts of Canada and the US. Whether or not you have a legal right, many employers will help with time off and safety. You never have to tell an employer the whole story.
---
You're sitting at your desk with a court letter in your bag and a message from them on your phone. Going back to work, or staying in work, after leaving can be hard. Court dates, moving house, children's needs, exhaustion. And you may have a gap to explain.

## Can I take domestic violence leave?
In some places, yes:

- **Australia:** employees can take 10 days of paid family and domestic violence leave a year.
- **New Zealand:** 10 days of paid domestic violence leave a year.
- **Ireland:** 5 days of paid domestic violence leave a year, since November 2023.
- **Northern Ireland:** a 2022 law will give 10 days of paid domestic abuse leave a year once its regulations take effect. Check whether it's in force yet.
- **Canada:** federally regulated employees have paid leave, and most provinces offer some, partly paid or unpaid.
- **United States:** no federal right, but several states require time off for survivors to go to court, see a doctor or move.
- **England, Wales and Scotland:** no general legal right to domestic abuse leave yet, but many employers have policies. Ask HR.

Search "[your country or state] domestic violence leave" for the current rules.

## Do I have to tell my employer what happened?
No. "I'm dealing with a personal family matter" is enough in almost every situation. You choose what to share. You might share more if you need leave, flexibility or safety measures, but even then you can keep the details private.

> What you share about this part of your life at work is your call, based on what helps you, not on what you think you owe.

## How do I stay safe at work?
If the person you left knows where you work:

- tell security or reception, and give them a photo
- ask to have calls screened, and your details removed from public directories
- ask about changing your hours, location or parking
- keep a record of anything that happens at work. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

See the work section of [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

## How do I explain a gap in my CV?
Keep it short and forward-looking:

> **Say:** "I took some time out to deal with a family matter, which is now resolved. I'm ready to focus on work, and I've kept my skills up by [anything you did]."

Interviewers rarely push further. If they do, you can say, "It was personal, and it's behind me."

## What if my ex is disrupting my job?
Calling your employer, turning up, or making handovers clash with your shifts are forms of financial abuse. Tell your employer what's happening in brief, and keep a record. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel). *At Will*, in this library, covers workplace rights and protections in depth.

## What employers can do
Many employers can help more than people expect:

- flexible hours for court dates and appointments
- paid or unpaid leave
- a change of shift, team or location
- screening calls and emails, and not confirming you work there
- security at the entrance, or an escort to your car
- an employee assistance programme with free counselling

Ask HR whether there's a domestic abuse policy. In Australia and New Zealand, employees are entitled to paid family and domestic violence leave. Some US states and other countries have their own rules.

## Returning after years out
If you were kept from working, or left a job because of the abuse, going back can feel huge.

- Start with what you can manage: part-time, volunteering, a short course.
- List what you did during the gap: raising children, running a home, caring, managing money under pressure. These are real skills.
- Look for return-to-work programmes, often run by charities, colleges or employers.

## Training and education
Many people use the years after leaving to train or study. Look for free or low-cost courses at local colleges and online, grants for people returning to education, and adult learning services. Some domestic abuse services run employment and training programmes.

## Interviews: the gap question
> **Say:** "I took some time out for family reasons, which are now resolved. During that time I [cared for my children / managed a household / volunteered]. I'm ready to focus on my career, and I've been building my skills through [course or activity]."

You don't have to mention the abuse. Practise the answer until it comes easily.

## Self-employment and safety online
If you work for yourself, your name, photo and location may be easy to find online. Use a business address, not your home. Keep personal and business social media separate. Think about what clients can see, like your schedule, your location and your children.

## How Omar went back to work
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Omar hadn't worked for six years: his ex had made it impossible. After leaving, he started volunteering two mornings a week at a food bank. It gave him a reference and a reason to get up. He took a free course at the local college, then applied for part-time jobs. In interviews he said he'd taken time out for family reasons and talked about the volunteering.

His first job paid little. It also gave him money of his own for the first time in years.

## One step towards work
Pick one step this month: a volunteering shift, a course, an updated CV or a conversation with a careers service.

## Sources
- Fair Work Act 2009 (Australia), section 106A (paid family and domestic violence leave).
- Domestic Violence—Victims' Protection Act 2018 (New Zealand).
- Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 (Ireland).
- Domestic Abuse (Safe Leave) Act (Northern Ireland) 2022 (regulations pending as of November 2026).
- Canada Labour Code, section 206.7 (leave related to family violence).
- US Department of Labor, Women's Bureau: state laws on leave for survivors.
- Fair Work Ombudsman: family and domestic violence leave (Australia); Employment New Zealand: family violence leave


=== a-home-that-feels-like-yours
title: A home that feels like yours
question: How do I start feeling safe at home after abuse?
seo: Feeling Safe at Home After Abuse
short: Feeling safe at home after abuse can take time, whether you've moved somewhere new or stayed in a place you shared. Practical security, small changes that make the space yours, and calm routines all help your body learn that home is safe now.
---
Feeling safe at home after abuse takes longer than changing the locks. You have a door that locks now. But you still lie awake listening. Or you're in the home you shared, and every room holds a memory.

## Why doesn't home feel safe yet?
Your nervous system learned that home was where danger lived. It takes time, and many calm days, for it to learn otherwise. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## What practical steps help?
- **Change the locks** if they ever had a key, and the alarm and garage codes.
- **Add what makes you feel secure:** a door chain, a video doorbell you control, outdoor lights, a window lock.
- **Know your exits** and keep your phone charged near you at night.
- **Check for devices** they set up. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- **Ask about help with costs.** Some domestic abuse services and councils fund security improvements, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.

## How do I make it feel like mine?
- **Change something you can see:** move the furniture, paint a wall, new bedding.
- **Remove or store reminders.** You don't have to keep their gifts, photos or the chair they always sat in.
- **Bring in things you love.** Plants, music, colours, smells. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- **Make a calm corner** where you go to rest: a chair, a blanket, a lamp.
- **Set your own rules:** eat when you like, play music loud, leave the dishes.

> Small choices that are yours alone add up: the mug, the lamp, the time you go to bed.

## What if I'm still in the home we shared?
Reclaiming a shared home is hard. Some people change one room at a time. Some do a deliberate "clearing" day with a friend. Some find they need to move eventually. All are fine.

## What helps at night?
A routine, a light within reach, a door you've checked once, and something calming to listen to. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).


## What if I've had to move somewhere I don't like?
A refuge, temporary housing, a smaller flat, a relative's spare room. It can feel like a step back. Make one corner yours: a photo, a lamp, a blanket, a smell you love. Home can start with one safe spot.

## What if they know where I live?
Keep the security steps from your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) in place, and record any visits. If they turn up or watch the house, that can be stalking. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment). Ask about a protective order that covers your address.

## What if the children bring the other home with them?
Bags, gifts and devices travel between homes. Check devices that come back from visits. See [Device and account audit](ch:device-and-account-audit). Let children keep comfort items from both homes. Your rules apply in your house.

## How do I sleep alone after years on alert?
Some people find a night light, white noise or a pet helps. [Sleep](ch:sleep) has more.

After years of listening for footsteps or a key in the door, your body may stay half awake. Check the doors and windows once, then tell yourself out loud that they're checked. Keep your phone charged within reach, with emergency numbers saved. Some people sleep better with a door alarm, a camera doorbell, or a radio playing low. If you wake at 3am, get up for a while and do something dull in low light, then go back to bed.

## A first-week setup
In the first week in a new place, focus on:

- a bed and bedding you like
- a working lock, and a chain or door alarm if it helps
- a few things that feel like you: a picture, a plant, a mug
- a light by the bed
- a list of local numbers by the door

The rest can come slowly.

## Furnishing from nothing
If you left with little, help exists:

- furniture charities and reuse schemes
- local crisis funds that provide beds, cookers and fridges
- community groups and online free-to-collect sites
- domestic abuse services that keep donation stores

Ask a domestic abuse worker what's available locally. Don't be shy about using it; that's what it's for.

## Sounds and smells
Senses carry memory. A smell like their aftershave or the way a door creaks can set you on edge. Change what you can: new scents, different music, a new routine. Notice the sounds of your new home during the day, so they're familiar at night.

## Inviting people in
Having friends over can help a place feel like home. Start small: one person, a cup of tea. Only share your address with people you trust completely, and ask them not to post photos that show where you live.

## The children's rooms
Let the children help choose colours, bedding or posters, even with a small budget. A space that's theirs helps them settle. Keep a few familiar things from before, if you have them. Put photos of them, and of people they love, where they can see them.

## One thing that's yours
Buy, find or make one thing for your home this week that the person you left would never have allowed. Put it where you'll see it every day.

## Sources
- Women's Aid (UK) and Refuge (UK): staying safe at home.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books (on establishing safety as the first stage of recovery).

=== pets-and-the-things-left-behind
title: Pets and the things left behind
question: My ex took my dog, or won't give back my things. What can I do?
seo: Ex Took My Dog or My Things? Pets and Belongings After Leaving
short: If your ex took your dog or is holding your belongings, you have options. Some protective orders can cover pets, pet foster schemes help people leaving abuse, and police in many places can attend while you collect belongings. Threats to pets are a recognised warning sign.
---
You left in a hurry, or you couldn't take everything. "My ex took my dog" is one of the most common things people say after leaving. Now they have your dog. Or your grandmother's ring, your documents, your clothes. And they know exactly how much it matters.

## Why are pets used against people leaving abuse?
Because pets are family, and controlling people use whatever matters most. Threats to hurt a pet, refusing to return it, or using it to make you come back are common. Researchers have found that many people delay leaving because they're afraid for their animals. The Decoder describes the general pattern in [children used as leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage); pets are used the same way.

Threats to harm or kill a pet are also a warning sign of wider danger. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

## What can I do about my pet?
- **Protective orders:** in many US states, and some other places, a protective order can include pets. Ask when you apply. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
- **Ownership records:** microchip registration, vet records, purchase receipts and licences in your name help show the animal is yours.
- **Pet foster schemes:** some charities look after pets while their owners are in refuge or temporary housing. In the UK, Dogs Trust's Freedom Project and Cats Protection's Paws Protect. In the US, RedRover runs programmes and grants, and many shelters now accept pets. Ask a domestic abuse service.
- **Police:** if a pet is being harmed or threatened, report it.

## How do I get my belongings back?
- **Ask in writing,** once, with a list and a proposed date. Keep it brief.
- **Ask the police to attend** while you collect your things. Many forces will do this, sometimes called a civil standby or police-assisted collection.
- **Send someone else:** a friend or family member, with your list.
- **Use your lawyer,** especially for valuables, documents or anything covered in a divorce.

If they destroy or sell your belongings, that may be criminal damage or theft. Keep a record.

## What about letting go?
Some things you'll get back. Some you won't. Write the losses down in your [record](ch:what-to-document-and-how) if they might matter in court. Losing them is a real grief, especially things that held memories. Some people find it helps to write down what was lost, and then decide what they're willing to keep fighting for and what to let go. Your safety is worth more than any object.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* includes pets in its go-bag list in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## Pet custody and ownership
In many places, pets are treated as property in law, so ownership matters. Keep proof that a pet is yours: purchase or adoption records, vet bills, microchip registration, insurance. In many US states, protective orders can include pets, and some states let courts consider a pet's welfare in a divorce. Ask your lawyer to include pets in any order.

## A police escort to collect things
If you need to collect belongings from a home you've left, ask the police whether they can go with you. In many places they can keep the peace while you collect essentials. Arrange it in advance, bring a list, and take someone with you. Don't go alone.

## What to prioritise
If you only get one chance, take:

- documents: ID, birth certificates, passports, financial papers
- medicine and medical equipment
- children's essentials and comfort items
- irreplaceable things: photos, heirlooms, a few sentimental items
- tools of your work: laptop, equipment

Furniture, clothes and most household items can be replaced.

## Photos and sentimental things
Losing photos and keepsakes can hurt more than losing anything else. Ask relatives and friends for copies of photos they have. Check old social media and cloud accounts you can still access. Some people make a new album of the years since leaving. It won't replace what's lost, but it starts a new record.

## When things are destroyed
Some abusers destroy belongings or pets' things to hurt the person who left. Photograph any damage you see, and report it. Destroying someone's property can be a crime. Grieve what's lost. The memories stay yours.

## How Chloe got her dog back
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Chloe left, her ex kept the dog and said she'd never see him again. She had the vet records and microchip registration in her name, and the receipt from the rescue centre. Her solicitor included the dog in her protective order application. The court ordered the dog returned to her.

Not everyone gets that outcome. Chloe said the paperwork she'd kept "just in case" made all the difference.

## Gather the proof
Find any proof of ownership for your pet, like adoption papers, vet records or microchip details, and keep copies somewhere safe.

## Sources
- Ascione, F. R., et al. (2007). Battered pets and domestic violence: Animal abuse reported by women experiencing intimate violence and by nonabused women. *Violence Against Women*, 13(4), 354–373.
- Animal Welfare Institute (US): state laws allowing pets in protective orders.
- Dogs Trust Freedom Project (UK); Cats Protection Paws Protect (UK); RedRover (US).

=== holidays-and-the-first-year
title: Holidays and the first year's calendar
question: How do I get through the first holidays after the divorce?
seo: First Holidays After Divorce or Leaving Abuse: A Plan
short: The first holidays after divorce or leaving an abusive relationship are often among the hardest days of the year: old traditions, children away, family pressure, and contact from your ex. Plan them early, decide how the children's time works in writing, and make a small new tradition of your own.
---
The first Christmas. The first birthday. The first school holidays. The first anniversary of the day you left. The first year is full of firsts, and each one can catch you off guard.

## Why are the first holidays so hard?
- Old traditions remind you of what's gone.
- The children may be with the other parent.
- Family gatherings bring questions and pressure.
- Your ex may use the day for contact, guilt or conflict. Hoovering often spikes around holidays. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## How do I plan the first holidays after divorce?
**Settle the children's time early, in writing.** Who has them when, where handovers happen, and what happens about presents. A clear plan leaves less to fight about. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).

**Plan your own day,** especially if the children are away. Be with people who feel safe, or plan a quiet day that's deliberately yours. Don't leave it empty by accident.

**Decide about contact in advance.** If they message, what will you do? Usually: nothing, or a short reply about the children only. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).

**Lower the bar.** You don't have to make it perfect. A smaller, calmer holiday is fine.

> You're allowed to do this year differently. You're allowed to do it badly. You're allowed to skip it.

## What about family pressure?
Prepare one line for questions you don't want to answer:

> **Say:** "I'd rather not talk about it today. Tell me about you."

If gatherings feel unsafe or too much, you can leave early or not go.

## How do I make new traditions?
Start small: a walk on the morning of the holiday, a meal you love, a film, a phone call with a friend. New traditions take a few years to feel like yours. That's fine.

## What about the other firsts?
Plan for anniversaries, the children's birthdays, and the day you left. See [The anniversary effect](ch:the-anniversary-effect). Put them in your calendar a week ahead.


## What about presents for the children?
Agree in writing who buys what, or let each parent do their own. Don't compete. Children remember calm more than gifts.

## What if the other parent uses the holiday to cause trouble?
Late returns, changed plans, guilt messages. Keep to the arrangement, write down what happens, and reply briefly. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use). If it breaks a court order, report it.

## What about the holidays when I don't have the children?
They can be the loneliest days of the year. Plan ahead: friends, volunteering, a trip, a film marathon, a long walk. Some people mark the day another way on purpose.

## What about New Year and other fresh-start days?
Big milestone days can bring pressure to feel better. You don't have to make resolutions. One small intention is enough.

New Year, birthdays and the start of spring can bring pressure to have moved on, or to have a plan for a new life. You don't need either. A single small intention is enough: sleep a bit better, see one friend a week, learn one new thing. Looking back at how far you've come can feel better than setting big goals for the year ahead.

## A month-by-month first year
- **Months 1 to 3:** survive. Basic routines, safety, money, children. Skip anything optional.
- **Months 4 to 6:** the first big dates often land here: birthdays, a holiday, a court hearing. Plan for each.
- **Months 7 to 9:** things may feel steadier, then a dip arrives. Dips are normal.
- **Months 10 to 12:** the anniversary of leaving. Plan the day with care. Look back at how far you've come.

Everyone's year is different. This is a rough shape, not a timetable.

## Birthdays
Your own birthday may feel strange: the first without them, or the first in years without a performance. Plan something small that you'd enjoy. For the children's birthdays, follow the court order or agreement, and try to keep the day about them, even if handovers are tense.

## School events
Plays, sports days and parents' evenings can bring you face to face with the other parent. Sit apart, arrive with someone, and keep any exchange polite and short. If there's a protective order, tell the school before the event.

## Gifts and money pressure
You may feel pressure to make up for the separation with big gifts. Children usually remember time and attention more than presents. Set a budget. Don't compete with the other parent's gifts.

## After the first year
Many people say the second year is easier, though not always. You'll have been through each date once, and you'll know what helps. Keep the plans that worked. Drop the ones that didn't.

## The first holiday alone
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

The children were with their father for the first Christmas after the separation. Hannah dreaded it. She planned the day: a walk with a friend in the morning, a film she'd always wanted to see, dinner with her brother's family. She let herself cry when she needed to. In the evening she video-called the children and kept it cheerful.

It wasn't a good day. It was a day she got through, and the next year she knew how.

## Plan the next big date
Look at the next holiday or birthday coming up, and write down who you'll be with and what you'll do.

## Sources
- US Department of Veterans Affairs, National Center for PTSD: Anniversary reactions to a traumatic event.
- Emery, R. E. (2004). *The Truth About Children and Divorce.* Viking (on holiday arrangements for children).

=== your-own-tastes-rediscovered
title: Your own tastes, rediscovered
question: How do I find myself again after an abusive relationship?
seo: Finding Yourself After an Abusive Relationship
short: Finding yourself after an abusive relationship often happens in small surprises: music you'd stopped playing, food you love, an opinion you'd stopped saying out loud. Coercive control narrows what feels safe to want. Getting it back is allowed to be light, and even fun.
---
Finding yourself after an abusive relationship rarely happens all at once. One day you notice you're humming a song you haven't let yourself play in years. Or you order something you'd never have ordered with them. Or you realise you have an opinion, and you say it.

## Why did I lose track of myself?
Coercive relationships often shrink what feels safe to want, prefer or enjoy. Your clothes, your music, your friends, your opinions: each one slowly bent towards what kept the peace. It happens so gradually you may not have noticed. The Decoder describes it as [identity erosion](/playbook/identity-erosion).

## How do I find myself again?
You don't have to find a whole new self. Start with small curiosities:

- **Try things without a goal.** A class, a recipe, a walk somewhere new, not because it's therapy but because it sounds good.
- **Notice what you like,** in a note on your phone: a colour, a song, a smell, a kind of day.
- **Go back to old loves.** What did you enjoy before the relationship?
- **Say an opinion out loud,** somewhere safe, and notice how it feels.
- **Let your home reflect you.** See [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours).

> This part is allowed to be light. Not every piece of recovery has to be heavy.

## What if I don't know what I like?
That's common. Treat it as an experiment. Try things and keep what works. Some of what you find will surprise you. That's part of the point. People who were isolated often find new friends through the same experiments. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).

## What if it feels selfish?
It isn't. Rediscovering what you enjoy is part of getting your life back. And people who are happier in their own skin tend to be steadier parents, friends and partners. See [The version of you from before](ch:the-version-of-you-from-before).


## What small experiments can I try?
- Buy one thing in a shop that only you would choose.
- Cook a meal they hated, or wouldn't allow.
- Make a playlist of songs from before the relationship.
- Rearrange one room for your own comfort.
- Say no to an invitation you don't want, and yes to one you do.
- Spend an afternoon with no plan, and notice what you drift towards.

## What if what I liked before doesn't fit any more?
Tastes change anyway, with age and experience. Some old loves will come back. Some won't. New ones will turn up. You're not restoring a museum. You're living.

## Why do I feel guilty enjoying things?
Some people feel they don't deserve fun while life is still hard, or while a court case is going on. Enjoying something doesn't prove you're "fine," and it doesn't prove nothing happened. It's part of getting your life back. See [Guilt and shame](ch:guilt-and-shame).

## A list of tiny experiments
Pick one a week. Notice how each feels, without deciding anything big.

- Order something you've never tried at a café.
- Listen to an album from a genre you'd never pick.
- Walk a different route home.
- Rearrange one corner of a room.
- Wear a colour you used to avoid.
- Go to a free museum or library talk.
- Cook a meal from a country you've never visited.
- Spend an hour doing something you loved as a child.

## Music, food, clothes and rooms
These are the everyday places where taste shows. They're also where control often reached: the music you weren't allowed, the clothes they mocked, the food you stopped cooking. Reclaim one thing at a time. Put on the song. Buy the shirt. Paint the wall.

## Ideas that cost nothing
- borrowing books, films and music from the library
- free walks, parks and community events
- online classes and talks
- swapping clothes with friends
- trying a new recipe from what's in the cupboard

Finding your tastes doesn't need money. It needs curiosity and permission.

## The body: what feels good
After abuse, many people feel disconnected from their bodies. Try small, kind things: a warm bath, stretching, a walk in the sun, a soft blanket, dancing alone in the kitchen. Notice what your body likes, and do more of it.

## Noticing your own "no"
Part of finding your tastes is noticing what you don't like. A small "no" counts: turning down a plan, sending back a meal, saying "I'd rather not." Each one strengthens the sense that your preferences matter.

## How Eva found her music again
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Eva's ex used to mock the music she liked, so she stopped listening to it. A year after leaving, she heard a song from her twenties in a shop and stood still. That night she made a playlist of everything she used to love. Some of it didn't fit any more. Some of it made her dance in the kitchen.

She said it was the first time in years she'd felt like herself, alone, on purpose.

## Try one thing this week
Choose one tiny experiment from the list and do it before the week is out.

=== trust-as-a-practice
title: Trust as a practice
question: How do I deal with trust issues after abuse?
seo: Trust Issues After Abuse: How to Trust Again, Slowly
short: Trust issues after abuse make sense: trust was used against you. You don't have to choose between total guard and total openness. Trust can be given in small amounts, matched to how people behave over time, and adjusted as you learn more.
---
You don't trust easily any more. Or you trust too fast and get hurt. Either way, the way you used to trust doesn't feel safe.

## Why do I have trust issues after abuse?
Because trust was used against you. Someone you let in used what you shared, and used your good faith. Being careful now is a sensible lesson, not a flaw.

## How do I learn to trust again?
Think of trust as a dial, not a switch. You can turn it up a little at a time:

1. **Start small.** Share something low-stakes, or rely on someone for something minor.
2. **Watch what happens.** Did they keep it private? Did they show up? Did they respect your "no"?
3. **Adjust.** Turn the dial up a little if they handled it well. Turn it down if they didn't.
4. **Repeat,** over weeks and months.

> Trust built slowly, on evidence, lasts longer than trust given all at once, and is easier to live with than trust never given.

*The Weighing*, in this library, sets out this way of judging people in [Trust as a skill you rebuild](/scale/trust-as-a-skill-you-rebuild).

## What signs are worth noticing?
**Signs to trust a little more:** they keep small promises, respect your limits, are the same in private and in public, and accept "no" without sulking.

**Signs to slow down:** pressure, rushing intimacy, big promises early, anger when you set a limit, stories that don't add up. See [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not).

## What about trusting myself?
Often that's the trust that took the biggest hit. It comes back the same way: small decisions, noticing they turned out fine, and building from there. See [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## What if someone from the past says they've changed?
Use the same dial. Real change shows over time, consistently, without pressure on you. See the section on telling hoovering from real change in [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).


## How do I trust my own judgment about people?
Write down your first impression of someone new. Look back after a month. You'll often find your first sense was right. That record rebuilds confidence in your judgment, one person at a time.

## What about doctors, lawyers and therapists?
The same dial works. Share a little first, watch how they respond, and change provider if they dismiss you. You're allowed to ask for someone else.

## What if I keep choosing people who hurt me?
Many survivors worry about this. Patterns from earlier life can make some kinds of attention feel familiar. Knowing the early warning signs and going slowly both help. See [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not). Therapy focused on relationships can help too. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What if I want to keep records on someone new?
With the person you left, records protect you. If you notice an urge to document a new friend or partner, pay attention to it. It may be a sign to slow down, or a sign that something is wrong.

## Trust in small steps
Trust grows like a ladder, one rung at a time:

1. Share something small and low-risk, like a preference or a minor worry.
2. Watch what they do with it. Do they remember? Respect it? Keep it private?
3. If it goes well, share a bit more.
4. If it doesn't, step back down.

You don't have to decide all at once whether someone is trustworthy. You can let them show you, over time.

## Trust and boundaries
A trustworthy person accepts your "no," doesn't push for more than you want to share, and doesn't punish you for limits. If someone resists your boundaries early on, that's useful information.

## Rebuilding trust with family
If family let you down during the relationship or after, trust can take time to rebuild. Look for consistent actions: turning up, respecting your wishes, not passing on information. Words of apology matter less than what they do next.

## Trust in institutions after being let down
If the police, courts or services failed you, you may find it hard to trust any of them again. That's understandable. Some ways forward: find one professional who does get it and work through them; bring a support worker to appointments; ask for everything in writing.

## Trusting your gut without over-reading
After abuse, your alarm system may go off often, sometimes at things that aren't dangerous. It helps to pause and check: "Is this person doing something harmful, or reminding me of someone who did?" Your gut is worth listening to. It's also worth checking against what's happening in front of you.

## Climbing the ladder with a new friend
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Alice met a new friend at work, she shared small things first: a film she liked, a worry about a deadline. The friend remembered and asked about them. Alice shared a bit more: that she'd left a difficult relationship. The friend didn't pry or pass it on. Months later, Alice told her the whole story.

Each step was small. Each one gave her evidence, not hope, that this person was safe.

## Notice one small kept promise
This week, notice one time someone does what they said they'd do. Small kept promises are how trust gets rebuilt.

- Rempel, J. K., Holmes, J. G., and Zanna, M. P. (1985). Trust in close relationships. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 49(1), 95–112.
- Herman, J. L. (1992). *Trauma and Recovery.* Basic Books.

=== dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not
title: Dating, when you're ready and when you're not
question: When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?
seo: Dating After an Abusive Relationship: Readiness and Red Flags
short: There's no set time for dating after an abusive relationship. Two pressures get in the way: rushing to prove you're fine, and waiting for a perfect "ready" that never comes. Better signs are curiosity about new people, and being able to handle ordinary uncertainty. Know the early warning signs, and go at your own pace.
---
Some people want to date straight away. Some can't imagine ever doing it again. Most land somewhere in between, and wonder if they're doing it wrong.

## When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?
There's no number of months. Two pressures can push you either way:

- **Rushing to prove you're fine.** Dating soon can be a way of saying "look, I've moved on," to yourself or to others. That's a different reason from wanting to meet someone.
- **Waiting for a perfect "ready."** The idea that one day you'll be fully healed and allowed to date. For most people that clean moment never comes.

More useful signs:

- You can be curious about a new person, instead of measuring them against your ex.
- You can live with the ordinary uncertainty of early dating without it setting off your alarm system too strongly. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- You'd be okay if it didn't work out.

None of these have to be perfect.

## What are the early warning signs?
Abusive relationships often start with intensity. Watch for:

- **Love bombing:** overwhelming attention, gifts, "I've never felt this way," very fast. The Decoder's [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing).
- **Future faking:** big plans very early: moving in, marriage, children. The Decoder's [future faking](/playbook/future-faking).
- **Pushing past "no,"** even in small things.
- **Jealousy framed as love.**
- **Running down every ex** as crazy.
- **Wanting you to themselves,** and resenting your friends.
- **Your gut saying "slow down."**

> Someone who's right for you will be comfortable going at your pace.

## How do I date safely?
- Meet in public, and tell a friend where you'll be.
- Keep your address private until you trust them.
- Go at your own pace with physical intimacy. See [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse) if that's relevant to you.
- Use the trust dial in [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).
- Get yourself there and back, and keep your drink with you.
- If something feels off, trust it and leave. You don't owe anyone an explanation.

## When do I tell them about my past?
When it feels right, and in proportion to how serious it's getting. See [Telling someone new](ch:telling-someone-new). If your ex might contact them, tell them earlier. See [The new partner](ch:the-new-partner).

## What if I never want to date again?
That's a valid choice. A full life doesn't require a partner.

Some people find, after leaving, that they're happier alone, and stay that way. Others feel like this for a year and then change their minds. Both are fine. Friendships, family, community, work, children and your own interests can make a full life. If people push you to "get back out there," you can say, "I'm happy as I am." If the feeling comes from fear more than choice, a therapist can help you tell the difference.

## What if I'm drawn to the same type?
Notice it without judging yourself. The pull towards intensity or charm can feel like chemistry when it's familiarity. Go slower with people who feel exciting in the same way your ex did, and give steadier people a second date.

## How do I date online safely?
- Use a first name only, and a photo that isn't on your other profiles.
- Do a video call before meeting.
- Meet in a public place, and tell a friend where you'll be and when you'll check in.
- Keep your address, workplace and children's school private until you trust them.
- Search their name and photos if anything feels off.

## What if a new partner does something that reminds me of my ex?
Ask whether it's a pattern or a single moment that set off an old alarm. Talk about it calmly. A good partner will listen and adjust. If it's a pattern, take that seriously. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

## A readiness checklist
You might be ready to date if:

- you're dating because you want to, not to fill a gap or prove something
- you can imagine being happy single
- contact with the person you left is limited or stable
- you can say no without fear
- you have people to talk to about how dates go

You don't need every box ticked. If several feel far off, it may be worth waiting.

## Green flags
Alongside the warning signs, look for good ones:

- they respect your pace
- they're consistent: they do what they say
- they're curious about you without prying
- they handle "no" well
- they have friendships and a life of their own
- you feel calm and like yourself around them

## Sex and intimacy after abuse
Intimacy can feel complicated after abuse, especially if there was sexual abuse or coercion. Go at your own pace. Agree on a way to pause or stop, and expect it to be respected. If memories or panic come up, it's okay to stop and talk. A therapist can help if it keeps happening. See [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse).

## When the past shows up in a new relationship
A raised voice, a late reply or a sulk can set off old alarms. Tell a new partner what helps: "If I go quiet after an argument, I need a few minutes. You haven't done anything wrong." A good partner will be patient and curious. If they use your history against you, that's a warning sign.

## How Jonah knew he was ready
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Two years after leaving, Jonah realised he was happy on his own. He wasn't lonely in the evenings any more. He decided to try dating, slowly. On a first date, the woman cancelled at short notice, and he noticed he felt mild disappointment, not panic. Later he met someone who respected his pace, introduced him to her friends and didn't mind when he said no to things.

He said the biggest sign he was ready was that he could walk away from a date that didn't feel right.

## Check yourself first
Before your next date, read the readiness checklist and answer each point honestly.

- Bancroft, L. (2002). *Why Does He Do That?* Putnam (on early warning signs).

=== the-version-of-you-from-before
title: The version of you from before
question: Will I ever be the same after abuse?
seo: Will I Ever Be the Same After Abuse?
short: Will you ever be the same after abuse? Not exactly, and that isn't bad news. Much of what felt lost comes back: humour, joy, confidence, the ability to connect. What's available isn't the old you, but a third version that carries what you've learned and isn't less for it.
---
You look at old photos and wonder where that person went. Will you get back to who you were before?

## Will I ever be the same after abuse?
The honest answer is more hopeful than a flat "no," and more honest than a comforting "yes."

**Much of you comes back.** Your sense of humour. Your capacity for joy. Your ability to trust and connect. Your confidence in your own judgment. For most people these return, rarely in a straight line and rarely on a schedule. The Decoder describes the wearing-away in [identity erosion](/playbook/identity-erosion). It can be reversed.

**Some of it doesn't.** A version of you who never knew what you know now, who has no memory of it, isn't coming back. Nobody who's lived through something big gets to be untouched by it.

## What's available instead?
A third version. One who carries the experience, has learned hard things from it, and isn't lesser for it. Often wiser about people. Clearer about what you will and won't accept. Kinder to others in trouble.

> "Getting back to normal" may not be possible, or even what you want. A life that fits who you are now is a better aim.

## Why does this reframe matter?
Because chasing your old self sets a target you can't reach, and leaves you feeling you're always falling short. Building forward from what's come back, plus what you've learned, is both truer and more achievable.

## How do I meet this new version?
- Notice what's returned. Write it down. It's easy to miss.
- Notice what's new: limits you now hold, people you now spot.
- Try things. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- Be patient with what's still healing. See [What "recovered" means](ch:what-recovered-actually-means).
- Let people meet the new you. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).


## What tends to come back first?
Many people notice small things first: laughing at something on TV, enjoying a meal, sleeping through the night. Bigger things, like confidence in your decisions or trust in new people, usually return more slowly. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

## What about the parts of me the relationship used?
Kindness, loyalty, patience, a habit of forgiving. Controlling people often lean on exactly these. You don't have to give them up. You can keep them and add limits: kind, with a boundary; loyal, to people who earn it.

## What if people say I've changed?
You have. Some people will welcome your new limits. Some won't, especially people who were used to you giving way. Their reaction tells you about them. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## Can anything good come out of this?
Researchers call it post-traumatic growth. Some people report closer relationships, new priorities or a stronger sense of their own strength after trauma. It isn't required, and it doesn't make what happened worth it. Growth and pain can sit side by side.

## What people describe changing
People who've left abuse often say they're:

- more cautious, at least at first
- quicker to notice manipulation
- clearer about what they will and won't accept
- less interested in pleasing everyone
- more compassionate towards others who are struggling

Some of these changes feel like losses. Many become strengths.

## Strengths built in hard times
Surviving abuse takes enormous skill: reading moods, planning, protecting children, holding a household together under pressure. Those skills don't disappear. They can become patience, resourcefulness and resilience in your new life.

## Reclaiming things they ruined
Some places, songs, foods or hobbies may feel tainted by the relationship. You can reclaim them slowly: go back to the place with a friend, listen to the song in a new setting, make the food your own way. Or let some go. Either is fine.

## More than "survivor"
"Survivor" can be a powerful word, but you're more than what happened to you. You're also a parent, a friend, a worker, a person with interests and plans. Over time, the abuse may become one chapter of your story, not the title.

## A letter to yourself
Some people find it helps to write a letter to the person they were before the relationship, or to the person they'll be in five years. What would you want them to know? What are you proud of? Keep the letter, and read it on hard days.

## Write the letter
Write a short letter to yourself in five years. Tell them what you're proud of now, what you're working on, and what you hope they've found. Keep it somewhere you'll find it again.

=== what-recovered-actually-means
title: What "recovered" means
question: What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?
seo: Recovery From Narcissistic Abuse: What It Looks Like
short: Recovery from narcissistic abuse, or any coercive relationship, means getting back choice and capacity, with hard days still allowed: making decisions from your own wishes, trusting your judgment, wanting things, and being present without the past running the show.
---
"You'll know it when you feel it" isn't something you can measure. So here's a working definition of recovery, stated plainly.

## What recovery is not
- **Never having a hard day again.** Nobody gets there, including people who've recovered by any reasonable measure.
- **Never thinking about them.**
- **Forgiving them.** See [Closure you give yourself, and whether you have to forgive](ch:closure-and-forgiveness).
- **Being exactly who you were before.** See [The version of you from before](ch:the-version-of-you-from-before).
- **A finish line on a date.** This book won't give you one.

## What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?
> Recovery is getting back choice and capacity. You make decisions from your own wishes, not from fear or old habit. You trust your judgment, want things and go after them, and the past breaks in less often.

## How do I know I'm recovering?
Some markers people find useful:

- You make decisions without checking what your ex would have thought.
- Hard days come and go, instead of becoming hard months.
- You're curious about new people and situations, as well as careful.
- Your sense of your own judgment has mostly come back.
- You laugh more.
- You can talk about what happened without being swept away by it.
- They take up less of your thinking.

None of these need to be perfect. None mean the hard feelings never return. They mean those feelings have stopped running the show.

## What if I'm not there yet?
Then you're somewhere on the way, which is where most people are. Recovery happens in waves, with good stretches and setbacks. A setback doesn't erase progress. See [The long after](ch:the-long-after), and if you're struggling more than a hard week, [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).


## What slows recovery down?
- Ongoing contact or court cases that keep your alarm running. See [When it stops](ch:when-it-stops).
- Having no one to talk to. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).
- Poor sleep, or depression that hasn't been treated. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).
- Money stress. See [Money from zero](ch:money-from-zero).
- Going back and forth with the person you left. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).

None of these mean you're failing. They're weights. Some can be lifted. Some have to be carried for a while.

## What helps it along?
Safety first, then routine, then people. Research on recovery after trauma describes three stages: getting safe, telling the story and grieving, and reconnecting with ordinary life. They overlap and loop back. You can be making new friends while you're still grieving.

## How do I measure progress without a timeline?
Compare yourself with yourself, months apart. Not with other people, and not with a date. Once a season, write down your answers to three questions:

1. What takes less effort now?
2. What scares me less?
3. What do I want that I couldn't let myself want before?

Keep the answers. On bad days, read them.

## The areas of recovery
Recovery isn't one thing. It tends to happen in several areas, at different speeds:

- **Safety:** feeling and being safe.
- **Body:** sleep, health, energy.
- **Mind:** fewer flashbacks, more calm, trusting your memory.
- **People:** friendships, family, maybe new relationships.
- **Money and work:** stability, independence.
- **Meaning:** a sense of purpose, joy, a future you want.

You may be strong in some and still working on others. That's normal.

## A self-check
Every few months, rate each area from 1 to 5. Don't judge the scores. Look at which have moved, and which might need help next. Keep the ratings, so you can see change over time that's hard to notice day to day.

## Setbacks
Setbacks are part of recovery, not a sign it's failed. A court date, a hoover, an anniversary or a new stress can knock you back for a while. When that happens, go back to what helped before. Each time, the recovery is often faster.

## Recovery with ongoing contact
If you share children with the person you left, contact may continue for years. Recovery is still possible. It may look like contact that no longer dominates your week, handovers that pass without a sleepless night, and a life that's mostly about you and your children, not them.

## Helping others when you're ready
Some people, in time, find meaning in helping others: volunteering, supporting a friend, sharing their story. There's no obligation. If you choose it, wait until you're steady enough that helping doesn't reopen your own wounds.

## How Grace saw her own recovery
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Grace rated her recovery every three months. At first: safety 2, body 1, mind 1, people 2, money 1, meaning 1. A year later: safety 4, body 3, mind 3, people 3, money 2, meaning 3. She still had bad weeks. But the numbers showed what she couldn't feel day to day: she was moving.

She kept rating. The money score was the slowest to change, and she stopped blaming herself for that.

## Rate yourself today
Give each area of recovery a score from 1 to 5 today, and put a reminder in your calendar to do it again in three months.
