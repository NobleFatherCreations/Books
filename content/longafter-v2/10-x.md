=== money-from-zero
title: Money from zero
question: How do I start rebuilding my finances after an abusive relationship?
seo: Rebuilding Finances After an Abusive Relationship
short: Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship starts with an honest list of what you have and owe, a budget built on today's numbers, and small steps on credit and savings. Coerced debt can be disputed. Starting from little is a common result of financial abuse, not a personal failing.
---
Rebuilding finances after an abusive relationship often starts from very little. Maybe you left with far less than you had, or with nothing. Maybe you were never allowed to see a bank statement. Maybe there are debts in your name you never agreed to.

Here's the good news, and it's a bit dull. Rebuilding money is slow and boring, and slow and boring is what works. Nobody's making a film about your spreadsheet, and that's fine.

## Where do I start rebuilding my finances?
1. **List what you have:** income, benefits, savings, anything you own.
2. **List what you owe:** every debt, with the lender, the amount and the minimum payment.
3. **Get your credit report** from each credit agency (free in most countries) and check every entry.
4. **Build a budget on today's numbers,** not the life you hope to have next year. Essentials first: housing, food, utilities, transport, the children.
5. **Open accounts in your name only,** at a bank they never used. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).
6. **Protect your income at work,** including any leave you're entitled to. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

> Boring works. A slow, steady rebuild beats a dramatic fix almost every time.

## What about debts I didn't agree to?
This is called coerced debt: loans, cards or bills run up in your name through pressure, lies or fraud. You can dispute them with the lender and the credit agency. A police report, a protective order or a letter from a domestic abuse service can back you up. What counts as proof varies by country and lender.

In the UK, Surviving Economic Abuse has guidance and templates. In the US, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how to dispute errors. The Decoder lays out the wider pattern in [Financial Control](/playbook/financial-control).

## How do I rebuild credit?
- Pay every bill on time, even the tiny ones.
- Keep any credit card balance low.
- Think about a credit-builder card or loan, if the fees are low.
- Make sure you're on the electoral roll or its equivalent, where that affects credit.
- Give it time. Credit scores recover over months and years, not weekends.

## How do I start saving?
Start tiny. A few pounds or dollars a week into a separate account builds the habit and a small buffer for emergencies. Many domestic abuse services can point you to emergency grants and matched-savings schemes.

## Where can I get free money advice?
- **United Kingdom:** Citizens Advice, StepChange, National Debtline.
- **United States:** non-profit credit counselling agencies (look for ones accredited by the National Foundation for Credit Counseling).
- **Australia:** the National Debt Helpline, 1800 007 007.
- **Ireland:** MABS, the Money Advice and Budgeting Service.
- **Canada:** non-profit credit counselling agencies.

## Is there funding to help me leave or start again?
Often, yes, though it varies a lot from place to place. Things to ask about:

- **Emergency grants** from domestic abuse charities, for deposits, travel or replacing essentials.
- **Benefits and housing help.** Many countries have faster routes for people fleeing abuse. See [Housing and benefits](ch:housing-and-benefits).
- **Victims' funds** where a crime was reported, in some states and countries.
- **Charity funds** for furniture, school uniforms or white goods.

A domestic abuse helpline knows what exists where you live. Ask them straight out: "What funding is there for someone who has left an abusive relationship?" [Find help](ch:find-help) lists the numbers.

## What are the steps to financial independence?
Small, and in this order:

1. A bank account only you can see, with paperless statements going to an email they don't know.
2. A list of every debt and every bill, even the ones you've been hiding in a drawer.
3. Your credit report, checked for anything opened in your name.
4. One small regular saving, even a tiny one, so the habit exists.
5. A plan for income, whether that's benefits, child support, work or training. See [Work again](ch:work-again).

Financial independence after leaving an abusive relationship rarely arrives in one go. Each step makes the next one less scary.

## Why does starting from zero feel so shameful?
Because it can feel like proof you failed. It isn't. Financial abuse leaves people with less money, wrecked credit and debts they didn't choose, and [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel) covers how it works.

Starting again from very little is one of the most common things a controlling relationship leaves behind. It says plenty about what was done to you and nothing about who you are.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* covers building a "freedom fund" and separate accounts in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top). [Untangling: the house, the car, the accounts and the paperwork](ch:untangling-the-paperwork) covers separating what's left.

## A first budget
A budget after leaving doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be real.

1. Write down everything coming in each month: wages, benefits, child support.
2. Write down the fixed costs: rent, bills, phone, travel, childcare, debt payments.
3. Write down a realistic amount for food and essentials. Realistic means the food shop you do, not the one you'd do if you were a better person.
4. Look at what's left. If it's negative, that's information. It tells you what help to ask for.

Look at it again every month. It will change as things settle.

## Bank accounts and identity safety
- Use an account at a different bank from the person you left, if you can.
- Pick new security answers they couldn't guess. They know your mother's maiden name and the name of your first pet.
- Turn on alerts for every transaction.
- Ask your bank whether it has a domestic abuse support team. Many do, and they can help with joint accounts and coerced debt.
- Check your credit report every few months for anything opened in your name.

## Benefits and tax credits to check
People leaving abuse are often entitled to help they've never heard of: child benefit, housing help, income top-ups, council tax or local tax reductions, free school meals, help with childcare costs.

- **United Kingdom:** free online calculators from charities like Turn2us or entitledto show what you might get.
- **United States:** use a government benefits finder and ask your local domestic violence program about state programmes.
- **Elsewhere:** search "[your country] benefits calculator" or ask a domestic abuse service.

## Income from child support
Child support is your children's money. It isn't a favour from your ex. Use an official service to set and collect it, so the money doesn't depend on contact. See [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel).

## Scams and predatory lenders
People rebuilding after abuse get targeted by high-cost lenders, "credit repair" firms and plain scams. Be wary of:

- loans that are easy to get but expensive to repay
- anyone charging to fix your credit record
- offers that need payment up front
- pressure to decide today

Free debt advice services can do what the paid firms promise, without the fees.

## A small cushion
An emergency fund, even a tiny one, makes the next crisis less frightening. Put a small amount each week into a separate account. Round up purchases, or bank any unexpected money. The habit matters more than the amount.

::: scene
## How Bea rebuilt from debt
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Bea left with £4,000 of debt her ex had run up in her name, and no savings. A free debt adviser contacted the lenders, explained the coerced debt and arranged payments she could afford. Two lenders wrote off part of it.

She opened a new account at a different bank, set up a small weekly saving and checked her credit report every three months. Two years later the debt was nearly gone and she had a few hundred in savings. It didn't feel rich. It felt like hers.
:::

::: action
## Write the budget tonight
Grab one sheet of paper. Write three lines: what comes in, what goes out, what's left. If the numbers don't work, that sheet is what you take to a free money adviser.
:::

When you're ready to look at income, [Work again](ch:work-again) covers leave, safety at work and that awkward gap on your CV.

=== work-again
title: Work again
question: Can I take domestic violence leave, and how do I explain a gap?
seo: Domestic Violence Leave and Going Back to Work
short: Domestic violence leave exists in a growing number of places, sometimes paid: Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, Northern Ireland and parts of Canada and the US. Whether or not you have a legal right, many employers will help with time off and safety. You never have to tell an employer the whole story.
---
You're at your desk with a court letter in your bag and a message from them lighting up your phone. Getting back to work, or staying in work, after leaving can be hard. There are court dates, a house move, the children, and a tiredness that coffee doesn't touch. You may also have a gap to explain.

## Can I take domestic violence leave?
In some places, yes:

- **Australia:** employees can take 10 days of paid family and domestic violence leave a year.
- **New Zealand:** 10 days of paid domestic violence leave a year.
- **Ireland:** 5 days of paid domestic violence leave a year, since November 2023.
- **Northern Ireland:** a 2022 law will give 10 days of paid domestic abuse leave a year once its regulations take effect. Check whether it's in force yet.
- **Canada:** federally regulated employees have paid leave, and most provinces offer some, partly paid or unpaid.
- **United States:** no federal right, but several states require time off for survivors to go to court, see a doctor or move.
- **England, Wales and Scotland:** no general legal right to domestic abuse leave yet, but many employers have policies. Ask HR.

Search "[your country or state] domestic violence leave" for the current rules.

## Do I have to tell my employer what happened?
No. "I'm dealing with a personal family matter" covers almost every situation. You choose what to share. You might share a bit more if you need leave, flexibility or safety measures, and even then the details can stay yours.

> What you share about this part of your life at work is your call. Share what helps you, not what you think you owe.

## How do I stay safe at work?
If the person you left knows where you work:

- tell security or reception, and give them a photo
- ask to have calls screened, and your details taken off public directories
- ask about changing your hours, location or parking
- keep a record of anything that happens at work. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).

The work section of [A safety plan for after you've left](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) goes further.

## How do I explain a gap in my CV?
Keep it short and point it forwards:

> **Say:** "I took some time out to deal with a family matter, which is now resolved. I'm ready to focus on work, and I've kept my skills up by [anything you did]."

Interviewers rarely push. If one does, "It was personal, and it's behind me" closes the door politely.

## What if my ex is disrupting my job?
Ringing your employer, turning up at your work, or making handovers clash with your shifts are all ways of hitting your income. That's financial abuse, covered in [Money as the channel](ch:money-as-the-channel). Tell your employer briefly what's going on, and keep a record of every incident.

::: bridge atwill:a-bad-job-versus-a-coercive-one
If your ex is going after your job, or work itself has started to feel like another controlling relationship, [At Will](/atwill/a-bad-job-versus-a-coercive-one) is another book in this library. It covers workplace rights and protections in depth.
:::

## What employers can do
Many employers can do more than people expect:

- flexible hours for court dates and appointments
- paid or unpaid leave
- a change of shift, team or location
- screening calls and emails, and not confirming you work there
- security at the entrance, or someone to walk you to your car
- an employee assistance programme with free counselling

Ask HR whether there's a domestic abuse policy. In Australia and New Zealand, employees are entitled to paid family and domestic violence leave. Some US states and other countries have their own rules.

## Returning after years out
If you were kept from working, or had to leave a job because of the abuse, going back can feel enormous.

- Start with what you can manage: part-time, volunteering, a short course.
- List what you did during the gap: raising children, running a home, caring, managing money under pressure. Those are real skills, and anyone who's budgeted a family on almost nothing can handle a spreadsheet.
- Look for return-to-work programmes, often run by charities, colleges or employers.

## Training and education
Plenty of people use the years after leaving to train or study. Look for free or low-cost courses at local colleges and online, grants for adults going back into education, and adult learning services. Some domestic abuse services run their own employment and training programmes.

## Interviews: the gap question
This is the longer version, for when you want to fill the gap with something good:

> **Say:** "I took some time out for family reasons, which are now resolved. During that time I [cared for my children / managed a household / volunteered]. I'm ready to focus on my career, and I've been building my skills through [course or activity]."

You don't have to mention the abuse. Practise the answer out loud until it bores you. By then it'll come out calm, even with nerves.

## Self-employment and safety online
If you work for yourself, your name, photo and location may be easy to find online. Use a business address, not your home. Keep personal and business social media separate. Think about what clients can see, such as your schedule, where you are, and your children.

::: scene
## How Omar went back to work
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Omar hadn't worked for six years, because his ex had made it impossible. After leaving, he started volunteering two mornings a week at a food bank. It gave him a reference and a reason to get up.

He took a free course at the local college, then applied for part-time jobs. In interviews he said he'd taken time out for family reasons and talked about the volunteering. His first job paid little. It also gave him money of his own for the first time in years.
:::

::: action
## One step towards work
Open your calendar now and book one step for this month: a volunteering shift, a course enquiry, an hour on your CV or a call to a careers service.
:::

Once money and work have some footing, [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours) is about making the place you sleep feel safe.

=== a-home-that-feels-like-yours
title: A home that feels like yours
question: How do I start feeling safe at home after abuse?
seo: Feeling Safe at Home After Abuse
short: Feeling safe at home after abuse can take time, whether you've moved somewhere new or stayed in a place you shared. Practical security, small changes that make the space yours, and calm routines all help your body learn that home is safe now.
---
Feeling safe at home after abuse takes longer than changing the locks. You've got a door that locks now, and you still lie awake listening. Or you're still in the home you shared, and every room has a memory sitting in it.

## Why doesn't home feel safe yet?
Your nervous system learned that home was where the danger lived. It takes time, and a lot of calm, uneventful days, to learn otherwise. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## What practical steps help?
- **Change the locks** if they ever had a key, plus the alarm and garage codes.
- **Add what makes you feel secure:** a door chain, a video doorbell you control, outdoor lights, a window lock.
- **Know your exits,** and keep your phone charged near you at night.
- **Check for devices** they set up. See [Surveillance and technology](ch:surveillance-and-technology).
- **Ask about help with costs.** Some domestic abuse services and councils pay for security improvements, sometimes called a sanctuary scheme.

## How do I make it feel like mine?
- **Change something you can see:** move the furniture, paint a wall, buy new bedding.
- **Remove or store reminders.** You don't have to keep their gifts, their photos or the chair they always sat in.
- **Bring in things you love.** Plants, music, colours, smells. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- **Make a calm corner** to rest in: a chair, a blanket, a lamp.
- **Set your own rules.** Eat when you like. Play music loud. Leave the dishes till morning. Have cereal for dinner standing up. Nobody is keeping score any more.

> Small choices that are yours alone add up: the mug, the lamp, the time you go to bed.

## What if I'm still in the home we shared?
Reclaiming a shared home is hard. Some people change one room at a time. Some pick a "clearing" day and get a friend round to help. Some find they need to move eventually. All of those are fine.

## What helps at night?
A routine, a light within reach, a door you've checked once, and something calming to listen to. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).

## What if I've had to move somewhere I don't like?
A refuge, temporary housing, a smaller flat, a relative's spare room. It can feel like a step backwards. Make one corner yours with a photo, a lamp, a blanket or a smell you love. Home can start with one safe spot.

## What if they know where I live?
Keep the security steps from your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) in place, and record any visits. If they turn up or watch the house, that can be stalking. See [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

Ask about a protective order that covers your address. What's available, and what it's called, depends on where you live, so [Where to find the right body](ch:where-to-find-the-right-body) is the place to start.

## What if the children bring the other home with them?
Bags, gifts and devices travel between homes. Check any device that comes back from a visit. See [Device and account audit](ch:device-and-account-audit). Let the children keep comfort items from both homes. Your rules apply in your house.

If anyone ever calls the device check snooping, it's the same safety check you'd run on your own phone, done for the children's sake.

## How do I sleep alone after years on alert?
Some people find a night light, white noise or a pet helps. [Sleep](ch:sleep) has more.

After years of listening for footsteps or a key in the door, your body may stay half awake. Check the doors and windows once, then say out loud that they're checked. It feels daft. It helps. Keep your phone charged within reach, with emergency numbers saved.

Some people sleep better with a door alarm, a camera doorbell, or a radio playing low. If you wake at 3am, get up for a while and do something dull in low light, then go back to bed.

## A first-week setup
In your first week in a new place, focus on:

- a bed and bedding you like
- a working lock, and a chain or door alarm if it helps
- a few things that feel like you: a picture, a plant, a mug
- a light by the bed
- a list of local numbers by the door

The rest can come slowly.

## Furnishing from nothing
If you left with little, help exists:

- furniture charities and reuse schemes
- local crisis funds that provide beds, cookers and fridges
- community groups and online free-to-collect sites
- domestic abuse services that keep donation stores

Ask a domestic abuse worker what's available near you. Don't be shy about using it. That's exactly what it's there for.

## Sounds and smells
Senses carry memory. A smell like their aftershave, or a door that creaks the way theirs did, can put you on edge. Change what you can: new scents, different music, a new routine. Get to know the sounds of your new home during the day, so they're familiar by night.

## Inviting people in
Having friends over helps a place feel like home. Start small: one person, one cup of tea. Only share your address with people you trust completely, and ask them not to post photos that show where you live.

## The children's rooms
Let the children help choose colours, bedding or posters, even on a tiny budget. A space that's theirs helps them settle. Keep a few familiar things from before, if you have them. Put up photos of them, and of people they love, where they can see them.

::: action
## One thing that's yours
This week, buy, find or make one thing for your home that the person you left would never have allowed. Put it where you'll see it every day.
:::

Next up, the things you couldn't bring with you: [Pets and the things left behind](ch:pets-and-the-things-left-behind).

=== pets-and-the-things-left-behind
title: Pets and the things left behind
question: My ex took my dog, or won't give back my things. What can I do?
seo: Ex Took My Dog or My Things? Pets and Belongings After Leaving
short: If your ex took your dog or is holding your belongings, you have options. Some protective orders can cover pets, pet foster schemes help people leaving abuse, and police in many places can attend while you collect belongings. Threats to pets are a recognised warning sign.
---
You left in a hurry, or you couldn't take everything. "My ex took my dog" is one of the most common things people say after leaving. Funny how someone who never once walked the dog suddenly can't live without him. Or they've got your grandmother's ring, your documents, your clothes. And they know exactly how much it matters.

## Why are pets used against people leaving abuse?
Because pets are family, and controlling people use whatever matters most. Threats to hurt a pet, refusing to give it back, or using it to make you come back are all common. Researchers have found that many people delay leaving because they're afraid for their animals.

The Decoder describes the general pattern in [Children as Leverage](/playbook/children-as-leverage) (using what you love to pull you back), and pets get used the same way.

Threats to harm or kill a pet are also a warning sign of wider danger. See [Warning signs to take seriously](ch:warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

## What can I do about my pet?
- **Protective orders:** in many US states, and some other places, a protective order can include pets. Ask when you apply. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders).
- **Ownership records:** microchip registration, vet records, purchase receipts and licences in your name help show the animal is yours.
- **Pet foster schemes:** some charities look after pets while their owners are in refuge or temporary housing. In the UK, there's Dogs Trust's Freedom Project and Cats Protection's Paws Protect. In the US, RedRover runs programmes and grants, and many shelters now take pets. Ask a domestic abuse service.
- **Police:** if a pet is being harmed or threatened, report it.

## How do I get my belongings back?
- **Ask in writing,** once, with a list and a proposed date. Keep it brief and polite.
- **Ask the police to attend** while you collect your things. Many forces will, and it's sometimes called a civil standby or police-assisted collection.
- **Send someone else:** a friend or relative, with your list.
- **Use your lawyer,** especially for valuables, documents or anything caught up in a divorce.

> **Say:** "I'd like to collect the items on the list below. Could I come on Saturday at 10am with a friend? Or, if you'd prefer, you could leave them with my sister."

If they ever show that message around, it reads as calm and fair, because it is. If they destroy or sell your belongings, that may be criminal damage or theft, depending on where you live. Keep a record.

## What about letting go?
Some things you'll get back. Some you won't. If losses might matter in court, write them down in your [record](ch:what-to-document-and-how). Losing them is a real grief, especially things that held memories.

Some people find it helps to list what was lost, then decide what's worth fighting for and what to let go. Your safety is worth more than any object.

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* includes pets in its go-bag list in [Appendix B, Liberation Logistics](/feminine/liberation-logistics#top).

## Pet custody and ownership
In many places the law treats pets as property, so ownership matters. Keep proof that a pet is yours: purchase or adoption records, vet bills, microchip registration, insurance. In many US states, protective orders can include pets, and some states let courts consider a pet's welfare in a divorce. Ask your lawyer to include pets in any order.

## A police escort to collect things
If you need to collect belongings from a home you've left, ask the police whether they can come with you. In many places they can keep the peace while you collect essentials. Arrange it in advance, bring a list, and take someone with you. Don't go alone.

## What to prioritise
If you only get one chance, take:

- documents: ID, birth certificates, passports, financial papers
- medicine and medical equipment
- children's essentials and comfort items
- irreplaceable things: photos, heirlooms, a few sentimental items
- tools of your work: laptop, equipment

Furniture, clothes and most household stuff can be replaced. The sofa was never that comfy anyway.

## Photos and sentimental things
Losing photos and keepsakes can hurt more than anything else. Ask relatives and friends for copies of photos they have. Check old social media and cloud accounts you can still get into. Some people start a new album of the years since leaving. It won't replace what's gone, and it does start a new record.

## When things are destroyed
Some abusers destroy belongings, or a pet's things, to hurt the person who left. Photograph any damage you see, and report it. In many places, destroying someone's property is a crime. Grieve what's lost. The memories stay yours.

::: scene
## How Chloe got her dog back
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Chloe left, her ex kept the dog and said she'd never see him again. She had the vet records and microchip registration in her name, plus the receipt from the rescue centre.

Where she lived (the rules vary a lot), a protective order could cover pets, so her lawyer included the dog in her application. The court ordered the dog returned to her.

Not everyone gets that outcome. Chloe said the paperwork she'd kept "just in case" made all the difference.
:::

::: action
## Gather the proof
Find one piece of proof that your pet is yours (adoption papers, a vet record, the microchip number) and take a photo of it now. Email it to an account only you can open.
:::

=== holidays-and-the-first-year
title: Holidays and the first year's calendar
question: How do I get through the first holidays after the divorce?
seo: First Holidays After Divorce or Leaving Abuse: A Plan
short: The first holidays after divorce or leaving an abusive relationship are often among the hardest days of the year: old traditions, children away, family pressure, and contact from your ex. Plan them early, decide how the children's time works in writing, and make a small new tradition of your own.
---
The first Christmas. The first birthday. The first school holidays. The first anniversary of the day you left. The first year is full of firsts, and each one can sneak up on you.

## Why are the first holidays so hard?
- Old traditions remind you of what's gone.
- The children may be with the other parent.
- Family gatherings bring questions and pressure.
- Your ex may use the day for contact, guilt or a row. Hoovering often spikes around holidays. See [Hoovering](ch:hoovering).

## How do I plan the first holidays after divorce?
**Settle the children's time early, in writing.** Who has them when, where handovers happen, and what happens about presents. A clear plan leaves less to fight about. See [Apps, calendars and the parenting plan](ch:apps-calendars-and-the-parenting-plan).

**Plan your own day,** especially if the children are away. Spend it with people who feel safe, or plan a quiet day that's deliberately yours. Don't leave it empty by accident.

**Decide about contact in advance.** If they message, what will you do? Usually nothing, or a short reply about the children only. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use).

**Lower the bar.** It doesn't have to be perfect. A smaller, calmer holiday is fine. Shop-bought mince pies are fine.

> You're allowed to do this year differently. You're allowed to do it badly. You're allowed to skip it.

## What about family pressure?
There's always one relative who leans in over the roast potatoes and asks, "So what happened, then?" Have one line ready:

> **Say:** "I'd rather not talk about it today. Tell me about you."

If a gathering feels unsafe or too much, you can leave early, or not go.

## How do I make new traditions?
Start small: a walk on the morning of the holiday, a meal you love, a film, a phone call with a friend. New traditions take a few years to feel like yours. That's fine.

## What about the other firsts?
Plan for anniversaries, the children's birthdays, and the day you left. See [The anniversary effect](ch:the-anniversary-effect). Put each one in your calendar a week ahead, so it can't ambush you.

## What about presents for the children?
Agree in writing who buys what, or let each parent do their own. Don't compete. Children remember calm more than gifts.

## What if the other parent uses the holiday to cause trouble?
Late returns, changed plans, guilt-trip messages. Stick to the arrangement, write down what happens, and reply briefly. See [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use). If it breaks a court order, report it.

## What about the holidays when I don't have the children?
They can be the loneliest days of the year. Plan ahead: friends, volunteering, a trip, a film marathon in your pyjamas, a long walk. Some people mark the day in a different way on purpose.

## What about New Year and other fresh-start days?
Big milestone days come with pressure to feel better. You don't have to make resolutions. One small intention is enough.

New Year, birthdays and the start of spring can bring pressure to have "moved on", or to have a shiny plan for a new life. You need neither.

Pick one small thing: sleep a bit better, see one friend a week, learn one new thing. Looking back at how far you've come often feels better than setting big goals for the year ahead.

## A month-by-month first year
- **Months 1 to 3:** survive. Basic routines, safety, money, children. Skip anything optional.
- **Months 4 to 6:** the first big dates often land here: birthdays, a holiday, a court hearing. Plan for each.
- **Months 7 to 9:** things may feel steadier, and then a dip arrives. Dips are normal.
- **Months 10 to 12:** the anniversary of leaving. Plan the day with care. Look back at how far you've come.

Everyone's year is different. This is a rough shape, not a timetable.

## Birthdays
Your own birthday may feel strange: the first without them, or the first in years without having to put on a show. Plan something small you'd enjoy. For the children's birthdays, follow the court order or agreement, and keep the day about them, even if handovers are tense.

## School events
Plays, sports days and parents' evenings can put you face to face with the other parent. Sit apart, arrive with someone, and keep any exchange polite and short. If there's a protective order, tell the school before the event. What those orders are called varies by country, and the school only needs a copy and the dates.

## Gifts and money pressure
You may feel pressure to make up for the separation with big gifts. Children usually remember time and attention more than presents. Set a budget. Let the other parent buy the giant inflatable reindeer if they want to.

## After the first year
Many people say the second year is easier, though not always. You'll have been through each date once, and you'll know what helps. Keep the plans that worked. Drop the ones that didn't.

## The first holiday alone
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

The children were with their father for the first Christmas after the separation. Hannah dreaded it, so she planned the day: a walk with a friend in the morning, a film she'd always wanted to see, dinner with her brother's family. She let herself cry when she needed to. In the evening she video-called the children and kept it cheerful.

It wasn't a good day. It was a day she got through, and the next year she knew how.

## Plan the next big date
Open your calendar and find the next holiday or birthday. In the note for that day, type who you'll be with and one thing you'll do.

=== your-own-tastes-rediscovered
title: Your own tastes, rediscovered
question: How do I find myself again after an abusive relationship?
seo: Finding Yourself After an Abusive Relationship
short: Finding yourself after an abusive relationship often happens in small surprises: music you'd stopped playing, food you love, an opinion you'd stopped saying out loud. Coercive control narrows what feels safe to want. Getting it back is allowed to be light, and even fun.
---
Finding yourself after an abusive relationship rarely happens all at once. One day you catch yourself humming a song you haven't let yourself play in years.

Or you order the thing you'd never have ordered with them. Or you eat a whole dinner and nobody comments on the salt, the portion or the way you hold your fork. Or you realise you have an opinion, and you say it out loud.

## Why did I lose track of myself?
Coercive relationships often shrink what feels safe to want, prefer or enjoy. Your clothes, your music, your friends, your opinions: each one slowly bent towards whatever kept the peace. It happens so gradually you may not have noticed. The Decoder calls it [Identity Erosion](/playbook/identity-erosion).

## How do I find myself again?
You don't have to find a whole new self. Start with small curiosities:

- **Try things without a goal.** A class, a recipe, a walk somewhere new. Do it because it sounds good, not because it's homework.
- **Notice what you like,** in a note on your phone: a colour, a song, a smell, a kind of day.
- **Go back to old loves.** What did you enjoy before the relationship?
- **Say an opinion out loud,** somewhere safe, and notice how it feels.
- **Let your home show who you are.** See [A home that feels like yours](ch:a-home-that-feels-like-yours).

> This part is allowed to be light. Not every piece of recovery has to be heavy.

## What if I don't know what I like?
Join the club. It's common. Treat it as an experiment. Try things and keep what works. Some of what you find will surprise you, and that's half the fun. People who were cut off from others often make new friends through the same experiments. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).

## What if it feels selfish?
It isn't. Rediscovering what you enjoy is part of getting your life back. People who are happier in their own skin also tend to be steadier parents, friends and partners. See [The version of you from before](ch:the-version-of-you-from-before).

## What small experiments can I try?
- Buy one thing in a shop that only you would choose.
- Cook a meal they hated, or wouldn't allow.
- Make a playlist of songs from before the relationship. Yes, including that one.
- Rearrange one room for your own comfort.
- Say no to an invitation you don't want, and yes to one you do.
- Spend an afternoon with no plan, and notice what you drift towards.

## What if what I liked before doesn't fit any more?
Tastes change anyway, with age and experience. Some old loves will come back. Some won't. New ones will turn up. You're living a life, and nobody needs you to rebuild a museum of who you were.

## Why do I feel guilty enjoying things?
Some people feel they don't deserve fun while life is still hard, or while a court case is running. Enjoying something doesn't prove you're "fine", and it doesn't prove nothing happened. It's part of getting your life back. See [Guilt and shame](ch:guilt-and-shame).

## A list of tiny experiments
Pick one a week. Notice how each one feels, without deciding anything big.

- Order something you've never tried at a café.
- Listen to an album from a genre you'd never pick.
- Walk a different route home.
- Rearrange one corner of a room.
- Wear a colour you used to avoid.
- Go to a free museum or library talk.
- Cook a meal from a country you've never visited.
- Spend an hour doing something you loved as a child. Colouring in counts.

## Music, food, clothes and rooms
These are the everyday places where taste shows. They're also where control often reached: the music you weren't allowed, the clothes they mocked, the food you stopped cooking. Reclaim one thing at a time. Put the song on loud. Buy the shirt. Paint the wall that colour.

## Ideas that cost nothing
- borrowing books, films and music from the library
- free walks, parks and community events
- online classes and talks
- swapping clothes with friends
- trying a new recipe from what's in the cupboard

Finding your tastes doesn't need money. It needs curiosity and permission, and you've got both.

## The body: what feels good
After abuse, many people feel cut off from their own bodies. Try small, kind things: a warm bath, a stretch, a walk in the sun, a soft blanket, dancing badly in the kitchen. Notice what your body likes, and do more of it.

## Noticing your own "no"
Part of finding your tastes is noticing what you don't like. A small "no" counts: turning down a plan, sending back a meal, saying "I'd rather not." Each one strengthens the sense that your preferences matter.

*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

=== trust-as-a-practice
title: Trust as a practice
question: How do I deal with trust issues after abuse?
seo: Trust Issues After Abuse: How to Trust Again, Slowly
short: Trust issues after abuse make sense: trust was used against you. You don't have to choose between total guard and total openness. Trust can be given in small amounts, matched to how people behave over time, and adjusted as you learn more.
---
You don't trust easily any more. Or you trust too fast and get burned. Either way, the way you used to trust doesn't feel safe now.

## Why do I have trust issues after abuse?
Because trust was used against you. Someone you let in used what you told them, and used your good faith. Being careful now is a sensible lesson, and there's nothing wrong with you for having learned it.

## How do I learn to trust again?
Think of trust as a dial you can turn up a little at a time, instead of a switch that's either on or off:

1. **Start small.** Share something low-stakes, or rely on someone for something minor.
2. **Watch what happens.** Did they keep it private? Did they turn up? Did they respect your "no"?
3. **Adjust.** Turn the dial up a little if they handled it well. Turn it down if they didn't.
4. **Repeat,** over weeks and months.

> Trust built slowly, on evidence, lasts longer than trust given all at once, and is easier to live with than trust never given.

*The Weighing*, in this library, sets out this way of judging people in [Trust as a skill you rebuild](/scale/trust-as-a-skill-you-rebuild).

## What signs are worth noticing?
**Signs to trust a little more:** they keep small promises, respect your limits, act the same in private as in public, and take "no" without sulking.

**Signs to slow down:** pressure, rushing closeness, big promises early, anger when you set a limit, stories that don't add up. See [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not).

## What about trusting myself?
Often that's the trust that took the biggest hit. It comes back the same way: small decisions, noticing they turned out fine, and building from there. See [Memory, and doubting yourself](ch:memory).

## What if someone from the past says they've changed?
Use the same dial. Real change shows over time, consistently, without pressure on you. The section on telling hoovering from real change in [Hoovering](ch:hoovering) goes into it.

## How do I trust my own judgment about people?
Write down your first impression of someone new. Look back after a month. You'll often find your first read was right. That record rebuilds confidence in your judgment, one person at a time.

## What about doctors, lawyers and therapists?
The same dial works. Share a little first, watch how they respond, and change provider if they brush you off. You're allowed to ask for someone else.

## What if I keep choosing people who hurt me?
Lots of survivors worry about this. Patterns from earlier in life can make some kinds of attention feel familiar. Knowing the early warning signs and going slowly both help. See [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not). Therapy focused on relationships can help too. See [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

## What if I want to keep records on someone new?
With the person you left, records protect you. If you notice an urge to document a new friend or partner, pay attention to it. It may be a sign to slow down, or a sign that something is wrong.

## Trust in small steps
If the dial doesn't click for you, try a ladder, one rung at a time:

1. Share something small and low-risk, like a preference or a minor worry.
2. Watch what they do with it. Do they remember? Respect it? Keep it to themselves?
3. If it goes well, share a bit more.
4. If it doesn't, step back down a rung.

You don't have to decide on day one whether someone is trustworthy. You can let them show you. You don't need a background check on the new colleague who offers you a biscuit. Accepting the biscuit is a very low rung.

## Trust and boundaries
A trustworthy person accepts your "no", doesn't push for more than you want to share, and doesn't punish you for having limits. If someone pushes against your boundaries early on, that tells you something useful.

## Rebuilding trust with family
If family let you down during the relationship or after, trust can take time to rebuild. Look for steady actions: turning up, respecting your wishes, not passing on information. An apology matters less than what they do next.

## Trust in institutions after being let down
If the police, the courts or services failed you, trusting any of them again can feel impossible. That makes sense. A few ways forward: find one professional who does get it and work through them, bring a support worker to appointments, and ask for everything in writing.

## Trusting your gut without over-reading
After abuse, your alarm system may go off often, sometimes at things that aren't dangerous. It helps to pause and check: "Is this person doing something harmful, or reminding me of someone who did?" Your gut is worth listening to. It's also worth checking against what's happening in front of you.

::: honest
## Gut feeling, or old alarm?
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

After abuse, your alarm can go off at real danger and at ordinary moments that only look like it. When you feel suspicious of someone, ask yourself:

- What did this person do, as opposed to what it reminds me of?
- Have they done it more than once?
- Would someone I trust read it the same way?
- Am I testing them in ways they couldn't know about?

There are no right answers to get. Listen to your gut, then check it against what's happening in front of you. If an answer stings, give it a day, or talk it over with one person you trust.
:::

::: scene
## Climbing the ladder with a new friend
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

When Alice met a new friend at work, she shared small things first: a film she liked, a worry about a deadline. The friend remembered and asked about them. Alice shared a bit more: that she'd left a difficult relationship. The friend didn't pry or pass it on.

=== dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not
title: Dating, when you're ready and when you're not
question: When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?
seo: Dating After an Abusive Relationship: Readiness and Red Flags
short: There's no set time for dating after an abusive relationship. Two pressures get in the way: rushing to prove you're fine, and waiting for a perfect "ready" that never comes. Better signs are curiosity about new people, and being able to handle ordinary uncertainty. Know the early warning signs, and go at your own pace.
---
Some people want to date straight away. Some can't imagine ever doing it again. Most land somewhere in between, scrolling an app at midnight and wondering if they're doing it wrong.

## When am I ready for dating after an abusive relationship?
There's no number of months. Two pressures can push you either way:

- **Rushing to prove you're fine.** Dating soon can be a way of saying "look, I've moved on," to yourself or to other people. That's a different reason from wanting to meet someone.
- **Waiting for a perfect "ready."** The idea that one day you'll be fully healed and allowed to date. For most people that clean moment never comes.

More useful signs:

- You can be curious about a new person, instead of measuring them against your ex.
- You can live with the ordinary uncertainty of early dating without it setting your alarm system off too hard. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).
- You'd be okay if it didn't work out.

None of these have to be perfect.

## What are the early warning signs?
Abusive relationships often start with intensity. Watch for:

- **Love bombing:** floods of attention, gifts, "I've never felt this way," all very fast. The Decoder's page is [Love Bombing](/playbook/love-bombing).
- **Future faking:** big plans very early, like moving in, marriage or children. The Decoder's page is [Future Faking](/playbook/future-faking).
- **Pushing past "no,"** even over small things.
- **Jealousy dressed up as love.**
- **Running down every ex** as crazy.
- **Wanting you to themselves,** and resenting your friends.
- **Your gut saying "slow down."**

> Someone who's right for you will be comfortable going at your pace.

## How do I date safely?
- Meet in public, and tell a friend where you'll be.
- Keep your address private until you trust them.
- Go at your own pace with physical intimacy. See [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse) if that's relevant to you.
- Use the trust dial in [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).
- Get yourself there and back, and keep your drink with you.
- If something feels off, trust it and leave. You don't owe anyone an explanation.

## When do I tell them about my past?
When it feels right, and in proportion to how serious it's getting. A first coffee isn't the place for the whole story. See [Telling someone new](ch:telling-someone-new). If your ex might contact them, tell them earlier. See [The new partner](ch:the-new-partner).

## What if I never want to date again?
That's a real choice, and a full life doesn't require a partner.

Some people find, after leaving, that they're happier alone, and stay that way. Others feel like that for a year and then change their minds. Both are fine. Friends, family, community, work, children and your own interests can make a full life.

If people keep telling you to "get back out there", you can say, "I'm happy as I am." If the feeling comes more from fear than from choice, a therapist can help you tell the difference.

## What if I'm drawn to the same type?
Notice it without beating yourself up. The pull towards intensity or charm can feel like chemistry when it's familiarity. Go slower with people who feel exciting in the same way your ex did, and give the steadier ones a second date.

## How do I date online safely?
- Use your first name only, and a photo that isn't on your other profiles.
- Do a video call before you meet.
- Meet in a public place, and tell a friend where you'll be and when you'll check in.
- Keep your address, workplace and children's school private until you trust them.
- Search their name and photos if anything feels off.

And if a profile announces "NO DRAMA" in capital letters, you're allowed to swipe on by.

## What if a new partner does something that reminds me of my ex?
Ask yourself whether it's a pattern, or a single moment that set off an old alarm. Talk about it calmly. A good partner will listen and adjust. If it's a pattern, take that seriously. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

## A readiness checklist
You might be ready to date if:

- you're dating because you want to, not to fill a gap or prove something
- you can picture being happy single
- contact with the person you left is limited or stable
- you can say no without fear
- you have people to talk to about how dates go

You don't need every box ticked. If several feel a long way off, it may be worth waiting.

## Green flags
Alongside the warning signs, look for the good ones:

- they respect your pace
- they're consistent: they do what they say
- they're curious about you without prying
- they handle "no" well
- they have friends and a life of their own
- you feel calm, and like yourself, around them

## Sex and intimacy after abuse
Intimacy can feel complicated after abuse, especially if there was sexual abuse or coercion. Go at your own pace. Agree on a way to pause or stop, and expect it to be respected.

If memories or panic come up, it's okay to stop and talk. A therapist can help if it keeps happening. See [If there was sexual abuse or reproductive coercion](ch:if-there-was-sexual-abuse).

## When the past shows up in a new relationship
A raised voice, a late reply or a sulk can set off old alarms. Tell a new partner what helps: "If I go quiet after an argument, I need a few minutes. You haven't done anything wrong." A good partner will be patient and curious. If they use your history against you, that's a warning sign.

::: honest
## Am I choosing, or repeating?
*An honest check, just for you. This is about what's in your hands now, not blame for what was done to you.*

After control, intensity can feel like love, and calm can feel boring. Ask yourself:

- Am I drawn to this person because they feel exciting, or because they feel safe?
- Am I ignoring something I'd warn a friend about?
- Am I dating to fill a gap, prove something to my ex, or feel wanted?
- Do I feel like myself around them?

You don't need perfect answers. If one of them stings, slow things down for a week, or talk it through with a friend or a therapist before the next date.
:::

::: scene
## How Jonah knew he was ready
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Two years after leaving, Jonah realised he was happy on his own. He wasn't lonely in the evenings any more. He decided to try dating, slowly. When a first date cancelled at short notice, he noticed he felt mild disappointment, not panic.

=== the-version-of-you-from-before
title: The version of you from before
question: Will I ever be the same after abuse?
seo: Will I Ever Be the Same After Abuse?
short: Will you ever be the same after abuse? Not exactly, and that isn't bad news. Much of what felt lost comes back: humour, joy, confidence, the ability to connect. What's available isn't the old you, but a third version that carries what you've learned and isn't less for it.
---
You look at old photos and wonder where that person went. Will you ever get back to who you were before?

## Will I ever be the same after abuse?
The honest answer is more hopeful than a flat "no," and more honest than a comforting "yes."

**Much of you comes back.** Your sense of humour. Your capacity for joy. Your ability to trust and connect. Your confidence in your own judgment. For most people these return, rarely in a straight line and rarely on a schedule. The Decoder describes the wearing-away in [Identity Erosion](/playbook/identity-erosion). It can be reversed.

**Some of it doesn't.** The version of you who never knew what you know now isn't coming back. Nobody who's lived through something big gets to stay untouched by it.

## What's available instead?
A third version. One who carries the experience, has learned hard things from it, and isn't lesser for it. Often wiser about people. Clearer about what you will and won't put up with. Kinder to others in trouble.

> "Getting back to normal" may not be possible, or even what you want. A life that fits who you are now is a better aim.

## Why does this reframe matter?
Because chasing your old self sets a target you can't hit, and leaves you feeling you're always falling short. Building forward from what's come back, plus what you've learned, is truer and far more doable.

## How do I meet this new version?
- Notice what's come back. Write it down, because it's easy to miss.
- Notice what's new: limits you now hold, people you now spot a mile off.
- Try things. See [Your own tastes, rediscovered](ch:your-own-tastes-rediscovered).
- Be patient with what's still healing. See [What "recovered" means](ch:what-recovered-actually-means).
- Let people meet the new you. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).

## What tends to come back first?
Many people notice small things first. You laugh out loud at something daft on TV and catch yourself doing it. You enjoy a meal. You sleep through the night. Bigger things, like confidence in your decisions or trust in new people, usually come back more slowly. See [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice).

## What about the parts of me the relationship used?
Kindness, loyalty, patience, a habit of forgiving. Controlling people often lean on exactly these. You don't have to give them up. You can keep them and add limits: kind, with a boundary; loyal, to people who earn it.

## What if people say I've changed?
You have. Some people will welcome your new limits. Some won't, especially the ones who were used to you giving way. Their reaction tells you about them. See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

## Can anything good come out of this?
Researchers call it post-traumatic growth. Some people report closer relationships, new priorities or a stronger sense of their own strength after trauma. It isn't required, and it doesn't make what happened worth it. Growth and pain can sit side by side.

## What people describe changing
People who've left abuse often say they're:

- more cautious, at least at first
- quicker to notice manipulation
- clearer about what they will and won't accept
- less interested in pleasing everyone
- kinder to others who are struggling

Some of these changes feel like losses. Many turn into strengths.

## Strengths built in hard times
Surviving abuse takes serious skill: reading moods, planning, protecting children, holding a household together under pressure. Those skills don't vanish. They can turn into patience, resourcefulness and staying power in your new life.

## Reclaiming things they ruined
Some places, songs, foods or hobbies may feel tainted by the relationship. You can win them back slowly. Go back to the place with a friend, play the song somewhere new, make the food your own way. Or let some of them go. Either is fine.

## More than "survivor"
"Survivor" can be a powerful word, and you're more than what happened to you. You're also a parent, a friend, a worker, a person with interests and plans. Over time, the abuse may become one chapter of your story instead of the title.

## A letter to yourself
Some people find it helps to write a letter to the person they were before the relationship, or to the person they'll be in five years. What would you want them to know? What are you proud of? Keep the letter, and read it on hard days.

::: action
## Write the letter
Set a timer for ten minutes and write a short letter to yourself in five years: one thing you're proud of now, one thing you're working on, one thing you hope they've found. Put it in an envelope with the date on it.
:::

=== what-recovered-actually-means
title: What "recovered" means
question: What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?
seo: Recovery From Narcissistic Abuse: What It Looks Like
short: Recovery from narcissistic abuse, or any coercive relationship, means getting back choice and capacity, with hard days still allowed: making decisions from your own wishes, trusting your judgment, wanting things, and being present without the past running the show.
---
People say "you'll know it when you feel it." Lovely, and useless when you're trying to tell whether you're getting anywhere. So here's a working definition of recovery, in plain words. Sadly, there's no certificate.

## What recovery is not
- **Never having a hard day again.** Nobody gets there, including people who've recovered by any reasonable measure.
- **Never thinking about them.**
- **Forgiving them.** See [Closure you give yourself, and whether you have to forgive](ch:closure-and-forgiveness).
- **Being exactly who you were before.** See [The version of you from before](ch:the-version-of-you-from-before).
- **A finish line on a date.** This book won't give you one.

## What does recovery from narcissistic abuse look like?
> Recovery is getting back choice and capacity. You make decisions from your own wishes, not from fear or old habit. You trust your judgment, want things and go after them, and the past breaks in less often.

## How do I know I'm recovering?
Some markers people find useful:

- You make decisions without checking what your ex would have thought.
- Hard days come and go, instead of turning into hard months.
- You're curious about new people and situations, as well as careful.
- Your sense of your own judgment has mostly come back.
- You laugh more.
- You can talk about what happened without being swept away by it.
- They take up less room in your head.

None of these need to be perfect. None mean the hard feelings never come back. They mean those feelings have stopped running the show.

## What if I'm not there yet?
Then you're somewhere on the way, which is where most people are. Recovery comes in waves, with good stretches and setbacks. A setback doesn't wipe out progress. See [The long after](ch:the-long-after), and if you're struggling with more than a hard week, [When it's more than aftermath](ch:when-its-more-than-aftermath).

## What slows recovery down?
- Ongoing contact or court cases that keep your alarm running. See [When it stops](ch:when-it-stops).
- Having no one to talk to. See [Making new people from scratch](ch:making-new-people-from-scratch).
- Poor sleep, or depression that hasn't been treated. See [Sleep](ch:sleep).
- Money stress. See [Money from zero](ch:money-from-zero).
- Going back and forth with the person you left. See [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond).

None of these mean you're failing. They're weights. Some can be put down. Some have to be carried for a while.

## What helps it along?
Safety first, then routine, then people. Research on recovery after trauma describes three stages: getting safe, telling the story and grieving, and reconnecting with ordinary life. They overlap and loop back. You can be making new friends while you're still grieving.

## How do I measure progress without a timeline?
Compare yourself with yourself, months apart. Not with other people, and not with a date. Once a season, write down your answers to three questions:

1. What takes less effort now?
2. What scares me less?
3. What do I want that I couldn't let myself want before?

Keep the answers. On bad days, read them.

## The areas of recovery
Recovery isn't one thing. It tends to happen in several areas, at different speeds:

- **Safety:** feeling and being safe.
- **Body:** sleep, health, energy.
- **Mind:** fewer flashbacks, more calm, trusting your memory.
- **People:** friendships, family, maybe new relationships.
- **Money and work:** stability, independence.
- **Meaning:** a sense of purpose, joy, a future you want.

You may be strong in some and still working on others. That's normal.

## A self-check
Every few months, rate each area from 1 to 5. Don't judge the scores. Look at which ones have moved, and which might need help next. Keep the ratings, so you can see change that's hard to notice day to day.

## Setbacks
Expect a few. They don't mean recovery has failed. A court date, a hoover, an anniversary or a new stress can knock you back for a while. When it happens, go back to what helped last time. Each time, you often bounce back faster.

## Recovery with ongoing contact
If you share children with the person you left, contact may go on for years. Recovery is still possible. It may look like contact that no longer swallows your week, handovers that pass without a sleepless night, and a life that's mostly about you and your children instead of them.

## Helping others when you're ready
Some people, in time, find meaning in helping others: volunteering, supporting a friend, sharing their story. There's no obligation. If you choose it, wait until you're steady enough that helping doesn't reopen your own wounds.

## How Grace saw her own recovery
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Grace rated her recovery every three months. At first: safety 2, body 1, mind 1, people 2, money 1, meaning 1. A year later: safety 4, body 3, mind 3, people 3, money 2, meaning 3. She still had bad weeks. But the numbers showed what she couldn't feel day to day. She was moving.

She kept rating. The money score was the slowest to change, and she stopped blaming herself for that.
