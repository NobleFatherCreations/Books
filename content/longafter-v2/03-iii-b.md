=== the-new-partner
title: The new partner
question: My ex is contacting my new partner. What do I do?
seo: Ex Contacting Your New Partner? What to Do
short: If your ex is contacting your new partner, it's a common pattern, not a sign your new relationship is doomed. It usually comes as concern or "warnings." Tell your partner about the history early, agree how you'll both respond, and keep a record. The same applies, in reverse, if your ex's new partner contacts you.
---
You've met someone. It's early, or it's serious. Then your ex finds out, and your new partner gets a message: "I think you should know some things about who you're with."

## Why is my ex contacting my new partner?
A new relationship can feel to the person you left like the final loss of their hold on you. Contact with your partner is a way to get some of that back: to unsettle the relationship, to recruit an audience, or to remind you they're still there.

It often looks like:

- a "friendly warning" about your past, your mental health or your parenting
- the same story they've told everyone else, now for your partner. See [The narrative campaign](ch:the-narrative-campaign).
- sudden charm towards your partner, or attempts to befriend them
- more contact with you, or more trouble over the children, just as the relationship becomes public

The Decoder calls the comparison game [triangulation](/playbook/triangulation). Stories told to turn people against you are covered in [smear campaigns](/playbook/smear-campaigns).

> The new relationship can survive this. What you're seeing is the old pattern noticing you've moved on.

## What do I tell my new partner?
Early, calm honesty helps most. Your partner doesn't need every detail. They need enough to recognise what's happening if it comes:

> **Say:** "My ex didn't take the break-up well, and they sometimes contact people in my life with stories about me. If they contact you, I'd rather you hear it from me first. You don't have to reply."

[Telling someone new](ch:telling-someone-new) covers how much to share and when.

## How should we both respond?
- **Don't reply,** or reply once and stop: "Please don't contact me again."
- **Screenshot first, then block.**
- **Don't argue the story.** A partner defending you to your ex gives your ex the reaction they want.
- **Keep a record** of the contact. If there's a protective order, contact with your partner may breach it. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **Watch for escalation.** A new relationship is one of the moments when risk can rise. Update your [safety plan](ch:a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left).

## What if my ex's new partner contacts me?
Sometimes the message comes the other way. Your ex's new partner reaches out, angry, or worried, or asking "was it that bad?"

You don't have to answer. If you do, keep it short and safe:

> **Say:** "I'm not going to discuss our relationship. I hope you're okay. If you ever need help, domestic abuse services are free and confidential."

Don't send a long warning. It rarely lands. It usually gets shown to your ex, and it can be used against you. If you believe someone is in danger, you can tell the police; some countries have disclosure schemes that let a new partner ask about past abuse. See [Police and reporting](ch:police-and-reporting).

## Should you warn their new partner?
Many people feel a strong pull to warn the next person. Sometimes that comes from care. Sometimes from wanting to be believed at last.

Think it through first:

- **It often doesn't land.** Early in a relationship, people tend to believe their partner's version, which may already describe you as the problem.
- **It can raise the risk to you,** and can be used against you in court as harassment or "obsession."
- **It may be safer through someone else.** If there are children involved and you're worried for their safety, tell a professional, not the new partner.

If you do decide to say something, keep it short, once, in writing, with no demands:

> **Say:** "I'm not trying to interfere. I'll say this once: there was abuse in our relationship. If you ever need help, domestic abuse services are free and confidential."

Then leave it. What they do with it is theirs.

## Bringing a new partner into the children's lives
Go slowly. Children need time to adjust to the separation before a new adult appears.

- Wait until the relationship is steady.
- Meet in neutral places first, and keep it short.
- Don't ask the children to keep the new person secret from the other parent.
- Expect the other parent to react, and plan for it. They may question the children or raise it in court.

Courts in most places don't object to a new partner. They focus on whether the children are safe and settled.

## When your partner wants to confront your ex
A protective new partner may want to step in: to reply to the messages, to go to the handover, to "sort it out." It usually makes things worse. It gives your ex a new target, a new story and sometimes evidence.

> **Say (to your partner):** "I know you want to protect me. The thing that helps most is staying out of direct contact with them, and being here when I need to talk."

## Comparison and jealousy
If your ex has moved on quickly, you may find yourself comparing: their new partner's looks, their holidays, how happy they seem online. If you're the one with a new partner, your ex may compare and resent.

Online lives are edited. A new relationship tells you nothing about whether your ex has changed. Read [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile) if you can't stop looking.

## What if the new relationship struggles under the pressure?
That's not your fault, and it doesn't mean you shouldn't date. Some relationships hold under strain and some don't. A partner who meets this with calm and curiosity is showing you something good about them. [Dating, when you're ready and when you're not](ch:dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not) covers the wider picture.

## When the messages reached the new partner
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

A few months into Elena's new relationship, her partner got a long message from her ex, describing Elena as unstable and dangerous. Her partner showed her the message straight away. They agreed together: no reply, screenshot it, block the account. Elena added it to her record.

Her partner said later that what convinced him wasn't anything Elena said. It was how calm she stayed, and how the message didn't match the person he knew.

## Talk before it happens
Tell a new partner early, briefly, that your ex may try to contact them, and agree what you'll both do if they do.

## Sources
- Stark, E. (2007). *Coercive Control: How Men Entrap Women in Personal Life.* Oxford University Press.
- Home Office (2016). *Domestic Violence Disclosure Scheme (Clare's Law) guidance* (England and Wales).
- Spearman, K. J., Hardesty, J. L., and Campbell, J. (2023). Post-separation abuse: A concept analysis. *Journal of Advanced Nursing*, 79(4).

=== hoovering
title: Hoovering
question: Why is my ex contacting me again, and what is hoovering?
seo: Hoovering After a Breakup: What It Is and What to Do
short: Hoovering is when an ex who hurt you tries to pull you back after a break-up, often just as you've started to feel steady. It can come as an apology, an emergency, nostalgia, a gift or a crisis. The name matters because a pattern you can see has less pull. You don't owe a reply.
---
It's been weeks of silence. You've started sleeping again. Then your phone lights up.

*"Hey. I was just thinking about you. I hope you're doing okay."*

Or: *"I've been in therapy. I finally understand what I did."* Or: *"I found your grandmother's ring. When can you come and get it?"* Or: *"I'm in hospital. Please, I just need to hear your voice."*

This has a name.

## What is hoovering?
Hoovering is an attempt to pull a former partner back into contact after a break-up or a period of silence. The name comes from the vacuum cleaner: it sucks you back in.

It isn't always an attempt to restart the relationship. Often the goal is access: to see if you'll still respond, to reopen a channel, to get a reaction. The Decoder's page on [hoovering](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait) gives the short definition. This chapter is about what to do when it happens after you've left.

> A return that feels uniquely meaningful, uniquely well-timed and uniquely sincere is often one of the most common shapes there is.

## What does hoovering look like?
These are the forms people describe most often. Most arrive as a text, a voicemail, a letter, or a message through someone else.

**The apology.** "I'm so sorry. I've changed. I'm getting help." Often detailed and moving. Often arriving without any change you can see.

**The nostalgia.** "Remember that summer in Cornwall?" A photo, a song, an in-joke. It goes straight past your thinking and into your chest.

**The emergency.** An illness, an accident, a death in the family, a breakdown. Some are real. Many evaporate once you respond.

**The practical excuse.** "I found some of your things." "There's post for you." "We need to talk about the car." Logistics as a door.

**The special date.** Your birthday, their birthday, an anniversary, a holiday. Warmth arrives exactly when you're most open to it. [Holidays and the first year's calendar](ch:holidays-and-the-first-year) helps you plan for those days.

**The third person.** A mutual friend, their mother, your child: "They miss you so much." See [Your family and mutual friends](ch:your-family-and-mutual-friends).

**The future.** "Let's start again. We'll move, get married, have the baby." The Decoder calls this [future faking](/playbook/future-faking).

**The charm offensive.** Flowers, gifts, attention, compliments, the person you first fell for. The Decoder calls this [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing).

**The threat dressed as a plea.** "If you don't answer, I don't know what I'll do." This can be real distress, a control tactic, or both. See below.

## Why do they come back when I'm finally doing well?
The timing is rarely an accident. Hoovering tends to arrive when you become visibly stable: a new job, a new partner, a happy photo, a quiet stretch with no replies. To someone used to influencing you, your stability can feel like the last of their hold slipping away. That triggers an attempt to get it back.

It also works because of how the relationship trained you. Warmth that comes and goes unpredictably creates a stronger pull than steady warmth. Psychologists call this intermittent reinforcement, and the Decoder describes it as [push-pull](/playbook/intermittent-reinforcement-push-pull). One warm message after months of nothing can feel enormous. [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond) explains why.

## Will a narcissist come back after a breakup?
Often, yes. Whatever label fits the person, returning after silence is one of the most common patterns after a break-up with a controlling partner. Some come back once. Some come back for years, every time you seem to be doing well.

## Hoovering, breadcrumbing and ghosting: what's the difference?
- **Hoovering** is an attempt to pull you back into contact after you've left, or after they've gone quiet. The aim is to get you reachable again.
- **Breadcrumbing** is giving just enough attention to keep you hoping: a like, a late-night "thinking of you," with nothing behind it.
- **Ghosting** is cutting off all contact without a word.

After an abusive relationship these often come as a cycle. They go silent, which hurts. Then a crumb arrives. Then a full hoover, when the silence hasn't worked. Knowing the cycle helps you see the next step coming. The Decoder has pages on [breadcrumbing](/playbook/bread-crumbing), [ghosting as control](/playbook/ghosting-as-control) and [the silent treatment](/playbook/withholding-silent-treatment).

## What about hoovering after a discard?
Sometimes they ended it, coldly, and then came back weeks later as if nothing happened. That's still hoovering. The discard and the return are two parts of the same pattern. If they left you, [If they left you](ch:if-they-left-you) is written for that.

## How long does hoovering last?
There's no fixed answer. For some people it stops after a few attempts that go unanswered. For others it returns on special dates or whenever life changes. What tends to make it fade is consistent silence, because each reply, even an angry one, tells them the channel still works. [When it stops](ch:when-it-stops) covers the range honestly.

## Should I reply?
You don't owe a reply. You don't owe closure, an explanation, or one last conversation to someone who harmed you.

There are two workable choices:

**No reply.** For most people this is the cleanest option. Read the message, record it if you're keeping a record, and don't answer. Not replying costs you nothing lasting, whatever it feels like in the moment.

**One closing reply, then silence.** Some people need to say one thing to feel finished. If that's you, keep it short and final, and don't respond to whatever comes back:

> **Say:** "I've read your message. I'm not going to reopen contact. Please don't contact me again."

If you share children, you may need to keep one channel open. Answer only what's about the children, and ignore the rest:

> **Say:** "I'll keep our messages to the children's arrangements. Pickup on Friday is at 5pm at school as agreed."

More replies are in [Words you can use](ch:words-you-can-use). For deciding what needs an answer at all, see [Messages you don't have to answer](ch:messages-you-dont-have-to-answer).

## What if the emergency is real?
Sometimes it is. You can respond to a real emergency without reopening the relationship. If someone says they're going to harm themselves, call emergency services and give them the details. That is the right help, and it doesn't have to come from you.

If the same "emergency" keeps happening, or disappears once you reply, that tells you what it was.

## I already replied. Have I ruined everything?
No. Nearly everyone replies at some point. It doesn't erase your progress. Notice what happened afterwards: did things change, or did the old pattern come straight back? Then decide what you'll do next time, and write that decision down while you're calm.

If you've gone further than replying, and you're back together or thinking of it, [If you go back](ch:if-you-go-back) is written without judgment.

## How do I tell hoovering from someone who has changed?
People can change. This book doesn't claim every returning ex is manipulating you. Real change tends to look like this:

- **Time.** It shows over many months, not in one message.
- **Consistency.** The same behaviour on good days and bad days, when you're watching and when you're not.
- **Accountability without conditions.** They name what they did without blaming you or asking for anything in return.
- **No pressure.** They respect a "no" and a "not now."
- **Change that doesn't depend on your return.** They're getting help because they need it, not as a bargaining chip.

Hoovering usually fails those tests. It arrives tied to your stability, asks for something fast, and turns cold or angry when you don't respond. If you're unsure, [Trust as a practice](ch:trust-as-a-practice) offers a slow, careful way to judge.

## What if I'm tempted?
That's normal. The pull tells you how strong the bond was. It tells you nothing about whether to go back. Some things that help:

- Write down three things that happened that you never want to happen again, and read them when the message arrives.
- Tell one trusted person before you reply to anything.
- Delay. Wait 24 hours before any reply. The urge usually fades.
- Read [The trauma bond](ch:the-trauma-bond) and [Checking their profile](ch:checking-their-profile).

## Hoovering through the children
Some hoovers come through the children: "The kids miss us being a family." "Can we all go to the school play together?" "She cried for you all weekend."

These are hard to resist, because you want what's best for your children. A useful test: does the request need you and your ex together, or could it be done separately? If it's about the children's needs, answer about the children. If it's about getting the two of you in a room, you can decline.

> **Say:** "I'll come to the play and sit with my mum. I'm glad they're excited."

## Hoovering through a crisis or health scare
"I'm in hospital." "I can't go on." "My dad died." A crisis is one of the most powerful hoovers, because ignoring it feels cruel.

You can respond to real danger without reopening the relationship:

- If someone is in immediate danger, call the emergency services for them.
- If they mention suicide, tell their family or a crisis service, and give them the number for a crisis line.
- You don't have to be the person who rescues them.

Their family, friends and professionals exist. Directing them there is not abandonment.

## What a real apology contains
Many hoovers come as apologies. A real apology usually has these parts:

- **It names what they did,** clearly, without "if" or "but."
- **It doesn't ask for anything back,** not contact, not forgiveness, not a meeting.
- **It shows change over time,** through behaviour, not promises.
- **It accepts your answer,** including silence or no.

An apology that comes with a request, a deadline or anger when you don't respond is a hoover dressed as an apology.

## A plan for the next hoover
Hoovers often come when you're tired, lonely or at an anniversary. Plan now, while you're clear:

1. **Write down your answer in advance.** Even if it's "I don't reply."
2. **Pick one person to message** when a hoover arrives, before you respond.
3. **Wait a day** before answering anything that isn't about children or safety.
4. **Read your record** of what happened, so the warm message isn't the only memory in front of you.

## Should I keep the messages?
Yes, if there's any chance you'll need them: for a protective order, a custody case, or a stalking report. Screenshot them with dates, then mute or block. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how). If hoovering turns into following or turning up, see [Stalking and harassment after a breakup](ch:stalking-and-harassment).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* calls hoovering "the return of the mask" in its chapter on [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept), and places it in [the manipulation cycle](/feminine/the-manipulation-cycle#core-concept).

## Three hoovers in one year
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

In January, Marcus's ex sent a long apology. He didn't reply. In April, she messaged that she was in hospital; he called her sister, who confirmed she was fine. In September, on their old anniversary, she sent a photo of their first holiday with "Do you remember?" He looked at it for a long time, then sent it to his friend instead of replying.

Each hoover was different. His answer was the same each time: no reply, record it, tell someone. By the third, it hurt less.

## Write your answer now
Write down, today, what you'll do the next time they reach out. When it comes, you won't have to decide in the moment.

=== when-they-say-youre-the-abuser
title: When they say you're the abuser
question: What is reactive abuse, and what do I do about false allegations?
seo: Reactive Abuse and False Allegations: What to Do
short: Reactive abuse is when someone provokes you for months until you snap, then holds up your reaction as proof that you're the abuser. False reports to the police, child services or your employer can follow. Stay calm, keep a record, get legal advice early, and describe the whole pattern, including your own reaction, honestly.
---
You shouted. Maybe you swore, or threw something, or pushed them away. It was the worst moment of a very long year. Now it's the centre of their story: you're violent, you're unstable, you're the abuser.

Or you did nothing at all, and a police officer, a social worker or your employer calls to say someone has made a complaint about you.

## What is reactive abuse?
"Reactive abuse" is a term people use for a pattern: one person provokes the other, often over a long time, until the other reacts. The reaction is then recorded, filmed or reported, and used as proof that the provoked person is the abuser.

It isn't a legal or clinical term. And a reaction is still a reaction: it isn't automatically excused. But it matters to describe the whole pattern, because a single moment pulled out of context can make the target look like the danger.

## Why would my ex say I'm the abuser?
Because it works. Accusing the other person first is a common move in post-separation abuse:

- **DARVO:** deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. The person who did harm denies it, attacks your credibility, and claims to be the victim. See the Decoder's [DARVO](/playbook/darvo).
- **Getting in first.** A complaint made early can shape how professionals see everything that follows. The Decoder describes this in [preemptive accusation](/playbook/preemptive-accusation-complaint-weaponization).
- **False reports** to the police, child services, your employer, a licensing body or your landlord. See the Decoder's [false reports](/playbook/false-reports).
- **Counter-applications** for a protective order, often filed after you filed yours.

Research on DARVO has found it can make observers believe the victim less and the person accused more. That's why calm facts matter so much.

> The goal of an accusation like this is often to make you defend yourself, so that you stop describing what they did.

## What do I do if I've been falsely reported?
1. **Stay calm with the professional.** Police officers, social workers and HR staff deal with competing accounts every day. Calm answers with dates and details help you.
2. **Don't contact your ex about it.** Anything you send can be used.
3. **Get legal advice before a formal interview,** especially with the police. You usually have the right to a lawyer. [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money) lists low-cost options.
4. **Gather your record:** messages, dates, witnesses, any earlier reports you made. See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
5. **Ask exactly what is alleged,** so you can answer it.
6. **Keep your answers to facts:** dates, what happened, who was there.

## What if I did react?
Be honest about it. Denying something that's on video or in a message will cost you more than admitting it. Then describe the context, factually:

> **Say:** "On 3 March I shouted at him and threw my phone. I'm not proud of it. It happened after eight months of messages like these, which I can show you, and after he followed me to work that morning."

Context isn't an excuse, and a professional may still have concerns. But it gives them the whole picture instead of one frame of it.

If you're not sure whether your reactions crossed a line, read the "Am I the abuser?" section in [Abuse, or a hard breakup?](ch:abuse-or-a-hard-breakup).

## What if social services contact me about my children?
Cooperate, stay calm, and focus on the children. Explain the separation and any pattern of control, with your record. Ask what the concern is and what they need from you. Get legal advice if it may lead to court. Child services are there to protect children, and a parent who is calm, cooperative and focused on the children's needs is what they want to see.

## What if they apply for a protective order against me?
Don't ignore it. Read it carefully, get legal advice, and go to any hearing. Bring your record. Don't contact them, even to sort it out, because that could break a temporary order. See [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders) and [Your day in court](ch:your-day-in-court).

## Counter-allegations in court
When one person raises domestic abuse, the other often makes allegations back. Courts know this happens, and they're meant to look at the evidence on each side.

- Answer each allegation briefly and factually. Don't ignore serious ones.
- Provide evidence where you have it: messages, witnesses, police reports.
- Keep to the pattern across the relationship, not just the worst moments.
- Don't exaggerate. One proven fact is worth more than three that collapse.

In England and Wales, courts can hold a "fact-finding hearing" to decide what happened before deciding arrangements for children. Ask your lawyer whether one is needed.

## Staying steady under accusation
Being called an abuser when you were the one who was harmed can feel unbearable. People in this position often describe shock, anger and a strong urge to prove themselves.

What tends to work better is calm and consistent behaviour. Keep your messages polite and short. Keep attending appointments. Keep your record. Let professionals see, over time, who you are. Find somewhere private to let the anger out, a therapist, a friend, a journal, so it doesn't spill into the case.

## Getting your own legal advice quickly
If you've been reported to police, social services or a court, get advice before you give statements or sign anything. Many domestic abuse services have legal advisers or can refer you. You're allowed to say: "I want to get legal advice before I answer that." See [Legal help with little or no money](ch:solicitors-legal-aid-and-no-money).

## If you're a man being accused
Men who are abused often face a quick assumption that they must be the aggressor. If you're in this position:

- Keep your record in detail, with dates and evidence.
- Seek help from services that work with men. In the UK, the Men's Advice Line is on 0808 801 0327.
- Avoid being alone with the person you left where nobody else can see what happens.
- Read *The Silence*, another book in this library, written for men.

## Rebuilding afterwards
Being falsely accused can leave a mark even when it's resolved. You may feel watched or doubted for a long time. It helps to name the injury: you were harmed twice, once by the abuse and once by the accusation. Give yourself time to trust institutions again, and lean on the people who believed you.

## How do I cope with being called the abuser?
Being accused of the thing that was done to you can feel like losing your mind. Hold on to your record, and to one or two people who know the truth. A therapist who understands coercive control can help too: see [Finding a therapist who understands this](ch:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this).

*The Sovereign Divine Feminine* lists false reports to employers, licensing boards, child services and the police among the forms of [post-separation control](/feminine/post-separation-control#core-concept).

## Staying calm under a false report
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

Two days after Jess got a protective order, police came to her door. Her ex had reported that she had threatened him. She was shaking. She said, "I'd like to get legal advice before I answer questions, and I'd like you to know there's a protective order against him." She gave them the order's reference number.

Her lawyer helped her give a calm statement with her record attached. The police took no further action. Jess still felt sick for a week. She also knew she had handled it well.

## The sentence to remember
"I want to get legal advice before I answer that." You're always allowed to say it.

- Dichter, M. E. (2013). "They arrested me—and I was the victim": Women's experiences with getting arrested in the context of domestic violence. *Women & Criminal Justice*, 23(2), 81–98.

=== when-it-stops
title: When it stops
question: Does post-separation abuse ever end?
seo: Does Post-Separation Abuse Ever End?
short: Does post-separation abuse ever end? For many people it does, often fading as the person who caused it finds a new focus or meets consistent limits and consequences. For others it carries on in a smaller form for years. Both are honest answers, and you don't have to wait for it to end to start living fully.
---
"How long will this go on?" may be the question people ask most. This chapter gives the honest answer, and the honest answer is a range.

## Does post-separation abuse ever end?
For many people, yes. It fades or stops. For others it continues in a smaller form for years, often flaring around court cases, holidays or changes in your life. Researchers and services see both outcomes, and nobody can tell you in advance which one you'll get.

> No list in this chapter is a formula. These are patterns seen across many situations, not promises about yours.

## How long does post-separation abuse last?
There's no reliable figure. Where there are no shared children and no shared property, it often fades sooner, because there are fewer channels. Where there are children, contact may continue until they're adults, and so may the openings for control. That doesn't mean it has to stay as bad as it is now.

## What tends to go with it stopping?
- **Their attention moves.** A new relationship, a new job, a move, or a new target. It's not fair that this is often what does it, but it's common.
- **Distance.** Moving further away, changing schools, fewer shared places.
- **Consistent limits.** Contact kept to writing, kept short, with no exceptions. This removes the occasional reward that keeps most of these patterns going.
- **Consequences.** A protective order that's enforced, a court that recognises the pattern, a judge who restricts repeat applications.
- **Less to fight over.** A final financial settlement, a clear parenting plan, a house that's finally sold.

## What keeps it going?
The most common thing is intermittent contact: long silence, then a reply; firm limits, then an exception. Each exception teaches the person that pressure still works sometimes, which is a stronger lesson than "it always works." The Decoder explains this in [push-pull](/playbook/intermittent-reinforcement-push-pull).

This isn't about blaming you for replying. Everyone does sometimes. It's about knowing what gives the pattern its fuel, so you can starve it when you're able.

## When does post-separation abuse stop for good?
Sometimes you only know looking back: one day you realise it's been months since anything happened. Sometimes it returns once more after a long gap. Many people describe a shift from constant pressure to occasional flare-ups, and then to quiet.

## What do I do while it's still going on?
- **Keep your record.** See [What to document and how](ch:what-to-document-and-how).
- **Keep limits steady.** See [No contact, low contact, or contact you can't avoid](ch:no-contact-low-contact).
- **Use the systems that create consequences:** [Protective orders](ch:police-and-protective-orders) and [Systems abuse and litigation abuse](ch:systems-abuse).
- **Build the rest of your life anyway.** Waiting for it to end before you live fully has its own cost. The rebuilding chapters, starting with [Money from zero](ch:money-from-zero), don't need it to be over first.

## Signs it's winding down
- Contact becomes less frequent and more businesslike.
- Handovers get shorter and calmer.
- Court applications slow or stop.
- Payments arrive on time more often.
- You notice you haven't checked your phone for a reaction in days.

These changes are often gradual. You may only see them by looking back over your record.

## What does quiet feel like?
Strangely, quiet can be uncomfortable at first. After months or years on alert, your body may not trust it. You may wait for the next thing, or feel flat and empty without the constant pressure.

That's your nervous system catching up. It usually eases with time. See [What your nervous system is doing](ch:what-your-nervous-system-is-doing).

## When it flares again
Flare-ups often come with change: a new partner, a move, a court review, a child's birthday, the other person's own life going badly. A flare-up after a quiet spell doesn't mean you're back at the start. Go back to your plan: written contact only, the record, the people who help. It tends to settle faster the second time, because you know what to do.

## Letting your guard down slowly
Maybe one morning you leave the curtains open, or walk to the shop the old way, and notice your shoulders drop. At some point you may want to relax your safety steps. Do it one at a time, and watch what happens:

- Unblock nothing at first; that can wait the longest.
- Loosen one routine, like varying your route less.
- Share a bit more on social media, and see whether anything comes back.

If anything restarts, tighten again. There's no shame in going slowly.

## If it moves to someone else
Sometimes the pattern stops for you because it's moved to a new partner. You may feel relief, guilt or worry for them. You're not responsible for warning or rescuing them, though you can, once, if it's safe. See [The new partner](ch:the-new-partner).

## What if it never fully stops?
Then the goal shifts from ending it to shrinking it: fewer channels, shorter contact, less of your attention. Many people who share children with a controlling ex describe getting to a place where it's still there, but it no longer runs their week. That's a real outcome, and it's worth working towards. [The long after](ch:the-long-after) describes what life can look like years out.

## How quiet arrived
*This example is made up, built from patterns many people describe.*

For two years after leaving, Ola's ex filed applications, sent messages and disrupted handovers. Then something shifted. He started a new relationship, and moved further away. The messages became shorter, then rare. Handovers became thirty seconds of nothing.

Ola didn't notice the change until she looked back through her record and saw that the entries had thinned from several a week to one a month. She cried, then went out for dinner with a friend. She kept her safety steps for another year, just in case.

## Looking back to see forward
Every few months, read your record from the start. Change often only shows from a distance.
