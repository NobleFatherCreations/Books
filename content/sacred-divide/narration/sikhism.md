## for-you structure
The Akal Takht's Jathedar is the highest moral office in Sikhi, and the SGPC can hire and fire him. It removed two in 2025. The SGPC is an elected body with a five-year term, yet its general house has not faced an election since 2011, and the electoral roll has halved. If you live in Punjab, you have a vote in this, or should have. When were you last asked to use it?

## for-you law
In Canada, a Sikh student took his kirpan to the Supreme Court and won in 2006. If you are Canadian, that is your religious freedom working. In India, the 1925 Gurdwara Act made the historic gurdwaras electable. The page calls that a genuine democratic victory, which also made the donation box an electoral prize. Which do you think the Gurus would recognize: an elected committee, or a committee that doesn't face elections?

## for-you money
The golak, the donation box, at the major gurdwaras holds substantial revenue, and the page calls it "the acknowledged prize" of SGPC elections. If you drop money into a golak this week, who will decide how it is spent? Can you read last year's accounts? The questions section says to ask, and to note whether the answer is a number or an insult.

## for-you cost
Leaving Sikhism formally is rarely the issue. The costs come from family honor, community standing when an Amritdhari lapses, and, for dera followers, losing a whole world of service and employment. The page also records the Akal Takht excommunicating a scholar in 2003 and asking Sikhs worldwide to "snap all ties" with him. If you disagreed publicly with the Akal Takht, what would your family say?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
Girls and women come first: dowry, sex-selective abortion and honor pressure, every one of them forbidden by doctrine. Punjab's sex ratio became one of the most skewed in India. Then come Dalit Sikhs, still facing caste exclusion, including separate gurdwaras, against explicit scripture. The page calls this one of the sharpest gaps between doctrine and practice in the book. Where do you see it in your own family?

## q1
why: Guru Gobind Singh ended human guruship, and the Guru Granth Sahib is the Guru. A living dera guru reintroduces what was abolished. The question asks followers to reconcile the two.
example: The cases section records the conviction of a large Punjab dera's head for rape in 2017, and concludes that it "demonstrates the risk of the living-guru model in a tradition whose scripture ended human guruship in 1708."

## q2
why: If scripture rejects caste, a family's objection to an inter-caste marriage has to come from somewhere else. The question asks the family to cite a line.
example: The glance table's unanswered question: "The Gurus abolished caste five centuries ago. Why do the panth's own matrimonial pages still sort by it?" The say-do table records that caste-based gurdwaras exist.

## q3
why: The golak is sacred giving. The question asks whether a committee elected to manage it will show the accounts.
example: The structure table records that the SGPC presidency "controls the golak revenues of the historic gurdwaras — the acknowledged prize of the elections."

## q4
why: When a practice condemned by doctrine produces measurable population effects, someone was silent. The question asks who.
example: The reach section records that female-selective abortion produced severely skewed sex ratios in Punjab "despite clear doctrinal condemnation," one of the sharpest gaps between doctrine and practice in the book.

## q5
why: Political support for a powerful dera buys votes. After a conviction, withdrawing that support publicly would be an act of principle. The question asks whether any politician did it.
example: The cases section records that the dera had operated at corporate scale with political reach, that its head was convicted of rape in 2017, and that he remains imprisoned.

## q6
why: Calling a practice "cultural" shields it from religious criticism. The question notices that culture wins whenever it conflicts with the Gurus.
example: The say-do table sets "Sikhism rejects caste absolutely" beside caste-based gurdwaras, and "Women are fully equal in Sikhi" beside women's near-absence from senior committee and kirtan roles at the historic gurdwaras.
