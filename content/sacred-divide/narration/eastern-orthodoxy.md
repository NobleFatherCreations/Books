## for-you structure
The Ecumenical Patriarch is "first among equals" and cannot command the other churches. That makes Orthodoxy genuinely decentralized. It also means there is no one above a patriarch who has gone wrong, and the scorecard finds no public removal procedure. If your patriarch blessed something you believe is sinful, who would you appeal to? The section's answer is "the Synod, in theory." Has the theory ever been tested in your church?

## for-you law
In Russia, "discrediting" the armed forces became an offence in 2022, and a priest was fined under it for a sermon. The state protects the patriarch, and the church disciplines the priests who disagree with the state. In Greece, clergy salaries come from the state budget. If you live in a majority-Orthodox country, your taxes may already fund your church. Did you choose that, or were you born into it?

## for-you money
Candles, prayer requests, fees for baptisms, weddings, funerals and house blessings: the money section calls these micro-payments for access to the sacred. Individually they are small. Together, and alongside state funding, they built major institutional wealth, and the scorecard finds no public accounts and no published pay. What did your family's last baptism or funeral cost, and could you find out where the fee went?

## for-you cost
Where faith and nationality are fused ("to be Serbian is to be Orthodox"), leaving can be read as betraying your people. That is a heavy price for a private change of belief. If you are a convert, you may have built your entire social world inside a single parish. Ask yourself honestly: if you came to disagree with your spiritual father, would you still have friends on Monday?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table names women excluded from governance, and held to menstrual restrictions that the section says no canon requires. It names LGBTQ people in Russia, where church teaching became criminal law, and priests who opposed a war. If you are a woman who has been kept from communion by a custom, you are entitled to ask to see the canon. Who taught you it existed?

## q1
why: The claim to be the unchanged apostolic faith sits awkwardly beside a history of state control and present state funding. The question asks which of the two actually shapes the church today.
example: The say-do table sets "The Church is unchanged and apostolic" beside the record: its governance was absorbed into a state ministry for two centuries under Peter I, and restructured again under Soviet management. Greece still pays clergy salaries from the state budget.

## q2
why: If a public blessing of an invasion is not something the laity may question, the question implies there is nothing left that they may question. It tests whether lay conscience has any real standing.
example: The say-do table records "We are apolitical and concerned only with souls" beside a patriarch who publicly blessed a war, and clergy who objected and were suspended.

## q3
why: How quickly an institution disciplines people reveals its priorities better than its statements do. The question compares two kinds of offense by the speed of the response.
example: The cases section documents a priest fined for a sermon in Russia (2022–2023), another defrocked in 2024 for refusing to say a prayer, and a Serbian bishop whose resignation was accepted only after video and recordings about sexual conduct with young clergy were published (2012–2013).

## q4
why: Spiritual-father obedience can reach into marriage, medicine and money, and there is no appeal. The question asks where the chain of authority ends for the lay person inside it.
example: The reach section records that spiritual-father obedience can extend to reading, medical, marital and career decisions in intense parishes. It calls this "an unregulated authority with no appeal structure."

## q5
why: A practice with no written basis cannot be defended by pointing to a text. The question invites the reader to look for the source, and to notice that no one can produce it.
example: The who-gets-hurt table records menstrual restrictions on women "imposed by custom no canon requires."

## q6
why: Prelest (spiritual delusion) is a real concept in Orthodox spirituality. When it is used against every criticism, it teaches a person to distrust their own perception first. The question asks who gains from that habit.
example: The reach section records that online convert culture treats Western scholarship, therapy and ecumenism as spiritual dangers. That closes off the outside sources a member would need in order to check a concern.
