## for-you structure
The Church of England governs itself by synod, and it has reformed itself by vote: women priests, women bishops, the end of blasphemy law. That is real. But the scorecard finds no written way to remove the top office, and when an archbishop did go in 2024, it took an independent review to bring that about. If you are a member, you have more voice than members of most churches in this book. Do you use it?

## for-you law
If you live in England, this church is part of your constitution whether or not you attend. Twenty-six of its bishops sit in the House of Lords and vote on the laws everyone lives under. The Equality Act also lets it refuse a licence to a priest in a same-sex marriage. Did you know that bishops of a church most people don't attend help pass your laws? Should that be settled by the church, or by voters?

## for-you money
This is one of the more transparent pages in the book. The Church Commissioners publish accounts for an £11.1bn endowment, and dioceses publish theirs. The parish share you give mostly pays for clergy stipends and housing. The page also records that part of the endowment's history traces to the slave trade, and that a £100m fund was announced in response. You can read the accounts. Have you ever looked?

## for-you cost
For lay members, leaving costs almost nothing: no shunning doctrine, no sanctions. The page says so plainly, as a strength. For clergy it is very different. A priest's home, income and vocation all depend on one licence that a bishop can withdraw. If you are a vicar's spouse or child, that licence is your front door too.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
Survivors of church-related abuse come first in this table. Two independent reviews found that the church protected its reputation before them. Then come clergy whose housing depends on a licence, LGBTQ clergy refused one, and women clergy whose oversight a parish may formally decline. If you sit on a parochial church council, you may one day vote on one of these. Which way would you vote, and why?

## q2
why: Disbanding an independent board is a structural choice. The question asks what took its place, and whether the replacement is as independent as the thing that was removed.
example: The say-do table records that the Independent Safeguarding Board was disbanded and its members dismissed in June 2023 [16]. A new independent charity was approved only in February 2026 [8].

## q3
why: When a priest's home depends on the same licence a bishop can withdraw, disagreeing with the bishop can cost a family its house. The question asks whether that is a fair basis for honest speech inside a church.
example: The law table records that clergy housing and income depend on the bishop's licence, and that a licence was refused after a same-sex marriage (*Pemberton v Inwood*, 2018) [14].

## q4
why: A fund to address a history of slavery is a real step. The question is about process: who sets the amount and decides who benefits, and whether the descendants of those harmed have any say.
example: The money section records the Grant Thornton research linking Queen Anne's Bounty to the South Sea Company, the £100m fund announced in 2023 [6], and an oversight group's call in 2024 for a £1bn target, which the Commissioners accepted [7].

## q5
why: Unelected seats in a legislature are a question for every citizen, not only for church members. The question asks whether a minority church should have a standing vote on everyone's law.
example: The law table records that 26 bishops sit in the House of Lords [25]. The structure table lists them as "Lords Spiritual" who vote on national law.

## q6
why: Consecrating women as bishops while letting parishes decline their oversight creates two classes of bishop. The question asks what equality means when it can be opted out of.
example: The reach section records that the 2014 "five guiding principles" let a parish request male-only oversight, so that a woman's ministry can be formally declined.
