## for-you structure
"We have no hierarchy" sounds like freedom. The say-do table points out the catch: with nothing above the founder-pastor, he may hold more unchecked authority than any bishop in this book. The board that could remove him is often one he chose. If you attend a large independent church, can you name your board members? Do you know who appointed them?

## for-you law
In the United States your church files no annual public return, so you cannot look it up the way you can look up a food bank. The law section also records that in 2025 the IRS agreed in a court filing that a church could endorse candidates to its own congregation, a deal a federal judge declined to approve in 2026. If your pulpit told you how to vote, would you know which rules it was operating under?

## for-you money
Here is a fact most churchgoers don't know: every time your congregation sings a worship song on Sunday, a licence fee flows through CCLI to the songwriters and publishers, and many of the biggest catalogs belong to megachurch labels. Add tithes, building campaigns and, in prosperity teaching, "seed" giving that promises a return. The Senate's Grassley inquiry documented private jets and parsonage mansions, and most ministries declined to disclose fully. Before your next gift, ask to see the budget and the pastor's total pay. The answer is the assessment.

## for-you cost
Megachurches deliberately build a total social world: small groups, childcare, school and often your job. That is what makes them good to belong to, and what makes them expensive to leave. Departing staff and members are sometimes labeled "divisive" or "bitter". If you left tomorrow, which of your friendships would survive? Try naming them.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The first row of the table is the one to sit with: poor and elderly donors, targeted hardest by prosperity and seed-faith teaching, often through broadcast, with no local relationship to appeal to. Do you have a parent or grandparent who gives to a television ministry? Have you ever talked with them about where the money goes?

## q1
why: Publishing three numbers (the budget, the salary and the housing allowance) costs a church nothing if it has nothing to hide. The question sets a deadline ("this week") so that the answer cannot become a promise.
example: The money section records that most of the six ministries in the Senate's Grassley inquiry (2007–2011) declined full disclosure, and that the inquiry ended without penalties.

## q2
why: A non-disclosure agreement turns an employee's memory of leadership conduct into a legal risk. The question asks why a church that preaches truth-telling needs one.
example: The say-do table sets "We would never silence a victim" beside the record: departing staff at multiple large churches signed non-disclosure agreements covering leadership conduct.

## q3
why: Who ran an investigation, who paid for it, and whether it was published in full together decide whether it was an investigation or a public-relations exercise. The question asks for all three.
example: The cases section includes the Guidepost report on the Southern Baptist Convention and an apologist's ministry investigating its own founder (2021). In both, the findings came from a firm the institution hired.

## q4
why: "The door is unlocked" is literally true, since no one is held against their will. The question asks what the room on the other side of the door looks like.
example: The cost section's ledger lists the loss of a "total social world" as documented, set beside the official denial: "They're free to go anywhere."

## q5
why: Prosperity teaching makes an empirical claim: giving produces a return. Empirical claims can be tested. The question asks for the data, and for who reliably profits from the claim.
example: The money section describes seed-faith donations framed as "an investment product" promising divine returns. The Grassley inquiry documented the teachers' private jets and parsonages.

## q6
why: Rules against doubt and "divisiveness" sound like they protect unity. The question asks you to notice whom they actually protect, and that it is never the person asking the question.
example: The reach section records that "touch not the Lord's anointed" and "spirit of Jezebel/Absalom" labels turn attempts at accountability into spiritual offenses. The say-do section calls "touch not the Lord's anointed" a verse deployed almost exclusively when a leader is under scrutiny.
