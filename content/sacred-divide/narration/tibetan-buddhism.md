## for-you structure
The Dalai Lama's office, the page notes, is "located in the man." In July 2025 he declared that the institution will continue, and that his own Gaden Phodrang Trust alone holds the authority to recognize the 15th. Beijing claims that authority too. For a Western student, the more immediate structure is simpler: your lama, your center's board, and the samaya vow that binds you. If your lama did harm, the page asks, what does your vow allow you to do?

## for-you law
Three governments have put this tradition into law. China's Order No. 5 (2007) requires state approval to recognize a reincarnated lama. The U.S. Tibetan Policy and Support Act (2020) says succession is for Tibetan Buddhists alone. In the UK, the Charity Commission permanently removed a Rigpa trustee in 2019 for failing to act on abuse allegations. If you practice at a registered center, the regulator is your protection too. Do you know which body regulates yours?

## for-you money
Empowerments, teachings, retreats, center dues and endowments: Western sanghas are a major source of funding for exile institutions. That money supports real cultural survival. It also gives the teachers who receive it a lot of power. If you pay for an empowerment, do you know where the fee goes, and whether the center publishes accounts?

## for-you cost
After tantric commitment, leaving can carry taught cosmic consequences ("vajra hell"). In documented cases, whistleblowers were exiled from their communities and accused of breaking samaya. That is a heavy weight to put on someone for speaking up. If you have stayed silent about something because of samaya, the page records that the Dalai Lama himself has publicly told students to expose abusive teachers by name.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table puts female students first, those in abuse framed as consort practice, taught that refusing would endanger their own attainment. Then tulku children recognized in childhood, whose life course is assigned before they can consent. If you are a parent, or a student, ask whether consent is even possible when refusing is framed as spiritual danger.

## q2
why: A vow that forbids all criticism of a teacher makes that teacher impossible to catch. The question asks what the vow protects, and whom.
example: The cases section records that Rigpa's commissioned investigation identified samaya as central, and that whistleblowers "had been shunned as samaya-breakers before the findings."

## q3
why: The detention of a child over a religious recognition is the clearest case in this book of a state seizing control of religion. The question asks which institutions have made his release a central demand, and which have not.
example: The law table records that the Panchen Lama recognized by the Dalai Lama was taken with his family in 1995, aged six, "and has not been seen since" [7]. The cases section records that Beijing installed its own Panchen Lama.

## q4
why: A distinction that can only be drawn after harm has occurred protects no one. The question asks for a test that could be applied in advance.
example: The structure section calls the samaya structure, with its cosmic penalties for criticizing the guru after initiation, the tradition's highest-risk mechanism, cited across its documented abuse cases.

## q5
why: Consent requires the freedom to say no. If refusing endangers a student's spiritual attainment, she is not free to say no. The question asks the teacher to answer that directly.
example: The who-gets-hurt table lists female students in consort-framed abuse, "taught that refusal endangered their own attainment," compounded by samaya, "which made speaking a cosmic risk."
