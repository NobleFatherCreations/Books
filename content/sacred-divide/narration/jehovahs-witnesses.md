## for-you structure
Eleven men in Warwick, New York, the Governing Body, appoint their own new members, and the glance table says only they can remove one of their own. There is no vote and no appeal above them. Appointments and departures are announced, never explained. Every rule a congregation follows, from shunning to blood, comes from that room. If you are a Witness, when did you last hear the reasoning behind a change, rather than just the change itself?

## for-you law
Governments have done what the organization would not. Australia's Royal Commission compelled documents. England's Charity Commission ran a nine-year inquiry. A U.S. jury awarded a survivor $28 million, reduced on appeal because the court found no duty to warn the congregation. Where you live decides whether anyone outside can make the organization answer. Which of these bodies exists in your country?

## for-you money
The page names the organization's real asset as your labor: preaching hours, construction work, Bethel service, and the halls it built. The Brooklyn headquarters properties sold for roughly a billion dollars. If your parents or grandparents volunteered on those buildings, their unpaid work helped create that capital. The scorecard finds no public accounts. Do you know where the proceeds went?

## for-you cost
This page records one of the heaviest exit penalties in the book. People who are disfellowshipped or disassociate are shunned by everyone, including their own families, and this is written in the organization's own instructions, not left to personal choice. Since 2024 you may greet them at meetings. If you have a son, a daughter or a parent outside, what is the rule actually asking you to do to them, and do you believe it?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The first row is abused children: 1,006 alleged perpetrators in the organization's own Australian files, none reported by it to police, and a rule requiring a second witness to a crime that is usually committed with none. If you are a parent in a congregation today, ask your elders one question: if my child disclosed abuse, would you call the police before anything else? Ask them to show you where that is written.

## q2
why: If shunning were truly personal, the organization would not need to publish instructions about it. The question asks readers to compare the claim with the written policy.
example: The say-do table sets "Shunning is a personal decision by individual members" beside the record: it is described as required in the organization's own published instructions.

## q3
why: If a policy described as scriptural can change after forty years, it was always changeable. The question asks what that says about the families who kept it in the meantime.
example: The history section records the 2023–2024 adjustments allowing members to greet disfellowshipped attendees, and calls them "external and litigation pressure producing internal change."

## q4
why: Failed predictions have a human cost when people act on them. The question asks whether the organization has ever taken responsibility for that cost.
example: The history section records that from 1966 to 1975 publications pointed to 1975 as a likely end date, that members sold homes and gave up education, and that afterward the blame shifted to members' own expectations.

## q5

example: The structure table lists the blood transfusion prohibition as "a doctrine repeatedly adjusted on fractions and components, applied to children," deciding "whether your child receives treatment."

## q6
