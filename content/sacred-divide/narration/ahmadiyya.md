## for-you structure
The Khalifa sits at the top of a disciplined worldwide administration: national amirs he appoints, local presidents, and secretaries for finance, discipline and marriage. The scorecard finds no written procedure for removing the office. Members describe deep love for the Khalifa, and nothing on this page questions that. The question for a member is narrower: if the office were wrong about something that affected your family, what route would you have to say so?

## for-you law
No community in this book knows better what it costs when a state decides who counts as a Muslim. Pakistan declared Ahmadis non-Muslim by constitutional amendment in 1974, and Ordinance XX (1984) made it a crime for them to call their places of worship mosques. The Supreme Court upheld it in 1993, and attacks on two Lahore mosques killed 94 people in 2010. If you are not Ahmadi, did you know any of this? If you are a Pakistani voter, what would you ask your representative about it?

## for-you money
If you are an earning member, the general contribution is one-sixteenth of your income after tax, and community guidance says it is "not a contribution at a person's own will." Wasiyyat pledges can reach a third of income and estate. In the UK the community's charities publish accounts because the Charity Commission requires it, which the page calls a genuine disclosure. Worldwide accounts are not published. If you give outside the UK, can you read where your money goes?

## for-you cost
Formal expulsion cuts a person off from the Jamaat. The community's own explanation says the person "will not have any connection" with it afterward, and former members describe expected social distancing (the page marks that testimony as not yet independently confirmed). The community's motto is "Love for all, hatred for none." If a brother of yours were expelled, what would that motto ask of you?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table names Ahmadis in Pakistan, under criminal law and violence with no state protection; women, who may not marry outside the community while men may, with permission; children pledged to Waqf-e-Nau before birth; and former members. Two different kinds of harm are recorded here: harm done *to* this community, and harm done *by* its rules. Does honoring the first make it harder to speak about the second?
