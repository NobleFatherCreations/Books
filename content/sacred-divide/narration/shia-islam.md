## for-you structure
Two very different kinds of authority sit on this page. In Iran, clerical rule was written into a state's constitution; the body that could in theory remove the Leader has never done so, and in 2026 it chose the late Leader's son. In Najaf, the senior marjaʿ holds no office and commands no army, yet his sermons helped bring down an Iraqi government. If you follow a marjaʿ, which model do you actually live under, and which would you choose?

## for-you law
If you are a Shia woman, the country decides almost everything. In Iran hijab is compulsory, and a harsher 2023 law was paused but the existing rules remain. A 2025 Iraqi code lets families opt into a personal-status code that Human Rights Watch says makes women "second class." Lebanon has no civil marriage and no clear minimum marriage age. In the UK you are free, but a religious-only marriage leaves you without legal status. Do you know which rules would decide your divorce?

## for-you money
If you pay khums, you give a fifth of your surplus income, half of it to your marjaʿ's office. The page records that no audited public accounting is required. In Iran, shrine conglomerates such as Astan Quds Razavi are among the largest economic entities in the country, tax-exempt and outside parliamentary oversight. The glance table asks the question plainly: who audits the deputy of the Imam? Have you ever asked for the accounts of the office you pay?

## for-you cost
In Iran, the state itself enforces religious compliance. Elsewhere the cost is emotional as well as social: the page notes that stepping back from devotional culture can feel like betraying Husayn and the family dead. That is a powerful bond. Could someone in your family keep mourning Husayn and still question the clerics who claim to speak for him?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table names Iranian women under compulsory dress, with unequal testimony, inheritance, custody and travel rights; protesters, many of them devout, killed and imprisoned since 2017, and their families prosecuted for speaking; and Bahá'ís, Sunnis and other minorities in Iran. If you are outside Iran, what do you owe the believers inside who are paying this price?

## q2
why: Karbala is the tradition's central story of refusing an unjust ruler. The question asks whether a state in which the ruler is also the chief jurist has become the thing Husayn refused.
example: The say-do table sets "Karbala teaches resistance to unjust rulers" beside the record: the commemoration is administered by the ruler and used to mobilize for state projects.

## q3
why: If the tradition's own senior center rejects clerical rule, then clerical rule is one contested theory, not the faith itself. The question uses the tradition's internal disagreement as evidence.
example: The history section records that Khomeini's velayat-e faqih was "a scholarly theory contested by many senior clerics, including quietists in Najaf," before it was written into a constitution.

## q4
why: If a death in custody while enforcing a religious rule costs no cleric his position, the rule is being enforced without accountability. The question asks who, if anyone, was held responsible.
example: The cases section records that Mahsa Amini, 22, died in the custody of Tehran's morality police in September 2022, and that the UN's independent fact-finding mission found her death unlawful and the state responsible.
