## for-you structure
This page follows its own rule: no private individual is named, because authority here lives locally, with elders, societies, families and councils, chosen by community and kinship. That is real self-government, and much of it survived deliberate attempts to destroy it. The page's unanswered question is careful: protection from outsiders is owed and overdue, but who protects the insider from the elder? If you are a member of a nation, who would you go to if an elder harmed you?

## for-you law
The largest harms on this page were done to these traditions by governments and churches. Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission called the residential school system "cultural genocide" and recorded about 3,200 deaths. The U.S. Interior Department confirmed at least 973 children died at federal boarding schools. The U.S. Code of Indian Offenses outlawed ceremonies from 1883. If you are Canadian, American or Australian, your governments did this within living memory. What do you know about the school nearest to where you live?

## for-you money
The money line has two parts. Inside communities there are ceremony fees, healing payments and offerings. Outside, there is ayahuasca tourism and appropriated ceremony sold at Western prices, "revenue frequently bypassing source communities." If you have paid for a retreat built on an Indigenous practice, the questions section suggests asking which community authorized it, and then asking that community. Did any of your money reach it?

## for-you cost
Where tradition, family and ethnicity are one fabric, questioning a practice can feel like betraying your identity. Blaming misfortune on angry ancestors taxes refusal without any explicit threat. If you have ever stayed silent about something because speaking would feel like siding with the colonizer, the page's last question is for you: has protecting an abuser ever helped your nation survive anything?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The first row is children taken into residential and mission schools: the largest documented religious harm in this profile, inflicted from outside, with effects that carry across generations in lost languages and in overrepresentation in child welfare. Then girls subjected to cutting or early marriage in specific contexts. The page holds both. Can you acknowledge harm done *to* a people without being silent about harm done *within* it?

## q1
why: Records held by the churches that ran the schools are records of what happened to specific children. The question asks whether they have been released in full, and if not, what is being protected.
example: The cases section records that the residential school system was "largely operated by churches under government contract," and that Catholic entities were released from a $25 million fundraising pledge in 2015 after raising under $4 million.

## q2
why: If nobody in a nation is empowered to hear a complaint against a ceremonial leader, young people have nowhere to go. The question asks whether that absence is tradition, or damage.
example: The glance table's unanswered question: "Protection from outsiders is owed and overdue. Who protects the insider from the elder?"

## q3
why: A ceremony sold by an outsider either has a community's authorization or it doesn't. The question asks for the name of that community, and then asks the community itself.
example: The cases section records James Arthur Ray's sweat lodge for paying participants, where three died, and notes that Lakota leaders had condemned the sale of their ceremonies in 1993 [8][9].

## q4
why: A harmful practice defended as tradition should be defended by name, in public. The question asks which elder will do so.
example: The who-gets-hurt table lists girls subjected to cutting or early marriage "in specific contexts."

## q5
why: Retreat money is traceable. The question asks you to follow it to the account and see whether it reaches the community whose practice is being sold.
example: The glance table records ayahuasca tourism, retreat industries and appropriated ceremony sold at Western prices, "revenue frequently bypassing source communities."

## q6
why: The argument that internal criticism helps the colonizer has real historical weight. The question asks whether protecting an abuser has ever actually helped a nation survive.
example: The law table records the U.S. Code of Indian Offenses (1883), which outlawed ceremonies, and the laws that reversed it in 1978 and 1990.
