## for-you structure
For most Jews outside Israel there is no single authority at all, and the page counts that as real freedom. Inside Israel it is the opposite. The Chief Rabbinate holds a legal monopoly over Jewish marriage and divorce, and gatekeeping over Orthodox conversion and most burial. It is chosen by a 150-member body weighted toward rabbinic insiders, and only the state that created the monopoly could end it. If you are Israeli, this office decides whom you may marry, whatever you believe. Did you ever vote for that?

## for-you law
If you are an American Jewish couple about to marry, here is the most practical sentence on this page. Religious divorce sits outside civil law, and the main protection is a halakhic prenuptial agreement with binding arbitration. The Rabbinical Council of America said in 2006 that no member should officiate without one. Does your rabbi require one? Did you sign one? If not, your daughter may need to know this before her own wedding.

## for-you money
Day-school tuition is, in the page's words, "a defining family cost." The day inside shows $34,000 a year for two children, more than the mortgage. The who-gets-hurt table lists families carrying six figures per child, with communal standing attached. If you are paying it, you already know. The question is the one the day inside leaves open: are you paying for education, or for belonging?

## for-you cost
The page says plainly that leaving a liberal Jewish community costs little, one of very few such statements in this book. The next page, Orthodox and Hasidic Judaism, is a very different story. If you are in a liberal community, it is worth knowing that your freedom to walk away without losing your family is not something every Jew has.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The first row is agunot: women chained in marriage by a husband who refuses a get, while the only remedy sits unused. Then Israelis of every belief, whose marriage, divorce and burial run through religious authority; converts whose status can be revoked retroactively; families under tuition load; and abuse survivors where the accused is a major donor. If a friend of yours were refused a get, who in your community would you call, and would they have any power to act?

## q1
why: Six years is not an edge case. It shows that "discretion" and "no remedy" work identically when no authority will name a husband's conduct a violation and attach a real consequence to it.
example: The glance table's unanswered question states it: "The rabbinic courts hold the only key to a get." The day inside shows Shira in the fourth year of a get refusal, told by the beit din that it cannot compel her husband.

## q2
why: If rabbis have publicly said the rule does not cover abuse, families who still lose standing for calling the police are being punished for something the rule no longer forbids. The enforcement is doing different work from the stated rule.
example: The genealogy section traces mesirah to medieval conditions and concludes that the reason has expired "completely": applying it to a child-abuse allegation in Brooklyn "protects only one party."

## q3
why: A monopoly that made sense as self-government under empire works very differently inside a sovereign democracy. The question asks which voter chose it.
example: The history section records the 1953 Rabbinical Courts Jurisdiction Law, which granted exclusive jurisdiction over Jewish marriage and divorce. The page notes that because a parliament passed it, a parliament can amend it.

## q4
why: A status that changes at a border is jurisdictional, not doctrinal. That matters to anyone whose marriage or whose children's recognition depends on which authority is asking.
example: The cases section records that a London school refused a boy whose mother converted, because the Office of the Chief Rabbi did not recognize him (the UK Supreme Court found this discriminatory). It also records that Reform and Conservative conversions in Israel were only recognized for citizenship in 2021.

## q5
why: This is a straightforward transparency test. Institutions with good policies rarely take long to produce them, so the delay itself is information.
example: The reach section records that communal reputation pressure discourages public discussion of abuse, "particularly where donors and institutions overlap."

## q6
why: A rule that treats internal criticism as help for antisemites ends the conversation before any specific claim is tested. The page takes the danger of antisemitism seriously, and still asks who benefits from the rule.
example: The forefront section concedes that antisemitic movements have always begun by describing Jewish institutions as sinister, and that vigilance is "earned rather than paranoid." The techniques section grades the suppression of internal criticism by invoking antisemitism as Contested, noting that Jewish writers and institutions publicly oppose it.
