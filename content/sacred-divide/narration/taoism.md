## for-you structure
There are two very different structures here. In China, clergy and temples must register through the state-supervised China Taoist Association, and the glance table says the Party chooses and removes its leaders. In the West there is often no structure at all: a self-described master with a website, a lineage you can't verify, and nobody to complain to. Which one are you in? And if something went wrong, who would you tell?

## for-you law
China regulates this tradition closely. In 2017 twelve central government departments barred companies from investing in or running Taoist and Buddhist temples. In 2018 officials covered a 38-meter Laozi statue in yellow cloth, and ordered a village's 18-meter statue demolished as "illegal construction." In the UK, by contrast, tai chi societies registered as charities file public accounts. If you take classes, is your school registered anywhere? If not, you have no regulator at all.

## for-you money
Funerals, exorcisms, blessings, fortune-telling, feng shui and a global market in qigong and wellness courses. The money section describes "priced life-crisis services with no standard rates; escalation during grief." If you are grieving or ill and someone offers a ritual for a price, it is fair to ask for the full price in writing before you agree. What would you think of a teacher who refused?

## for-you cost
Leaving costs little formally. The risk the page describes is being captured by a master in a small school, where "transmission" is used to justify control and fees. If you have been told your doubts are a sign of rigidity, the forefront section's answer is worth reading: Zhuangzi's whole project was deflating people who claimed spiritual authority.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table's first row is students of self-certified masters: financial and, in documented cases, sexual exploitation with no body to report to, where "dual cultivation" framing turned refusal into a spiritual defect. Then the bereaved and the ill, sold outcome-promising rituals at the worst moments of their lives. If someone you love is paying for a cure, how would you ask them about it gently?

## q1
why: A lineage is a factual claim about people and dates. The question asks for facts, and notes when the answer is a lesson instead.
example: The day inside shows a student who asks her teacher where the lineage comes from. After a pause, he says the transmission cannot be put into words. The glance table asks: why can no lineage be verified "before the money changes hands?"

## q2
why: A price list written in advance protects a student from escalation. The question tests whether one exists.
example: The money section records ritual services with "no standard rates," and the global qigong and wellness course market. The say-do table notes teachings "said by people selling words, in graded and priced levels."

## q3
why: If only the teacher can perceive what is wrong with you, nothing you experience can prove him wrong. The question asks what evidence could.
example: The day inside shows a teacher telling a student over Zoom that her "liver qi is stagnant," which is why her shoulder hasn't healed. She writes it down.
