## for-you structure
In Thailand the King has named the Supreme Patriarch since a 2016 amendment to the Sangha Act, and only the Crown can remove him. In the West, a meditation center usually answers to one teacher and a board. Either way, the page's point is that Buddhism already contains an accountability system, the Vinaya, with public confession and expulsion rules. If your teacher did something wrong, who at your center would apply those rules to him?

## for-you law
If you meditate at a center registered as a UK charity, the Charity Commission can investigate its trustees, and in 2020 it found that Rigpa's former trustees had failed to act on what they knew. In Thailand the state disrobes monks and prosecutes officials. In Myanmar a UN mission documented hate speech by nationalist monks against the Rohingya. Is your center a registered charity? If so, you can look up its trustees and accounts today.

## for-you money
Dana, merit-making, Japan's funeral economy, and in the West retreat and course fees. The say-do table sets "the dharma is offered freely" beside priced retreats and unpaid work-exchange labor. If you have paid for a retreat or volunteered your labor, you have funded this structure. The last question on this page is a good one for your next donation: if giving improves your rebirth, why is the beneficiary an institution that could be audited and isn't?

## for-you cost
Leaving is usually easy on paper. The cost is emotional: years of practice, a community, and a teacher you trusted. Monks and nuns who disrobe in traditional societies can lose status. If your practice genuinely helped you, the page's point is that you can keep it. The breath and the sitting don't belong to any single teacher or center.

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table puts nuns and female practitioners first: denied full ordination, seniority and resources in traditions where they are funded least. Then child novices, ordained partly as poverty relief, with documented abuse and weak oversight. If you are a Western practitioner who admires Theravada, did you know that full ordination of women is not recognized in much of it?

## q1
why: The tradition wrote its own accountability system over two thousand years ago. The question asks why centers waited for outside investigators instead of using it.
example: The history section records that the Vinaya established public confession and expulsion rules around 400 BCE. It adds that what was missing "was anyone willing to apply it to the person it was written for."

## q2
why: If every complaint can be reframed as the complainant's own ego, then no evidence could ever establish misconduct. The question points out that the reframing makes the teacher unaccountable.
example: The forefront section calls this "a specific inversion": ideas about ego applied to a student's complaint by the teacher it concerns.

## q3
why: If the tradition's founder ordained women, the barrier is historical rather than doctrinal. The question asks who benefits from leaving the lineage broken.
example: The who-gets-hurt table lists nuns and female practitioners "denied full ordination, seniority, and resources," and the branches table records that full ordination of women is not recognized in Theravada countries.
