## for-you structure
This is one of the few religious authorities in the book that you can actually vote out. The Bombay Parsi Punchayet's seven trustees are elected by an electorate of more than 25,000. Those trustees control the housing trusts, the Towers of Silence and, in effect, the boundary of who counts as Parsi. If you are on the electoral roll, your vote helps decide whether your niece's children can be initiated. Do you know where your trustees stand?

## for-you law
Indian courts have been deciding who is Parsi since *Petit v. Jijibhai* in 1908. The Gujarat High Court held that a Parsi woman stops being Parsi when she marries out. In 2017 the Supreme Court let Goolrokh Gupta attend her parents' funeral rites, and the question has now reached a nine-judge bench. If you are a Parsi woman planning to marry outside the community, your legal status inside it depends on a case that is still open. Did anyone warn you?

## for-you money
The community's trusts are enormous: housing colonies, hospitals and funds. The glance table calls this "benevolence that is also leverage," because access can depend on communal standing and marriage choices. If your family lives in trust housing, ask for the allocation criteria in writing, and check whether marriage appears in them. Would you still give to a fund that might one day exclude your own grandchildren?

## for-you cost
For a Parsi woman who marries out, the cost can include temple access, communal housing, funeral rites at the Towers of Silence, and her children's initiation. A Parsi man who marries out loses none of these. If you are a brother, a father or a trustee, does that asymmetry sit right with you? Who in your family would it fall on?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table puts Parsi women who marry out first, then their children, excluded from the faith by parentage "into adulthood, permanently, with no route in." Then reformist priests who initiate those children and are sanctioned. The say-do table records that this rule is a documented driver of the community's decline. Which is doing more damage to a shrinking community: criticism of the rule, or the rule itself?

## q1
why: A rule about ancestry that depends on which parent married out cannot be about ancestry alone. The question asks the high priests to explain why it tracks the parent's sex.
example: The say-do table sets "Descent rules are ancient and unchangeable" beside the record: they "track the sex of the parent who married out." One of the cases records a Nagpur panchayat rule that excludes women who marry out, and their children, "while imposing nothing comparable on men."

## q2
why: If a shrinking community excludes its own grandchildren, the rule may be accelerating its decline. The question asks who keeps the rule anyway, and what they gain.
example: The say-do table sets "We are dying out and must preserve ourselves" beside the record: "The rule that excludes intermarried women's children is a documented driver of the decline." The law table records the state-funded *Jiyo Parsi* fertility scheme.

## q3
why: Housing allocation is a practical test of whether marriage choices carry a penalty. The question asks for the criteria in writing.
example: The cases section records Parsi women who married out being barred from Parsi institutions and communal housing, with mixed court rulings.

## q4
why: If a priest is sanctioned for initiating a child, the charge should be nameable in the tradition's own scriptures. The question asks what the heresy is.
example: The day inside mentions a priest in the suburbs who performs navjotes for children of intermarried women "and who has been told not to."

## q5
why: The tradition's central idea is free moral choice. The question asks how a child can be excluded for a choice made before they were born.
example: The who-gets-hurt table lists the children of women who married out: "navjote refused; religious existence denied by parentage."

why: A community that feels fragile may treat criticism as a threat. The question asks honestly whether the criticism or the rule is doing more harm.
