## for-you structure
These are the oldest churches in Christianity, and governments have repeatedly reached into them. Eritrea removed a patriarch. An Ethiopian prime minister brokered a synod settlement. Armenia prosecutes its Catholicos. If you belong to one of these churches, the page asks a hard question: when your church's leadership changes, who actually made the decision?

## for-you law
In Egypt, a Copt's marriage and divorce go through the church, because the state offers no civil marriage. In 2010 the Supreme Constitutional Court confirmed that arrangement. So a Coptic woman in a failed marriage has no civil route out. If you are Egyptian, did you know that the courts, not only the church, closed that door? If you live abroad, would you accept that arrangement where you live now?

## for-you money
Much of the money here is land and property: church estates, monasteries and diaspora parishes. In India, two factions of the Malankara church fought over about 1,100 churches until a 2017 Supreme Court ruling. Diaspora parishes registered as charities in the UK or US file accounts. Most of the rest disclose very little. If you give to a diaspora parish, ask whether any of it is sent home, and who accounts for it there.

## for-you cost
Leaving is rarely a formal act here. The cost comes through family law, family honor and a community that is also an ethnic identity. For Coptic women especially, the page shows that "leaving" a dead marriage can be impossible without leaving the church, and the church can be impossible to leave without losing family. Which of those doors is open to the women in your family?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table puts women first: purity rules, divorce rules and an all-male clergy, compounded by personal-status law. Then Eritrean believers under state control, and minority congregations under persecution. Two quite different kinds of harm are recorded here: harm done *to* these churches by states, and harm done *by* church rules to their own members. Does acknowledging the first make it harder to talk about the second?

## q2
why: Autocephaly means self-governance. If a government can remove the head of a self-governing church, the word no longer describes what happens. The question asks readers to check the word against the event.
example: The say-do table records that Eritrea removed a patriarch [2]. The reach section adds that the patriarch's refusal to excommunicate members of a Sunday-school renewal movement was among the charges used against him.

## q3
why: Property disputes show where authority really lives. If the answer to "who owns this church?" took decades of litigation, then the people who pray there did not decide it.
example: The money section records that the Supreme Court of India gave the Malankara Orthodox faction the right to administer about 1,100 churches in 2017. The say-do table adds that implementation still stalls.

## q4
why: A purity rule that depends on the sex of the newborn is hard to explain as anything other than a judgment on women. The question asks who made it, and on what authority.
example: The reach section records the Ethiopian Orthodox practice [14]: after childbirth, women stay away from church for 40 days after a boy and 80 days after a girl. The who-gets-hurt table lists purity rules as the first burden on women.

## q5
why: Money that crosses borders is hard to trace from either end. The question asks whether anyone, in either country, can see the whole flow.
example: The money section records that diaspora parishes registered as charities in the UK and US file accounts, but that financial disclosure is otherwise "generally minimal and jurisdiction-dependent."
