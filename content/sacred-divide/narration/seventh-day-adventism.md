## for-you structure
This is one of the few pages in the book where you really do have a vote. Delegates elect the General Conference president, and sessions have replaced presidents. The glance table calls it the strongest row in the entire book. But a vote only protects you if you use it. If you are a member, do you know who represents your congregation at constituency meetings, and what they voted for last time?

## for-you law
Adventists have used the civil courts against their own institutions and won. In the 1970s Merikay Silver's equal-pay case against Pacific Press ended in a federal ruling that she had been denied allowances paid to men and fired in retaliation. When the Davenport investment scheme collapsed, bankruptcy courts, not church rules, sorted it out. If you work for a church institution, ordinary employment law may protect you more than you realize. Do you know which parts of your job it covers?

## for-you money
Here is the fact most members never think about: your tithe does not stay in your church. It goes up to the conference, which pays pastors, while your local church runs on separate offerings. So the money you give is deliberately separated from the church you can see. And the structure table calls executive pay in the health and university systems "the least-surfaced number in Adventist life." Have you ever seen what your conference did with last year's tithe?

## for-you cost
There is no shunning doctrine, and the page credits that. The cost is your livelihood. In towns like Loma Linda, where the hospital, the university and the school are all Adventist, a change of belief can mean a change of career. If you work in the system and your beliefs shifted, could you say so out loud and keep your job?

## for-you who-gets-hurt
The table names employees whose careers depend on belief; pastors and theologians whose dissent is met with credential withdrawal; women in ministry, voted against at world level in 2015; children taught end-time doctrine at ages that produce anxiety; and members in the Global South, who are most of the church but hold little of its wealth. If you are one of those children grown up, did anyone ever tell you that the doctrine that frightened you was once debated and questioned by leaders who didn't tell the members?

## q1
why: Tithe that flows upward and out of sight is only accountable if someone downstream can see where it went. The question asks for that in writing, at congregation level.
example: The glance table records that tithe goes to the conference, which pays pastors' salaries, rather than to the local congregation, "deliberately separated from the church they can see."

## q2
why: If the church's own commissioned study found substantial literary dependence, how her authority is taught should reflect that. The question asks whether it does.
example: The history section records Walter Rea's *The White Lie* (1982), and a church-commissioned study by Fred Veltman that later confirmed substantial dependence in *The Desire of Ages*. The glance table's unanswered question is: "What was the study for?"

## q3
why: Asking what evidence would have been enough tests whether the doctrine could ever have been revised. If no evidence would have been enough, the hearing was not an inquiry.
example: The history section records that at Glacier View in 1980, theologian Desmond Ford challenged the investigative-judgment doctrine and had his ministerial credentials withdrawn.

## q4
why: Leaders who privately discuss the limits of a prophetic claim, and then file the discussion away, are deciding what members may know. The question asks who made that decision.
example: The history section records that the 1919 Bible Conference debated the nature of Ellen White's inspiration, and that the transcripts were filed away and not published for decades.

## q5
why: Church-affiliated health systems are large employers funded partly by members' trust. The question asks for the one number the page calls hardest to find.
example: The structure table lists Adventist Health and the university systems as corporate boards where "executive compensation here is the least-surfaced number in Adventist life."

## q6
why: Withdrawing credentials over a theological position ends an argument without answering it. The question asks for the procedure and the review.
example: The who-gets-hurt table records that doctrinal dissent "is answered by credential withdrawal rather than argument," where the credential is also the visa, the housing and the pension.
