## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

*Nita · A Tuesday during Paryushan · Ahmedabad*

Day five of the Paryushan fast (the annual Jain festival of fasting and reflection). She has done this since she was fourteen. Her body is quiet and her mind is unusually clear and there is something in it that is genuinely not about anyone watching, and she wants that noted.

Her daughter Aarti is nine and is on day two. Aarti wants to reach four days because Priya from the building reached four days last year and there was an announcement at the temple. Nita has told her that four is not necessary. Aarti has heard that four is available.

At ten the auction. The right to perform tomorrow's rite goes for a figure that is read aloud, and the family that wins is applauded, and everyone in the hall now knows what everyone in the hall can afford. Nita's husband bid twice and stopped. She watched his face when he stopped.

At lunch — which she does not eat — her sister-in-law mentions that the Mehtas' son is not fasting at all this year, and that people have noticed, and that it is a shame because the girl they were considering for him is from a very observant family. Nothing further is said. Nothing further needs to be.

In the afternoon a supplier calls her husband about credit. The supplier is from the community. The conversation is friendly and it is also, Nita understands completely, a conversation about their standing at the temple. These are the same conversation. They have always been the same conversation.

At nine Aarti asks if she can stop, and then, immediately, asks whether Nita will be disappointed. Nita says of course not, sweetheart. And then she hears herself add — lightly, almost as a joke — *but you were so close.* And she lies awake later, hungry and clear-minded and completely awake, thinking about the four words she added and about who exactly they were for.

### The strongest objection, answered

**The objection.** Nobody in this community forces anyone to do anything.

**What is true in it.** The most accurate defense in the codex, and accurate for the monastic orders: nothing in them enforces lay conformity. It is not accurate for every community body. Caste and community councils have imposed social boycotts on Jain families, and Maharashtra legislated against the practice in 2016 [14][15].

**The answer.** Which is exactly the finding. In a community this small and this commercially interwoven, total visibility makes force unnecessary — religious standing and business credit are the same asset. Anekantavada (the doctrine that reality has many sides and no single view captures it) is the tradition's own instrument for examining that, and it was not meant to be pointed only outward.

## 5. History {#history}

Jainism is contemporary with early Buddhism. Mahavira (5th c. BCE) is regarded as the last of 24 tirthankaras (the teachers who, in Jain belief, established the path to liberation). Ascetics and laity depend on each other: ascetics depend entirely on laity, and laity earn merit by supporting ascetics.

### Timeline

```timeline
c. 9th–5th c. BCE | Tradition of 24 tirthankaras; Parshvanatha and then Mahavira (c. 599–527 BCE) | Radical nonviolence and asceticism established as a full path outside Vedic priesthood.
c. 300 BCE–100 CE | Svetambara/Digambara division; Agamas (the canonical scriptures) compiled | Institutional split over monastic practice, especially clothing and women's capacity for liberation.
100–1200 CE | Royal patronage in western and southern India; temple building; monastic orders formalize | Wealthy lay patronage creates a durable temple economy.
1200–1800 | Muslim and then Maratha-era adaptation; merchant communities consolidate | The community's commercial specialization becomes its social infrastructure.
1800s | Colonial-era reform; print culture; diaspora trading networks | Community identity codified; institutions modernize.
1900s | Global diaspora (East Africa, UK, US); institutional philanthropy expands | Diaspora wealth funds temples and community institutions internationally.
1970s–present | Diaspora temple building; animal-welfare and vegetarian advocacy | Animal-welfare and vegetarian advocacy reaches beyond the community; this page does not measure how far.
2015 | Rajasthan High Court rules santhara (sallekhana, the ritual fast toward death) illegal; Supreme Court stays the ruling | The state and the tradition collide over religious dying and family pressure. [COURT RECORD]
2000s–present | Debates over women's monastic status, caste practice, and business-community insularity | Internal reform pressure in a small, tightly networked community.
```

### Moments in the room

::: card
#### 1949 — The Anuvrat movement

Acharya Tulsi launched the Anuvrat (“small vows”) movement on 1 March 1949 at Sardarshahar, taking a monastic discipline and offering it to laypeople as small, voluntary vows [13]. This page records no enforcement mechanism for them.

**Why it matters.** Recorded as one example of what the healthy column on this page could look like: a demanding ethical discipline offered to laypeople as voluntary vows.
:::

::: card
#### 2015 — The court rules on santhara, then the stay

Rajasthan's High Court held the fast unto death to be illegal; within weeks the Supreme Court stayed the ruling after mass protest. The community's autonomy was upheld and the underlying question — how voluntary is a fast performed under communal honour — was never adjudicated.

**Why it matters.** A rare case in this codex where the community won and the question stayed open. Both halves are recorded because the codex reports outcomes, not preferences.
:::

::: card
#### 2008–2025 — The child renunciants reach the courts

Cases concerning the initiation of minors into monastic life reached Indian courts, beginning with a Bombay High Court observation in 2008 and including the Surat stay of December 2025 [4], raising whether a child can consent to a lifelong renunciation.

**Why it matters.** The consent question that this tradition's own ethic of non-harm makes unusually sharp: a movement this careful about the smallest life is the one best positioned to examine what it asks of its youngest members.
:::

## 6. Branches & variants {#branches}

The Svetambara tradition is further divided into Murtipujaka (image worshippers), Sthanakvasi and Terapanth (orders that do not worship images), and the Digambara tradition stands beside it. There are numerous monastic lineages.

| Branch | Where | What differs on this page's questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Śvetāmbara — Murtipujaka** | Gujarat, Rajasthan, diaspora | Its members worship images in temples, and its monks and nuns wear white [6] |
| **Śvetāmbara — Sthanakvasi and Terapanth** | Rajasthan, Gujarat, diaspora | Neither order worships images, and the Terapanth has a single Acharya (Mahashraman, since 2010) [5] |
| **Digambara** | Karnataka, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh | Its monks go naked, and it holds that women cannot attain liberation without rebirth as men [6][7] |

About **4.45 million** Jains in India (2011), with literacy of 94.9%, the highest of any religious community [1].

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

::: card
#### Purity ranking through austerity

**Origin.** Purity ranking arose from ancient ascetic practice in which renunciation genuinely was the path, undertaken by monastics who had left everything. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

**What it was for.** The practice was voluntary, radical and total, for people who had already given up family, property and status. It ranked no one because everyone practicing had exited the status economy entirely.

**Why that reason expired.** Transplanted into lay life with public visibility, austerity became a competitive display. Families accrue status from a child's fast. That inverts the original meaning completely: renunciation now buys the very status it was meant to abandon.

**Who benefits now.** Family reputation benefits, as do the community elites who set the standard. The cost falls on children pushed past their limits and families of modest means who cannot compete.
:::

::: card
#### Auctioned ritual honors

**Origin.** The auction is a temple funding practice in which the right to perform certain rites is bid for. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

**What it was for.** It was a transparent way to fund temple upkeep from a prosperous merchant community.

**Why that reason expired.** It is the most literal transaction in this codex: wealth converted directly into sanctity, in public, with a price. Non-possession is the tradition's central ethic, and the ritual economy ranks people by possession.

**Who benefits now.** Temple trusts benefit, as does the status of the families that win. A community founded on non-attachment now auctions proximity to the sacred.
:::

::: card
#### Digambara doctrine that women cannot attain liberation

**Origin.** The doctrine comes from a classical sectarian dispute, and Svetambara texts disagree with it. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

**What it was for.** It was argued from claims about the female body and nudity requirements for final renunciation.

**Why that reason expired.** The Svetambara position proves the doctrine is contested, not necessary. It is a metaphysical ceiling on half the population, defended by anatomy, in a tradition whose nuns outnumber its monks.

**Who benefits now.** Male monastic precedence and authority benefit. Nothing else does.
:::

::: card
#### Child renunciation (bal diksha)

**Origin.** Youthful monastic entry is a long-standing practice. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

**What it was for.** It allowed early entry into a lifelong discipline in a world where adult life choices were made young anyway.

**Why that reason expired.** A minor cannot meaningfully consent to permanent renunciation of family, property, education, and sexuality. Indian courts and public criticism have raised exactly this, and family honor is an obvious confounding motive. [COURT RECORD / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]

**Who benefits now.** Monastic orders benefit by gaining lifetime members, and families benefit by gaining prestige from a child's renunciation.
:::

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- Information control is minimal doctrinally — anekantavada (many-sidedness) is an explicit epistemic humility principle, one of the strongest native anti-dogmatism resources in this codex.
- Practical control runs through reputation instead: what the community knows about a family circulates instantly through business and marriage networks. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Monastic authority over lay questions can discourage dissent on practices such as extreme fasting. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

### Children

- Dietary restriction from birth (no root vegetables in strict practice, no eating after dark in some households) makes the child's body a site of religious identity from infancy.
- Child fasting during festivals (Paryushan) earns family status; the honor incentive can push children past healthy limits. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Child renunciation (bal diksha) — minors taking monastic vows — has been publicly criticized and litigated in India as a consent problem. [COURT RECORD / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]

### Bodies

- Digambara tradition historically holds that women cannot attain liberation in a female body; Svetambara disagrees — a doctrinal gender asymmetry stated in the sources. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
- Nuns outnumber monks in several orders yet remain subordinate in authority and ritual precedence.
- Menstrual exclusion from temple and ritual participation is common practice.
- Marriage within community and caste is strongly expected; dowry persists despite the ethic of non-possession. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

## 12. The 30 techniques {#techniques}

Thirty named techniques from domestic-abuse and social-psychology research, applied to institutions, in the eight stages of the cycle. Each carries an evidence grade for this tradition.

### Stage 1 · Idealize {#stage-1}

::: stage
**You arrive with a need and are met with more warmth than you have had in years.**

Moral seriousness, radical nonviolence, discipline, and a community that admires restraint. Admiration increases with every step you take.

*What it asks of you:* You were praised for what you gave up. Notice how quickly that became the exchange rate.
:::

::: tactic n=1
#### 1 · Love Bombing {#t-1}

*Overwhelming affection on arrival to create emotional dependency before terms are revealed.*

**How it shows here**

- A seeker is drawn by radical nonviolence, vegetarianism, discipline, meditation, simplicity, and the moral seriousness of ahimsa.
- The community may offer admiration to those who adopt stricter diets, fasting, restraint, ritual participation, and reverence for monks or nuns.
- The love can become conditional on purity: what you eat, what you touch, how much harm you avoid, how strictly you fast, how much you renounce.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is ethical seriousness, not emotional manipulation.

**The counter.** Ethical seriousness becomes coercive when moral worth is tied to impossible purity. If love increases as the person self-denies and decreases when they live normally, the community is not merely honoring discipline. It is training dependency through approval.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Community admiration for restraint is the welcome, and it is entirely social.
:::

::: tactic n=2
#### 2 · Weaponized Generosity {#t-2}

*Giving help that installs unspoken obligation.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain communities may offer support through food, business networks, family systems, temple life, education, ritual events, and ethical community.
- The moral beauty of ahimsa can attract people who want a disciplined, nonviolent, principled life.
- Generosity can become obligation through dietary purity, fasting expectations, ritual giving, reverence for monks and nuns, family pressure, and community reputation.
- Wealthy lay support of temples, monks, events, and community institutions can create a prestige economy where generosity buys moral status.

**The strongest defense.** Jain giving supports nonviolence, monks, education, and ethical living.

**The counter.** Giving to support ethics is not the problem. The problem is when generosity becomes a hierarchy of purity. If people are valued according to how strictly they fast, donate, renounce, or conform, then generosity no longer liberates. It ranks souls.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The support offered through food, networks and ritual life is given informally, and the prestige that giving earns comes from community regard, not from any written rule.
:::

### Stage 2 · Hook {#stage-2}

::: stage
**You are given a future that cannot be verified, and a rope for whenever you drift toward the door.**

Liberation through accumulated restraint, karmic purification measured across lifetimes — no arrival date and no observer. Retrieval arrives as an opportunity: a public fast at Paryushan that would restore the family's standing.

*What it asks of you:* An offer to restore your reputation through visible hunger is not a spiritual invitation. It is a reputational transaction.
:::

::: tactic n=3
#### 3 · Future Faking {#t-3}

*Promising a future that keeps you invested but never has to arrive.*

**How it shows here**

- Jainism offers liberation from karma, escape from rebirth, purity of soul, nonviolence, and eventual release through discipline.
- The future promise is moral perfection: reduce harm, reduce attachment, fast, renounce, purify, and the soul rises.
- Because perfect nonviolence is impossible in ordinary life, the believer is always in deficit. Eating, walking, working, desiring, possessing, and breathing can all become morally loaded.
- Fasting, austerity, monk reverence, and ritual restraint can become proof that one is moving toward liberation, even when the body suffers.
- The end goal is so absolute that ordinary human life looks permanently contaminated. The future pure soul is used to shame the present human body.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism is radically ethical. Its future goal reflects the seriousness of nonviolence.

**The counter.** Ethical seriousness becomes future faking when purity is impossible but demanded anyway. If people are praised the closer they come to self-erasure and guilted for ordinary survival, then liberation becomes a promise purchased with the slow rejection of being human.

**Evidence grade.** [[Taught]] Liberation through accumulated austerity is taught doctrine with no timeline and no observer.
:::

::: tactic n=4
#### 4 · Hoovering {#t-4}

*Pulling someone back after they've started to leave, through guilt, love, or fear.*

**How it shows here**

- A Jain leaving strict practice may be told they are accumulating karma, abandoning ahimsa, becoming spiritually careless, or dishonoring family discipline.
- Food becomes a powerful hoovering site: vegetarianism, veganism, root vegetables, fasting, festival observance, and purity rules.
- Family members may use moral disappointment: “How can you eat that?” “How can you harm life knowingly?” “You were raised better.”
- A person who stops fasting or attending temple may be treated as spiritually lazy or ethically degraded.
- Community prestige can pull them back: participation in rituals, donations, vows, fasts, and temple life signals family honor.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is rooted in nonviolence and moral seriousness.

**The counter.** Moral seriousness becomes coercion when it is used to shame ordinary human limits. If someone cannot loosen practice without being treated as violent, impure, selfish, or karmically reckless, then ethics has become a guilt machine.

**Evidence grade.** [[Taught]] The claim that laxity accumulates karma is taught doctrine; the guilt, disappointment and prestige that draw a person back are applied by family and community.
:::

### Stage 3 · Devalue {#stage-3}

::: stage
**You are taught that you are broken, that your perception is unreliable, and that both exits from the trap lead back inside.**

Not strict enough, not fasting long enough, not pure enough. There is always someone with a harsher observance and the community keeps score.

*What it asks of you:* Purity ladders have no top rung. That is not an oversight.
:::

::: tactic n=5
#### 5 · Devaluation {#t-5}

*Reducing your sense of worth so you become dependent on the institution for identity.*

**How it shows here**

- Jainism can devalue through impossible purity. Ordinary human life constantly causes harm: eating, moving, working, desiring, consuming, owning, and surviving all carry moral weight.
- The ideal of radical nonviolence can make the ordinary person feel perpetually contaminated.
- Fasting, renunciation, restraint, monk reverence, and dietary strictness can become measures of worth.
- Someone who relaxes practice may be treated as careless, impure, violent, karmically burdened, or spiritually lazy.
- The more human the person is, the less pure they appear.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism’s strictness reflects moral seriousness about nonviolence.

**The counter.** Moral seriousness becomes devaluation when it teaches people to experience normal existence as failure. If the highest praise is reserved for self-denial and the body is treated as a liability, then ethics has become a war against being alive.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Purity ranking is produced by public visibility in a small community, not by any ruling.
:::

::: tactic n=6
#### 6 · Gaslighting {#t-6}

*Making you doubt your own experience so you trust the institution's interpretation instead.*

**How it shows here**

- Someone says fasting or purity expectations are harming them, and the response is, “You are attached to the body.”
- A person loosens dietary rules and is made to feel violent, careless, or spiritually degraded.
- Ordinary survival is treated as moral failure because life inevitably causes harm.
- A person’s need for health, pleasure, or normalcy can be reframed as karmic bondage.
- The system makes the human body look like an ethical liability.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism is serious about nonviolence and self-discipline.

**The counter.** Discipline becomes gaslighting when physical harm is renamed spiritual progress. If the body’s distress is dismissed as attachment, then the body has been stripped of its right to testify.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The reply that a person is attached to the body is a conversational reframing, and no document or body requires it.
:::

::: tactic n=7
#### 7 · Double Bind {#t-7}

*A trap where both options lead to compliance. No exit serves you.*

**How it shows here**

- Jains are taught radical nonviolence including speech — so naming a teacher's harm 'violently' is itself a violation. Gentle naming is ignored; forceful naming is himsa (violence, the opposite of ahimsa).
- Asceticism is the honored path, but a young person questioning pressure toward extreme fasts or renunciation is exhibiting attachment — the very thing the pressure claims to cure.
- Sallekhana (ritual fasting toward death) must be free of coercion — while family honor accrues visibly to households whose elders undertake it.

**The strongest defense.** Jain vows are entirely self-imposed; no one is compelled toward austerity.

**The counter.** Self-imposition inside a community that ranks families by austerity is not weightless. When honor flows to the household and the cost lands on one body, 'voluntary' deserves a closer audit.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The bind comes from two things together, a nonviolence teaching that extends to speech and an honor economy that rewards austerity, and no body enforces either against a questioner.
:::

### Stage 4 · Confuse {#stage-4}

::: stage
**The rewards become unpredictable, the standard keeps moving, the answers stop meaning anything, and the accusation gets turned around.**

Question a practice and you are lax, materialistic, insufficiently serious about ahimsa. The framework's own humility principle — anekantavada — gets used to dismiss you as holding merely one view.

*What it asks of you:* Many-sidedness is a beautiful doctrine. Watch for the moment it is deployed only against you.
:::

::: tactic n=8
#### 8 · Intermittent Reinforcement {#t-8}

*Unpredictable reward that creates addictive emotional dependency.*

**How it shows here**

- Austerity is publicly honored on an irregular schedule — the eight-day faster celebrated at one Paryushana and unnoticed the next year — and households chase the community's shifting attention with escalating vows.
- Monastic blessing and notice fall unpredictably on lay families; hosting a revered monk is a jackpot of status that funds years of competitive piety.
- Karma theory meters outcomes ambiguously: prosperity confirms merit, hardship signals old karma burning off — both readings invoice further austerity and dana (giving).

**The strongest defense.** Austerity in Jainism is self-liberation, pursued for the soul, not for applause.

**The counter.** The doctrine says soul; the banquet honoring the faster says otherwise. When the community's brightest, least predictable spotlight lands on self-denial, self-denial becomes a lottery ticket — and someone else prints the tickets.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The unpredictable honoring of austerity and of monastic notice is produced by community attention, not by any schedule or rule.
:::

::: tactic n=9
#### 9 · Moving the Goalposts {#t-9}

*The standard of success keeps shifting so you can never arrive.*

**How it shows here**

- A Jain follows vegetarianism. Then stricter expectations appear: no root vegetables, fasting, festival vows, purity rules, temple attendance, monk reverence, and public restraint.
- A person fasts, but if they stop for health, they are told they are attached to the body.
- Ethical seriousness becomes endless deficit: no matter how carefully one lives, there is always more harm to avoid and more karma to reduce.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism takes nonviolence seriously; restraint naturally deepens.

**The counter.** Restraint becomes moving the goalposts when ordinary survival is always morally insufficient. If the body can never be innocent, purity becomes an unreachable finish line.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The rising expectations that follow vegetarianism are produced by community custom and comparison, not by a published standard.
:::

::: tactic n=10
#### 10 · Strategic Ambiguity {#t-10}

*Language that sounds profound but means nothing specific — functioning as a shutdown to real questions.*

**How it shows here**

- Dietary rules may be framed as personal discipline, family expectation, karmic necessity, compassion, or purity depending on the audience.
- Fasting is described as voluntary, but refusal can invite shame, comparison, or accusations of attachment.
- Nonviolence is clear when judging food choices but vague when confronting emotional coercion.

**The strongest defense.** Jain ethics are voluntary disciplines aimed at reducing harm.

**The counter.** Voluntary discipline becomes strategic ambiguity when social punishment does the work that doctrine denies doing. If refusal costs dignity, the practice is not merely optional.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The shifting account of why the dietary and fasting rules apply, and the shame attached to refusal, are social practice; no rule states them.
:::

::: tactic n=11
#### 11 · Projection {#t-11}

*Accusing the outside world of the exact thing the institution practices.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain communities may accuse people of violence for relaxing dietary rules while ignoring emotional violence caused by shame, purity ranking, and moral pressure.
- A person who stops extreme fasting may be accused of attachment to the body while the community is attached to public austerity and prestige.
- Wealthy donors may gain moral status through temple giving while ordinary people are judged harshly for food choices.
- Normal survival is treated as karmic impurity.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is rooted in nonviolence and restraint.

**The counter.** Nonviolence loses credibility when it becomes psychologically violent. If a person’s body is shamed in the name of harmlessness, the institution has projected harm onto the one trying to survive.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Accusing those who relax practice of violence is conversational; it draws on the community's moral vocabulary and is not backed by any ruling.
:::

::: tactic n=12
#### 12 · DARVO {#t-12}

*Deny the wrongdoing. Attack the person who raised it. Reverse victim and offender.*

**How it shows here**

- A person says fasting pressure, purity expectations, food shame, or family control harmed them. The response may deny harm and frame the concern as lack of discipline.
- Then the person is attacked as attached, careless, violent, indulgent, or spiritually lazy.
- If someone stops strict dietary practice, the community may act as though the person has morally injured others.
- The body’s distress is ignored; the community’s purity ideal becomes the victim.
- The person harmed by impossible standards becomes the offender for lowering them.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is voluntary and rooted in nonviolence.

**The counter.** Voluntary discipline stops being voluntary when refusal brings shame. If a person cannot protect their body without being accused of violence or impurity, then nonviolence has been reversed into psychological harm.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The reversal of harm and accusation described here happens in conversation and family pressure, and no ruling or document records it.
:::

::: tactic n=13
#### 13 · Normalization / Desensitization {#t-13}

*Practices that would seem extreme to an outsider feel normal to insiders because they were installed before critical evaluation developed.*

**How it shows here**

- Dietary restriction can escalate from vegetarianism to stricter rules, fasting, festival vows, avoidance of root vegetables, purity concerns, and shame around ordinary eating.
- Fasting becomes normalized as spiritual achievement even when the body is distressed.
- The believer may become desensitized to guilt because ordinary survival always causes some harm.
- Children may learn early that food, desire, comfort, and bodily needs are morally suspect.

**The strongest defense.** Jain practice cultivates nonviolence, restraint, and compassion for all life.

**The counter.** Compassion becomes desensitization when the person’s own body is excluded from compassion. If someone learns to ignore hunger, illness, or shame because purity is praised, nonviolence has been turned inward as harm.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Competitive childhood fasting is driven by family status with no institutional requirement.
:::

### Stage 5 · Isolate {#stage-5}

::: stage
**Your world narrows until every voice you hear is inside the system, and everything you came for now runs through a middleman.**

Marriage networks, business credit, and religious standing are one system. Your reputation is your creditworthiness, literally.

*What it asks of you:* Ask what disagreeing publicly would cost you commercially. That number is the enforcement mechanism.
:::

::: tactic n=14
#### 14 · Isolation {#t-14}

*Shrinking your world until the only voices you hear are inside the system.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain isolation can happen through strict dietary rules, fasting culture, temple community, marriage expectations, business networks, family honor, and moral ranking around purity.
- Food becomes a social boundary. If you cannot eat what others eat, share kitchens freely, attend normal meals, or relax around diet, your social world narrows.
- A person who loosens practice may feel unable to eat openly, date freely, attend non-Jain events, or admit ordinary desires without moral scrutiny.
- Community prestige around fasting, renunciation, and restraint can isolate those who are less strict, sick, doubtful, or unwilling.
- The outside world is not always called evil; it is simply seen as careless, violent, impure, or morally dull.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline around food and nonviolence is ethical seriousness, not isolation.

**The counter.** Ethics becomes isolation when normal social life is treated as contamination. If a person cannot share food, admit limits, or loosen practice without shame, then nonviolence has become a boundary that wounds.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Community integration of religious standing and commercial credit makes dissent expensive without any enforcement body.
:::

::: tactic n=15
#### 15 · Triangulation {#t-15}

*Inserting intermediaries between you and the thing you came for, so the institution is always in the middle.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain triangulation works through family, temple community, monks, nuns, dietary rules, fasting culture, karma, purity, and moral comparison.
- A person who relaxes food restrictions may be compared to stricter relatives, disciplined elders, monks, nuns, or children who “understand ahimsa better.”
- Family members triangulate through shame: “What will people think if they see you eating that?” “How can you call yourself Jain?”
- Fasting becomes social proof. Those who fast more visibly can become examples used to pressure others.
- The body is triangulated against the soul: physical distress is dismissed because karmic purification supposedly matters more.
- The person is surrounded by moral witnesses: family table, temple events, festival vows, dietary scrutiny, and communal reputation.

**The strongest defense.** Jain practice encourages nonviolence, discipline, and learning from those more advanced in restraint.

**The counter.** Moral example becomes triangulation when it is used to shame normal limits. If the monk, the fasting relative, the temple community, and karma are all invoked to make someone distrust their body, then nonviolence has become socially violent.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Integration of marriage networks and business credit makes insularity economic rather than doctrinal.
:::

### Stage 6 · Extract {#stage-6}

::: stage
**Now the harvest: your labor, your money, your identity, your silence, your children's schooling, your capacity to trust yourself.**

Auctioned ritual honors converting wealth directly into sanctity. Temple trusts run by boards of prominent donor families. Children fasting competitively for family status. Marriage networks and business credit that are the same asset, so dissent is priced commercially. And minors taking permanent monastic vows.

*What it asks of you:* In a community founded on non-possession, proximity to the sacred is sold at auction in public. Say that sentence twice.
:::

::: tactic n=16
#### 16 · Flying Monkeys {#t-16}

*The community enforces the institution's norms without being asked — mobilized by culture, not memos.*

**How it shows here**

- A Jain who loosens dietary restrictions or stops fasting may be pressured by parents, relatives, temple members, monks, nuns, and community friends.
- Food choices become public moral evidence: relatives may shame the person through comparison with stricter family members or respected ascetics.
- A person who stops extreme fasting for health may be told by multiple people that they are attached to the body.
- Community prestige around austerity creates enforcers: people praise the strict and shame the ordinary.

**The strongest defense.** Jain communities encourage nonviolence and restraint.

**The counter.** Encouragement becomes FLYING MONKEY behavior when moral pressure is delivered through family and community surveillance. If everyone at the table becomes an inspector of purity, nonviolence has become socially violent.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The pressure comes from relatives, temple members and friends acting on community norms, and no body directs them.
:::

::: tactic n=17
#### 17 · Smear Campaign {#t-17}

*Rewriting your character after departure to protect the institution from the implications of your leaving.*

**How it shows here**

- A person who stops fasting or relaxes dietary restriction may be smeared as indulgent, violent, impure, attached, lazy, or spiritually careless.
- Health boundaries may be dismissed as weakness.
- Those who question purity pressure may be accused of lacking compassion for life.
- Community comparison can make ordinary eating look like moral failure.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is about reducing harm.

**The counter.** Reducing harm becomes smear when the person protecting their own body is painted as morally degraded. If nonviolence produces character assassination, it has contradicted itself.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The smearing of those who relax practice is done by community members in conversation, and no ruling or document records it.
:::

::: tactic n=18
#### 18 · Silent Treatment / Stonewalling {#t-18}

*Refusing to engage with legitimate questions in order to maintain control.*

**How it shows here**

- A person says fasting, food shame, or purity pressure is harming them and is told they are attached to the body.
- Family or temple members may refuse to discuss the psychological cost of extreme discipline.
- Health concerns are spiritualized rather than addressed.
- The person’s distress is met with silence, comparison to stricter practitioners, or moral disappointment.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline requires restraint and serious moral commitment.

**The counter.** Restraint becomes stonewalling when bodily harm cannot be named without being called attachment. If nonviolence cannot hear the body’s protest, it has become violence with clean hands.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The silence and deflection described are family and temple habits, and no rule requires them.
:::

::: tactic n=19
#### 19 · Manufactured Consent {#t-19}

*Creating the appearance of choice when the conditions of choice were shaped before you could evaluate them.*

**How it shows here**

- A person “chooses” strict diet after family, temple, karma, purity, and comparison to ascetics make ordinary eating feel morally contaminated.
- Fasting may be called voluntary, but praise for austerity and shame for refusal shape the choice.
- Children may consent to restraint before understanding the psychological cost of purity anxiety.
- Health boundaries are overridden because the community treats bodily need as attachment.

**The strongest defense.** Jain vows and disciplines are voluntary ethical commitments.

**The counter.** Voluntary discipline becomes manufactured consent when social shame punishes refusal. If the body says no but the community calls that weakness, consent has been extracted from guilt.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The pressure described here, praise for austerity and shame for refusal, is family and community custom, and no document requires it. Minor renunciation has been litigated in India as a consent problem (section 8), but that concerns initiation, not the dietary pressure this entry describes.
:::

::: tactic n=20
#### 20 · Trauma Bonding {#t-20}

*The wound and the bandage come from the same hand.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain practice can create cycles of purity anxiety and relief: food rules, fasting, restraint, karma, harm, and moral contamination.
- A person feels guilty for eating, desiring, resting, or failing austerity; relief comes through fasting, vows, stricter diet, temple participation, or monk approval.
- The body becomes the problem; discipline becomes the temporary relief.
- Shame returns because ordinary life inevitably causes some harm.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism cultivates compassion and nonviolence through restraint.

**The counter.** Compassion becomes TRAUMA BONDING when the person’s own body is treated as the enemy. If guilt is endless and relief only comes through deeper self-denial, nonviolence has been turned into a cycle of self-harm and approval.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The cycle of guilt and relief through fasting and vows is a pattern of practice and family approval, and no document records it.
:::

::: tactic n=21
#### 21 · Learned Helplessness {#t-21}

*Teaching you that your own judgment is unreliable until you stop trusting it entirely.*

**How it shows here**

- A person tries to relax food rules, stop fasting, or care for their body and is told they are attached, impure, violent, or karmically careless.
- No matter how careful they are, ordinary life still causes harm.
- The moral standard is so high that failure is constant.
- Eventually they stop seeking a healthy balance and accept permanent guilt.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline honestly confronts the harm inherent in life.

**The counter.** Moral honesty becomes LEARNED HELPLESSNESS when the person is given no livable way to be good. If ordinary survival is failure, guilt becomes the atmosphere.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The constant sense of failure follows from the high standard of nonviolence and from the community's response, and no institution enforces it.
:::

::: tactic n=22
#### 22 · Benevolent Control {#t-22}

*Control wrapped in the language of care, so that resisting the control feels like rejecting the love.*

**How it shows here**

- Food restrictions, fasting, restraint, and purity rules are framed as compassion for all life.
- Family may pressure members into stricter practice while saying it protects karma and moral discipline.
- Health concerns may be minimized because bodily comfort is treated as attachment.
- Children may internalize shame around ordinary eating or desire as ethical seriousness.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is about nonviolence and moral care.

**The counter.** Nonviolence becomes BENEVOLENT CONTROL when it harms the person practicing it. If the community protects insects more tenderly than a member’s body, compassion has become selective.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The framing of restriction as compassion is family and community custom, and no body requires it.
:::

::: tactic n=23
#### 23 · Infantilization {#t-23}

*Positioning the adult believer as permanently childlike and dependent on institutional authority.*

**How it shows here**

- Adults may be corrected like children over food, fasting, purity, temple attendance, and restraint.
- A person who listens to their body may be called attached or weak.
- Family and community may praise strict practitioners as mature and shame others as morally childish.
- Children may grow up with purity anxiety that follows them into adulthood.

**The strongest defense.** Jain restraint teaches discipline and moral responsibility.

**The counter.** Discipline becomes INFANTILIZATION when adults are not trusted with their own bodies. If hunger, illness, or limits are treated as childish weakness, moral seriousness has become bodily control.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The correction of adults over food, fasting and temple attendance is family and community custom, and no body requires it. The doctrinal dispute over women's liberation is recorded in sections 10 and 14, not in this entry.
:::

::: tactic n=24
#### 24 · Identity Erosion {#t-24}

*Gradually replacing your identity with a role the institution provides, until you can't separate who you are from where you belong.*

**How it shows here**

- The person may become defined by purity, restraint, diet, fasting, harm avoidance, karma reduction, and moral discipline.
- Ordinary appetite becomes suspect. Pleasure becomes attachment. Comfort becomes weakness.
- The body is treated as something to overcome rather than inhabit.
- Public austerity can become identity: the stricter person is the better person.
- The individual self shrinks beneath the impossible ideal of harmlessness.

**The strongest defense.** Jain identity centers radical nonviolence and ethical seriousness.

**The counter.** Ethical identity becomes IDENTITY EROSION when the person can no longer exist without guilt. If being alive feels morally contaminated, the doctrine has not refined the self. It has made the self ashamed of embodiment.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The identity built on purity, diet and fasting is formed by community regard and family status, not by any institutional role.
:::

::: tactic n=25
#### 25 · Spiritual Bypassing {#t-25}

*Using spiritual language to avoid engaging with real pain, real questions, and real accountability.*

**How it shows here**

- Bodily harm from fasting or strict practice may be bypassed as detachment.
- Anxiety around food may be spiritualized as compassion.
- Shame may be reframed as moral seriousness.
- Family pressure may be bypassed as concern for karma.
- Ordinary human need is treated as attachment to overcome.

**The strongest defense.** Jainism teaches restraint to reduce harm.

**The counter.** Restraint becomes SPIRITUAL BYPASSING when it ignores harm to the practitioner. If the body’s distress is dismissed as attachment, ethics has bypassed embodiment.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The reframing of bodily harm as detachment, and of shame as seriousness, is conversational, and no document prescribes it.
:::

::: tactic n=26
#### 26 · Financial Control {#t-26}

*Extracting money through spiritual obligation while offering no transparency about where it goes.*

**How it shows here**

- Jain financial control may appear through temple donations, festival sponsorships, monk/nun support, charity, wedding costs, business networks, and prestige around giving.
- Wealthy donors can gain moral status through visible philanthropy.
- Families may pressure members to fund rituals, fasts, events, or community institutions.
- Business and marriage networks may reinforce compliance with dietary, caste-like, or family expectations.

**The strongest defense.** Jain giving supports nonviolence, ascetics, temples, and charity.

**The counter.** Giving becomes FINANCIAL CONTROL when generosity ranks souls. If moral prestige is tied to public donation, austerity sponsorship, and community visibility, money has become a ladder disguised as compassion.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] The auctions are conducted openly and the bids are read aloud (section 2), but this page cites no document that codifies them. The wider pressure to donate, sponsor and meet wedding costs runs by custom.
:::

#### 30 · Plausible Deniability {#t-30}

*The institution is never responsible, because the authority is attributed to karma and the ascetic tradition, which no one can cross-examine.*

**How it shows here**

- Strict food rules and fasting are said to be voluntary, but family and community shame make refusal morally costly.
- A person who stops austerity for health may be told they are attached to the body, while the community denies pressure.
- Wealthy donors and visible fasters gain prestige, but the community frames it as admiration for virtue, not status ranking.

- Emotional harm is denied because the stated value is nonviolence.

**The strongest defense.** Jain discipline is voluntary. Ahimsa requires seriousness. Austerity is admired, not forced.

**The counter.** Voluntary practice becomes PLAUSIBLE DENIABILITY when refusal brings shame. Nonviolence is not serious if it ignores psychological harm to practitioners. Admiration becomes coercion when public praise for austerity creates fear of ordinary life.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Authority attributed to karma and the ascetic tradition, with no body that could be petitioned or held responsible.

## 13. The loops {#loops}

::: lede
The seven loops below show how the practices connect, so that each step makes the next one easier and the last step feeds the first. The loops are analysis built from findings recorded elsewhere on this page [PATTERN OBSERVED]; each step names the section where its fact is recorded.
:::

::: card
#### 1 · Money to Doctrine to Money

Auctioned honors convert wealth into sanctity, funding trusts controlled by the families who win them.

**How it runs.**

1. Ritual honors, such as the right to perform a rite, are auctioned to the highest bidder, in public (sections 3 and 9).
2. Winning converts wealth directly into sanctity and raises the standing of the winning family (sections 9 and 10).
3. The proceeds fund temple upkeep, and the temple trusts are run by boards drawn from prominent donor families (sections 7 and 10).
4. Those boards hold the temple funds and the auctioned honors (sections 7 and 16).
5. The families whose standing rests on winning go on bidding, and the next auction follows (sections 2 and 10).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized Generosity](#t-2), [26 · Financial Control](#t-26), [30 · Plausible Deniability](#t-30).

**Why it closes.** The auction turns wealth into standing, and standing belongs to the same donor families whose members sit on the boards that hold the money. One group sets the terms and benefits from them. This page cites no accounts for Indian temple trusts or auction proceeds (section 9), so it cannot say how far an outside reader could follow the money.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens wherever the money becomes visible. The Oshwal Association of the UK files public accounts because charity law requires it (sections 8 and 9), and state trust registrars in India can compel an answer from trusts (section 8). A trustee or donor can ask for the proceeds of each auction to be published. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 2 shows the auction at ten o'clock on a day of fasting: the winning figure is read aloud, the winning family is applauded, and “everyone in the hall now knows what everyone in the hall can afford.”
:::

::: card
#### 2 · Fear to Dependence to Fear

Karmic consequence supplies the fear, public austerity supplies the relief, and visibility does the enforcing.

**How it runs.**

1. Karmic consequence supplies the fear. Perfect nonviolence is impossible in ordinary life, so the believer is always in deficit (technique 3; section 15).
2. A person who loosens practice may be told they are accumulating karma or are spiritually careless (technique 4).
3. Public austerity supplies the relief. Guilt eases through fasting, vows, a stricter diet or monastic approval, and the community honors these publicly (technique 20; section 9).
4. Everyone can see who fasts and who has stopped, so visibility does the enforcing (sections 7 and 11).
5. The relief does not last, because ordinary life still causes harm, and the guilt returns (technique 20).

**Techniques that feed it.** [3 · Future Faking](#t-3), [4 · Hoovering](#t-4), [20 · Trauma Bonding](#t-20), [21 · Learned Helplessness](#t-21).

**Why it closes.** The fear and its relief come from the same source. The doctrine that makes ordinary life a source of harm also offers fasting and vows as the way to reduce it, and the community that honors the fasting is the one that sees who has stopped.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a person can end a fast for health with no cost to standing. Section 20 names this as the change that would revise the austerity findings on this page. That lies with families, pathshala teachers and temple communities (section 18). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Nita tells her nine-year-old daughter that four days of fasting is not necessary, then adds, lightly, “but you were so close.” She lies awake afterwards, hungry and clear-minded, thinking about who the words were for.
:::

::: card
#### 3 · Children to Members to Children

Dietary and fasting identity attaches in infancy, and competitive fasting recruits the next generation through praise.

**How it runs.**

1. Dietary restriction begins from birth and makes the child's body a site of religious identity from infancy (section 11).
2. Children fast during festivals such as Paryushan, and the family earns status from it (sections 10 and 11).
3. Praise rewards the child who fasts longer. In section 2 there was an announcement at the temple, and section 14 records that a child's fast is publicly credited to the family's standing.
4. Some children take monastic vows as minors, which gives the orders lifetime members and the family prestige (section 10).
5. Children raised this way become the adults who fast, sponsor and marry within the community (sections 7 and 11), and the practice passes to the next generation.

**Techniques that feed it.** [13 · Normalization / Desensitization](#t-13), [19 · Manufactured Consent](#t-19), [22 · Benevolent Control](#t-22), [23 · Infantilization](#t-23).

**Why it closes.** Each generation takes on the practice before it can weigh the cost, and is praised for doing so. Recruitment runs through family pride, and no institution has to require it.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a child's fast or vow is not credited to the family's standing, and where a court is asked to review a minor's initiation (sections 8 and 19). That lies with parents, pathshala teachers (section 18) and the courts. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 19, a father went to court to stop the initiation of his seven-year-old daughter as a nun, and the Surat court stayed it.
:::

::: card
#### 4 · Aid to Legitimacy to Leverage to Aid

Community philanthropy generates standing that protects the trusts and the networks.

**How it runs.**

1. The community gives generously and is known for philanthropy and animal-welfare advocacy (sections 16 and 20).
2. Visible giving earns moral status for donors (techniques 2 and 26; section 16).
3. Standing protects the trusts and the networks, because religious standing and commercial credit are the same asset (sections 7 and 16).
4. The protected trusts continue under boards drawn from donor families, and this page cites no accounts for them in India (sections 9 and 16).
5. That wealth and credit fund further giving, and the next round begins from a stronger position.

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized Generosity](#t-2), [22 · Benevolent Control](#t-22), [26 · Financial Control](#t-26), [30 · Plausible Deniability](#t-30).

**Why it closes.** Giving produces standing, and standing protects the bodies that hold the money. The generosity is real, and it is also the source of the legitimacy that shields the institutions behind it.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where trusts publish accounts, as the Oshwal Association does in the UK (section 9), and where state trust registrars exercise oversight (section 8). That lies with trustees and registrars. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 9 records the Oshwal Association's 2025 income of £2.10m. The accounts are public because UK charity law requires it.
:::

::: card
#### 5 · Unpaid Labor to Assets to Power to More Labor

Lay wealth and labor build temples held by donor-family boards.

**How it runs.**

1. Lay donors and volunteers build and sustain temples and community institutions; wealthy lay patronage created a durable temple economy (sections 5 and 9).
2. Monastic life also rests on the laity: ascetics depend entirely on laity for support (section 5).
3. The temples and their wealth are held by trusts, with boards drawn from prominent donor families (sections 7 and 9).
4. Donor prestige hierarchies determine influence over those boards (section 16).
5. Families of modest means, who hold no such influence, compete in a visible sponsorship economy and give more (sections 16 and 17).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized Generosity](#t-2), [26 · Financial Control](#t-26), [29 · Replacement](#t-29).

**Why it closes.** Labor and wealth move in one direction, toward assets that the boards hold, and those who supply them hold no office over the result.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where the assets answer to those who supply them: through published trust accounts (section 9) and through state trust registrars (section 8). That lies with trustees and registrars. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 18 describes monastic attendants and lay organizers who support ascetics and arrange public events, and who are asked to maximize participation.
:::

::: card
#### 6 · Scandal to Removal to Reform Theatre to Continuity

Ordinary lapses are adjusted silently and go unrecorded; where a crime is involved, the courts act.

**How it runs.**

1. A person departs from community expectations by stopping a fast, relaxing diet or questioning purity pressure (sections 12 and 15).
2. No formal process follows, because the monastic orders have no excommunication apparatus for ordinary members (section 15).
3. Standing adjusts at once: marriage prospects, business trust and family standing shift together (technique 28; section 12, stage 7).
4. Nothing is announced. Section 14 records no public election or removal.
5. The trusts and acharyas continue unchanged, and the trust accounts remain unpublished (sections 9 and 14).

**Techniques that feed it.** [17 · Smear Campaign](#t-17), [18 · Silent Treatment / Stonewalling](#t-18), [28 · Discard](#t-28), [30 · Plausible Deniability](#t-30).

**Why it closes.** Because the adjustment is informal, it leaves no record and no decision-maker to hold to account. Nothing is reformed, because nothing was officially done.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** The one case on this page that reached a formal verdict did so through the criminal courts: a Digambar monk sentenced by a sessions court in 2025 (section 19). Police and courts can act where the community does not (section 24), and state trust registrars can compel answers from trusts (section 8). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 14 predicts it: “Nothing will be announced. Standing will adjust, quietly, and the trust accounts will remain unpublished.”
:::

::: card
#### 7 · Persecution to Solidarity to Insulation to Unaccountability

The community's minority status could be invoked to make internal criticism seem disloyal to a small, vulnerable people. The page records no instance of this, so the loop is its reading, not a documented finding.

**How it runs.**

1. Jains are a small minority: 4.45 million in India in 2011, 0.4% of the population (section 6; source [1]).
2. The community's small size and commercial integration mean that everyone is known (section 7).
3. Internal criticism can then be framed as disloyalty to a small people. The page records no instance of this wording; the step follows the loop as stated.
4. Objections are met with replies such as “Business is business” and “Nothing is compulsory in Jainism” (sections 3 and 15).
5. The community's practices go unexamined, and the next criticism is met the same way.

**Techniques that feed it.** [11 · Projection](#t-11), [12 · DARVO](#t-12), [16 · Flying Monkeys](#t-16), [17 · Smear Campaign](#t-17).

**Why it closes.** Each criticism can be read as an attack on a vulnerable people, so criticism strengthens the solidarity that shields the community from it.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where criticism comes from inside, as when anekantavada is used on the community's own certainties (sections 3 and 23), and where courts hear the questions (section 22). That lies with members and the courts. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 3 records the objection “Nobody in this community forces anyone to do anything,” which the page calls the most accurate defense in the codex and then answers.
:::

## 16. The ledger {#ledger}

### Who benefits

- Trust boards and community elites control ritual honors and match networks, and families accrue status from members' austerities.

### Money out, leverage back

- Community integration means religious standing and commercial credit are the same asset — dissent is expensive in a currency that has nothing to do with belief. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Temple trusts are run by boards drawn from prominent donor families, with donor prestige hierarchies determining influence. This page names no trust and cites no accounts for one. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- The community's philanthropy and animal-welfare advocacy are widely known and worth stating plainly; this page does not measure their reach.

### Who pays

- Women pay, under liberation-capacity doctrine, ritual exclusion and dowry practice.
- Children pay, pushed into competitive fasting or minor renunciation.
- Elderly people pay in contested santhara cases, where family honor may shade voluntary dying.
- Families of modest means pay, competing in a visible sponsorship economy.
- Anyone whose independence would cost them the credit and marriage network at once pays as well.

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. Non-possession is your central ethic. Explain the auction for ritual honors.
2. If a child's fast brings the family status, ask honestly whose fast it is.
3. The Svetambara tradition says women can attain liberation. Ask a Digambara authority why his position, which excludes half of humanity, should be the one that stands.
4. A minor cannot sign a contract. Explain how a minor can renounce the world permanently.
5. Ask your temple trust for accounts. Note whether the request is answered or absorbed.
6. Nobody here forces anything — that is your community's most accurate claim and its most complete defense. Ask why total visibility makes force unnecessary.

### In closing

Anekantavada — the doctrine that reality has many sides and no single view captures it — is the single best anti-dogmatism principle any religion produced. It belongs to you. Use it on your own community's certainties, not only on outsiders'.

Your ethic of nonviolence is not diminished by asking who benefits from a child's hunger or a woman's silence. It is completed by it. Ahimsa that stops at the community's reputation was never ahimsa; it was etiquette.

## 24. Leaving safely here {#leaving}

Practical guidance, not legal advice.

1. **Lay Jains can leave or lapse freely;** the costs are family and business networks.

3. **If a monk or nun abuses you,** go to the police; a court has convicted a monk [9].
