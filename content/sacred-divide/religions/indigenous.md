## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

*Tanya · A Tuesday in berry season · A First Nation in northern Canada, and the town an hour south*

Up at six, coffee, and she watches the light come over the water where her grandmother's grandmother's people watched it come over the water, and there is no sentence in English that gets this right so she has stopped trying.

She teaches language at the school. Eleven kids in her Tuesday group. Two of them have grandparents who were at the school in Fort Alexander and who do not speak the language because it was beaten out of them, and Tanya is teaching their grandchildren the words back. She would do this for free. She nearly does.

At ten she takes a call about the records. Fourth call this year. The archdiocese has responded to say the material is under review. It has been under review since 2019.

At noon she sees an ad on her phone: a wellness retreat, four hours south, non-Indigenous facilitator, $2,800 for four nights, using her nation's word for the ceremony in the headline. She screenshots it. She has a folder of these. The folder has forty-one screenshots in it.

In the afternoon a girl of about fifteen stays behind after class and doesn't say anything for a while, and then talks about her uncle, who is also a ceremonial helper, and who everyone respects, and who she doesn't want to be in the room with. Tanya listens. She knows exactly who. She knows there is no one to report to, because the nearest thing to a body is a circle of people who are all related to him, and because the last time someone raised something about a respected man it turned into a story about the person who raised it.

At night she sits on the step. She thinks: everything that nearly destroyed us came from outside, and that is the truest thing about my life. And then she thinks about a fifteen-year-old who cannot say a name out loud. And she understands that both of those are the same fight, that they have always been the same fight — protecting our children — and that almost nobody will let her say them in the same sentence.

## 3. The forefront {#forefront}

### The strongest objection, answered

**The objection.** Any criticism of these traditions continues a colonial project that nearly destroyed them.

**What is true in it.** The largest harm in this profile was inflicted from outside, by other religions' institutions, and is documented by national commissions including unmarked graves. That framing is not a preamble; it is the substance.

**The answer.** And sovereignty includes the authority to hold your own accountable. Protection from outsiders and protection from insiders are not opposites — nations that built their own protocols for ceremony, research, and safeguarding demonstrate that. The codex names the bind rather than resolving it, because it is real.

## 4. What healthy looks like here {#healthy}

::: lede
Hundreds of millions of people practice indigenous, folk, and ancestral traditions worldwide — land-based, orally transmitted, kinship-woven lifeways that survived colonization, missionary suppression, and extraction.
:::

- Community-accountable eldership; nations setting their own protocols for ceremony and research; revival movements pairing sovereignty with internal accountability — protection from outsiders without protection from insiders' own people.

## 5. History {#history}

No founding moment: these are the oldest continuous religious forms on earth. The relevant modern history is what was done to them — banned ceremonies, residential schools, stolen land — and the ongoing revival now navigating authenticity, authority, and commerce.

### Moments in the room

::: card
#### 1883–1951 — The Potlatch and Sun Dance bans

Canada and the United States criminalised central ceremonies for decades. People were imprisoned and regalia confiscated; some of it sits in museums still.

**Why it matters.** This page's inversion of the codex's usual question. The apex here was imposed from outside, by statute, and its harms are the documented ones.
:::

::: card
#### 1996 and 2015 — The last school closes; the Commission reports

Canada's last residential school closed in 1996. In 2015 the Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented the system and named it cultural genocide, documenting more than 3,200 child deaths; the national memorial register listed 4,037 names in 2019 and added more than 1,100 in 2025, and potential unmarked graves have been identified since 2021.

**Why it matters.** The most thoroughly documented case in this codex of religious institutions operating a state programme against children — and the only page where the codex's usual accountability question points outward at churches and governments rather than inward.
:::

::: card
#### 1993 and after — The Declaration of War against Exploiters of Lakota Spirituality

Lakota leaders issued a formal declaration against non-Native sale of ceremony. It has been reissued and reaffirmed since, and remains routinely ignored by the retreat industry.

**Why it matters.** Where authority in this tradition actually spoke, in public, in its own name. That page's compel column defers to it — because the communities' own repudiations are the authority that matters.
:::

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

::: card
#### Missionary and residential schooling

**Origin.** Church-run residential and boarding schools operating from the 1870s in Canada, the U.S., and Australia — the last closing within living memory. [GOVERNMENT REPORT: Canada's TRC]

**What it was for.** Stated as civilizing and Christianizing; in practice, the explicit destruction of language, ceremony, and family.

**Why that reason expired.** This is the codex's clearest case of religion used as a weapon by outsiders. Deaths, abuse, and unmarked graves are documented by national commissions. It has not expired in effect: the trauma, the language loss, and the child-welfare overrepresentation are present-tense.

**Who benefits now.** Historically: colonial states and the churches contracted to run the schools. Now: the institutions that have still not fully disclosed records or paid what they promised. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
:::

::: card
#### Criminalization of ceremony

**Origin.** Potlatch and Sun Dance bans, the U.S. 'Religious Crimes Code'; American Indian Religious Freedom Act only passed in 1978. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

**What it was for.** Suppression of political and economic structures that colonial administration could not control.

**Why that reason expired.** Legally, yes. But the interruption is why so much transmission is broken and so much authority is now unverifiable — which is precisely the vulnerability that outside appropriators exploit.

**Who benefits now.** The appropriation economy, which fills a vacuum that suppression created.
:::

::: card
#### Unaccountable individual authority

**Origin.** Oral, kinship-based transmission in which the elder or healer is also relative, doctor, and community authority. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

**What it was for.** In an intact community, that concentration was checked by everyone knowing everyone — accountability was total precisely because the community was small and permanent.

**Why that reason expired.** Where colonization broke communities, dispersed families, and severed knowledge chains, the checks broke with them. What remains is authority without the community structure that used to regulate it — and abuse by a ceremonial figure is nearly impossible to report when he is also your uncle and the only healer for two hundred miles.

**Who benefits now.** Specific individuals, in specific communities. Naming this is not colonial; refusing to name it protects the same people the boarding schools protected.
:::

::: card
#### 'Plastic shaman' commercialization

**Origin.** Expanded from the 1960s counterculture onward; Lakota elders issued a formal declaration against exploiters in 1993. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

**What it was for.** None. This is theft with incense.

**Why that reason expired.** It was never legitimate, and Indigenous nations have said so in writing. Untrained outsiders sell ceremony at Western prices — in one documented case with three deaths and a criminal conviction. [COURT RECORD]

**Who benefits now.** Non-Indigenous entrepreneurs, retreat operators, and the wellness industry. Source communities generally see none of it.
:::

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- Sacred knowledge is legitimately restricted by initiation and kinship — this is genuine intellectual sovereignty, not information control, and the codex distinguishes them.
- The control risk is that the same secrecy can shield a specific abusive elder from community accountability. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Outsider extraction is the larger documented problem: researchers, tourists, and self-appointed shamans publishing and selling restricted knowledge without consent. [INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]
- 'You'll dishonor the ancestors' can suppress internal questioning — an outsourcing of authority to people who cannot be consulted. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

### Children

- Initiation and coming-of-age rites are typically communal and consensual within tradition; the practice at issue is where they are conducted without the young person's real ability to decline. [VARIES BY COMMUNITY]
- The overwhelming documented harm to Indigenous children in this profile was inflicted by outside religious institutions: residential and mission schools, with confirmed deaths and unmarked graves. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Female genital cutting occurs in some traditional contexts and is defended in cultural terms; it is a documented harm to children regardless of framing. [GOVERNMENT REPORT: WHO]
- Child witchcraft accusations in parts of West and Central Africa — often at the intersection of traditional belief and Pentecostal deliverance ministries — have led to abandonment and violence. [GOVERNMENT REPORT / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]

### Bodies

- Many traditions were and are matrilineal, with women holding ceremonial and political authority — the colonial imposition of patriarchy is itself a documented harm. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
- Two-spirit and third-gender roles were recognized in numerous nations and were specifically targeted by missionaries. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
- Where control patterns do appear internally: arranged or early marriage, menstrual restriction, and elder authority over women's choices in some communities. [VARIES BY COMMUNITY]
- Abuse by ceremonial leaders is documented and structurally hard to report where the leader is also kin, healer, and community authority. [FORMER MEMBER TESTIMONY]
- Missing and murdered Indigenous women is the overwhelming gendered harm here, and its source is external. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]

## 12. The 30 techniques {#techniques}

Thirty named techniques from domestic-abuse and social-psychology research, applied to institutions, in the eight stages of the cycle. Each carries an evidence grade for this tradition.

### Stage 1 · Idealize {#stage-1}

::: stage
**You arrive with a need and are met with more warmth than you have had in years.**

Ceremony, ancestry, land, songs, elders, and the promise of recovering what colonization took. It touches identity at a level deeper than belief — blood, soil, memory, survival.

*What it asks of you:* What you were offered was not a doctrine. It was your own inheritance, which is why refusing any part of it feels like refusing yourself.
:::

::: tactic n=1
#### 1 · Love Bombing {#t-1}

*Overwhelming affection on arrival to create emotional dependency before terms are revealed.*

**How it shows here**

- People may be drawn through ceremony, ancestry, land, elders, songs, healing rituals, initiation, and the promise of returning to what colonization or modernity stole.
- The emotional hook is powerful because it touches identity deeper than belief: blood, soil, memory, tribe, ancestors, survival.
- Love becomes coercive when elders, shamans, family systems, or communal authorities use ancestry to demand obedience, silence abuse, enforce gender roles, or punish departure as betrayal of the people.

**The strongest defense.** These traditions were oppressed; criticizing them repeats colonial disrespect.

**The counter.** Oppression by outsiders does not make internal control sacred. A wounded tradition can still wound people. Respecting survival does not require pretending every elder is safe, every ritual is harmless, or every demand made in the name of ancestors is love.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Ceremony and kinship welcome are communal and, unlike most of this codex, offer something that is genuinely the reader's inheritance.
:::

::: tactic n=2
#### 2 · Weaponized Generosity {#t-2}

*Giving help that installs unspoken obligation.*

**How it shows here**

- Indigenous, folk, and ancestral traditions may offer ceremony, healing, elder guidance, songs, land connection, initiation, family belonging, and recovery of identity after displacement or colonization.
- The generosity can be profound because it restores what modernity, migration, or historical violence severed: ancestry, place, ritual, memory, and communal meaning.
- But that same restoration can become leverage when elders, shamans, family authorities, or community leaders demand obedience, secrecy, money, sexual access, silence, or loyalty in the name of tradition.
- Outsiders or reconnecting descendants may receive belonging, but later be told that questioning a leader, ritual, payment, or abuse is disrespecting the ancestors.

**The strongest defense.** These traditions were oppressed and exploited. Criticizing them risks repeating colonial harm.

**The counter.** Oppression by outsiders does not make internal coercion sacred. A tradition can deserve protection from erasure and still require accountability. If generosity restores identity but then uses that identity to demand silence, the ancestors have been turned into debt collectors.

**Evidence grade.** [[Contested]] Ceremony reciprocity is legitimate within tradition; the documented extraction is overwhelmingly by outside commercial operators. *(sourced)*
:::

### Stage 2 · Hook {#stage-2}

::: stage
**You are given a future that cannot be verified, and a rope for whenever you drift toward the door.**

Restoration of what was taken, connection to land and ancestors, healing across generations — and unlike almost everything else in this codex, a promise that is substantially real. Retrieval is a ceremony, a funeral, an appeal to what your grandparents survived to give you.

*What it asks of you:* What you were offered is your own inheritance, which is why refusing any part of it feels like refusing yourself.
:::

::: tactic n=3
#### 3 · Future Faking {#t-3}

*Promising a future that keeps you invested but never has to arrive.*

**How it shows here**

- Indigenous, folk, and ancestral systems can future-fake through ancestral blessing, restored identity, healing of bloodlines, land reconnection, initiation, protection, fertility, communal restoration, and return to original ways.
- The promise is emotionally profound: what was stolen can be recovered; what was broken in the lineage can be healed; the ancestors are waiting; the old ways will make you whole.
- Elders, shamans, healers, or family authorities may claim access to future blessing or protection through ceremony, offerings, secrecy, obedience, and initiation.
- For reconnecting people, the future promise can become identity salvation: if you submit to this path, you will finally belong; if you question it, you are betraying your ancestors.
- Some folk systems tie misfortune to ritual neglect: sickness, infertility, bad luck, family conflict, or spiritual attack may be explained as failure to honor the spirits properly.
- The promised future may be cultural survival. That is powerful. It can also be used to make individuals carry obligations they did not consent to.

**The strongest defense.** Ancestral traditions were attacked, colonized, and erased. Their restoration is healing, not manipulation.

**The counter.** Restoration is not automatically freedom. A tradition can be wounded by history and still wound its own people. If ancestors are used to demand silence, money, obedience, secrecy, or submission to unsafe leaders, then the future of the lineage has been turned into a weapon against the living.

**Evidence grade.** [[Contested]] What is promised — restoration of what was taken — is substantially real; the risk is a gatekeeper controlling access to it.
:::

::: tactic n=4
#### 4 · Hoovering {#t-4}

*Pulling someone back after they've started to leave, through guilt, love, or fear.*

**How it shows here**

- Someone leaving ancestral, folk, or Indigenous religious practice may be pulled back through elders, family, initiation obligations, ancestral fear, land connection, or claims of spiritual protection.
- The pressure may sound like: “Your ancestors are calling you,” “You owe the spirits,” “Do not abandon the old ways,” “Bad things happen when people neglect the rites.”
- Reconnecting people may be especially vulnerable because the tradition offers identity repair. Leaving can be framed as betraying ancestors, culture, colonized memory, or the community’s survival.
- If the person questions a healer, elder, shaman, or ritual authority, they may be told they are disrespectful, colonized, spiritually dangerous, or cut off from protection.
- Ceremony, songs, kinship, and land can be used to reattach the person before abuse, exploitation, or coercion is addressed.

**The strongest defense.** Ancestral traditions were suppressed and deserve protection, continuity, and respect.

**The counter.** Respect does not require surrendering discernment. A tradition can be historically wounded and still produce internal control. If ancestors are used to drag the living back into unsafe authority, then memory has become a chain.

**Evidence grade.** [[Contested]] What is promised — restoration of what was taken — is substantially real; the risk is a gatekeeper controlling access to it.
:::

### Stage 3 · Devalue {#stage-3}

::: stage
**You are taught that you are broken, that your perception is unreliable, and that both exits from the trap lead back inside.**

Not traditional enough, not fluent enough, too urban, too assimilated, too white-acting. Authenticity becomes a hierarchy and someone always ranks above you.

*What it asks of you:* Colonization made authenticity scarce, and then people started charging admission to it.
:::

::: tactic n=5
#### 5 · Devaluation {#t-5}

*Reducing your sense of worth so you become dependent on the institution for identity.*

**How it shows here**

- Indigenous, folk, and ancestral systems can devalue through lineage obligation, ancestral fear, elder authority, ritual purity, initiation hierarchy, and belonging anxiety.
- A person may be told they are disconnected, colonized, spiritually unprotected, disrespectful, cursed, polluted, or betraying the ancestors if they question or leave.
- Reconnecting people are especially vulnerable because they may already feel identity loss. The system can offer belonging while also telling them they are incomplete without ceremony, elders, bloodline repair, or ritual approval.
- Family or ritual authorities may use misfortune as proof: sickness, bad luck, dreams, infertility, conflict, or anxiety become signs that the ancestors or spirits are displeased.
- The individual self is reduced beneath the dead, the land, the lineage, the spirits, or the elder.

**The strongest defense.** These traditions were colonized and suppressed, so restoration of ancestral identity is healing.

**The counter.** Restoration becomes devaluation when it tells the living they are spiritually defective without submission to unsafe authority. A tradition can deserve protection from erasure and still require protection for the people inside it. The ancestors should not be used to make the living feel small.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Authenticity hierarchies are produced by dispossession rather than doctrine, and are enforced socially.
:::

::: tactic n=6
#### 6 · Gaslighting {#t-6}

*Making you doubt your own experience so you trust the institution's interpretation instead.*

**How it shows here**

- A person questions an elder, healer, shaman, ritual authority, or family tradition, and is told they are disrespecting the ancestors.
- Misfortune may be interpreted as proof that spirits are displeased.
- Someone who leaves may be told they are colonized, disconnected, cursed, unprotected, or betraying the old ways.
- Reconnecting people may doubt themselves because they already fear being inauthentic or rootless.
- Abuse can be hidden behind sacred secrecy, initiation hierarchy, or claims that outsiders cannot understand.

**The strongest defense.** Ancestral traditions were violently suppressed and deserve respect, protection, and continuity.

**The counter.** Respect does not require surrendering reality. A tradition can be oppressed by outsiders and still be abusive inside. If ancestors are used to make people distrust their own fear, pain, or moral alarm, then the dead have been turned into a weapon against the living.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Authenticity hierarchies are produced by dispossession rather than doctrine, and are enforced socially.
:::

::: tactic n=7
#### 7 · Double Bind {#t-7}

*A trap where both options lead to compliance. No exit serves you.*

**How it shows here**

- Reconnecting descendants are told the tradition is their birthright — and that questioning an elder's demands proves colonization has claimed their mind. Their heritage is inside; their judgment must wait outside.
- Sacred knowledge requires secrecy — so harm that occurs inside ceremony cannot be named without profaning the ceremony. The protection of the sacred and the protection of the abuser share one lock.
- Refusing an initiation, payment, or match is free — and misfortune afterward will be read aloud as the ancestors' answer.

**The strongest defense.** Secrecy and elder authority protected these traditions from extermination; outside frameworks were the weapon.

**The counter.** Both things are true: the wall was necessary, and some now use it as a hiding place. Honoring survival does not require pretending the wall has no shadows. Accountability to one's own people is not colonization — it is what the ancestors' trust was for.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Authenticity hierarchies are produced by dispossession rather than doctrine, and are enforced socially.
:::

### Stage 4 · Confuse {#stage-4}

::: stage
**The rewards become unpredictable, the standard keeps moving, the answers stop meaning anything, and the accusation gets turned around.**

- Ayahuasca and psychedelic retreat tourism generates very large revenues, frequently operated by non-Indigenous entrepreneurs, with source communities bypassed and safety screening inadequate. [INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. Which church ran the school your family was taken to, and has it released its records in full? If not, ask what it is protecting after a hundred years.
2. If a ceremonial leader harmed a young person, ask who in your nation is empowered to hear it — and if the answer is nobody, ask whether that is tradition or damage.
3. Ask a non-Indigenous person selling your ceremony to name the community that authorized them. Then ask that community.
4. If a cut, an early marriage, or an accusation is defended as tradition, ask which elder will state it publicly in their own name.
5. Ask who receives the money from a retreat run on your practices. Follow it all the way to the account.
6. You may have been taught that internal criticism helps the colonizer. Ask honestly whether protecting an abuser has ever helped your nation survive anything.

### In closing

Your traditions are the only ones in this codex that were nearly exterminated by another religion's institutions, and the accounting for that is nowhere near complete. Nothing on this page reduces that, and anyone who quotes it to excuse what was done to your family has misread it deliberately.

And still: sovereignty means governing yourselves, which includes the authority to hold your own accountable. Protection from outsiders and protection from insiders are not opposites — nations that have built their own protocols for ceremony, research, and safeguarding have shown that. The most anti-colonial thing available is a community that does not need anyone's permission to protect its own children.
