## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

- **name:** Nusrat
- **when:** A Tuesday in the week before Jalsa (the community's annual convention)
- **where:** A town in southern England

1. She is on the duty rota of Lajna, the community's women's organization, for Jalsa again: registration desk, Saturday and Sunday. She volunteered in March and has already been reminded twice. She doesn't mind the work. She loves Jalsa: the marquees, the tens of thousands, her grandmother's face when the Khalifa speaks.
2. At lunch she opens the jamaat's (the local branch's) online chanda (contribution) portal [19] and sees the figure entered against her name, estimated from her last payslip. It is a little higher than she earns now that she's part-time. She means to correct it and doesn't, because correcting it feels like asking for less.
3. Her cousin in Lahore sends a photo of the mosque door with the minaret boarded over, by police order this time, not a mob. Nobody in the family group chat says anything for twenty minutes. Then everybody sends the same prayer.
4. After work the marriage secretary's wife calls her mother again. There is a good boy in Birmingham, a doctor, from a family with a wasiyyat. Her mother says Nusrat is thinking. Nusrat is thinking about a colleague who is not Ahmadi, which is a thought she has never said out loud to anyone.
5. In the evening she watches the Friday sermon her father recorded. The Khalifa speaks about obedience and about the martyrs of 2010. She cries at the part about the martyrs, as she always does, and means it.
6. Before bed she drafts an email to the finance secretary correcting the figure, reads it back, and deletes the second sentence, the one that explains why. Then she deletes the email. She will pay the higher figure. It is easier than being the person who asked.

*Nusrat is a composite drawn from documented patterns. No real person is depicted.*

## 3. The forefront {#forefront}

::: question
If the Khalifa errs, what written procedure corrects him — and why is there none?

### The strongest objection, answered

- *Attack:* "You are adding fuel to the fire of people who kill us."
- *Concede:* The persecution is real, lethal, legally codified in Pakistan, and documented on this very page before anything else. No criticism here lessens it.
- *Answer:* Persecution from outside does not make an internal rule beyond question. A community that has spent a century demanding that no state decide its faith is the strongest possible advocate for the same freedom for its own members who leave.

## 4. What healthy looks like here {#healthy}

::: lede
Independent sources estimate at least 10–20 million members worldwide, as summarized in a reference work [32]; the community's own estimates are far higher ("tens of millions"). It is a messianic reform movement within Islam, founded in 1889 by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian, Punjab. It follows a living spiritual leader, the Khalifa, and is known for its motto "Love for All, Hatred for None", its global missionary and relief work, and its rejection of violent jihad.
:::

- In the UK the community's charities are on the public register [13].
- The community has kept to non-violence under extreme provocation: its response to massacres has been prayer, litigation and documentation, not reprisal.
- Girls receive a strong education, and women have their own organization (Lajna Imaillah) with its own elected leadership.
- The community explicitly renounces violent jihad, and its humanitarian work (Humanity First) is open to all.

Some of that standard is already on the record. The UK association files public accounts with the Charity Commission [13][24], and it publishes a safeguarding policy, issued in 2022 and revised in 2024, that refers criminal concerns to police and statutory bodies [21]. Contribution rates, marriage rules and the grounds for expulsion are published in the community's own literature [1][11][12], so a member can read the rules that bind them.

## 5. History {#history}

Founded in British India when Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (1835–1908) took the pledge of allegiance (bayʿa) from followers in 1889, claiming to be the Promised Messiah and Mahdi (the divinely guided figure many Muslims expect in the last days). After his death a caliphate (Khilafat) was established. In 1914 it split into the majority Qadian community and the smaller Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement, which rejects the caliphate [20]. After Partition, headquarters moved to Rabwah (now Chenab Nagar), Pakistan. After 1984 criminal laws made practice there near-impossible, the Khalifa moved to London; on 15 April 2019 headquarters moved to Islamabad, Tilford, Surrey [10].

## 6. Branches & variants {#branches}

There are two branches: the Ahmadiyya Muslim Community (caliphate, Qadian lineage) and the much smaller Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement, which has no caliphate and regards the founder as a reformer, not a prophet.

| Branch | Where | What differs on this page's questions |

## 7. Structure {#structure}

### Size and shape

- **adherents:** Membership is at least 10–20 million by independent estimates, as summarized in a reference work [32], and the community claims tens of millions.
- **regions:** The community is present in Pakistan (largest by community estimates, but undercounted: the 2023 census recorded 162,684 Ahmadis, and many boycott a census that does not let them record themselves as Muslim [31]), India (Qadian), Ghana, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Indonesia, Bangladesh, the UK, Germany, Canada and the US.
- **branches:** There are two: the Ahmadiyya Muslim Community (caliphate, Qadian lineage) and the much smaller Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement, which has no caliphate and regards the founder as a reformer, not a prophet.
- **trend:** The community is growing in West Africa and by conversion; in Pakistan, it faces pressure and emigration.
- **participation:** Institutional participation is very high. Contribution, auxiliary membership and duty rosters are organized and recorded, which is itself part of the machinery.

### Authority

- Authority rests with a single living Khalifa (Khalifatul Masih), elected for life by an electoral college and regarded by members as divinely guided. Obedience to the Khalifa and to the community's administrative system (Nizam-e-Jamaat) is taught as a religious duty [18]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Beneath him is a tiered administration: national amirs (national heads) appointed by the Khalifa, local jamaat presidents, departmental secretaries (finance, general affairs/discipline, marriage, education), a trained missionary corps, and auxiliary organizations by age and sex (Khuddam for young men, Ansar for older men, Lajna for women, Atfal for boys, Nasirat for girls). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- The Sadr Anjuman Ahmadiyya (the central administrative body) and the Tahrik-e-Jadid and Waqf-e-Jadid schemes (two long-standing funds for missionary work) administer property, missions and funds. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

## 9. Money {#money}

### Where it comes from

- Money comes from graduated compulsory contributions (chanda). The general contribution (Chanda Aam) is compulsory for every earning member at one-sixteenth of income after tax (6.25%), and the community's own guide calls it "the basic and compulsory Chanda … obligatory upon every earning Ahmadi" [1]. Members who join the Wasiyyat scheme pledge one-tenth to one-third of their estate at death and pay one-tenth to one-third of income instead of Chanda Aam [2]. Further schemes (Tahrik-e-Jadid, Waqf-e-Jadid, Jalsa Salana, auxiliary dues) sit on top [1]. A member who does not pay the obligatory chandas cannot vote in community elections or become an office-bearer; a "chanda payer" is defined as one with no more than six months of arrears, with exceptions where the center has allowed instalments or a reduced rate [1]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Children can be dedicated before birth to lifelong service under Waqf-e-Nau, launched on 3 April 1987; dedication must be made before the child is born [15]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- In the UK the community operates through registered charities on the public register, including the Ahmadiyya Muslim Association United Kingdom (charity no. 299081) [13]. Public registration is a genuine disclosure, stronger than many traditions in this book. The older entity, no. 299081, reported nil income for FY2025; the community now also operates through a newer registered entity, no. 1208543 [13]. That newer entity reported income of £41.46m, all from donations and legacies, and spending of £31.65m for the year to 30 June 2025 [35]. [REGULATORY FILING]

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

1. **Chanda as a fixed percentage of income**
   - *Origin:* The rule began with the founder's call for financial sacrifice, which set the Chanda Aam and Wasiyyat rates [1][2]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
   - *Then:* A small persecuted movement with no state, no endowment and no patrons had to fund itself entirely.
   - *Expired?* The reason has partly expired. It is now a global organization with property, media and charities in dozens of countries, and the contribution rates have not been reduced.
   - *Benefits now:* The central administration and its schemes benefit. Where charities are registered (UK), disclosure exists [13]; elsewhere it depends on the jurisdiction.
2. **Obedience to the Nizam (the system)**
   - *Origin:* Unity under the Khalifa became the defining lesson of the 1914 split [20]; obedience to the Nizam is taught as a duty [18].
   - *Then:* Obedience protected the community against schism and against state persecution that sought to break it apart.
   - *Expired?* The external threat is real, but it is now also the reason internal dissent is unspeakable.
   - *Benefits now:* The office-bearer structure benefits, and for it loyalty, not audit, is the measure.
3. **Marriage only within the community (for women)**
   - *Origin:* The rule comes from rulings under the Khalifas, and permission remains at the Khalifa's discretion [11]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
   - *Then:* The rule served community survival.
   - *Expired?* The asymmetry (men may, with permission; women may not) is not a survival rule. It is a control rule.
   - *Benefits now:* Family and community authority over adult women's choices benefits.
4. **Expulsion and social boycott**
   - *Origin:* It began as administrative discipline for "un-Islamic" conduct or refusal to accept the community's arbitration (Qaza) decisions [12]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
   - *Then:* It enforced unity without any power of the state.
   - *Expired?* Where, as former members report, families are expected to stop speaking to a member who left, the sanction reaches past the institution into the kitchen.
   - *Benefits now:* The system benefits from its ability to make exit socially unaffordable.

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- Friday sermons by the Khalifa are broadcast worldwide on the community's own channel (MTA, since 1994) and form the shared weekly instruction [16]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Members are taught that criticism of the Khalifa or the system is spiritually dangerous. Former members report pressure against reading ex-member or opposing material. [FORMER MEMBER TESTIMONY]
- The external information environment in Pakistan is itself hostile: Ordinance XX criminalizes Ahmadi religious expression [3]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### Children

- Auxiliary membership begins at seven (Atfal for boys, Nasirat for girls), with attendance, study and contribution expectations. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Waqf-e-Nau children are dedicated by their parents before birth, follow a structured syllabus by age, and are asked to renew the pledge themselves at fifteen [15][33]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- In Pakistan, community elders report discrimination against Ahmadi students in educational institutions, as the UK Home Office records [31]. The harm to children recorded on this page is overwhelmingly external. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]

### Bodies

- Women have a separate organization (Lajna) with its own elected officers, but the apex, the national amirs and the missionary corps are male. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- An Ahmadi woman may not marry a non-Ahmadi man; men may marry outside the community with permission, at the Khalifa's discretion [11]. The rule is asymmetric. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Purdah (the practice of veiling and of separating women from unrelated men) and dress norms are enforced through the auxiliary structure rather than the state. [OFFICIAL POLICY / FORMER MEMBER TESTIMONY]

The techniques are set out in the table in section 12.
:::

::: card
#### 1 · Money

Chanda funds the missionaries and media who teach that chanda is the measure of faith.

**How it runs.**

1. Every earning member pays Chanda Aam, one-sixteenth of income after tax, and Wasiyyat members pay more; further schemes sit on top (section 9).
2. Contributions are recorded per member, and the local finance secretary sees who is in arrears (sections 9 and 18).
3. The money goes to the central administration and its missions (section 9, Follow the money), which direct the salaried missionaries and MTA (sections 7 and 16).
4. The Khalifa's Friday sermon, broadcast on MTA, is the shared weekly instruction, and sacrifice is taught as the measure of faith (section 11; section 12, stage Devalue).
5. Members hear that teaching and pay again, and each level of sacrifice exposes the next: Chanda Aam leads to Wasiyyat, which leads to new schemes (technique 9).

**Techniques that feed it.** [26 · Financial control](#techniques), [9 · Moving the goalposts](#techniques), [10 · Strategic ambiguity](#techniques), [5 · Devaluation](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The office that sets the rates also directs the missionaries and the broadcast that teach members to pay them, and outside the UK a member cannot read where the money goes (section 9). The payment funds the teaching, and the teaching renews the payment.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens wherever the money becomes visible. The UK association already files public accounts because charity law requires them (section 9), and section 20 names published, audited worldwide accounts as a change that would revise this page. A member can ask for the accounts of their own country before paying, as section 23 asks. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Nusrat sees a chanda figure estimated above what she now earns, drafts an email to correct it, deletes it, and decides to pay the higher figure.
:::

::: card
#### 2 · Fear

Fear of losing God's favor and the Khalifa's prayers is relieved by renewed sacrifice, and children meet the same expectations from age seven.

**How it runs.**

1. Members are promised nearness to God through obedience, and the Khalifa's prayers (technique 3; section 12, stage Hook).
2. Weak sacrifice, missed duties and an unrenewed pledge are named as spiritual failure (technique 5).
3. Criticism of the Khalifa or the system is taught as spiritually dangerous, and doubts are reframed as weak faith (section 11, Information; technique 6).
4. Renewed sacrifice is praised in public, and the lapsed meet silence (technique 8).
5. Children join the auxiliaries at seven and meet the same attendance, study and contribution expectations, so the fear passes to the next generation (section 11, Children; technique 13).

**Techniques that feed it.** [3 · Future faking](#techniques), [5 · Devaluation](#techniques), [6 · Gaslighting](#techniques), [8 · Intermittent reinforcement](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The relief on offer, renewed sacrifice, is defined by the same system that names the failure, and the standard only ever rises (technique 5). Doubt about the arrangement is itself treated as the danger, so the fear cannot be examined from inside it.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a member can name the level at which they have given enough, which is the counter to technique 9, and where parents present the auxiliary expectations to a child as a choice. That lies with members and parents. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Nusrat does not correct her chanda figure "because correcting it feels like asking for less", and that evening she watches the Friday sermon on obedience.
:::

::: card
#### 3 · Children

Waqf-e-Nau and auxiliary membership from age seven produce the next cohort of office-bearers.

**How it runs.**

1. Parents may dedicate a child to lifelong service under Waqf-e-Nau, and the dedication must be made before the child is born (section 9).
2. From age seven, children join Atfal or Nasirat, with attendance, study and contribution expectations (section 11).
3. Waqf-e-Nau children follow a structured syllabus by age and are later asked to renew the pledge themselves (section 11; technique 19).
4. The scheme supplies the missionary and administrative workforce (section 9, Follow the money), and Jamia Ahmadiyya trains the missionary corps (section 7).
5. Those missionaries and office-bearers teach the next generation, and the next parents pledge their own children (section 16).

**Techniques that feed it.** [19 · Manufactured consent](#techniques), [13 · Normalization](#techniques), [24 · Identity erosion](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The pledge is made before the child can consent, and by the time the child is asked to reaffirm it, a lifetime of expectation and the family's standing stand behind the answer (section 17). Each cohort staffs the system that enrolls the next.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a Waqf-e-Nau child who declines at maturity keeps the family's standing; section 23 asks what happens to such a child and their family. Parents decide whether to make the pledge, and section 24 records that the pledge does not bind the child in law. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 17 lists Waqf-e-Nau children among those who carry the most weight: a vocation assigned before birth, compounded by "parental standing tied to their choices".
:::

::: card
#### 4 · Aid

Humanitarian work (Humanity First) earns genuine public standing, which can make the internal rules harder to question.

**How it runs.**

1. The community runs humanitarian work through Humanity First, open to all, and renounces violent jihad (section 4).
2. Its motto, "Love for All, Hatred for None", describes its public conduct under persecution (sections 4 and 14).
3. That conduct earns genuine public standing (section 4).
4. Critics of the internal rules are cast as disloyal, or as conspiracies against the Jamaat (techniques 11 and 17).
5. The standing makes the internal rules on contribution, marriage and expulsion harder to raise, and the public work continues (section 14).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized generosity](#techniques), [11 · Projection](#techniques), [17 · Smear campaign](#techniques), [30 · Plausible deniability](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The public work is real, and so is the standing it earns; the same standing is what an internal critic has to argue against. Each year of good works makes the internal rules look less like a fair subject of complaint.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where the standing and the rules are judged separately. Section 14 records that the motto is true of the community's public conduct "and harder to square with boycotting its own former members". Partners and officials who work with the community can ask about the published rules. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 14 sets "Love for all, hatred for none" beside the boycott of former members that they report, and grades the gap as a pattern observed.
:::

::: card
#### 5 · Labor

Volunteer duty is organized and recorded from age seven, and property and funds are administered centrally; this page records no figure for the property built.

**How it runs.**

1. Duty rosters are organized and recorded, and they are part of ordinary life from age seven (sections 7 and 11; technique 13).
2. The whole system is staffed by volunteer office-bearers (section 15, How the cost is denied).
3. Property, missions and funds are administered by the Sadr Anjuman Ahmadiyya and the central schemes, under officers the Khalifa appoints (section 7).
4. Service is framed as joy, and the page notes that service which cannot be declined is not service (technique 13).
5. A volunteer who steps away is replaced, and the system continues untouched (technique 29; section 12, stage Replace).

**Techniques that feed it.** [13 · Normalization](#techniques), [29 · Replacement](#techniques), [26 · Financial control](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The people who give the labor hold no office that controls what it builds, and the property is held centrally (section 7). A volunteer who stops is replaced, so the work does not depend on any one person's consent.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where volunteer duty can be declined without cost to a member's standing, which is the counter to technique 13. Section 18 names the local office-bearers who could refuse to make standing depend on service or payment. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Nusrat volunteered in March for the Jalsa registration desk and "has already been reminded twice".
:::

::: card
#### 6 · Scandal

No adjudicated internal scandal was found. A report of a 2022 Charity Commission inquiry after abuse allegations appears only on a former-member blog. It is not relied on here, because the Commission's own record of it has not been found. The steps below are built from the complaint routes this page records; none of them is a documented case.

**How it runs.**

1. Complaints about office-bearers are routed back into the same hierarchy (technique 18).
2. A member who complains against an office-bearer can become the problem for "creating disorder" (technique 12).
3. Every route of complaint ends at an appointee of the Khalifa (technique 21; section 7).
4. Internal discipline proceedings are not published beyond the sanction (section 15, How the cost is denied).
5. No internal case reaches the public record, and section 14 finds no documented internal case of accountability (sections 14 and 19).

**Techniques that feed it.** [18 · Stonewalling](#techniques), [12 · DARVO](#techniques), [21 · Learned helplessness](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The body that hears a complaint is staffed by the colleagues of the person complained about, and its proceedings are not published, so neither the complaint nor its outcome becomes visible. A loop of this kind would leave no record of itself, and this page records no case.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** The UK safeguarding policy already refers criminal concerns to police and statutory bodies (section 20), and charity regulators can compel answers from community charities (section 8). Section 20 names a written procedure for appeal against expulsion as a change that would revise this page. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 19 closes its list of cases with "Internal machinery: No case was found in the public record."
:::

::: card
#### 7 · Persecution

Real persecution converts internal criticism into betrayal. This is the loop that protects the other six.

**How it runs.**

1. The persecution is real and written into law: Pakistan's 1974 amendment, Ordinance XX in 1984, and the 2010 Lahore attacks that killed 94 (sections 5 and 8).
2. Shared persecution grief binds members to the institution itself (technique 20).
3. A member who questions the system is told they are weakening a persecuted community in front of its enemies (section 12, stage Confuse; technique 27).
4. Community writing frames organized critics as conspiracies against the Jamaat, and family, office-bearers and the Khalifa's sermons align against a doubter (techniques 17 and 15).
5. The internal rules stay unexamined, and each new attack renews the argument (section 16, Money out, leverage back).

**Techniques that feed it.** [27 · Manufactured crisis](#techniques), [20 · Trauma bonding](#techniques), [17 · Smear campaign](#techniques), [15 · Triangulation](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The persecution does not stop, so the argument against internal criticism never expires. Each new attack strengthens the solidarity that protects the internal rules from question.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** Section 3 answers the argument directly: persecution from outside does not make an internal rule beyond question. The loop weakens when members hold their own institution to the freedom of belief the community asks of states (section 23, In closing). That lies with members. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 16 records that persecution "is real [3][5][6] and is also the most effective argument against internal criticism: to question the system is to side with the people attacking the mosques."
:::

## 15. Cost & cover {#cost}

### What leaving costs

- Formal expulsion from the administrative system cuts a member off from the Jamaat; the community's own explanation describes it as organizational removal, after which the person "will not have any connection with the Jama'at" [12]. [OFFICIAL POLICY] Former members describe expected social distancing by other members; this rests on their testimony alone, and no community text on members' social contact with the expelled has been found. [FORMER MEMBER TESTIMONY]
- Leaving can cost marriage and family standing: an Ahmadi woman may not marry a non-Ahmadi man, while men may marry outside the community with the Khalifa's permission [11]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- For those leaving *toward* mainstream Islam the social cost is internal. For Ahmadis who stay, the legal cost in Pakistan is imposed by the state. The two costs must not be confused.

### The ledger of exit

| Cost | Documented? | Detail | The official denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expulsion | Yes, as the community's own sanction [12] | The person is removed from the system, and former members report social distancing | "Discipline is administrative; the person remains an Ahmadi." [12] |
| Marriage | Yes [11] | Women may not marry out | "Marriage within the faith protects the family." |
| Housing (Rabwah) | Not found | No independent source was found for housing loss on leaving. | — |
| Legal jeopardy | Yes — imposed by Pakistan, not the community [3][14] | Ordinance XX, the passport declaration and a separate electoral list impose it | (external: "Ahmadis are free to be non-Muslims.") |

### How the cost is denied

| Channel | Level | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit policy | Low | Contribution rates [1], marriage rules [11] and grounds for expulsion [12] are published. |
| Informal enforcement | Medium | Former members report that the expelled are shunned by members. |
| Leadership distance | Low | One named office sits at the top and speaks weekly. |
| Doctrinal ambiguity | Low | Rules are codified, not contested. |
| Cultural outsourcing | Medium | Purdah and marriage pressure are attributed to South Asian family culture. |
| Volunteer enforcement | High | The whole system is staffed by volunteer office-bearers. |
| Sacred secrecy | Medium | Internal discipline proceedings are not published beyond the sanction. |
| Exit cost denial | Medium | "No compulsion" is sincerely taught, and former members report that a boycott is still expected. |

## 16. The ledger {#ledger}

### Who benefits

- The central administration and the office of the Khalifa benefit; they direct the worldwide contribution system, property and media (MTA). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Salaried missionaries and office-bearers benefit, because their standing rests on the system.
- In Pakistan, *politicians and clerical movements* benefit from anti-Ahmadi law: it is a reliable mobilizing issue, as the 1954 Punjab inquiry found [9]. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]

### Money out, leverage back

- Persecution is real [3][5][6] and is also the most effective argument against internal criticism: to question the system is to side with the people attacking the mosques. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- The Wasiyyat scheme links a member's estate and final resting place to lifelong good standing [2]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Waqf-e-Nau creates a generation whose path was assigned before birth, though they are asked to reaffirm it at fifteen [15][33]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### Who pays

- Ahmadis in Pakistan pay: they are criminally liable for calling themselves Muslim, calling their building a mosque, or using Islamic greetings [3][5], and they are excluded from the general voter list unless they renounce their faith [14]. [OFFICIAL POLICY] [COURT RECORD]
- Women pay, under the asymmetric marriage rule and a male apex.
- Waqf-e-Nau children pay, because their vocation was pledged before birth.
- Former members pay, under expulsion and, by their own accounts, social boycott.
- Lahore-movement Ahmadis pay, and so do those who question the caliphate from inside.

## 17. Who gets hurt most {#who-gets-hurt}

### Where the weight lands

The costs in section 15 do not fall evenly. Two kinds of harm are recorded on this page: harm done to the community by states, and harm done by its own rules to the people inside it. The weight falls hardest where a person has the least power inside the institution and the fewest places to go outside it. The table names who carries the most, how, and what makes it worse.

| Who | How | Compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Ahmadis in Pakistan | They face criminal law [3], the passport declaration and a separate electoral list [14], and violence [6]. | The absence of state protection compounds it. |
| Women | They bear the marriage restriction and a male apex. | Family honor compounds it. |
| Waqf-e-Nau children | Their vocation is assigned before birth. | It is compounded by parental standing tied to their choices. |
| Former members | They face expulsion and, by their own accounts, boycott. | Tight diaspora communities compound it. |

## 21. Voices from inside {#voices}

- **Abdus Salam.** An Ahmadi physicist, he shared the 1979 Nobel Prize, Pakistan's first. The word "Muslim" was erased from "the first Muslim Nobel Laureate" on his gravestone at Rabwah under Ordinance XX [25].
- **The Lahore movement.** It rejected the caliphate itself in 1914, and still does [20].

## 22. Regional variants {#regional}

::: card
### Pakistan
- **apex:** The community has no apex of its own there; the state defines the community's legal identity [4].
- **law:** The 1974 amendment, Ordinance XX (1984) and blasphemy laws govern Ahmadis [3][4][5].
- **documented:** The record includes the 2010 Lahore mosque attacks, in which 94 were killed [6], the erased gravestone [25], the exclusion from voting [14] and two deaths in May 2025 [34].
- **exit:** Leaving the community is legally easy; staying is criminal in its practice.
- **regulator:** The courts regulate, and they upheld the ordinance [5].
- **tell:** It is the clearest case in this book of a state deciding a religion's identity, and then prosecuting its members for disagreeing.
:::

::: card
### United Kingdom
- **apex:** The Khalifa has his headquarters here, and a National President (Amir) leads the UK community [10][22].
- **law:** Charity law requires public accounts and trustees [13][24].
- **documented:** The record includes a published safeguarding policy that refers criminal concerns to police [21].
- **exit:** Leaving is legally free; the costs of leaving are social and family.
- **regulator:** The Charity Commission regulates the community's charities [13].
- **tell:** Persecution moved the community's center into a jurisdiction that requires it to publish accounts — so the most transparent Ahmadiyya institution is the one in exile.
:::

### Indonesia
- **apex:** Jemaat Ahmadiyah Indonesia leads the community, under the Khalifa.
- **law:** The 2008 joint ministerial decree restricts the community [7].
- **documented:** The record includes the 2011 Cikeusik killings, in which three Ahmadis died and the attackers were sentenced to three to six months [8].
- **exit:** —
- **regulator:** No effective regulator exists; the decree is the state's position [7].
- **tell:** A decree told a minority to be quiet, a mob then killed three of them, and the attackers received sentences of months.

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. You give a sixteenth of your income. In your country, can you read the audited accounts that show where it went? If not, why is that available in the UK but not to you?
2. What is the written procedure by which a Khalifa could be removed? If there is none, who corrects the office when it is wrong?
3. Why may an Ahmadi man marry outside the faith with permission while an Ahmadi woman may not?
4. When someone is expelled, who decided, on what evidence, and could they reply?
5. Your community has suffered some of the worst religious persecution of the modern era, and has answered it with dignity. Does that persecution make it wrong to ask the questions above?
6. If a child pledged to Waqf-e-Nau chooses another life at eighteen, what happens to them and their family's standing?

### In closing

1. No community in this book has more right to talk about the cost of the state deciding who counts as a Muslim. Yours has written that experience into law reports, inquiry records and gravestones. That is exactly why the same standard, that belief cannot be compelled, applies inside as well as out.
2. You can love the Khalifa and ask for the accounts. You can keep every Friday sermon and still refuse a boycott of your own brother. That is not weakness of faith. It is the motto on your own mosques, applied to the people who leave.

## 24. Leaving safely here {#leaving}

Practical guidance, not legal advice.

1. **Expect social, not legal, costs outside Pakistan.** Expulsion is published [12]; former members report that members may be expected to stop associating with you.
2. **Women face a specific cost:** marriage outside the community is restricted for women, at the Khalifa's discretion [11].
3. **Waqf-e-Nau pledges were made for you before birth** [15]; you are not legally bound by them.
4. **In Pakistan,** your legal status as an Ahmadi does not change with leaving, and the risk you face comes from the law and the crowd, not from the community [3].
5. **Find support** from organizations that know high-control communities, listed in section 25 [26][27].
