## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

- **name:** Mariam
- **when:** A Tuesday in the Fast of the Apostles
- **where:** Cairo, Shubra

1. She wakes before six and does not eat, as she has not eaten before the Liturgy since she was a girl. The fast is a kind of quiet she has always loved.
2. On the way to work she passes the church where her aunt was married, and remembers the police at the door after Palm Sunday. The guards are still there. Nobody remarks on them any more.
3. At lunch her friend Nevine says the church council that rules on marriages will not hear her case this year. Nevine's husband left for the Gulf eight years ago. She is not divorced, cannot remarry, and cannot become anything else without losing her family and her name.
4. In the afternoon Mariam's mother calls about the priest's visit on Friday and whether the flat is ready.
5. In the evening she goes to vespers (evening prayer). The hymns are in Coptic, which she half understands, and it doesn't matter. She stands where the women stand and feels, as always, that this is the oldest thing she will ever be part of.
6. Walking home she thinks about Nevine and the council that will not hear her, and realizes she cannot imagine anyone she could ask to change it — not a priest, not a judge, not a president. She says a prayer for Nevine, because that is the only door she can see.

*Mariam is a composite drawn from documented patterns. No real person is depicted.*

## 3. The forefront {#forefront}

Nothing here makes light of that. This page is about what happens when a church survives by bargaining with states, and about who pays the price of the bargain inside it.
:::

### The strongest objection, answered

- *Attack:* "You are criticizing persecuted Christians."
- *Concede:* These churches are among the most persecuted institutions in this book. The bombings are recorded first [9][10].
- *Answer:* Persecution does not make a divorce rule just [4] or a state-managed patriarchate legitimate [2]. The martyrs' standard, refusing rulers, applies to the patriarchate as well.

## 4. What healthy looks like here {#healthy}

::: lede
Oriental Orthodoxy has about 60–70 million faithful [1] in six autocephalous (self-governing) churches. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church is by far the largest; estimates range from about 36 to 50+ million [1]. The others are the Coptic Orthodox Church, the Armenian Apostolic Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church (about 2 million) [2], the Syriac Orthodox Church and the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church of India.

They are among the oldest Christian churches, known for ancient liturgies (Coptic, Ge'ez, Classical Armenian, Syriac), long fasting calendars, monasticism, and survival as minorities.
:::

- These churches have kept an extraordinary continuity of liturgy, language and learning under centuries of pressure.
- Their monasteries historically offered refuge and education.
- The Coptic Church runs welfare and schools where the state provides little.
- The Armenian Church gives the laity real power: two-thirds of the assembly that elects the Catholicos (the church's supreme patriarch) are lay delegates [15].
- Twenty-five Armenian bishops publicly backed their Catholicos against state pressure [13]. Solidarity against the state is also a check.

Some of that standard is already on the record. The Coptic Orthodox Church's UK body files public accounts with the charity regulator, discloses its highest earners in pay bands and declares a safeguarding policy [24]. Abune Antonios, Eritrea's patriarch, refused to excommunicate members of a Sunday-school renewal movement and called for the release of prisoners, and he was removed for it [2]. Each is a place where the institution met the standard this page applies, and each is recorded here as credit.

## 5. History {#history}

These churches separated from Rome and Constantinople after the Council of Chalcedon (451), over the formulation of Christ's nature; the Oriental churches hold a "miaphysite" Christology (the teaching that Christ has one nature, at once divine and human). Each developed under a different state:
- The Coptic Church developed under Byzantine and then Islamic rule.
- The Ethiopian Church was a Christian empire's state church until the 1974 revolution [5].
- The Armenian Church developed in the first state to adopt Christianity (traditionally 301).
- The Syriac Church developed across the Middle East.
- The Malankara Church developed in Kerala, with ancient roots and later Syriac ties.

## 6. Branches & variants {#branches}

The six autocephalous churches are in communion with one another. There are also internal splits: between the Malankara Orthodox and the Jacobites (the Malankara faction loyal to the Syriac Orthodox patriarch) [3]; regional crises in Ethiopia [7]; and the Tigrayan church's formation of its own administration in May 2021 after the Tigray war [17].

## 7. Structure {#structure}

### Size and shape

- **adherents:** Oriental Orthodoxy has about 60–70M adherents (72M in a 2020 estimate cited by reference works) [1].
- **regions:** The churches are concentrated in Ethiopia, Egypt, Armenia, Eritrea, Syria, Iraq, Lebanon and India (Kerala), and they have large diasporas.
- **branches:** The six autocephalous churches are in communion, and there are internal splits (between the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite factions [3]; Ethiopian regional crises [7]; the Tigrayan church's formation of its own administration in May 2021 after the Tigray war [17]).
- **trend:** The churches are growing in Ethiopia; in the Middle East they are declining, and members are emigrating.
- **participation:** Ritual participation (fasting, feasts) is very high in Ethiopia and Egypt, where church identity is ethnic identity.

### Authority

- Each church has its own patriarch or catholicos and holy synod.
- Selection differs by church:
  - The Coptic Pope is chosen by lot: a blindfolded boy draws one of three shortlisted names at the altar (Pope Tawadros II, 4 Nov 2012) [6].
  - The Armenian Catholicos is elected for life by a National Ecclesiastical Assembly: about two-thirds lay delegates and one-third clergy, elected by dioceses worldwide (Karekin II was elected in 1999 by about 450 delegates) [15].
- States reach into leadership:
  - Eritrea's government backed the removal of its Patriarch (2006), who died under house arrest in 2022 [2]
  - Ethiopia's prime minister brokered the 2023 settlement of a synod schism [7]
  - Armenia's government has brought criminal cases against senior clergy and against the Catholicos himself (2025–26) [12][13]

### Who can compel an answer

Egyptian courts can compel an answer on personal status, and they confirmed the church's jurisdiction [4]. Indian courts can on property [3], and Armenian criminal courts have brought cases against clergy [12][13]. In Eritrea no domestic body can. International bodies such as USCIRF (the US Commission on International Religious Freedom) and the UN report without power to compel [2].

## 9. Money {#money}

### Where it comes from

- The money comes from church property, monasteries, land and diaspora parish giving.
- In Ethiopia the church lost all its land when the Derg nationalized rural land in March 1975. Estimates of prior church holdings range from 10 to 20% of cultivated land [5].
- The 2023 Ethiopian settlement allocated funding and resources to churches in Oromia [7], so money is part of how schisms are settled.
- In India, the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite factions litigated for decades. In 2017 the Supreme Court gave the Orthodox faction the right to administer about 1,100 churches [3].
- Financial disclosure is generally minimal and jurisdiction-dependent. Diaspora parishes registered as charities (UK, US) file accounts.

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

1. **Church control of marriage and divorce (Egypt)**
   - *Origin:* Personal-status systems delegated family law to each religious community. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* The system gave each community autonomy, and protection from majority family law.
   - *Expired?* With no civil-marriage alternative, the state's highest court confirmed that the church alone decides [4]. Autonomy becomes control over members with no exit.
   - *Benefits now:* The clerical hierarchy benefits.
2. **The patriarch as the state's interlocutor**
   - *Origin:* The community head answers to the ruler for the community. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* The arrangement secured the community's survival.
   - *Expired?* It lets states manage millions through one office. Eritrea removed its patriarch for refusing to excommunicate a youth movement and for demanding prisoners' release [2]. Ethiopia's prime minister settled a schism [7]. Armenia's government prosecutes its Catholicos [13].
   - *Benefits now:* States benefit, and so does whichever faction the state favors.
3. **Purity rules**
   - *Origin:* The rules come from Levitical purity traditions preserved in early canons (church laws) [14].
   - *Then:* They kept ritual order.
   - *Expired?* Applied to women's bodies, they restrict half the church's access to its sacraments.
   - *Benefits now:* No one identifiable benefits. The custom is enforced by clergy.

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- Clerical authority over teaching is strong; lay theological critique is rare. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Under state control (Eritrea [2]; historically the Derg [5]), church communication is itself controlled.

### Children

- Long fasting seasons are practiced from childhood.
- Church schools and Sunday schools are primary socialization. The Eritrean patriarch's refusal to excommunicate members of a Sunday-school renewal movement was among the charges used to remove him [2].

### Bodies

- The priesthood and the episcopate (the order of bishops) are all male, and women are barred from the altar area. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- In Ethiopian Orthodox practice, menstruating women do not enter the church for seven days, and after childbirth women stay away for 40 days (boy) or 80 days (girl) [14].
- In Egypt, church-controlled divorce falls hardest on spouses trapped in failed or abusive marriages [4].

## 13. The loops {#loops}

::: lede
The seven loops below show how the practices connect, so that each step makes the next one easier and the last step feeds the first. The loops are analysis built from findings recorded elsewhere on this page [PATTERN OBSERVED]; each step names the section or technique where its fact is recorded. The techniques are set out in the table in section 12.
:::

::: card
#### 1 · Money

Sacramental access funds the clergy who administer it.

**How it runs.**

1. Salvation is taught as coming through sacraments that only this church administers (section 12, stage Hook; technique 3).
2. Local clergy mediate access to the rites, and sacramental and ritual fees are paid for services (section 9, Follow the money).
3. The fees go to parishes and clergy, and diaspora giving is remitted to the patriarchates with little reporting outside countries with charity law (section 9).
4. Most of these churches publish no accounts; the Coptic Orthodox Church's UK body, which files with the charity regulator, is the exception the scorecard rates (sections 1 and 9).
5. The clergy sustained this way administer the same sacraments on the same terms, and the fees and giving continue (technique 26).

**Techniques that feed it.** [3 · Future faking](#techniques), [26 · Financial control](#techniques), [8 · Intermittent reinforcement](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The office that controls access to the sacraments also receives the fees and the giving that sustain it, and outside countries with charity law no one publishes what is received or sent. A member cannot reach the rites without the clergy, and cannot see the accounts without their permission.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens wherever accounts are published. Diaspora bodies registered as charities in the UK file accounts and disclose pay bands (sections 1 and 9), and section 12 states the remedy for technique 26: publish what is sent and settled. A donor can ask a diaspora parish for its accounts before giving. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In 2024 the Coptic Orthodox Church Centre in the UK took £446,130 of its £523,050 income from donations and legacies (section 9). Most churches in this family publish no comparable figure.
:::

::: card
#### 2 · Fear

Salvation is taught to come through the church's sacraments, and excommunication is treated as spiritual death.

**How it runs.**

1. Salvation is promised through the church's sacraments, and access to them is controlled by the office (technique 3).
2. Failing to fast, and ritual impurity, are treated as unworthiness, so a member always has something to answer for (technique 5; section 11).
3. The office holds the final sanction: excommunication for members, and for priests who obey a court, the threat of defrocking (technique 28; section 15).
4. Leaving costs both church and people, because church, ethnicity and self are fused (techniques 14 and 24).
5. With no other route to the sacraments and no identity outside the church, the member stays, and the sanction keeps its force (section 15).

**Techniques that feed it.** [3 · Future faking](#techniques), [5 · Devaluation](#techniques), [28 · Discard](#techniques), [24 · Identity erosion](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The office that defines unworthiness is the only one that can lift it, and the sanction it holds costs a member family and people as well as the sacraments. Fear of the sanction keeps members inside, where the sanction applies.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where church and ethnic belonging can be separated; in the diaspora, leaving is legally free (section 24). It also weakens where clergy stop treating fasting failure and impurity as unworthiness, which is in the hands of parish priests and bishops. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** When a court ordered the church in 2010 to license a divorced man's remarriage, the Pope refused and threatened to defrock priests who complied (section 15).
:::

::: card
#### 3 · Children

Baptism, fasting and Sunday school fuse faith and ethnic identity before choice.

**How it runs.**

1. Infant baptism confers a lifelong identity before the child can choose (technique 19).
2. Long fasting seasons are practiced from childhood, and purity restrictions begin in childhood (section 11; technique 13).
3. Church schools and Sunday schools are the primary socialization (section 11).
4. Church, ethnicity and self are fused, so leaving later means losing family and people (technique 24; section 15).
5. Those children grow up, baptize their own children and send them to the same Sunday schools.

**Techniques that feed it.** [19 · Manufactured consent](#techniques), [13 · Normalization](#techniques), [24 · Identity erosion](#techniques), [14 · Isolation](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** Each generation is enrolled before it can choose and formed before it can compare, and by the time a choice is possible, faith and ethnic identity have become one thing. The loop does not need anyone to decide to continue it; it continues unless someone decides to stop.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where children are taught the faith without being taught that leaving it means leaving their people. That choice sits with parents and with Sunday-school teachers; the Eritrean Sunday-school renewal movement shows that Sunday schools can also be a place of dissent (section 11). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Mariam, the composite in section 2, has not eaten before the Liturgy since she was a girl, and cannot imagine anyone she could ask to change the rule that traps her friend.
:::

::: card
#### 4 · Aid

Church welfare in weak states buys loyalty and state tolerance, and state settlements buy the church's quiet [7].

**How it runs.**

1. The Coptic Church runs welfare and schools where the state provides little (section 4; technique 2).
2. Help that is the only help creates debt, and loyalty to the church follows (technique 2).
3. The patriarch serves as the state's interlocutor for the community, which lets a state manage millions through one office (section 10).
4. When the church splits, the state settles it: the 2023 Ethiopian settlement, brokered by the prime minister, included funding and resources for churches in Oromia (sections 8 and 9).
5. The question this page could not answer follows from it: when the state removes a patriarch, who in the church says no? (section 3).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized generosity](#techniques), [22 · Benevolent control](#techniques), [26 · Financial control](#techniques), [30 · Plausible deniability](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The church's standing with its members rests on help the state does not give, and its standing with the state rests on managing those members. Each side supplies what the other needs to keep the arrangement.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where the state provides welfare itself, where synods settle their own disputes, and where settlements and their funding are published, as section 12 asks under technique 26. That lies with governments and with the synods. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In February 2023 the prime minister brokered the settlement of the Oromia synod schism, in which about 30 people had been killed, and the settlement allocated funding and resources to churches in Oromia (sections 5 and 9).
:::

::: card
#### 5 · Labor

Monastic and parish labor sustains the institution.

**How it runs.**

1. Monasteries historically offered refuge and education, and the Coptic Church runs welfare and schools where the state provides little (section 4).
2. The church takes labor as well as fees and loyalty (section 12, stage Extract).
3. Diaspora parish boards raise funds and remit them, and are asked to do so without published accounts (section 18).
4. Monastic superiors are asked to keep internal conflict inside, and monastic and synodal discipline is closed (sections 15 and 18).
5. The work goes on under patriarchs and holy synods, which govern each church (section 7).

**Techniques that feed it.** [26 · Financial control](#techniques), [14 · Isolation](#techniques), [24 · Identity erosion](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The work and the money move up to patriarchates and synods, and the discipline that governs the people doing the work comes back down from them. Only the Armenian Church gives lay delegates a majority in the body that chooses its head (section 4).

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where diaspora boards publish what they send; section 18 names this as the point where a board could refuse. The Armenian assembly, two-thirds lay, shows another model within this family (sections 4 and 7). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 18 records diaspora parish boards that raise and send funds, see the accounts, and are asked to remit without published accounts.
:::

::: card
#### 6 · Scandal

A monastic killing was prosecuted by the state [11]; otherwise, synodal discipline is closed.

**How it runs.**

1. In 2018 the abbot of St Macarius Monastery, Bishop Epiphanius, was murdered (sections 5 and 19).
2. The state, not the church, prosecuted: a former monk was executed in 2021, and an accomplice's sentence was reduced to life (section 19).
3. Monastic and synodal discipline is otherwise closed (section 15, sacred secrecy).
4. Monastic superiors are asked to keep internal conflict inside (section 18).
5. Complaints about clergy are framed as weak faith (technique 6), and what becomes public is what a state court handles.

**Techniques that feed it.** [6 · Gaslighting](#techniques), [27 · Manufactured crisis](#techniques), [30 · Plausible deniability](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** Discipline is exercised by the synod and published by no one, so the only record that reaches members is one a state produces. A complaint about clergy framed as weak faith never reaches that stage.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a synod publishes its disciplinary decisions, and where monastic superiors decline to keep conflict inside (section 18). The first lies with the patriarch and synod of each church. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** The murder of Bishop Epiphanius in 2018 is the one internal case on this page with a public record, and that record exists because it went to a criminal court (section 19).
:::

::: card
#### 7 · Persecution

This is the strongest loop on this page. Real martyrdom [9][10] makes internal criticism unspeakable.

**How it runs.**

1. Persecution is real: the 2011 Alexandria bombing killed 23, and the 2017 Palm Sunday bombings killed about 45 (section 5).
2. Shared memory of martyrdom binds members to the hierarchy (technique 20).
3. Criticism is framed as helping the persecutors of Christians, and critics are cast as agents of hostile outsiders (section 12, stage Confuse; technique 11).
4. Outside threats are cited to end internal debate (technique 27).
5. Internal questions go unasked, and the next attack renews the memory.

**Techniques that feed it.** [20 · Trauma bonding](#techniques), [11 · Projection](#techniques), [27 · Manufactured crisis](#techniques), [14 · Isolation](#techniques).

**Why it closes.** The more real the threat, the stronger the case for silence. Each attack strengthens the solidarity that protects the hierarchy from its own members' questions.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where members separate grief for the martyrs from deference to the office; section 12's counter to technique 20 puts it plainly. Section 3 answers the objection directly: persecution does not make a divorce rule just. The twenty-five Armenian bishops who backed their Catholicos show that solidarity can also check a state (section 4). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 3 sets out the objection this loop produces, "You are criticizing persecuted Christians", and answers it.
:::

## 15. Cost & cover {#cost}

### What leaving costs

- **Egypt: marriage and divorce go through the church.** The Coptic Church permits divorce essentially only for adultery and controls permission to remarry [4]:
  - 2010: the Supreme Administrative Court ordered the Pope to license a divorced man's remarriage.
  - The Pope refused and threatened to defrock priests who complied.
  - The Supreme Constitutional Court then held that marital matters belong to the church, because Egypt does not recognize civil marriage without a religious ceremony [4].
- **Eritrea:** Practicing outside state-registered faiths risks detention, and the Orthodox church itself is under state control [2].
- **Minority communities:** where church and ethnic identity coincide, leaving carries high social and family costs.

### The ledger of exit

| Cost | Documented? | Detail | The official denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divorce and remarriage (Egypt) | Yes [4] | Church permission is required, and adultery is essentially the only ground | "What God has joined together let no man separate." [4] |
| Unregistered practice (Eritrea) | Yes [2] | Unregistered practice risks detention | (state) "Registered faiths are free." |

## 16. The ledger {#ledger}

### Who benefits

- States benefit when they manage minority churches through their patriarchs, and patriarchates benefit when they trade loyalty for protection [2].
- Winning factions in property disputes benefit [3].
- Clerical hierarchies benefit from holding personal-status permissions [4].

### Money out, leverage back

- Persecution is real and continuous [9][10], and it makes internal critique feel like betrayal of martyrs.
- States gain leverage by recognizing, mediating between or prosecuting patriarchs [2][7][13].
- The church controls personal status in Egypt [4].

### Who pays

- Coptic spouses in failed marriages pay [4].
- Eritrean believers of every church pay [2].
- Congregations caught in property wars in Kerala pay [3].
- Ethiopian faithful caught between synod politics and ethnic conflict pay [7].
- Women pay, under purity rules [14] and an all-male clergy.
- Communities that others have murdered, bombed or displaced pay [9][10]. This is the page's first fact.

## 17. Who gets hurt most {#who-gets-hurt}

### Where the weight lands

The costs in section 15 do not fall evenly. They fall hardest where a person has the least power inside the institution and the fewest places to go outside it: a Coptic woman whose marriage has failed and who has no civil route out [4], or a believer in Eritrea, where practice outside the registered faiths risks detention [2]. The table names who carries the most, how, and what makes it worse.

| Who | How | Compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Women | They bear purity rules [14], divorce rules [4] and an all-male clergy. | State personal-status law compounds it. |
| Eritrean believers | The state controls their church [2]. | The risk of detention compounds it. |
| Minority congregations | They face persecution [9][10]. | Emigration compounds it. |

## 18. The middle tiers {#tiers}

Most of the institution's work is done below the patriarch and synod, by people who see decisions without making them. Parish priests, the clerical councils that rule on marriage, monastic superiors and diaspora parish boards each carry part of the load, and each stands at one point where the next decision could be declined. The table sets out what each tier does, what it sees, what it is asked to do and where it could refuse.

| Role | Does | Sees | Asked | Could refuse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parish priests | Administer the sacraments and give counsel | Failing and abusive marriages | To counsel reconciliation | To send a spouse back to violence |
| Clerical personal-status councils | Decide divorce and remarriage [4] | The cases | To apply narrow rules | To delay a case for years |
| Monastic superiors | Keep discipline | Internal conflict [11] | To keep it inside | To keep it inside |
| Diaspora parish boards | Raise and send funds | The accounts | To remit | To remit without published accounts |

## 21. Voices from inside {#voices}

- **Abune Antonios.** Eritrea's patriarch, he was removed in 2006 after refusing, among other things, to excommunicate members of a Sunday-school renewal movement, and he died under house arrest in 2022 [2].
- **The twenty-five Armenian bishops.** They publicly backed their Catholicos against the government in 2025–26 [13].

## 22. Regional variants {#regional}

::: card
### Egypt
- **apex:** The Coptic Pope — Tawadros II, chosen by altar lot in 2012 [6].
- **law:** The church controls personal status [4], and the 2016 church-construction law governs church building [8].
- **documented:** The record includes the 2011 Alexandria and 2017 Palm Sunday bombings [9][10], the 2018 monastery murder [11] and the remarriage cases [4].
- **exit:** Leaving the church can mean leaving the only lawful route to marriage and divorce available to you [4].
- **regulator:** The courts regulate, and they returned marriage to the church [4].
- **tell:** This is a minority church that needs the state's protection, in a state that leaves the church in charge of Christian marriage.
:::

::: card
### Ethiopia
- **apex:** The Patriarch and Holy Synod lead the church.
- **law:** The Derg's 1975 land nationalization took the church's land [5].
- **documented:** The record includes the 1979 execution of Patriarch Theophilos [5], the 2023 schism and settlement [7] and the Tigrayan church's separate administration [17].
- **exit:** Where church and ethnicity coincide, leaving carries family costs.
- **regulator:** In practice the federal government regulates the church [7].
- **tell:** The largest Oriental church has twice had its future decided in the prime minister's office.
:::

::: card
### Eritrea
- **apex:** The patriarch leads the church; since 2025 he has been Abune Basilios [18].
- **law:** The state registers the faiths permitted to practice [2].
- **documented:** The record documents Abune Antonios's removal and house arrest [2].
- **exit:** Practicing outside the registered faiths risks detention [2].
- **regulator:** No independent regulator exists.
- **tell:** It is the plainest case in this book of a state choosing a church's head.
:::

::: card
### Armenia
- **apex:** The Catholicos of All Armenians leads the church; Karekin II was elected in 1999 [15].
- **law:** The state has brought criminal prosecutions against clergy in 2025–26 [12][13].
- **documented:** The record documents the 2026 trial of the Catholicos and six bishops [13].
- **exit:** Leaving is legally free.
- **regulator:** The courts regulate, under a government in open conflict with the church [12].
- **tell:** Here the pressure runs the other way: a national church with an elected, largely lay assembly, facing a state that wants its leader gone.
:::

::: card
### India
- **apex:** Two rival authorities lead the church in Kerala: the Malankara Orthodox Catholicos and the Jacobite faction under the Syriac patriarch [3].
- **law:** The 2017 Supreme Court judgment governs who administers the churches [3].
- **documented:** The record documents decades of litigation over about 1,100 churches [3].
- **exit:** Leaving is legally free.
- **regulator:** The courts and the state government regulate [3].
- **tell:** The judgment settled ownership on paper, and the state says it cannot enforce it.
:::

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. If a Coptic woman cannot end a failed marriage and remarry in her church, and her state offers no civil marriage [4], whose protection is the rule?
2. When a government removes a patriarch [2], what does "autocephaly" mean?
3. Who owns the church you pray in, and why did it take a Supreme Court to decide [3]?
4. If women may not enter church for weeks after giving birth to a daughter [14], who decided the body God made is unclean?
5. What does the diaspora parish send home, and who accounts for it?
6. The martyrs refused to bend to rulers. When a prime minister brokers your synod's peace [7] or prosecutes your Catholicos [13], who is bending?

### In closing

1. Your church outlasted empires by refusing to bend where it mattered. That is the precise standard for a church that bends to a state to keep its patriarch, or bends a woman's life to keep a canon.
2. You can keep every fast and still ask who decides whether your sister may remarry. The oldest churches have always argued. That is what the councils were.

## 24. Leaving safely here {#leaving}

Practical guidance, not legal advice.

1. **In Egypt,** marriage and divorce for Copts run through the church; take legal advice before you act on either [4].
2. **In Eritrea,** religious practice outside the registered faiths risks detention [2]; if you are abroad, take asylum advice before returning.
3. **In the diaspora,** leaving is legally free; the costs are family and community ones.
4. **If you were abused,** go to the police, not the priest or bishop.
5. **Find support** from the organizations in section 25 [20][21][22].
