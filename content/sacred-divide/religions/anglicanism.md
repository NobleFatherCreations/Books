## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

- **name:** Clare
- **when:** A Tuesday in Advent
- **where:** A rural benefice (a group of parishes served by one priest) of six parishes in the East of England

1. Morning Prayer is at seven, in the one church that still has heating. Two parishioners come, and the dog of one of them. She loves this more than any service in the diary, and would not be able to explain that to the diocese.
2. The parish share letter arrives: the six parishes owe more than they raised last year. The letter is kind and mentions "sustainable patterns of ministry", which everybody knows means merging with the next benefice.
3. At eleven she does safeguarding training on a laptop in the vicarage kitchen. The module references the Makin Review [3], the independent review of how the church handled John Smyth's abuse of boys and young men connected to church camps. She thinks about the boy from her own university years who went to those camps, and stops thinking about it.
4. She has lunch with a churchwarden (an elected lay officer of the parish) who wants the roof fixed and the parish share cut, and who asks, not unkindly, whether the vicarage could be sold.
5. At two she takes the funeral of a man who had not been in church since his wedding. The family are grateful and pay the statutory fee, and she means every word of the committal.
6. In the evening her husband asks what happens to them if the benefice merges. She says the diocese will find something. He asks where. She realizes she does not know whose decision the house is, and that it is not theirs.

*Clare is a composite drawn from documented patterns. No real person is depicted.*

## 3. The forefront {#forefront}

::: lede
You may love your parish church: the building, the words of Evensong (the evening service, usually sung), the people who turned up with casseroles when your mother died. Nothing here is about that. This page is about three things that live above your pew: a church fused with a state, a clergy whose roof depends on a bishop's signature, and a safeguarding record that the church's own commissioned reviewers called a failure. The church published those reports. That counts, and it is recorded here.
:::

### The unanswered question

::: question
Who, fully outside the church, now holds the power to compel it on safeguarding?
:::

### The strongest objection, answered

- *Attack:* "The church has apologized, commissioned reviews, and an archbishop resigned. What more do you want?"
- *Concede:* The church has done more than most institutions in this book, and that is recorded on this page as a strength.
- *Answer:* The answer is independent oversight. The 2024 Jay review (Professor Alexis Jay's independent review of how the church's safeguarding should be run) recommended it [8]; the church chose to introduce it in phases (2025) before approving a new independent charity (2026) [8].

## 4. What healthy looks like here {#healthy}

::: lede
About 85 million people in the Anglican Communion worldwide, in 42 member churches (provinces) [12]. The figure counts affiliates; active attendance is far lower (in England, 1.009 million regular worshippers in 2024 [10]). The Church of England is the mother church and the established church of England (the church recognized in law as the national church). It is known for a liturgy shared through the Book of Common Prayer, for bishops in apostolic succession (an unbroken line of ordination traced back to the apostles), for synodical government (government by elected councils of bishops, clergy and laity), and for holding catholic and protestant traditions in one body.
:::

- The church is governed by elected synods (church councils) that include a House of Laity, the elected lay members. This is some of the most democratic church governance in this book.
- Accounts are published at national and diocesan level. The Church Commissioners publish the accounts of the endowment [5], and in 2023 they published research tracing part of its history to the slave trade [6].
- The ordination of women as priests (1994) and bishops (2014) was achieved by synodical vote.
- An established church commissioned, published and acted on reports that condemned it [4][22], and its most senior archbishop resigned [3].
- There is no shunning (cutting off those who leave) and no compulsory giving.
- In July 2025 Synod approved a £150m independent redress scheme for abuse survivors [9].

## 5. History {#history}

The church was formed when Henry VIII broke with Rome (Act of Supremacy, 1534), made the English crown head of the church, and dissolved the monasteries (1536–41). Doctrine was settled under Elizabeth I (1559 settlement; Thirty-Nine Articles, 1571, the church's founding statement of doctrine). The church spread with the British Empire. The worldwide Communion took shape through the Lambeth Conferences (gatherings of Anglican bishops called by the Archbishop of Canterbury) from 1867. The member churches are autonomous: the Archbishop of Canterbury is a focus of unity, not a pope.

The church has evangelical, catholic (Anglo-Catholic, the wing closest to Catholic worship and teaching) and liberal wings. The GAFCON / Global South realignment since 2008 has seen some provinces break or impair communion (formally restrict shared worship and recognition) with Canterbury over sexuality.

## 7. Structure {#structure}

### Size and shape

- **adherents:** The Anglican Communion has ~85M members in 42 provinces [12]. The Church of England had 1.009 million regular worshippers and an average Sunday attendance of 581,000 (2024) [10].
- **regions:** The church is concentrated in England; in Nigeria, Uganda and Kenya, the largest provinces by active membership; and in Australia, Canada, the United States (the Episcopal Church), South Africa and South Asia.
- **branches:** The church has evangelical, catholic (Anglo-Catholic) and liberal wings. The GAFCON / Global South realignment since 2008 has seen some provinces break or impair communion with Canterbury over sexuality.
- **trend:** The church has declined over a long period in England and is growing in parts of Africa.
- **participation:** In England, affiliation is mostly nominal, with occasional use (baptisms, weddings, funerals, schools). The machinery bears on clergy and on children in church settings far more than on the average affiliate.

### Authority

- The monarch is Supreme Governor of the Church of England. Diocesan bishops are appointed by the Crown on the Prime Minister's advice, after nomination by the Crown Nominations Commission (a church committee of bishops, clergy and lay members that selects candidates). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- General Synod (houses of bishops, clergy and laity) passes Measures that become law once Parliament approves them and they receive Royal Assent. The laity have a genuine elected voice, and a real check. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Twenty-six bishops sit in the House of Lords as Lords Spiritual. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Parish clergy hold office under Common Tenure (the terms on which most clergy have held office since 2011). A bishop's licence governs their ministry, their stipend and usually their home. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### The top of the chain

The apex combines an elected legislature, a crown and a primate who resigned. It has been corrected from within, which is why its unfinished corrections are fair to name.

| Office | Who sits in it now | Chosen by | Removable by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supreme Governor | The reigning monarch holds the office. | The office passes by hereditary succession. | Parliament can remove the holder, by statute. |
| Archbishop of Canterbury | Sarah Mullally has held the office since 2026 [13]. | Crown Nominations Commission → PM → Crown | The holder can resign, as in 2024 [3]; no removal procedure has been identified. |
| General Synod | Members of its three elected houses sit in it. | Members are chosen in diocesan elections. | Elections are held every five years. |

## 9. Money {#money}

### Where it comes from

- The Church Commissioners manage a historic endowment of £11.1bn at the end of 2024, contributing around 20% of the Church of England's running costs; the accounts are published [5]. [FINANCIAL RECORD]
- Parishes pay a "parish share" (common fund) to their diocese, largely to fund clergy stipends and housing. Dioceses publish accounts. [FINANCIAL RECORD]
- Statutory fees for weddings and funerals are set by a Parochial Fees Order. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- In January 2023 the Commissioners published research (by Grant Thornton) finding that Queen Anne's Bounty, a forerunner of the endowment, had invested in the South Sea Company while it traded enslaved Africans, and announced a £100m fund [6]. In March 2024 an independent oversight group urged a £1bn target, which the Commissioners accepted [7]. Some historians dispute the research's framing [6]. [FINANCIAL RECORD] [OFFICIAL POLICY]

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

1. **Establishment**
   - *Origin:* It began with the Act of Supremacy (1534). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
   - *Then:* It served a crown asserting sovereignty against Rome, and national unity through one church.
   - *Expired?* Most English people are not Anglican. Establishment now buys seats in Parliament, a coronation and civic presence for a minority institution.
   - *Benefits now:* It benefits the bishops in the Lords and the church's civic standing.
2. **The tied house and the licence**
   - *Origin:* It comes from the parson's freehold (a parish priest's tenure of the post for life) and glebe (land held to support the priest), reformed into Common Tenure (2011). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
   - *Then:* It gave clergy independence. A priest who held the freehold was hard to remove.
   - *Expired?* Common Tenure kept the house but made the licence the lever.
   - *Benefits now:* It strengthens diocesan authority over clergy who cannot easily afford to dissent.
3. **Deference to rank**
   - *Origin:* It grew from hierarchical ecclesiology (the theology of church order) and from class. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* It served order and stability.
   - *Expired?* IICSA [1] and Makin [3] found it protected abusers and silenced victims. It is now a documented harm.
   - *Benefits now:* Senior figures have benefited, historically.

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- It is not a closed information system. Theological debate is open, reports are published and the press is free. That is to its credit.
- The documented information failure is institutional. IICSA [1] and Makin [3] found knowledge held and not acted on, and records not shared with the authorities. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]

### Children

- Church of England schools educate over a million children in about 4,600 schools [11]; faith-based admissions criteria operate in voluntary-aided schools (state-funded schools in which the church appoints most governors and sets admissions). [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Abuse in church settings (parishes, choirs, camps connected to church networks) and the church's handling of it were examined by IICSA [1] and by the Makin Review [3]. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- In Canada, the Anglican Church ran residential schools for Indigenous children; the church apologized in 1993 [18]. [GOVERNMENT REPORT: Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada]

### Bodies

- Women can be priests and bishops, but the "five guiding principles" (2014) let parishes request male-only oversight, so a woman's ministry can be formally declined by a parish. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Many Communion provinces do not ordain women. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### LGBTQ+

- Clergy may not enter a same-sex marriage under House of Bishops guidance. A priest who did was refused a licence; the Court of Appeal upheld the refusal under the Equality Act's occupational-requirement exception (*Pemberton v Inwood* [2018] EWCA Civ 564) [14]. [COURT RECORD]
- Prayers of Love and Faith (2023) allowed blessings of same-sex couples in some services, prompting a realignment threat from GAFCON provinces. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Some provinces have backed criminalizing laws: the Church of Uganda welcomed the Anti-Homosexuality Act 2023, while opposing its death penalty [15]; the Archbishop of Canterbury publicly criticized that support [15].

## 13. The loops {#loops}

::: lede
The seven loops below show how the practices connect, so that each step makes the next one easier and the last step feeds the first. Several are weak here, and each card says so. The loops are analysis built from findings recorded elsewhere on this page [PATTERN OBSERVED]; each step names the section or technique where its fact is recorded.
:::

::: card
#### 1 · Money

The money loop is weak here. Giving is voluntary, and the parish share is the pressure point.

**How it runs.**

1. Parishes pay a parish share to their diocese, largely to fund clergy stipends and housing (section 9).
2. The diocese sets the demand, and parishes that cannot pay face merger or closure (section 9, Follow the money).
3. Merger is described as "sustainable patterns of ministry" and carried out through "pastoral reorganization", which makes property decisions administrative (section 14, Words used here).
4. The clergy whose stipends and homes the share pays for hold both on a bishop's licence (sections 7 and 15).
5. The parishes that remain carry the next year's share, and the decline narrative is used to justify the next closure (technique 27).

**Techniques that feed it.** [26 · Financial control](#t-26), [27 · Manufactured crisis](#t-27), [5 · Devaluation](#t-5), [22 · Benevolent control](#t-22).

**Why it closes.** The diocese that sets the parish share also decides which parishes are merged when the share is not met, and the parish that pays does not set the demand. The loop stays weak because lay giving is voluntary and leaving costs a lay member nothing formal (section 15).

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a diocese publishes the accounts of need behind a share demand, which section 18 names as the point where churchwardens and parochial church councils (PCCs, the elected bodies that run a parish with its priest) could refuse. Dioceses already publish accounts (section 9), so the material exists. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Clare's six parishes owe more than they raised last year, and the parish share letter mentions "sustainable patterns of ministry", which everybody knows means merging with the next benefice.
:::

::: card
#### 2 · Fear

The fear loop is weak for most members. It is strong in some conservative evangelical settings, where hell and purity are taught.

**How it runs.**

1. Some evangelical outreach draws people in through intensive courses and camps (technique 1; section 12, Idealize).
2. Some traditions use the language of sin, and eternal life is the promise held out (techniques 3 and 5).
3. Historically, closed church-linked camps and choir schools left a child with few people to tell (technique 14; section 12, Isolate).
4. When abuse happened in those settings, complainants were treated as reputational threats and forgiveness language was urged on survivors (techniques 12 and 25).
5. The setting and its teaching continue, and the next group of young people arrives.

**Techniques that feed it.** [1 · Love bombing](#t-1), [5 · Devaluation](#t-5), [14 · Isolation](#t-14), [25 · Spiritual bypassing](#t-25).

**Why it closes.** Where it operates, the same setting teaches the fear and offers the only relief from it, and a child inside a closed camp has no one outside to tell. For most members it does not close, because there is no shunning doctrine and leaving costs nothing formal (section 15).

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where camps and youth settings answer to safeguarding that is independent of them, which is what the charity approved in February 2026 is meant to provide (section 5), and where a child has an adult outside the setting to tell. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, Clare thinks about the boy from her own university years who went to those camps. The Makin Review examined the church's handling of a barrister's abuse of boys and young men connected to church camps (section 19).
:::

::: card
#### 3 · Children

Church schools and choirs recruit families before children can consent.

**How it runs.**

1. Church of England schools educate over a million children in about 4,600 schools (section 11).
2. In voluntary-aided schools, faith-based admissions criteria can favor worshipping families, so a child's school place is tied to attendance (sections 11 and 15).
3. Families attend to secure places, and the church gains a pipeline of families and civic standing (section 9, Follow the money; technique 2).
4. Choirs and church-linked camps bring children into settings that IICSA and the Makin Review examined (section 11).
5. The schools sustain the church's standing, and the standing sustains the schools (section 16).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized generosity](#t-2), [14 · Isolation](#t-14).

**Why it closes.** The school place is the incentive to attend, and attendance is the qualification for the school place. Each group of families that attends to secure a place keeps the arrangement in place for the next.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where admissions criteria do not depend on worship; the church's own answer is that most places are open to all (section 15). Parents can read a school's admissions criteria before applying (section 24). Governors of voluntary-aided schools set those criteria. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** The ledger of exit in section 15 records worship-based admissions beside the official denial: "Most places are open to all."
:::

::: card
#### 4 · Aid

Civic standing, earned through schools, chaplaincies and food banks, sustains establishment privilege.

**How it runs.**

1. The church runs about 4,600 schools and holds a civic role through establishment, the coronation and civic ritual (section 16).
2. That standing sustains establishment: 26 bishops sit in the House of Lords (sections 7 and 8).
3. Establishment gives the church a legislative voice and civic role disproportionate to its active membership (section 16, Money out, leverage back).
4. Bishops in the Lords vote on national law, and Parliament approves the Measures that govern the church (section 7).
5. Establishment keeps the church at the centre of civic life, which renews the standing it rests on (section 10, Establishment).

**Techniques that feed it.** [2 · Weaponized generosity](#t-2), [13 · Normalization](#t-13).

**Why it closes.** Civic service justifies the privileged position, and the privileged position keeps the church at the centre of civic life. Most English people are not Anglican (section 10), and the arrangement renews itself without asking them.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where Parliament decides the question. Parliament has acted on the church's position before, abolishing the offence of blasphemy in 2008 (section 20). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Section 10 records that establishment now buys seats in Parliament, a coronation and civic presence for a minority institution.
:::

::: card
#### 5 · Labor

Volunteers maintain buildings the diocese holds.

**How it runs.**

1. Unpaid lay labor keeps church buildings open (section 12, Extract).
2. Churchwardens and PCCs run parishes and are asked to pay the parish share (section 18).
3. Decisions about the buildings are made through "pastoral reorganization", the legal process for merging benefices (section 14).
4. A parish that cannot pay faces merger or closure (section 9).
5. The volunteers who remain keep the remaining buildings open on the same terms.

**Techniques that feed it.** [26 · Financial control](#t-26), [27 · Manufactured crisis](#t-27), [29 · Replacement](#t-29).

**Why it closes.** The work flows one way, from volunteers to buildings, and decisions about those buildings are made elsewhere. Section 14 puts it plainly: "The building is being decided about elsewhere."

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where a parish is shown published accounts of need before a closure, the refusal section 18 names for churchwardens and PCCs. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** In section 2, a churchwarden wants the roof fixed and the parish share cut, and asks whether the vicarage could be sold.
:::

::: card
#### 6 · Scandal

An archbishop resigns and a review is published, and the structural question of independent oversight is deferred. The Independent Safeguarding Board was disbanded in 2023 [16], Model 3 (the external scrutiny model) was endorsed in 2025, and an independent charity was approved in 2026 [8].

**How it runs.**

1. Abuse is handled inside the church, and reviews later find deference and reputation-first handling (sections 5, 10 and 14).
2. A review is published: the Gibb review in 2017, the IICSA report in 2020 and the Makin Review in 2024 (section 5).
3. Accountability reaches the top once: the Archbishop of Canterbury resigned in November 2024 (sections 5 and 8).
4. Independence is promised and redesigned. The Independent Safeguarding Board was disbanded and its members dismissed in June 2023, and a new independent charity was approved only in February 2026 (sections 5 and 14; technique 9).
5. "Lessons learned" converts the findings into a future tense (section 14), and the next promise of independence follows.

**Techniques that feed it.** [9 · Moving the goalposts](#t-9), [18 · Stonewalling](#t-18), [13 · Normalization](#t-13), [12 · DARVO](#t-12), [30 · Plausible deniability](#t-30).

**Why it closes.** Each review ends in a promise of independence, and the design of that independence is decided by the institution the reviews criticized. Until the new body operates, the next case is handled by structures the church controls (section 7, National Safeguarding Team).

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** Section 20 names the change that would break it: statutory, fully independent safeguarding oversight. That lies with Synod and, because Measures need its approval, with Parliament (section 7). This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** Jasvinder Sanghera, a dismissed member of the Independent Safeguarding Board, told Synod: "We were too independent. We did our job too well." (section 21).
:::

::: card
#### 7 · Persecution

The persecution loop is largely absent in England. In the Communion, persecution of Anglicans elsewhere is real and is invoked in realignment disputes.

**How it runs.**

1. In England the church is established and has no shunning doctrine, so there is no persecuted-minority frame (sections 7 and 15).
2. Across the Communion, provinces disagree over sexuality; Prayers of Love and Faith (2023) prompted a realignment threat from GAFCON provinces (section 11).
3. Some provinces have backed criminalizing laws; the Church of Uganda welcomed the Anti-Homosexuality Act 2023 (sections 8 and 11).
4. Criticism from Canterbury is answered by realignment: in October 2025 GAFCON announced it would leave the Communion and form a rival network (sections 5 and 6).
5. The persecution of Anglicans elsewhere is invoked in these disputes; this step is recorded only in this loop's summary, with no source on this page.

**Techniques that feed it.** [11 · Projection](#t-11), [10 · Strategic ambiguity](#t-10).

**Why it closes.** Each province is self-governing and the Archbishop of Canterbury is a focus of unity, not a pope (section 6), so criticism from outside a province cannot compel it. A province that is criticized can leave rather than answer.

**Where it could be broken, and by whom.** It weakens where member churches treat criticism across provincial lines as part of their own record. Canterbury's public criticism of the Church of Uganda's support for the Act shows such criticism can be made (section 22); any change inside a province rests with that province's own synod. This paragraph is analysis, not a documented finding.

**An example from this page.** The Uganda card in section 22 records one Communion giving two answers to the same law: Kampala welcomed it, and Canterbury criticized the welcome [15].
:::

## 15. Cost & cover {#cost}

### What leaving costs

- For lay members, there is no formal exit cost. There is no shunning doctrine, no membership roll with sanctions, and giving is voluntary. That is a strength.
- For clergy, leaving or losing a licence can mean losing home, income and vocation at once, because clergy housing is tied to the post. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- For families in church schools, admissions criteria can favor worshipping families, creating an attendance incentive tied to a child's school place. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### The ledger of exit

| Cost | Documented? | Detail | The official denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lay membership | There is no formal cost. | Leaving is free. | The statement is accurate: leaving really is free for lay members. |
| Clergy livelihood | It is documented. | Licence, stipend and housing are linked. | "Clergy are office-holders, not employees." |
| Clergy in same-sex marriages | It is documented. | The licence is refused. | "The church's teaching on marriage is unchanged." |
| School place | It is documented. | Some admissions are based on worship. | "Most places are open to all." |

| Informal enforcement | Low | There is little community enforcement on laity. |
| Leadership distance | Medium | Diocesan autonomy disperses responsibility, as in "a matter for the diocese". |
| Doctrinal ambiguity | Medium | Breadth lets any position be "one tradition among several". |
| Cultural outsourcing | Low | — |
| Volunteer enforcement | Low | — |
| Sacred secrecy | High (historically) | Reviews found deference and reputation management protected abusers. |
| Exit cost denial | Low | Exit costs for laity are genuinely minimal. |

## 16. The ledger {#ledger}

### Who benefits

- The institution benefits from its establishment status: bishops in Parliament, coronation, civic ritual, and about 4,600 schools in England educating over a million children [11]. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Senior clergy and the diocesan apparatus benefit, historically protected by a culture of deference that two independent reviews named. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Endowment beneficiaries benefit, including from the historic investment returns now acknowledged as linked to slavery. [FINANCIAL RECORD]

### Money out, leverage back

- Establishment gives the church a legislative voice and civic role disproportionate to its active membership. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- The tied house makes clergy dissent expensive. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Church schools make worship a school-admissions asset in some areas. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### Who pays

- Children abused in church settings pay when their reports are not acted on, as IICSA [1] and the Makin Review [3] found. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Survivors pay, having waited years for a response and redress. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Clergy pay, because their homes depend on a bishop's licence.
- LGBTQ clergy and couples pay, as the licence refusal recorded in section 11 shows [14].
- Indigenous children in Canadian residential schools paid. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- The descendants of enslaved people whose labor generated part of the endowment pay. [FINANCIAL RECORD]

## 17. Who gets hurt most {#who-gets-hurt}

### Where the weight lands

The costs in section 15 do not fall evenly. They fall hardest where a person has the least power inside the institution and the fewest places to go outside it: on survivors whose reports went unanswered [1][3], and on clergy whose home, pay and ministry hang on one licence. The table names who carries the most, how, and what makes it worse.

| Who | How | Compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Survivors of church-related abuse | They met delay, disbelief and reputation-first handling (per the reviews). | It compounds with the church's civic standing. |
| Clergy | One decision can take their licence, stipend and home. | It compounds where they have families. |
| LGBTQ clergy | They can be refused a licence [14]. | It compounds with the loss of the home. |
| Women clergy | Parishes may decline their oversight. | It compounds with the provinces that refuse to ordain women at all. |

## 18. The middle tiers {#tiers}

Most of the institution's work is done below the bishop, by people who see the decisions without making them. Safeguarding advisers, archdeacons, churchwardens and PCCs, and diocesan secretaries each carry part of the load, and each stands at one point where the next decision could be declined. The table sets out what each tier does, what it sees, what it is asked to do and where it could refuse.

| Role | Does | Sees | Asked | Could refuse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diocesan safeguarding advisers | Handle allegations | Every case, and every delay | To follow process | To let a case wait on a senior figure's comfort |
| Archdeacons | Oversee clergy discipline and buildings | Which clergy are vulnerable | To manage quietly | To trade a complaint for a quiet move |
| Churchwardens and PCCs | Run parishes | The parish share demand | To pay it | To accept closure without published accounts of need |
| Diocesan secretaries | Administer housing and money | Licences and housing | To enforce | To remove a family from a home over doctrine |

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. Two independent reviews found the church protected its reputation before children. What has structurally changed, and who is independent of the church in that structure now?
2. Why was the Independent Safeguarding Board disbanded in 2023, and what replaced it?
3. Why does a priest's home depend on the same licence a bishop can withdraw?
4. The Commissioners traced part of the endowment to the slave trade. Who decides whether £100m is the right answer?
5. Why do twenty-six bishops of a minority church sit in a national legislature?
6. If a parish can formally decline a woman bishop's oversight, what does her consecration mean in practice?

### In closing

1. The Church of England's own synods voted to ordain women, and the church published reports that ended an archbishop's tenure. That is more self-correction than most institutions in this book have managed. It is also why the standard is fair: the church has shown it can do this, so the question is only whether it will finish.
2. You can love your parish and still ask where the parish share goes. You can sing Evensong and still ask why a survivor waited years for an answer. That is Anglican. The tradition's own genius is argument in synod.

## 24. Leaving safely here {#leaving}

Practical guidance, not legal advice.

1. **For lay members, leaving costs nothing formal.** There is no shunning doctrine and giving is voluntary. This is a strength, and it is recorded here.
2. **For clergy,** losing a licence can mean losing home and income together; take advice before you resign.
3. **Church school places** may depend on worship attendance; check a school's admissions criteria.
4. **If you were abused,** go to the police first. You can also contact the church's safeguarding team, and use the redress scheme Synod approved in 2025 [9].
5. **Find support** that is independent of the church [27][28].
