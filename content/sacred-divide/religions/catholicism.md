## 2. A day inside {#a-day-inside}

*Maria · A Tuesday in Lent · A parish town in a Catholic country*

Mass at seven, because Tuesday is the day she goes before work, and because Father Dominic notices. Not unkindly. He simply notices, and being noticed is how she has understood her standing for thirty years.

She does not go up for communion. She has not gone up since the annulment paperwork stalled, which was two years ago, and which means she is married to Paolo in the eyes of the state and to nobody in the eyes of the Church. She kneels while the others pass her pew. Her mother, who is eighty-one and sits with her, has stopped asking.

At the office she calls the diocesan tribunal for the fourth time. A woman takes her details again. The file is with the judge. There is no timeline. She says thank you, twice, and hangs up, and then sits with her hands flat on the desk for a moment.

Her son Matteo is at the Catholic school, which is the good school, which costs what it costs. His teacher emails about a form. At lunch Maria reads that the diocese three provinces over has filed something in court about parish assets, and that the parish involved is one where a priest was moved twice. She reads two paragraphs and then puts the phone in her bag.

Her mother mentions, at dinner, that Nonna's funeral was beautiful and that she hopes hers will be in the same church. Maria says of course. She does not say that she has been told her own burial may be a question, because she does not actually know whether that is true, and there is nobody she can ask who is not also the person who decides.

At eleven she prays the rosary because her hands know it, and the words go where words have always gone, somewhere older than the tribunal and the file and the woman on the phone. That part is intact. It is the only part she is certain of, and she does not examine the fact that she has begun to think of it as separate from the rest.

## 4. What healthy looks like here {#healthy}

::: lede
The largest single Christian institution (~1.4 billion baptized members), led by the Pope and organized through a global hierarchy of dioceses, religious orders, schools, hospitals, and charities.
:::

- Religious orders and parishes practicing open-book finances; lay review boards with real power.
- Reform currents: Vatican II's conscience teaching, liberation theology's 'preferential option for the poor,' survivor advocacy groups (SNAP, ECA) forcing the inquiries the hierarchy resisted.

## 5. History {#history}

Continuous institutional development from the early church of Rome; papal primacy consolidated across the first millennium; medieval Christendom fused the Church with European political power; the modern Vatican City State (1929, Lateran Treaty) made the Church also literally a sovereign government.

### Timeline

```timeline
1st century | Christian community at Rome; tradition of Peter's ministry there | Foundation of a later primacy claim.
2nd–4th c. | Roman bishops assert precedence; imperial establishment | The see of Rome gains political as well as spiritual weight.
5th–11th c. | Papal claims consolidate; Papal States established | The Church becomes a territorial sovereign — a government with sacraments.
1054 | East–West Schism | Rome's universal jurisdiction claim becomes the permanent dividing line.
1150–1300 | Canon law systematized; Inquisition established; universities founded | A parallel legal system emerges that can prosecute belief.
1545–1563 | Council of Trent responds to the Reformation | Clerical control of sacraments and interpretation is hardened by design.
1870 | First Vatican Council defines papal infallibility; Papal States lost | Doctrinal authority is maximized precisely as temporal territory disappears.
1929 | Lateran Treaty creates Vatican City State | The Church regains sovereignty — with diplomatic immunity and no external audit.
1962–65 | Second Vatican Council | Conscience, vernacular liturgy, and lay participation open genuine reform channels.
1985–present | Global abuse crisis: Boston 2002, Australian Royal Commission 2017, French CIASE 2021, U.S. grand juries | Independent inquiries document that the pattern was institutional management, not isolated failure. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
2013–present | Financial reform attempts; Cardinal Becciu convicted 2023, partial retrial ordered 2026 | First criminal conviction of a cardinal in the Vatican's own court — partial transparency under external pressure. [COURT RECORD]
```

### Moments in the room

::: card
#### 1962 — Crimen sollicitationis

A Holy Office instruction directed that clergy solicitation cases be handled under the secret of the Holy Office, binding participants — including, in effect, victims — to silence under threat of excommunication. It was sent to bishops and not published.

**Why it matters.** Not a failure of a system but the system working. Every national inquiry that followed found bishops who had files and rules for keeping them, and this is the document the rules descended from.
:::

::: card
#### January 2002 — The Boston Globe publishes the pattern, not the priest

Spotlight's first story was about John Geoghan. The second was about the archdiocese's own records showing the transfers — the pattern of moving priests between parishes. Court-ordered document release did what forty years of internal process had not.

**Why it matters.** The pivot from 'a bad priest' to 'a filing system.' Every subsequent commission — Ireland, Australia, Pennsylvania, France, Germany — followed documents rather than testimony, because Boston proved the documents existed.
:::

::: card
#### October 2021 — 216,000, and then the number moves

France's independent CIASE commission reported an estimated 216,000 minors abused by clergy since 1950, rising to around 330,000 including lay church personnel. The Church itself had commissioned it. Days later the head of the bishops' conference said the seal of confession was "stronger than the laws of the Republic", rejecting the report's central reporting recommendation.

**Why it matters.** An institution paid for the count and then contested it in public within a week. This is what accountability looks like when the accountable party keeps the right to interpret the audit.
:::

### Authority

- The most formally centralized religious hierarchy on earth: Pope → Curia → bishops → priests, with exclusive power to ordain, absolve, excommunicate, and define doctrine.
- Sacramental monopoly: valid access to confession, Eucharist, marriage, and last rites runs through the ordained. That monopoly is the leverage.
- Canon law operates as a parallel legal system historically used to process abuse allegations internally. [GOVERNMENT REPORT: multiple national inquiries — Australia Royal Commission 2017, French CIASE 2021, U.S. grand juries]

## 10. Genealogy {#genealogy}

::: card
#### Sacramental monopoly

**Origin.** Consolidated over the first millennium: valid ordination becomes the sole channel of forgiveness, communion, marriage, and last rites. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

**What it was for.** In a fragmenting post-Roman Europe, a single credentialing system prevented doctrinal chaos and gave communities a recognizable authority when no state functioned.

**Why that reason expired.** There is now a functioning state, universal literacy, and open access to scripture. The chaos the monopoly prevented has other remedies. What remains is a chokepoint: your access to grace, your marriage's legitimacy, and your burial all pass through one office.

**Who benefits now.** The hierarchy, absolutely and by design. A monopoly on salvation is the most inelastic market in existence — you cannot shop elsewhere, and you cannot refuse to buy.
:::

::: card
#### Canonical secrecy in handling allegations

**Origin.** Canon law developed a parallel internal legal system across the medieval period, with confidentiality norms for sensitive proceedings. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

**What it was for.** When the Church was a sovereign among sovereigns, internal jurisdiction protected clergy from arbitrary secular kings and mob justice — a real protection in a lawless era.

**Why that reason expired.** You live in a jurisdiction with police, mandatory reporting, and courts. Multiple national inquiries — Australia's Royal Commission, France's CIASE, U.S. grand juries — found that the same confidentiality was used to move abusers between parishes rather than to police. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]

**Who benefits now.** The institution's legal exposure and reputation. Not the children. Every inquiry reached that conclusion independently, on the Church's own documents.
:::

::: card
#### Clerical celibacy

**Origin.** Enforced in the Latin Church from the 11th–12th century reforms (Gregorian reform, Second Lateran Council 1139). [OFFICIAL POLICY]

**What it was for.** Substantially about property: married priests produced heirs who inherited Church land. Celibacy kept the estate in the institution's hands.

**Why that reason expired.** The property question is settled by modern corporate law. What remains is a structure that concentrates unmarried men with sacramental authority over families — a configuration every abuse inquiry identified as an accountability risk, not a spiritual necessity. Eastern Catholic churches ordain married men, which proves it is discipline, not doctrine.

**Who benefits now.** The institution's asset base and its mobile, dependent, fully controllable workforce. A priest with no family has nowhere else to go.
:::

::: card
#### Annulment tribunals

**Origin.** Medieval canonical jurisdiction over marriage validity, when the Church was the only marriage registry in Europe. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

**What it was for.** Someone had to adjudicate legitimacy and inheritance in the absence of civil registries. The Church was the recordkeeper.

**Why that reason expired.** Civil registries exist everywhere. The tribunal now performs no legal function — it exists only to decide whether your second family is spiritually real.

**Who benefits now.** Clerical authority over the most intimate decision in your life. A structure in which you must apply to a panel of celibate men for permission to have loved someone.
:::

## 11. Reach {#reach}

### Information

- The Index of Forbidden Books formally regulated reading until 1966; the imprimatur/nihil obstat system still governs approved teaching materials. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Abuse allegations were processed under canonical secrecy for decades, with documents kept from civil authorities — the confidentiality was the mechanism. [GOVERNMENT REPORT: Australian Royal Commission; French CIASE]
- Theologians teaching in Catholic institutions can lose their mandatum to teach for dissenting on doctrine, narrowing internal debate. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

### Children

- Infant baptism enrolls the child before consent; first confession and first communion around age 7 begin adult-mediated examination of a child's conscience.
- Catholic school systems are among the world's largest, integrating education, identity, and institutional loyalty from age 4 onward.
- Child sexual abuse within these structures is the most heavily documented institutional harm in this codex, established by multiple national inquiries. [GOVERNMENT REPORT / COURT RECORD]

### Bodies

- Ordination is restricted to men — authority, and therefore control over sacraments and money, is structurally male. [OFFICIAL POLICY: Canon 1024]
- Contraception and abortion are prohibited; in countries where the Church shapes law and hospital protocol, this becomes state coercion, not personal teaching. [OFFICIAL POLICY / GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Divorce is not recognized; remarriage requires annulment granted by clerical tribunals — the institution controls the legitimacy of a member's next family.
- Mandatory clerical celibacy concentrates unmarried men with sacramental authority over families, a structure inquiries repeatedly linked to accountability failure. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- LGBTQ Catholics: same-sex activity is doctrinally 'disordered'; blessings permitted (2023) while marriage remains excluded. [OFFICIAL POLICY]

## 12. The 30 techniques {#techniques}

Thirty named techniques from domestic-abuse and social-psychology research, applied to institutions, in the eight stages of the cycle. Each carries an evidence grade for this tradition.

### Stage 1 · Idealize {#stage-1}

::: stage
**You arrive with a need and are met with more warmth than you have had in years.**

You are absorbed into something ancient and beautiful — incense, Latin, the Real Presence, two thousand years of continuity. Converts are told they have come home to the one true Church. The offer is not merely community. It is historical destiny, and it is intoxicating.

*What it asks of you:* Beauty is real and it is also the thing that lowered your defenses. Both are true. Which one did you examine?
:::

::: tactic n=1
#### 1 · Love Bombing {#t-1}

*Overwhelming affection on arrival to create emotional dependency before terms are revealed.*

**How it shows here**

- A wounded person is folded into parish life through Mass invitations, parish meals, RCIA classes, confession, spiritual direction, saints, candles, ritual beauty, and the promise of finally belonging to something ancient and stable.
- Converts are often made to feel they are entering “the one true Church,” not merely joining a community. The emotional hook is not just friendship; it is historical destiny.
- The institution offers a mother-language: Holy Mother Church, father-priests, confession, absolution, sacraments, homecoming. The convert is not merely welcomed; they are absorbed into a family hierarchy.

**The strongest defense.** Catholicism is sacramental and communal. Welcoming converts is not manipulation.

**The counter.** The manipulation begins when the welcome is fused with dependency. If access to grace, forgiveness, belonging, marriage legitimacy, burial rites, and moral identity all run through the institution, then the embrace is not neutral. It is a velvet gate.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Parish welcome is local practice; nothing in canon law governs it.
:::

::: tactic n=2
#### 2 · Weaponized Generosity {#t-2}

*Giving help that installs unspoken obligation.*

**How it shows here**

- Catholic parishes, schools, charities, hospitals, shelters, and social services often provide real material help: food, education, counseling, adoption support, immigration assistance, burial rites, and crisis care.
- The institution can become the gatekeeper of both physical and spiritual aid: sacraments, confession, Catholic schooling, parish approval, annulments, weddings, funerals, and social legitimacy.
- A family may receive help from the parish for years, but if they challenge the priest, leave the Church, divorce, remarry outside Catholic rules, or publicly dissent, that support can become socially or spiritually conditional.
- Catholic schooling can operate as generosity and dependency at once: tuition assistance, moral formation, community belonging — but also institutional loyalty built into childhood.

**The strongest defense.** Catholic charity is one of the largest humanitarian forces in the world. The Church helps people regardless of belief.

**The counter.** Large-scale charity can be real while local dependency is still coercive. The question is not whether Catholics help people. They obviously do. The question is whether the institution later treats that help as a reason you owe silence, obedience, doctrinal compliance, or shame. When grace has paperwork and belonging has gatekeepers, the gift is not clean.

**Evidence grade.** [[Cultural]] Parish welcome is local practice; nothing in canon law governs it.
:::

### Stage 2 · Hook {#stage-2}

::: stage
**You are given a future that cannot be verified, and a rope for whenever you drift toward the door.**

Beatific vision, the communion of saints, your grandmother waiting — and the sacraments as the only route there, administered by a specific man on specific terms. Drift, and the door is always open: come back to confession, your mother is praying.

*What it asks of you:* You were given an eternity that requires a middleman, and the middleman decides your eligibility. Sit with the structure of that.
:::

::: tactic n=3
#### 3 · Future Faking {#t-3}

*Promising a future that keeps you invested but never has to arrive.*

**How it shows here**

- Catholicism offers a massive future economy: heaven, purgatory, indulgences, masses for the dead, confession, sacraments, final rites, sainthood, and eternal union with God.
- The institution positions itself as the manager of the soul’s postmortem route. Baptism begins the journey. Confession restores it. Eucharist sustains it. Last rites prepare it. Masses may aid the dead. The Church becomes the travel agency for eternity.
- Purgatory is especially effective as future leverage. The dead may still need purification. The living can pray, pay, offer masses, and remain connected to the sacramental system on their behalf.
- The faithful are told that suffering can be “offered up.” Pain becomes spiritual currency. Instead of asking why people are suffering under bad systems, the system teaches them to deposit the pain into an invisible account.

**The strongest defense.** Catholic teaching on heaven, purgatory, and sacraments is about mercy, purification, and communion, not manipulation.

**The counter.** Mercy becomes leverage when the institution controls the channels. **The counter.** Mission becomes REPLACEMENT when continuity matters more than accountability. If the Church can replace workers, donors, and wounded families without confronting why they left, permanence has become indifference.

**Evidence grade.** [[Codified]] Doctrinal authority is located in the magisterium by published constitution, which is the deniability structure itself.
:::

::: tactic n=30
#### 30 · Plausible Deniability {#t-30}

*The institution is never responsible, because the authority is attributed to God — who isn't available for cross-examination.*

**How it shows here**

- The Church can say it does not control individual conscience, while also teaching that conscience must be “properly formed” by Church authority. If the conscience agrees, it is conscience. If it disagrees, it is malformed.
- A diocese may claim ignorance of abuse or mishandling because decisions passed through layers: priest, bishop, personnel office, canonical process, legal counsel, review board.
- A Catholic school can enforce sexuality, gender, dress, and religious expectations while claiming parents freely chose the institution.
- A priest may pressure a penitent through confession, but the Church can frame the exchange as voluntary sacramental care.
- The Church can exalt women as holy, Marian, maternal, and spiritually dignified while denying them actual sacramental authority — then deny misogyny by pointing to symbolic reverence.

**The strongest defense.** Catholic teaching is clear, but pastoral application varies. The Church has procedures and cannot comment on every case. People voluntarily choose Catholic schools, sacraments, and confession.

**The counter.** “Pastoral variation” becomes PLAUSIBLE DENIABILITY when local leaders enforce harsh outcomes and the hierarchy later calls them isolated or contextual. Procedure becomes concealment when responsibility disappears into offices, boards, dioceses, and canon law. Consent is compromised when the Church controls access to grace, legitimacy, schooling, marriage, confession, and burial.

**Evidence grade.** [[Codified]] Doctrinal authority located in the magisterium, with the Holy See holding sovereign and diplomatic immunities no other religion possesses. *(sourced)*

## 15. Cost & cover {#cost}

### What leaving costs

- Excommunication and denial of sacraments; for the devout, this is framed as risking eternal loss.
- Family and cultural rupture in strongly Catholic societies; annulment system controls remarriage legitimacy.
- For clergy and religious: loss of housing, pension, and entire social world upon laicization or departure.

### The ledger of exit

| Cost | Documented? | Detail | The official denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sacramental access | Yes | Denial of communion, absolution, marriage, and Catholic burial | “Sacraments require disposition, not punishment.” |
| Family and cultural loss | Often | Strong social pressure in majority-Catholic societies; family rupture over remarriage or apostasy | “The Church only teaches; families decide.” |
| Marriage legitimacy | Yes | Annulment tribunals control whether a second marriage is recognized | “It's a finding of fact about the first marriage.” |
| Livelihood (clergy and religious) | Yes | Loss of housing, stipend, pension, and entire social world on departure | “Laicization is voluntary.” |

### How the cost is denied

| Channel | Level | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit policy | Low | Canon law, catechism, and Vatican norms are published — much of the control is literally codified. |
| Informal enforcement | Medium | Parish-level social pressure supplements the written rules. |
| Leadership distance | High | Rome disclaims diocesan conduct; dioceses disclaim orders; orders disclaim individual priests. |
| Doctrinal ambiguity | Medium | 'Pastoral discretion' allows the same rule to be enforced or waived depending on the person. |
| Cultural outsourcing | High | Harm is attributed to national culture or 'that era' rather than structure. |

## 16. The ledger {#ledger}

### Who benefits

- The institution itself as a corporate person: sovereignty, diplomatic status, legal immunities, and asset protection unavailable to any other religion. [OFFICIAL POLICY: Lateran Treaty; Holy See UN observer status]
- Curial and diocesan officials with lifetime tenure and internal-only accountability.
- States that co-govern through concordats — e.g., Germany collects billions annually in church tax on the Church's behalf. [FINANCIAL RECORD: German Bishops' Conference — €6.75bn in 2025]

### Money out, leverage back

- The Holy See is a sovereign state with UN observer status and diplomatic immunity — the only religion on earth with them. It uses that standing to lobby against reproductive and LGBTQ language in international agreements, exercising state power that no other church possesses and that no Catholic voted to grant. [OFFICIAL POLICY / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]
- Concordats bind national governments to Church privileges. Germany collects church tax on the Church's behalf through the state revenue system, and Austria enforces the churches' own contribution through its civil courts — billions annually, with membership functioning as a tax status you must formally file to escape. [OFFICIAL POLICY: Kirchensteuer]
- Catholic hospital networks in secular healthcare systems decline to provide contraception, sterilization, and in some cases emergency obstetric intervention — meaning Church doctrine determines the care available to non-Catholic patients who had no other hospital within reach. This is the clearest live example of aid becoming leverage. [GOVERNMENT REPORT / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]
- Development and relief work in the Global South is substantial and genuine. It has also been used to condition sexual-health messaging in populations dependent on the clinic. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

### Who pays

- Children in Church-run institutions, per multiple national inquiries.
- Women, excluded from every decision-making office that governs their bodies and marriages.
- Divorced and remarried Catholics, whose standing depends on tribunal outcomes.
- LGBTQ members and their families.
- Abuse survivors, who faced canonical secrecy, litigation, and asset-shielding.
- Nuns and religious sisters, whose labor built institutions they never governed. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]

## 19. Documented cases {#cases}

::: case
### CIASE report on abuse in the French Church

- **when:** October 2021
- **what:** An independent commission established by the French bishops estimated approximately 216,000 victims of clerical abuse since 1950, rising to around 330,000 including lay Catholic institutions. It found the institution had systematically prioritized its own reputation and had used canonical confidentiality to keep matters from civil authorities.
- **record:** CIASE (Sauvé Commission) report, commissioned by the Church itself
- **outcome:** Bishops acknowledged institutional responsibility and established a compensation body. The commission was created only after decades of external pressure.
- **tactics:** 12, 18, 25
- **grade:** Documented
:::

::: case
### Conviction of Cardinal Becciu

- **when:** December 2023
- **what:** The Vatican's own criminal court convicted a cardinal of financial crimes in connection with a London property investment funded partly through Peter's Pence — donations members understood to be papal charity.
- **record:** Vatican City State criminal court judgment
- **outcome:** The first trial and conviction of a cardinal by the Vatican's own court — now being retried in part. In March 2026 the Vatican’s appeals court found procedural errors and ordered a partial retrial, which began in June; the 2023 verdict stands until it ends. Demonstrates the institution possesses a functioning criminal court, and that it has never used it to convict a bishop for concealing abuse.
- **tactics:** 18, 26
- **grade:** Documented
:::

::: case
### State-collected church tax

- **when:** Ongoing
- **what:** Germany collects church tax through the state revenue system on behalf of registered religious bodies, generating billions annually; Austria’s church contribution is collected by the churches themselves but enforced through the civil courts. Membership functions as a tax status, and leaving requires a formal civil declaration.
- **record:** National tax law (published policy)
- **outcome:** In force. A rare case where the exit procedure is literally a government form — and where the institution's revenue depends on members not filing it.
- **tactics:** 2, 26
- **grade:** Codified
:::

## 22. Regional variants {#regional}

::: card
### Ireland

- **apex:** The Archbishop of Dublin and the Irish Episcopal Conference — but the operative authority since 2009 has been the state.
- **law:** The Ryan (2009) and Murphy (2009) Commissions, the Cloyne Report (2011), and the Mother and Baby Homes Commission (2021) were statutory inquiries with compulsion powers. Ireland now has mandatory reporting under the Children First Act 2015 with no clergy exemption.
- **documented:** Ryan documented systemic abuse across industrial and reformatory schools; Murphy found the Dublin archdiocese had operated on a policy of avoiding scandal and protecting assets. The Taoiseach's 2011 Dáil statement condemning the Vatican was without precedent in Europe.
- **exit:** Collapsed within a generation. Mass attendance fell from near-universal to a minority practice; the social cost of leaving in urban Ireland is now close to zero, and in some settings the reputational cost runs the other way.
- **regulator:** Tusla (child and family agency) — mandatory reports go here. Charities Regulator for financial matters.
- **tell:** The clearest natural experiment in this codex: doctrine unchanged, exit costs collapsed. What changed was disclosure and the state's willingness to compel it.
:::

::: card
### Poland

- **apex:** The Polish Bishops' Conference, operating with substantial political protection.
- **law:** No equivalent statutory commission. A 2019 state commission on paedophilia was established with limited powers. Concordat arrangements and close alignment with governing parties have historically constrained scrutiny.
- **documented:** Independent documentary films — notably 'Tell No One' (2019), released two months after the bishops published their first abuse figures — reached tens of millions of viewers and did work no state inquiry had done.
- **exit:** Still substantial in rural areas and among older generations; falling rapidly among the young, where Polish secularisation is now among Europe's fastest.
- **regulator:** Prosecutors and the state commission. Practical access has been limited; this is one of the cards where the honest answer is that the compel route is weak.
- **tell:** Journalism substituted for a statutory inquiry. That worked once and is not a system.
:::

::: card
### United States

- **apex:** The USCCB — which is a conference, not a superior; each diocesan bishop answers only to Rome.
- **law:** No federal inquiry. Accountability has run through state attorneys general (the Pennsylvania grand jury, 2018, and inquiries announced by attorneys general in at least fourteen states), civil litigation, and diocesan bankruptcies. Churches file no Form 990.
- **documented:** Boston (2002) established the documentary method. The Pennsylvania grand jury named over 300 priests across six dioceses. Some forty US dioceses and religious orders have filed for bankruptcy protection, which has the effect of consolidating and capping claims.
- **exit:** Low in most of the country; higher in dense ethnic-Catholic communities and where schooling and employment are parish-linked.
- **regulator:** State attorneys general — the most effective route in this jurisdiction by a wide margin. State child-protection agencies. Clergy mandatory-reporting exemptions persist in many states.
- **tell:** Where a state AG opened a grand jury, records emerged. Where none did, none did. The variable is one elected official's decision.
:::

::: card
### The Philippines

- **apex:** The Catholic Bishops' Conference of the Philippines, in a country of roughly 80 million Catholics with no divorce law.
- **law:** The Philippines is the only state other than Vatican City without divorce. Church influence over reproductive-health legislation has been sustained and documented across decades.
- **documented:** The reproductive-health law fight (2012, upheld 2014) is the clearest documented case in this codex of a religious body shaping a nation's health policy.
- **exit:** High, and materially different from the Western pattern: leaving affects marriage, family standing, and in many provinces schooling and employment.
- **regulator:** Congress on legislation. The Commission on Human Rights. Church financial disclosure is minimal and largely unregulated.
- **tell:** Where a tradition is the majority, the accountability question stops being about the institution and becomes about the legislature.
:::

## 23. The questions {#questions}

1. If there was nothing to hide, why did it take a government commission — not a synod, not a pope — to produce the documents?
2. Why was a priest credibly accused of abusing children moved to a new parish instead of handed to police, and why has no bishop who did the moving lost his office over it?
3. The Church has a criminal court capable of convicting a cardinal of financial crimes. Why has it never used that court to convict a bishop of covering up abuse?
4. Why is a divorced woman denied communion while the man who transferred her daughter's abuser retired with honors?
5. If celibacy is spiritually necessary, why are married Eastern Catholic priests validly ordained? And if it is not necessary, what is it actually for?
6. You were taught that criticizing the Church harms the faith. Ask who benefits from you believing that the institution and God are the same thing.

### In closing

The Church's own claim is that truth withstands examination — that faith and reason cannot finally conflict. Take it at its word. A body confident in its own holiness does not need canonical secrecy, does not need diplomatic immunity, and does not need you to stop reading. The fact that it has used all three is not an argument against God. It is information about an organization.

You can keep the Eucharist and lose your deference. Millions of Catholics have — the ones who forced the inquiries, who ran the survivor networks, who kept faith while refusing the men. That is not apostasy. On the Church's own theology of conscience, it may be the most Catholic thing available to you.

## 24. Leaving safely here {#leaving}

Practical guidance, not legal advice.

1. **In Germany,** leaving is a formal declaration at the registry office or local court, and it ends the church tax [20].
2. **Marriage.** A civil divorce does not end a Catholic marriage in church law; remarriage in the church requires an annulment from a church tribunal [23].
3. **If you were abused,** civil reporting is the first route — in Ireland even what is said in confession must be reported [17]; survivor networks can help [26][27].
4. **Your records.** Sacramental records (baptism, marriage) are kept permanently in parish registers; leaving does not erase them.
5. **Find support** that is not run by the church [26][39][40].
