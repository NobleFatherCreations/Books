# Oriental Orthodoxy — draft record

- **id:** `oriental-orthodoxy`
- **name:** Oriental Orthodoxy (Coptic, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Armenian, Syriac, Malankara)
- **family:** Christianity
- **status:** DRAFT, pre-fact-check
- **insider readers needed:** one Coptic and one Ethiopian or Eritrean Tewahedo reader at minimum; ideally an Armenian and a Malankara reader too. These six churches differ sharply by country.
- **the unusual thing about this page:** six autocephalous churches, most of which live as minorities under states that pressure them (Egypt, Eritrea, Syria) or are entangled with states in ways that cut both ways (Ethiopia, Armenia). The record of harm *to* these churches (massacres, bombings, confiscation, the Armenian genocide) is enormous and must come first. The internal machinery is best documented in four places: personal-status law (divorce), state capture of patriarchs, property litigation, and monastic and clerical discipline. English-language sourcing is thinner than for Western churches, which the page must disclose.

## neutral
An estimated 60 million faithful *(verify)*, in six autocephalous churches:

- Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo (by far the largest, ~36–45M *(verify)*)
- Coptic Orthodox (~10M *(verify)*)
- Armenian Apostolic (~8–9M)
- Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo
- Syriac Orthodox
- Malankara Orthodox Syrian (India)

Among the oldest Christian churches, they are known for ancient liturgies (Coptic, Ge'ez, Classical Armenian, Syriac), long fasting calendars, monasticism, and survival as minorities.

## origin
Separated from the churches of Rome and Constantinople after the Council of Chalcedon (451), over the formulation of Christ's nature. The Oriental churches hold a "miaphysite" Christology. Each developed under a different state: Coptic under Byzantine, then Islamic, rule; Ethiopian as a Christian empire's state church until 1974; Armenian as the first state to adopt Christianity (traditionally 301); Syriac across the Middle East; the Malankara church in Kerala, with ancient roots and later Syriac ties.

## authority
- Each church has its own patriarch or catholicos and holy synod: the Coptic Pope of Alexandria; the Ethiopian and Eritrean Patriarchs; the Armenian Catholicos of All Armenians (Etchmiadzin) and the Catholicos of Cilicia; the Syriac Patriarch of Antioch; the Malankara Catholicos. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- Selection differs. The Coptic Pope is chosen by lot: a child draws one of three shortlisted names at the altar. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- The state reaches into selection and removal: Eritrea's government-backed removal of its Patriarch (2006); Egypt's presidential ratification of the Coptic Pope *(verify)*; Armenia's 2025 government confrontation with the Catholicos *(verify)*. [GOVERNMENT REPORT / INVESTIGATIVE REPORT]

## money
- Church property, monasteries, land, and diaspora parish giving. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- In Ethiopia, historic church landholding was vast until the 1975 land nationalization under the Derg. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
- Fees and donations for sacraments and ritual services, set locally. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- In India, decades-long litigation between Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite factions over thousands of churches and their assets. [COURT RECORD]
- Financial disclosure is generally minimal and jurisdiction-dependent. Diaspora parishes registered as charities (UK, US) file accounts. [REGULATORY FILING]

## exit
- For Copts in Egypt, personal-status law routes marriage and divorce through the church. The Coptic church permits divorce only on narrow grounds and controls permission to remarry, so leaving a marriage can mean leaving the church or converting. [OFFICIAL POLICY / COURT RECORD]
- Conversion out of Christianity in Egypt is legally possible, but conversion *back* has been bureaucratically obstructed, with ID-card disputes litigated for years *(verify)*. [COURT RECORD]
- In Eritrea, practising outside the four state-registered faiths risks detention. The Eritrean church itself is under state control. [GOVERNMENT REPORT]
- Social and family costs of leaving are high in minority communities where church and ethnic identity coincide. [PATTERN OBSERVED]

## whoBenefits
- States that manage minority churches through their patriarchs (Egypt, Eritrea), and patriarchates that trade loyalty for protection. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
- Ruling factions in intra-church property disputes (India). [COURT RECORD]
- Clerical hierarchies controlling personal-status permissions (Egypt). [OFFICIAL POLICY]

## healthy
- Extraordinary continuity of liturgy, language and learning under centuries of pressure.
- A monastic tradition that historically offered refuge and education.
- Welfare and schools run by the Coptic church in the absence of state provision.
- Lay councils in some churches (e.g. the Coptic Community Council, the Armenian National Representative Assemblies) giving laity a formal role in electing catholicoi and managing property *(verify composition and powers)*.

## opening
You may keep a fast longer than most people in the world have ever fasted, sing a liturgy older than most nations, and belong to a church that has buried more of its martyrs than any page in this book can count. Nothing here makes light of that. This page is about what happens when a church survives by bargaining with states, and about who pays the price of the bargain inside it.

## genealogy
1. **Church control of marriage and divorce (Egypt)**
   - *Origin:* Ottoman and Egyptian personal-status systems delegated family law to each religious community. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* Communal autonomy, and protection from Islamic family law.
   - *Expired?* When the church narrows divorce grounds and refuses remarriage, autonomy becomes control over members who have no civil alternative.
   - *Benefits now:* The clerical hierarchy's authority over family life.
2. **The patriarch as the state's interlocutor**
   - *Origin:* The dhimma and millet systems: the head of the community answers to the ruler for it. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* Survival.
   - *Expired?* It lets states manage millions of people through one man (Eritrea, 2006), and lets patriarchs speak for people who did not choose them.
   - *Benefits now:* States and patriarchates, jointly.
3. **Purity rules**
   - *Origin:* Levitical purity traditions preserved in early canons. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* Ritual order.
   - *Expired?* Applied to women's bodies, it restricts half the church's access to its own sacraments.
   - *Benefits now:* No one, identifiably. It is custom enforced by clergy.

## hardQuestions
1. If a Coptic woman cannot leave an abusive marriage and remarry in her church, and her state gives her no civil alternative, whose protection is the rule?
2. When a government removes a patriarch, as in Eritrea in 2006, what does "autocephaly" mean?
3. Who owns the church you pray in, and why did it take a Supreme Court to decide (India)?
4. If purity rules keep women from communion, who decided that the body God made is unclean?
5. What does the diaspora parish send home, and who accounts for it?
6. The martyrs refused to bend to rulers. Does the church bend now?

## closing
1. Your church outlasted empires by refusing to bend where it mattered. That is the precise standard for a church that bends to a state to keep its patriarch, or bends a woman's life to keep a canon.
2. You can keep every fast and still ask who decides whether your sister may remarry. The oldest churches have always argued. That is what the councils were.

## day
- **name:** Mariam
- **when:** A Tuesday in the Fast of the Apostles
- **where:** Cairo, Shubra

1. She wakes before six and does not eat, as she has not eaten before the Liturgy since she was a girl. The fast is a kind of quiet she has always loved.
2. On the way to work she passes the church where her aunt was married, and remembers the police at the door on Palm Sunday four years ago. The guards are still there. Nobody remarks on them any more.
3. At lunch her friend Nevine says she has been told again that the council will not hear her case this year. Nevine's husband left for Kuwait eight years ago. She is not divorced, cannot remarry, and cannot become anything else without losing her family and her name.
4. In the afternoon Mariam's mother calls about the priest's visit on Friday and whether the flat is ready.
5. In the evening she goes to vespers. The hymns are in Coptic, which she half understands, and it doesn't matter. She stands where the women stand and feels, as always, that this is the oldest thing she will ever be part of.
6. Walking home, she thinks about Nevine and about the council of priests who will not hear her, and realizes she cannot imagine anyone she could ask to change it — not a priest, not a judge, not a president. She says a prayer for Nevine, because that is the only door she can see.
