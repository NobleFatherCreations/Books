## neutral
About 60–70 million faithful [1], in six autocephalous churches:
- Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo (by far the largest; estimates range from about 36 to 50+ million) [1]
- Coptic Orthodox
- Armenian Apostolic
- Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo (about 2 million) [2]
- Syriac Orthodox
- Malankara Orthodox Syrian (India)

They are among the oldest Christian churches, known for ancient liturgies (Coptic, Ge'ez, Classical Armenian, Syriac), long fasting calendars, monasticism, and survival as minorities.

## origin
These churches separated from Rome and Constantinople after the Council of Chalcedon (451), over the formulation of Christ's nature; the Oriental churches hold a "miaphysite" Christology. Each developed under a different state:
- Coptic under Byzantine and then Islamic rule
- Ethiopian as a Christian empire's state church until the 1974 revolution [5]
- Armenian as the first state to adopt Christianity (traditionally 301)
- Syriac across the Middle East
- Malankara in Kerala, with ancient roots and later Syriac ties

## authority
- Each church has its own patriarch or catholicos and holy synod.
- Selection differs by church:
  - The Coptic Pope is chosen by lot: a blindfolded boy draws one of three shortlisted names at the altar (Pope Tawadros II, 4 Nov 2012) [6].
  - The Armenian Catholicos is elected for life by a National Ecclesiastical Assembly: about two-thirds lay delegates and one-third clergy, elected by dioceses worldwide (Karekin II was elected in 1999 by about 450 delegates) [15].
- States reach into leadership:
  - Eritrea's government-backed removal of its Patriarch (2006), who died under house arrest in 2022 [2]
  - Ethiopia's prime minister brokered the 2023 settlement of a synod schism [7]
  - Armenia's government has brought criminal cases against senior clergy and against the Catholicos himself (2025–26) [12][13]

## money
- Church property, monasteries, land, and diaspora parish giving.
- In Ethiopia the church lost all its land when the Derg nationalized rural land in March 1975. Estimates of prior church holdings range from 10 to 20% of cultivated land [5].
- The 2023 Ethiopian settlement allocated funding and resources to churches in Oromia [7], so money is part of how schisms are settled.
- In India, decades of litigation between Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite factions. In 2017 the Supreme Court gave the Orthodox faction the right to administer about 1,100 churches [3].
- Financial disclosure is generally minimal and jurisdiction-dependent. Diaspora parishes registered as charities (UK, US) file accounts.

## exit
- **Egypt: marriage and divorce go through the church.** The Coptic Church permits divorce essentially only for adultery and controls permission to remarry [4]:
  - 2010: the Supreme Administrative Court ordered the Pope to license a divorced man's remarriage.
  - The Pope refused and threatened to defrock priests who complied.
  - The Supreme Constitutional Court then held that marital matters belong to the church, because Egypt does not recognize civil marriage without a religious ceremony [4].
- **Eritrea:** practising outside state-registered faiths risks detention, and the Orthodox church itself is under state control [2].
- **Minority communities:** where church and ethnic identity coincide, leaving carries high social and family costs.

## whoBenefits
- States that manage minority churches through their patriarchs, and patriarchates that trade loyalty for protection [2].
- Winning factions in property disputes [3].
- Clerical hierarchies holding personal-status permissions [4].

## healthy
- Extraordinary continuity of liturgy, language and learning under centuries of pressure.
- Monasteries that historically offered refuge and education.
- Welfare and schools run by the Coptic Church where the state provides little.
- Real lay power in the Armenian Church: two-thirds of the assembly that elects the Catholicos are lay delegates [15].
- Twenty-five Armenian bishops publicly backed their Catholicos against state pressure [13]. Solidarity against the state is also a check.

## demographics
- **adherents:** about 60–70M (72M in a 2020 estimate cited by reference works) [1].
- **regions:** Ethiopia, Egypt, Armenia, Eritrea, Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, India (Kerala); large diasporas.
- **branches:** six autocephalous churches in communion, plus internal splits (Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite [3]; Ethiopian regional crises [7]; the Tigrayan church's formation of its own administration in May 2021 after the Tigray war [17]).
- **trend:** growth in Ethiopia; decline and emigration in the Middle East.
- **participation:** very high ritual participation (fasting, feasts) in Ethiopia and Egypt, where church identity is ethnic identity.

## info
- Clerical authority over teaching is strong; lay theological critique is rare. [PATTERN OBSERVED]
- Under state control (Eritrea [2]; historically the Derg [5]), church communication is itself controlled.

## children
- Long fasting seasons are practised from childhood.
- Church schools and Sunday schools are primary socialization. The Eritrean patriarch's refusal to excommunicate members of a Sunday-school renewal movement was among the charges used to remove him [2].

## gender
- An all-male priesthood and episcopate; women are barred from the altar area. [OFFICIAL POLICY]
- In Ethiopian Orthodox practice, menstruating women do not enter the church for seven days, and after childbirth women stay away for 40 days (boy) or 80 days (girl) [14].
- In Egypt, church-controlled divorce falls hardest on spouses trapped in failed or abusive marriages [4].

## opening
You may keep a fast longer than most people in the world have ever fasted, sing a liturgy older than most nations, and belong to a church that has buried more of its martyrs than any page in this book can count. Nothing here makes light of that. This page is about what happens when a church survives by bargaining with states, and about who pays the price of the bargain inside it.

## genealogy
1. **Church control of marriage and divorce (Egypt)**
   - *Origin:* Personal-status systems that delegated family law to each religious community. [ACADEMIC SOURCE] *(add source)*
   - *Then:* Communal autonomy, and protection from majority family law.
   - *Expired?* With no civil-marriage alternative, the state's highest court confirmed that the church alone decides [4]. Autonomy becomes control over members with no exit.
   - *Benefits now:* The clerical hierarchy.
2. **The patriarch as the state's interlocutor**
   - *Origin:* The community head answers to the ruler for the community. [ACADEMIC SOURCE]
   - *Then:* Survival.
   - *Expired?* It lets states manage millions through one office. Eritrea removed its patriarch for refusing to excommunicate a youth movement and for demanding prisoners' release [2]. Ethiopia's prime minister settled a schism [7]. Armenia's government prosecutes its Catholicos [13].
   - *Benefits now:* States, and whichever faction the state favors.
3. **Purity rules**
   - *Origin:* Levitical purity traditions preserved in early canons [14].
   - *Then:* Ritual order.
   - *Expired?* Applied to women's bodies, they restrict half the church's access to its sacraments.
   - *Benefits now:* No one identifiable. Custom enforced by clergy.

## hardQuestions
1. If a Coptic woman cannot end a failed marriage and remarry in her church, and her state offers no civil marriage [4], whose protection is the rule?
2. When a government removes a patriarch [2], what does "autocephaly" mean?
3. Who owns the church you pray in, and why did it take a Supreme Court to decide [3]?
4. If women may not enter church for weeks after giving birth to a daughter [14], who decided the body God made is unclean?
5. What does the diaspora parish send home, and who accounts for it?
6. The martyrs refused to bend to rulers. When a prime minister brokers your synod's peace [7] or prosecutes your Catholicos [13], who is bending?

## closing
1. Your church outlasted empires by refusing to bend where it mattered. That is the precise standard for a church that bends to a state to keep its patriarch, or bends a woman's life to keep a canon.
2. You can keep every fast and still ask who decides whether your sister may remarry. The oldest churches have always argued. That is what the councils were.

## loopHere (the seven loops)
1. **Money:** Sacramental access funds the clergy who administer it.
2. **Fear:** Salvation through the church's sacraments; excommunication as spiritual death.
3. **Children:** Baptism, fasting and Sunday school fuse faith and ethnic identity before choice.
4. **Aid:** Church welfare in weak states buys loyalty and state tolerance; state settlements buy the church's quiet [7].
5. **Labor:** Monastic and parish labor.
6. **Scandal:** A monastic killing prosecuted by the state [11]; synodal discipline otherwise closed.
7. **Persecution:** The strongest loop on this page. Real martyrdom [9][10] makes internal criticism unspeakable.

## day
- **name:** Mariam
- **when:** A Tuesday in the Fast of the Apostles
- **where:** Cairo, Shubra

1. She wakes before six and does not eat, as she has not eaten before the Liturgy since she was a girl. The fast is a kind of quiet she has always loved.
2. On the way to work she passes the church where her aunt was married, and remembers the police at the door after Palm Sunday. The guards are still there. Nobody remarks on them any more.
3. At lunch her friend Nevine says the council will not hear her case this year. Nevine's husband left for the Gulf eight years ago. She is not divorced, cannot remarry, and cannot become anything else without losing her family and her name.
4. In the afternoon Mariam's mother calls about the priest's visit on Friday and whether the flat is ready.
5. In the evening she goes to vespers. The hymns are in Coptic, which she half understands, and it doesn't matter. She stands where the women stand and feels, as always, that this is the oldest thing she will ever be part of.
6. Walking home she thinks about Nevine and the council that will not hear her, and realizes she cannot imagine anyone she could ask to change it — not a priest, not a judge, not a president. She says a prayer for Nevine, because that is the only door she can see.
