🎛️ THE TAPERS’ SECTION

Harm Reduction · Acute Crisis Response · Good Samaritan Law

Brief. Then directly into the practical content.

Harm reduction in its clinical form is the public-health principle that people will use substances regardless of legal status or moral instruction, and the goal is reducing the harms associated with that use: testing, information, crisis support, Narcan, clean equipment, non-punitive medical access. In scene language: DanceSafe, Zendo Project, Bunk Police, Wharf Rats, the medical tent, the harm-reduction volunteer with the vest and the fanny pack and the warm face that says “I am not here to judge you, I am here to make sure you don’t die.”

Acute crisis response in festival settings is a specific skill set that is different from default-world first aid in key ways: the substances involved are often unknown, the setting is loud and disorienting and dark, the person in crisis may be terrified of medical intervention for legal reasons, and the babysitter — the friend, the stranger, the unicorn onesie — has often had no training. The protocols in this chapter are derived from Zendo Project, DanceSafe, and MAPS-affiliated training. They are simplified for use by people without formal training in the worst possible moment.

Good Samaritan law applies in all fifty US states in some form as of 2026. Most protect both the person in crisis and the person who calls for help from prosecution for possession when a medical emergency is being reported in good faith. The specifics vary by state — consult DanceSafe’s state-by-state guide, linked in the Safety Appendix. The law exists. Know it before you need it.

The Discernment Hook

The manipulation in this chapter is upstream of the crisis. By the time Marco’s lips go gray, the Wook is at a different stage and the evidence of the play is gone. This is what makes the Free Cap Funnel harder to name than the Overt Lot Rat or the Covert Facilitator: it looks indistinguishable, in the moment, from genuine generosity. The forty-five minutes was real. The warmth was real. The cap is also real and it is also untested and also the person who gave it to you has no receipts and is not in the porta line with you.

The read is not in the cap. The read is in the test kit that was not run and the name of the substance that was not provided and the conversation that was forty-five minutes of warm and three seconds of logistics.

Here's the read on it. Before accepting any substance from anyone at any stage of familiarity: What is it specifically? Where did it come from? Has it been tested with what kit? What is the expected dose? What is the expected onset? What is the expected duration?

These are not paranoid questions. They are the same questions a pharmacist asks. The festival setting does not change the pharmacology.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Four Questions Before Any Cap. Step 1 — “What is it?” Specific substance name. Not “just molly.” What is it, specifically.

Step 2 — “Has it been tested? With what?” Marquis for MDMA. Fentanyl strips for everything. Both.

Step 3 — “What’s the dose?” Milligrams. Not “it’s light” or “it’s standard.” Numbers.

Step 4 — “What’s your source?” Not suspicious. Just: the information you would want before any medical decision.

If any of these cannot be answered specifically — the cap is not yours tonight. This is not rude. This is the same care you would apply to a pill from a pharmacy.

Quick vibe check. Some people offer genuinely tested, sourced, accurately dosed substances to people they have gotten to know and the whole thing is clean and fine and the forty-five minutes was a real conversation with a real person and the cap is exactly what it was described as. That exists. The four questions still apply, because the four questions are how you know.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever given someone a substance without telling them what it was specifically? Have you ever said “it’s fine, it’s the same stuff” without having tested it yourself? Have you ever been the warm forty-five-minute conversation before the cap you did not know was cut? If yes: the warmth was real. The receipts were also missing. Both were real.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **First-Timer:** The first person who offers you something at your first festival feels like the scene vouching for you. The scene is not vouching for you. One warm person is vouch-ing for you. Run the four questions regardless of how the vouching feels.

**BIPOC Festie:** In scenes where racial dynamics mean you are already navigating who to trust and who might be a plant, the warm-generous-stranger is an especially complex read. The four questions are a color-neutral tool that gives you information regardless of the social dynamics.

**Harm-Reduction Crew:** If you are staffing a DanceSafe or Zendo booth, the Free Cap Funnel is where a significant percentage of your most serious cases originate. When someone comes to you after taking something from a warm stranger, the first question is: “Do you know specifically what it was?” The answer is usually no. Build the response from there.

This runs in every direction. The Free Cap Funnel is run by the shirtless guy with the cooler who’s suddenly your best friend, the camp mom two rows over, the touring DJ’s merch girl, and the sober-looking stranger who bought you water first. Forty-five minutes of warmth, then the bag. Warmth is not gendered. Neither is the absence of a test kit.

## 🎬 VIDEO 4 — Track 02 — THE WHISPER NETWORK LAG
*~901 spoken words · est. 6:00 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 02 — THE WHISPER NETWORK LAG
*(delayed harm-information cascade)*

Here's the behavior. By the time you know the batch is bad, someone has already taken it.

The information travels through the festival at person-to-person speed. The first reports come in from the medical tent around midnight. They go into a group chat. The group chat has four hundred people in it. Some of those four hundred people read the group chat at one a.m. Some of them read it at three a.m. Some of them will read it tomorrow morning over coffee and experience the specific horror of reading “don’t take anything from the guy in the red jacket, medical tent confirmed bad batch” while remembering that they took something from a guy in a red jacket at eleven p.m.

The person who took the cap at eleven p.m. is now in the porta line. The information was in the group chat at midnight. The gap between midnight and eleven p.m. is the Whisper Network Lag.

The Lag is not a moral failure of the network. The network is working as fast as a human social network can work. The Lag is a structural feature of informal harm-information systems operating in real time against the physics of a crowded outdoor event with limited cell service and four hundred people who are not all checking the same group chat at the same time.

The Wook benefits from the Lag. Not because he calculated it, usually — he just moved before the network could. He handed the cap and moved to the next person and the network had not yet received the information that made the cap identifiable as dangerous.

Here's the read on it. The Lag is not something you can eliminate. You can narrow it. The way to narrow it is: test before you take. Every time. Not because the network is unreliable. Because the network is always behind the chemistry.

**The secondary read:** when you are in a crisis situation with someone, the harm information that would help you — what they took, when, from whom, what else — travels through the same slow network. Ask the person next to them. Ask the person who was with them at the stage. Ask the nearest harm-reduction resource. Build the picture from multiple sources simultaneously.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Pre-Event Harm Network Scan. Step 1 — Before the event, join the relevant harm-reduction channels for that specific event: DanceSafe festival pages, event-specific harm-reduction groups, Bunk Police alert networks. Turn on notifications for those specific channels.

**Step 2 — At the event:** know the physical location of the DanceSafe or Bunk Police booth. Not from memory — walk past it at entry and note the location on your phone’s map.

Step 3 — When you hear any report of a bad batch — secondhand, in a group chat, from a friend of a friend — treat it as signal to stop distribution in your immediate network. Text the people you are with.

**Step 4 — If you are in a crisis situation:** ask the nearest harm-reduction resource what they know. They have information before it hits the general group chat because they are in contact with the medical tent in real time.

Quick vibe check. Harm networks in festival communities have gotten better every year for two decades and they save lives. The Lag is real and it is structurally unavoidable with current technology and current social infrastructure. The solution is not more trust in the network. The solution is: test first, network second.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever received harm information about a batch and waited to send it forward because you were not sure it was confirmed? The Lag is partly structural and partly behavioral. Every person in the network who sits on information for twenty minutes is twenty minutes of lag. Send the information. Send it with appropriate uncertainty flagging — “unconfirmed but flagging” — but send it fast.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Camp Leads:** Your camp is a node in the harm network. Build a policy: any batch concern reported to any camp member gets forwarded to the camp lead immediately, who forwards to the event’s harm-reduction team and the camp’s group chat simultaneously. No waiting for confirmation.

**Sober Anchor:** If you are the sober person in your crew, you are the Lag’s best countermeasure. You are on the phone. You are checking the network. You are the one who reads the group chat at midnight instead of at three a.m.

This runs in every direction. The Lag slows down whether the message starts at the medic tent, the Bunk Police table, a camp group chat, or one sober friend who heard something at 2 a.m. It’s a physics problem, not a character problem: the faster the message moves through any of those relays, the smaller the Lag becomes for the next person.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The product recall that is announced on a Thursday and reaches the regional distribution network by Monday. The gap between the finding and the message reaching the end user is a Lag. In product safety, the Lag is the measure of the institution’s commitment. In festival harm reduction, the Lag is the measure of the community’s response time. Both can be shortened.

## 🎬 VIDEO 6 — Track 04 — THE BUDDY WHO BAILED
*~911 spoken words · est. 6:04 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 04 — THE BUDDY WHO BAILED
*(abandonment mid-crisis)*

Here's the behavior. The buddy system is real and it fails in predictable ways.

At the beginning of the night, you and your friend agree to look out for each other. This agreement is sincere. Both of you mean it. The agreement also has an implicit expiration that neither of you named, which is: until something more interesting comes along.

**The something more interesting can be:** a person. A stage. A spontaneous group that formed around a fire. A secret set announced thirty minutes ago. The summit of someone’s emotional availability that you have been waiting for all weekend. A situation that you told yourself would “just be thirty minutes.”

The thirty minutes becomes two hours.

The two hours becomes four a.m.

And somewhere in the intervening time, Marco’s escort to the medical tent became a stranger in a unicorn onesie instead of the friend who said they would be there.

The Buddy Who Bailed is not always a villain. Sometimes they just got lost in the night. Sometimes they themselves got into a situation that needed help. Sometimes the festival is four days long and maintaining continuous buddy-system accountability across four days is harder than it sounds when you are nineteen and the headliner is playing and there is someone you have been talking to all day.

The chapter’s specific context is the Wook-adjacent version: the buddy who bails because the same person who gave Marco the untested cap also, at some earlier point in the evening, made very sure that the buddy was occupied in a different direction. The Skim and the Buddy Who Bailed sometimes run together. The Wook creates the crisis and removes the support infrastructure in the same set of moves.

Here's the read on it. Clock the buddy’s availability. Is your person currently reachable? When did you last check in? What would it take to get to them right now?

If the answer is “I don’t know where they are” — that information is due.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Four-Hour Rule. The four-hour rule is the single most effective harm-prevention protocol in the scene’s informal toolkit, and it is achievable by anyone without training or equipment.

**Every four hours, either:** make physical contact with your buddy, or confirm via text. Not “I’ll text if I need something.” Proactive contact. Both ways. “I’m good, where are you?” Every four hours. No exceptions.

Step 1 — Set the rule explicitly before the event starts, out loud, with your buddy.

Step 2 — Set a physical meeting point that does not require cell service: the big oak by family camp, the southwest corner of the main stage, the water truck. A specific physical location. If your phone is dead, you go there.

**Step 3 — Set the check-in interval:** four hours or shorter. Two hours for higher-stakes settings or higher-dose nights.

Step 4 — Name the code word for extraction. A specific phrase that means “I need you now, come get me, no questions.” Something that sounds normal but is the signal. “Where’s my charger?” “Have you seen my jacket?” Something that can be texted or said without announcing the situation.

**Step 5 — If your buddy misses a check-in:** go looking. Not in twenty minutes. Now.

Quick vibe check. Festivals are large and complicated and losing track of someone for a few hours is not automatically a crisis or a failure of care. The four-hour rule is not about surveillance. It is about maintaining the basic accountability thread that means you know whether your person needs you.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever been the buddy who bailed? Not maliciously — just: got pulled into something and lost track and it was fine but it was also lucky that it was fine? The four-hour rule is not a punishment for past bailing. It is a structure that makes future bailing less likely to land badly.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **First-Timer:** Your first festival, you do not yet have the navigational knowledge to be confident you can find someone again once you lose them. Do not lose them.

**Solo Festie:** If you are attending solo, the four-hour rule applies to your default-world anchor: one person who knows where you are, who you check in with every four hours, and who calls for help if you miss two check-ins. The anchor does not need to be at the festival. They need to have your location and your check-in schedule.

**Large Festival (50,000+):** The four-hour rule becomes the two-hour rule and cell service becomes unreliable enough that the physical meeting point is mandatory, not backup.

This runs in every direction. The Buddy Who Bailed is the best friend who went to find the afters, the hookup who left for a different stage, the camp mate who assumed someone else had it covered, and the person who genuinely thought you’d be fine. Any gender, any configuration, any level of scene experience. The four-hour rule and the extraction code word don’t care which one left.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The hiking partner who goes ahead and loses you on the trail. Not malicious. Just: the agreement to stay together dissolved gradually and nobody named the dissolution. The trail version and the festival version have the same solution: explicit check-in intervals and a specific meeting point.

🔰 SAFETY APPENDIX — CHAPTER 11
- Concrete operational content. Screenshot this. Keep it on your phone.
- THE RECOVERY POSITION
- If someone is unconscious or semi-conscious and breathing:
- 1. Turn them onto their left side.
- 2. Bend the upper knee forward to stabilize — like a running position.
- 3. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open.
- 4. Check that the mouth is clear and nothing is blocking the airway.
- 5. Stay with them. Monitor breathing. Do not leave them on their back.
- The recovery position prevents aspiration — choking on vomit — which is the primary cause of preventable death in unconscious intoxicated individuals.
- WHEN 911 IS NON-NEGOTIABLE
- Loss of consciousness
- Gray, blue, or white lips
- Breathing that is labored, absent, or rattling
- Seizure
- Unresponsive to voice or firm touch
- Extreme temperature (seizure-hot skin, hypothermia-cold)
- Chest pain or inability to breathe
- Significant blood loss
- Do not do the Medical Tent Math when these signs are present. Call. Move.
- GOOD SAMARITAN AMNESTY — KNOW YOUR STATE
- As of 2026, all fifty US states have some form of Good Samaritan law for drug-related medical emergencies. Most protect both the person calling for help and the person in crisis from prosecution for possession when calling in good faith.
- State-specific details: DanceSafe.org/good-samaritan-laws — updated regularly, searchable by state.
- Key points for most states:
- The protection applies when you are calling for medical help, not when you are discovered in possession by law enforcement independently.
- The protection covers the caller and the person in crisis.
- The protection does not cover trafficking or distribution charges.
- You should still consult local legal resources for your specific situation.
- THE FOUR WORDS
- When you reach any medical resource — medic, Zendo volunteer, ranger, harm-reduction worker:
- “We don’t know what.”
- This phrase communicates: the substance is unknown, the dose is unknown, the timeline is uncertain. It gives the medic the correct clinical posture without introducing inaccurate information. It also avoids creating legal exposure through specific assertions about controlled substances.
- Do not say: “She took molly.” Do not say: “He did one cap of MDMA.” Say: “We don’t know what.” And then answer specific questions as accurately as you can.
- TEST KIT BASICS
- Marquis reagent: for MDMA/MDEA/MDA. Purple-black is positive. Orange is amphetamines. No reaction or unexpected color: flag.
- Fentanyl strips: for everything. Dip in water with residue or small dissolved sample. One line = positive for fentanyl. Two lines = negative. Do not take anything that tests positive for fentanyl.
- Mecke reagent: for DXM/opiates/MDMA. Additional confirmation.
- Source: DanceSafe.org, Bunk Police. Bring both to every festival.
- WHERE HARM REDUCTION IS
- Before you go in, locate and physically walk past:
- DanceSafe booth (if present)
- Bunk Police booth (if present)
- Zendo tent / peer support (if present)
- Medical tent
- Rangers / security
- Note locations relative to: main stage, porta line, your camp. Write them in your phone. Not because you expect to need them. Because at three a.m. with eleven percent battery, you will not remember.
- ZENDO PROJECT
- Zendo provides psychedelic peer support — specifically, non-medical, non-law-enforcement crisis support for people having difficult psychological experiences. They do not facilitate arrests. They are not medical staff and do not provide clinical treatment. They do provide: safe space, calm presence, experience, and the specific ability to help someone navigate a difficult altered state without panic or intervention.
