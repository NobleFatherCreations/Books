## 🎬 VIDEO 3 — Track 01 — THE GLITTER FILTER
*~855 spoken words · est. 5:42 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 01 — THE GLITTER FILTER
*(confirmation bias)*

Here's the behavior. You see what supports the story you already want to be true.

If you want the linen man to be a healer, every calm sentence becomes wisdom. If you want the producer to be your big break, every late-night text becomes mentorship. If you want the camp to be safe, every person saying family becomes evidence. If you want the chaos goblin to be misunderstood rather than dangerous, every tiny kindness becomes a documentary about their beautiful soul and every boundary violation becomes they’re just dysregulated, which is a real clinical concept that has been carrying an extraordinary amount of freight recently.

The Glitter Filter doesn’t create the Wook. It bedazzles the Wook. It takes the one good thing — and there is always one good thing, the Wook is never all bad, that is not how Wooks work — and shines it directly into your eyes until you stop seeing the twenty weird things wearing Crocs behind it.

“He checked on me after my bad trip.”

Yes. He also separated you from your crew before it happened.

“She gave me a free pass to the afterparty.”

Yes. She also wants you working three unpaid shifts and defending her in the group chat when the receipts surface.

“They seem so connected and grounded.”

Yes. So is mold.

The filter also runs in the other direction — you filter out the information that doesn’t fit. People who’ve had problems with the Wook seem like they misunderstood. The pattern that your friend named seems like a coincidence. The discount at which you’re selling your own experience seems like open-mindedness.

Here's the read on it. The Glitter Filter’s signature is a single question you are not asking: What evidence would change my mind?

If you cannot answer that — if there is no information that would shift your read — you are not evaluating. You are defending. The filter is running.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Opposing Brief. A marketing director wrote this one. It works.

Step 1 — Write down what you currently believe about the person or situation. Two or three sentences. Actual sentences, not vibe.

Step 2 — Write the opposing brief. Same length. Pretend you are a lawyer arguing the opposite case. What is the evidence against your current read? What would a skeptic say? What would your friend who doesn’t know this person say if you showed them the receipts cold?

**Step 3 — Ask:** which brief has more behavioral evidence, and which has more feeling?

Step 4 — Act on the behavioral evidence. Not the feeling. Not yet.

Step 5 — Ask the question you’ve been not asking: “What would change my mind?” Write the answer. If the answer is “nothing” — that is the Glitter Filter, confirmed, glittering.

Quick vibe check. Sometimes a person is genuinely good and your positive read is accurate and the friend who is worried about them is operating from their own stuff. This happens. The test is not have you ever been right about a person who others questioned. The test is: are you answering on behavioral evidence or on emotional need.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever been the Wook someone was running the Glitter Filter on? Have you ever noticed that someone was explaining away something you did — and let them? The filter runs on both ends. It is a gift you accept when you should say: “No, actually, here is the accurate version.”

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **ND Festie:** Pattern recognition that diverges from neurotypical consensus is not always the Glitter Filter — sometimes you are correctly perceiving something others are missing. The difference: your read is based on specific behavioral data, not on emotional investment in the outcome.

**Recovery Lane:** Wanting to believe in someone’s recovery can activate the Glitter Filter at high intensity. One sober month is real. It is also not a credential that removes the behavioral history. Both things.

**Camp Leads:** The filter runs on camps as well as people. “Our camp doesn’t do that” is a Glitter Filter applied to a collective. Behavior is behavior regardless of the logo on the shade structure.

This runs in every direction. The Glitter Filter is equally available on the beloved femme founder who has done too much for you to question, the male camp elder whose history is load-bearing for too many people, the queer community organizer whose work is real and whose conduct is a separate question, and the sober mentor whose clean time has been converted into unchallengeability. The glitter does not have a gender. It does have a function: it makes the hard question feel like a betrayal.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The colleague who has been so genuinely helpful in two situations that you cannot bring yourself to notice they’ve been quietly wrong about six others. The filter is running. The helpful things were real. The wrong things were also real. Both facts are in the same building.

## 🎬 VIDEO 4 — Track 02 — THE FIRST PRICE IS THE SPELL
*~700 spoken words · est. 4:40 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 02 — THE FIRST PRICE IS THE SPELL
*(anchoring bias)*

Here's the behavior. The first number you hear becomes the reference point for every number that follows. The first story you hear becomes the template against which you evaluate every story that follows. Solstice mentioned $2,400 when she poured tea — not pushed it, not pitched it, just noted it conversationally in the context of “it fills fast” — and from that moment, $2,400 is the established fact. Not a lot for a week in Tulum with curated retreat programming. Not nothing. But the reference point has been set and the evaluation happens relative to the reference point, not relative to what is this actually worth to me, specifically, in my specific financial life, with my specific prior knowledge of this organization’s track record.

Anchoring runs forward and backward. It runs forward on prices, timelines, and agreements: the first offer seems reasonable and the counter seems rude. It runs backward on stories: I’ve heard this person is amazing means every interaction with them gets evaluated through that frame.

The Wook who sets the anchor first wins. The Wook who gets someone else to set the anchor for them wins even better, because the anchor appears to come from neutral ground.

Here's the read on it. **Ask:** Who set that number? Who told me that story first? Why was that the first thing I heard? First information is not automatically wrong. But it is always worth asking whose interest is served by it being the first thing you know.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Reset Price. Step 1 — When you notice you’re evaluating relative to an anchor: pause. Write the anchor down.

Step 2 — Imagine you’ve never heard that number or story. What would you pay? What would you believe? What would you require to feel safe?

Step 3 — Compare the reset to the anchor. The gap between them is how much the anchor is doing.

Step 4 — Research the reference class. Not is $2,400 reasonable for a retreat, but what has this specific organization delivered to previous participants, and what did they say about the value?

Quick vibe check. First prices are not automatically wrong. Some things are appropriately priced and the anchor is accurate. The test is not did I hear this first. The test is: am I evaluating it on its own merits or only relative to what I was told first.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever set the anchor in a negotiation, a story, or an intro deliberately — framing yourself or the situation so that the other person’s evaluation would start from the frame you wanted? That is anchoring as a tool. Notice where you aim it.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Vendor/Artist:** Festival economics are built on anchoring. “Exposure” as payment was an anchor set long ago that has calcified into an industry standard in spaces where it was never actually standard. Your labor has a real price. Reset it from your costs, not from what they say it’s worth.

**First-Timer:** You do not yet have a reference class. Everything you hear first about the scene will function as an anchor. Build the reference class deliberately — talk to people who have been around for years, across multiple scenes, before you let one person’s framing become your map.

This runs in every direction. The anchor is set by whoever speaks first, loudest, or with the most apparent authority — regardless of gender, tenure, or platform. The femme retreat host who names the price casually over tea. The male booking agent who establishes “this is how splits work in this scene.” The nonbinary elder who says “that’s just how this camp operates.” Whoever sets the first frame owns the subsequent evaluation.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. “Our standard rate for this kind of work is X.” Who decided X was standard? When? Based on what? The first person to name a price in a negotiation statistically gets a better outcome. Notice who names first and ask if they’ve earned the right to set the reference.

## 🎬 VIDEO 5 — Track 03 — THE HALO IN THE PASHMINA
*~651 spoken words · est. 4:20 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 03 — THE HALO IN THE PASHMINA
*(halo effect)*

Here's the behavior. One genuinely good thing makes everything else seem good. Or safe. Or true.

He’s a brilliant sound engineer, therefore he’s probably safe. She runs the cleanest harm-reduction tent at the regional, therefore her judgment about everything is reliable. They make extraordinary art, therefore their conduct is probably also extraordinary, or at least forgivable, or at least complicated enough that simple judgment is unfair.

The halo effect is the brain’s efficiency play: assess one trait well and extrapolate it across all traits, because full individual assessment of every attribute is expensive and you have a bass stage to get to.

River is present and warm and has good eyes. Therefore his aura read is probably accurate. Therefore the thing he is connecting to the throat chakra is probably real. Therefore the Tulum thing being framed through the throat chakra is probably a genuine alignment. The halo of River’s warmth has spread to everything River has touched in the conversation.

The Wook knows this, or has learned it empirically: if they trust the thing they can see, they will trust the things they can’t.

Here's the read on it. **Ask:** Does this specific trait actually reduce this specific risk? Being a good artist doesn’t mean being a safe housing provider. Being a competent harm-reduction volunteer doesn’t mean being reliable with money. Having extraordinary stage presence doesn’t mean being honest in private. The halo is an inference. Ask for the evidence on the specific thing.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Trait Audit. Step 1 — Name the halo. What is the one good thing that is illuminating everything else?

Step 2 — List the specific thing you are now trusting or evaluating differently because of the halo.

**Step 3 — Ask:** does excellence at [halo thing] actually predict [specific thing]? Is there a mechanistic reason they would be related, or am I just assuming?

Step 4 — Require evidence on the specific thing, independent of the halo.

Quick vibe check. People can be genuinely excellent at one thing and genuinely trustworthy across many things. The halo is not always wrong. The issue is using the halo as a substitute for evidence rather than as a starting hypothesis that still requires data.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever relied on your reputation in one domain to deflect scrutiny in another? Have you ever presented your scene contributions as character evidence in an accountability conversation? “After everything I’ve built” is the halo running in reverse — using the good thing to shield the specific thing from examination.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Disabled Festie:** Activists and advocates often build halos through their public work that become protections for private conduct. The halo of someone’s advocacy work does not make their behavior toward you safe. Both things can be simultaneously true without contradiction.

**Queer Scene:** Queer organizing and queer art carry real halos in queer spaces. That is right and earned. It does not extend to every other domain of the person’s behavior. The halo of someone’s queerness is not conduct insurance.

This runs in every direction. The halo is available on the celebrated femme artist, the male sound engineer legend, the nonbinary healer with the three-year waitlist, the queer cultural organizer whose work has mattered to thousands, the sober elder whose recovery story is real and load-bearing. Excellent things are done by people who are also sometimes doing other, worse things. Both are real. The halo is the brain’s attempt to make them exclusive. They aren’t.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. “She’s brilliant at her job” as a response to “she keeps taking credit for other people’s work in meetings.” Brilliant at the job. Also doing the other thing. Both real. The halo does not eliminate the receipts.

The deal that expires tonight. All of this is the Wook scheduling their pitch to coincide with peak Now Goblin activity.

Here's the read on it. **The Now Goblin’s signature:** urgency that comes from inside rather than from the situation. You feel like you have to decide now. The decision does not actually require now. ## 🎬 VIDEO 7 — Track 05 — THE SUNK COST SWAMP
*~742 spoken words · est. 4:57 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 05 — THE SUNK COST SWAMP
*(sunk cost fallacy)*

Here's the behavior. You have already paid — in money, in time, in emotional investment, in identity, in relationships built inside the thing — and the amount you have already paid is being used as a reason to keep paying, even when the evidence clearly says the exit is the right move.

I already drove three hours. (Now you’re in a van going somewhere you shouldn’t go.)

I already paid for the retreat. (Now you’re at the retreat defending the retreat.)

I’ve been part of this camp for six years. (Now you’re explaining away conduct that, in any other context, you would name immediately.)

I’ve already invested so much in this relationship. (Now you’re tolerating things you would not tolerate on day one.)

The swamp works because leaving costs more than it did before — but continuing also costs more than it did before. The sunk cost isn’t a reason to continue. It is a weight that makes the exit harder to see. The Wook benefits from you staying in the swamp. Every year of your sunk cost is another year of leverage on your silence.

Here's the read on it. **The Sunk Cost Swamp’s signature:** “I’ve come this far” as a reason. Ask: “Does how far I’ve come change what is true right now?” It doesn’t. The past investment is not recoverable regardless of what happens next. The only question is what the next step costs and what it returns. That question has nothing to do with what came before.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Forward-Only Ledger. Step 1 — Write two columns. Left column: what will this cost me going forward if I stay? Right column: what will this cost me going forward if I leave?

Step 2 — Do not write what you have already paid in either column. It is not relevant to either option. It happened. It is not changeable. Close that tab.

Step 3 — Make the decision based only on what the next step costs and what it returns. Nothing else.

Step 4 — If you find you cannot stop writing the history in the forward ledger — if you keep returning to but I’ve already— — you are in the swamp. Name it. “I’m in the Sunk Cost Swamp.” The naming doesn’t get you out automatically. But it stops the swamp from pretending to be reasoning.

Quick vibe check. Continuity, commitment, and loyalty are virtues. Not everything that is hard to leave should be left. The test is: are you staying because the forward ledger says stay, or because the backward ledger is too heavy to lift?

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever held on to a situation you knew was wrong because the exit would mean admitting the investment was wasted? Have you ever pressured someone else to stay by reminding them of what they had already put in? “But after everything you’ve built here” is a sunk cost argument used as a trap.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **BIPOC Festie:** In predominantly white scenes, BIPOC artists and organizers sometimes absorb enormous sunk costs of labor and community-building before the culture of the space reveals itself as extractive. The swamp is particularly deep here because the investment was real and the community was real and the harm is also real. All three are true simultaneously.

**Artist:** The career swamp is specific and vicious. Years of your best work in a relationship, on a label, in a management structure that is costing you more than it returns. The sunk cost is your actual artistic life. The forward ledger still has to be run.

This runs in every direction. The Sunk Cost Swamp is exploited by the camp founder who reminds you of your history there, the long-term partner who references everything you’ve built, the management structure that lists all it has done for you before making the next ask, and the community that says “you’re one of us, after all this time.” The swamp is timeless, genderless, and has particularly good retention rates.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. “I’ve been here for eight years, I can’t just—” Yes you can. The eight years were the eight years. What happens in year nine is a separate question. The swamp does not actually connect them. It just feels like it does.
