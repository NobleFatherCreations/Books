🎛️ THE TAPERS’ SECTION

Harm Reduction · Supply Chain Accountability · Moral Licensing

Quick theory. Then the practical content.

Supply chain accountability is the principle that everyone in the distribution chain of a substance bears partial accountability for the harm downstream. The source, the mid-level, the person who fronts a few for friends — all of them are nodes in the chain, and the chain produces outcomes at the end of it regardless of how the nodes feel about their role. This is not a legal analysis. It is a harm-reduction framework. In harm-reduction terms: you are accountable for what you distribute, regardless of what you call yourself.

Moral licensing is the psychological phenomenon where one positive self-perception licenses a subsequent failure of care. I am generous, therefore my product is good. I have been doing this for two years without incident, therefore this instance will also be without incident. The good history becomes the permission slip for the next decision. The license is not valid. The license is also very convincing to the person holding it.

The test-kit imperative is not a suggestion in harm-reduction practice. It is the baseline. No batch, no matter how solid the source, no matter how long the relationship, no matter how inconvenient the moment, is safe to distribute without testing. Not because sources lie — because chemistry is unpredictable, because the supply chain above your source has unknown links, because fentanyl contamination has reached substrates that were previously considered clean. Because the test takes three minutes. Because the girl in the Santa hat takes the rest of your life.

### TRACK 01 — THE NOT-REALLY-A-DEALER
*(identity laundering / role distancing)*

Here's the behavior. The internal narrative requires a specific self-image to function. The self-image is: I am not a dealer. I am a guy who helps out friends. I am a logistics person. I am a generous person with connections who happens to be able to bring good stuff at a fair price to people I care about.

The self-image functions as a permission structure. A dealer has responsibilities — to test, to disclose, to be accountable for the downstream effects of their distribution. A guy who helps out friends does not carry those responsibilities, because the relationship is defined as friendship and friendship does not have a liability protocol.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the fiction that the social definition of the transaction changes its legal and ethical content. It does not. Distributing a substance for profit — even at forty dollars, even to friends, even with a two-year source relationship — is distribution. The legal definition does not care about the friendship framing. The harm-reduction accountability does not care about the friendship framing. The girl in the medical tent does not care about the friendship framing.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the first Track because it is the one that makes all the other Tracks possible. If you are a dealer, you test. If you are just helping out friends, you skip the line.

Here's the read on it. **The question is not:** “Am I a dealer?” The question is: “Am I distributing a substance for profit or access?” If the answer is yes — the responsibilities of distribution apply regardless of the relational framing.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Role Audit. Step 1 — Name the transaction accurately. Not “helping out” or “bringing some for friends.” What is the transaction?

I am acquiring a substance from a source.

I am transporting it.

I am distributing it to people.

I am charging money.

**Step 2 — Ask:** what are the responsibilities of someone who does those four things? Testing. Accurate disclosure about contents. Capacity to address harms downstream. Those are the responsibilities. They apply regardless of what the relationship is called.

Step 3 — Decide whether you are willing to take those responsibilities seriously. If yes: run the protocols. If no: stop distributing.

### TRACK 02 — THE MY GUY IS SOLID
*(trust transitivity error / chain-of-trust fallacy)*

Here's the behavior. Your source is solid. You have two years of evidence. Nothing bad has happened in two years. Therefore: the current batch is solid.

This is the trust transitivity error. It treats the source’s historical track record as evidence about the current batch, when the current batch is a separate object with its own contents that your source’s history cannot speak to. Your source’s two-year record is real data about your source’s two-year period. It is not data about the batch you received on Thursday.

**The reason it is not data:** your source has a supply chain. Your source’s supply chain has unknown links above it. The chemistry of a batch can change based on factors that have nothing to do with your source’s intentions or character. Fentanyl contamination has been documented in MDMA, cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin, counterfeit pills, and multiple other substances that were previously considered clean. The contamination often comes from above the source in the chain — from the synthesis, the cutting, the pressing — and it is invisible to your source unless your source tests, which your source may not do for the same reasons you don’t: because the source’s source has been solid, and the chain of trust extends upward without interruption until the batch provides the interruption.

The My Guy Is Solid is not evidence about the batch. It is evidence about your relationship. Both are real. One of them is useful for predicting today’s product. That one is the test.

Here's the read on it. The test. Every time. Not sometimes. Not when you are suspicious. Not when you have a reason to doubt your source. Every time. Because the absence of a reason to doubt is not evidence that nothing is wrong — it is the default state that the test is supposed to update from.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Chain Audit. **Step 1 — Ask your source, every transaction:** “Have you tested this batch?” Not rhetorically. Literally: what kit, what result?

Step 2 — Verify with your own kit regardless of what they say. Not because you distrust them. Because the chain above them is unknown to both of you.

**Step 3 — Marquis for MDMA:** purple-black is correct. Yellow, orange, or no reaction: do not distribute.

**Step 4 — Fentanyl strip:** always. On everything. Regardless of substrate. The positive result is one line. One line: destroy the batch. Do not distribute a single unit of a batch that tests positive for fentanyl.

Step 5 — Document the test. Photo of the result. Timestamped. In your camera roll. So that “I didn’t know” can also be “and here is the test I ran.”

Quick vibe check. Good sources exist. Long-term reliable supply relationships exist. Trust, built through evidence over time, is real and useful. None of that replaces the test. The test is what turns trust into information.

### TRACK 03 — THE SKIPPED TEST
*(availability heuristic override of risk assessment)*

Here's the behavior. The Bunk Police line was twenty people. Twenty minutes.

He had thirty caps to move before sunset.

The availability heuristic is the cognitive shortcut that treats the ease of imagining a bad outcome as a measure of its probability. The bad outcome — someone getting hurt from an untested batch — was not easy to imagine in Thursday’s context, because the context was busy and good and his source had been solid for two years and twenty minutes in a line is a real cost against a hypothetical risk.

The hypothetical risk was not hypothetical.

The hypothetical risk had a name and a Santa hat and a mother in Fremont who was going to receive a phone call.

The Skipped Test is not a single decision. It is the accumulation of smaller decisions — the line was long, the moment was busy, I’ll do it later, it’s probably fine, my source is solid — each of which is individually defensible in the moment and collectively produced the decision to distribute an untested substance to people who believed it was clean.

The Skipped Test is the chapter’s central safety content. Not because testing is complicated — because it is not, it takes three minutes, the kits are available, the protocol is simple. But because the decision to skip it is made under exactly the conditions that make every other shortcut in this book appealing: momentum, social pressure, the window, the belief that history predicts the future.

Here's the read on it. One rule. No exceptions.

Test every batch you distribute. Every time. Before you distribute. Not after. Not after a few units have gone out and nothing bad has happened yet. Before.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Three-Minute Gate. Step 1 — Before distributing any batch, test it. Marquis and fentanyl strip, minimum. Both.

**Step 2 — The three-minute gate is this:** if you do not have three minutes to run the test, you do not have the time to distribute safely. Stop. Wait. Come back when you have three minutes.

Step 3 — The window does not outrank the test. The social pressure does not outrank the test. The line being long does not outrank the test. The source being solid does not outrank the test. Nothing outranks the test.

**Step 4 — If you cannot test:** do not distribute. This is the only rule in the chapter that has no exceptions or qualifications.

Step 5 — Carry the kits. At every festival, in your own kit, separate from any supply. Marquis. Mecke. Simons. Fentanyl strips. They cost less than the ticket. They cost less than the phone call.

Quick vibe check. Testing does not prevent all harm. A fentanyl-negative result does not mean the substance contains exactly what is labeled. Unknown adulterants, dosing errors, and individual tolerance variations can all produce harm in tested substances. The test is necessary. It is not sufficient. What it is: the minimum accountability threshold for anyone distributing anything to anyone.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever distributed something without testing it? Have you ever told yourself you would test it “later” in a way that meant you would test it after the window for the test to be useful had closed? This runs in every direction. The Skipped Test gets skipped by the touring vet who’s done this a hundred times, the first-timer who doesn’t own a kit, the camp lead in a hurry before doors, and the distributor who’s never once had a bad batch. ## 🎬 VIDEO 6 — Track 04 — THE SCREENSHOT DROP
*~858 spoken words · est. 5:43 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 04 — THE SCREENSHOT DROP
*(reputation cascade and the no-recall problem)*

Here's the behavior. His name is in a screenshot.

The screenshot is in a group chat of eighty people. Each of those eighty people is in other group chats. The screenshot will be in those group chats within the hour. The content of the screenshot is his name and the information that his batch is involved in a medical emergency. By the time he sees the screenshot, the screenshot is already downstream of the original send. By the time he finishes reading the screenshot, his name is probably in forty more group chats.

He cannot recall the screenshot.

This is the no-recall problem. Information distributed through informal social networks cannot be centrally removed. Every attempt to delete it from one location does not remove it from the locations it has already reached. Every send multiplies the locations it has reached. The cascade is exponential and it is irreversible and the only variables that were available to affect it were the variables that existed before the batch was distributed.

The Screenshot Drop is not a punishment imposed by the scene. It is a function of how information travels in peer networks. It is also, in this specific instance, the correct function. The information “don’t take anything from [name], the batch is involved in a medical emergency” is harm-reduction information of the highest urgency. The people sending the screenshot are doing the right thing. The problem is that they are doing the right thing with his name in it, and his name is there because he did not do the right thing first.

Here's the read on it. The Screenshot Drop is not something you can manage after the fact. It can only be prevented by the test that was not run. But it can be partially addressed by the recall play — the set of actions available in the first ten minutes after the information that the batch is bad.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Recall Play. If you have distributed a batch and receive information that the batch is causing harm:

Step 1 — Contact every buyer immediately. Every single one. “Stop. Don’t take anything you got from me. There may be something wrong with the batch.” Text, call, find in person. Every one. Within ten minutes.

Step 2 — Stop distribution immediately. Cap the container. Do not sell or give another unit.

Step 3 — Contact the medical tent or harm-reduction team directly. Before they find you. This is critical. Before they find you. “I think I may have distributed something that is causing harm. I want to help. What do you need from me?” This call, made proactively, before the Rangers arrive at the tent, is the difference between cooperation and apprehension.

Step 4 — Destroy the remaining supply. Dispose of it in a way that cannot be recovered.

Step 5 — Give the medical team everything you know. Your source. The form of the substance. Where it was distributed. When. To whom. All of it. “We’re not cops. We just need to know what was in it” is the sentence you are going to hear. Answer it completely. The girl in the medical tent is more important than your source’s identity.

Quick vibe check. The recall play does not undo the harm. There is no counter-drop that undoes the harm. What the recall play does is limit the additional harm — the caps that have not yet been taken, the people who have not yet received the information, the medical team that does not yet know what they are treating.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever received information that something you distributed was causing harm and not immediately acted on it? Have you ever waited — for more information, for certainty, for the situation to clarify — while the clock on the recall window was running? The ten minutes is not approximate. The ten minutes is the interval between the batch’s first harm and the point at which the additional harm is preventable.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Harm-Reduction Volunteers:** When someone contacts you proactively with the recall information, that person deserves protection, not prosecution. Build a culture where the proactive call is possible and rewarded. The call saves lives. The fear of consequences for making the call costs lives.

This runs in every direction. - Procedure:
- 1. Dissolve a small amount of the substance in water (approximately one-quarter teaspoon per cap or equivalent).
- 2. Dip the strip in the water for fifteen seconds.
- 3. Lay flat and read at two to five minutes.
