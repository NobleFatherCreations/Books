## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

It is Sunday at two-twelve p.m. and the tent is down and the car is packed and the wristband is still on her left wrist and Ines is driving home on a four-lane highway with four days of accumulated experience sitting in her chest like something that has not yet found its container.

The drive is seven hours.

The car is quieter than the drive up. Everyone is running the specific exhaustion of four days at a festival: not sick, not broken, just emptied and refilled with something they haven’t sorted yet. Her friend Cam is asleep in the passenger seat with the specific peace of someone who has been continuously present for four days and has finally, mercifully, run out of things to be present for. Her friend Ro is in the back with headphones on, looking out the window at the flat agricultural corridor that exists between the festival and the city, which has the specific visual flatness of a world that does not know it is being returned to.

Ines is awake because she is driving. She is also awake in the way the festival’s ending produces — hyper-present, emotionally near the surface, still partly inside the thing, the gate not quite closed, the world not quite yet the world that Tuesday will be.

Her phone has been receiving messages since ten a.m.

Some of them are from people she met at the festival. One from the woman who shared her emergency electrolyte supply on Thursday, warm and specific and real in the way of connections forged at three in the morning near a water station. One from the DJ she photographed on Friday — a short thank you, a mutual follow, nothing more, which is exactly what it should be. Two from Jonah, who she danced with on Saturday and who has now sent four messages in six hours and whose fourth message ends with a specific question she is not ready to answer on a highway at two-twelve p.m. with Cam asleep and Ro in the headphones.

And one from Damian.

Damian is not someone she met at the festival. Damian is someone she has known for three years — a person in her extended festival community, warm and intermittently present, who has been peripherally available for most of that time in the way that certain people become peripheral presences in a social network. He did not text during the festival. Not once, across four days, did he text.

He texts at two-twelve p.m. on the drive home.

“watched your stories all weekend. you looked free out there. been thinking about you.”

She reads the message.

The message lands in her chest in the specific way that the drive home produces — everything landing with equal and probably disproportionate weight, the festival’s warmth still present and available as an emotional amplifier, the normal Tuesday-level assessment of what this text means or does not mean temporarily unavailable.

Her thumb starts composing a reply.

She puts the phone face-down.

She drives.

She does not know why she put the phone face-down. She does not have a name for what she does in that moment. She has, somewhere in the accumulated knowledge of four years and four festivals, a sense that the drive home is a specific kind of moment and that the specific kind of moment requires a specific kind of caution, but the sense is not articulated and it is not named and without a name it lives in the pre-verbal register where things can be overridden by the next warm text.

At the rest stop — the one with the Carl’s Jr. and the crow who has decided that this parking lot is his kingdom and that he is being magnanimous in allowing the humans access — she reads the message again.

She reads it at the rest stop because the rest stop is more Tuesday than the highway and the text lands differently there. At the rest stop she can feel, faintly, the gap between how the text landed on the highway and how it is landing now, between the first reading and the second, between the person she is at two-twelve p.m. with the wristband still on and the person she will be on Wednesday morning with a cup of coffee and a clear horizon.

She does not text back.

She gets back in the car.

She drives the rest of the way.

On Monday night, she opens a new note in her phone. She titles it “festival stuff” because she is the kind of person who keeps her notes vague enough to not be embarrassed by them if someone else sees her phone. She types:

Damian texted on the drive home. It felt like something. Do I want to see where this goes?

**She adds:** Answer this Tuesday.

She puts the phone on the charger.

She sleeps for ten hours.

On Tuesday she opens the note.

She reads it.

She thinks about Damian on Tuesday — not on the highway at two-twelve p.m. with the festival still in her body, not at the rest stop next to the Carl’s Jr., but on Tuesday, in her apartment, with coffee, in her own clothes, with the full Tuesday-level cognitive resources that the highway and the rest stop and the drive home did not provide.

The feeling is still there. Smaller. In the correct proportion.

**She texts Damian on Tuesday:** “How are you? Good to hear from you.”

They make plans for Thursday.

She meets him on Thursday. He is warm. He is the same person the text described. He is also, on Thursday, someone she can evaluate at full capacity — the version of her who has all her edges back, who can feel warmth without it being amplified by four days of festival warmth still present in her system as a standing emotional tide.

It goes okay. Not extraordinary. Okay. Which is the correct proportion for someone she barely knows, and a proportion she could not have accessed at two-twelve p.m. on the highway, and a proportion that protects her from a commitment the highway would have made and Thursday would have had to undo.

She keeps the wristband on for six more days.

She takes it off on the following Sunday, in her apartment, with Cam over for dinner. They order Thai food and she cuts the wristband off with kitchen scissors and she puts it in the small wooden box where the other wristbands are.

The box has eleven wristbands in it now.

She has not thought about Damian while looking at them.

That is the correct proportion.

Here is what the chapter needs to say about the window:

The window is thirty-six hours, give or take. It begins when the tent comes down and ends somewhere on Tuesday when the default world has fully reestablished its claims on your nervous system. In those thirty-six hours, everything is slightly amplified: the emotional register is higher than baseline, the cost of the no is heavier than baseline, the warmth of a received message is more available than baseline, and the decision-making capacity that the festival spent four days running on reduced cognitive resources is not yet back to its full Tuesday specifications.

The window is not a malfunction. It is the aftermath of four days of genuine peak experience, still in the body, still metabolizing, still producing the specific openness that the festival is designed to produce.

The problem is that the window is visible from the outside. The person who timed their contact for the drive home — who did not text during the festival, who waited for the corridor between the festival’s end and Tuesday — knows what the window feels like. They know because they have felt it themselves. They know because it produced something for them before. They know because the timing was not random.

The chapter names the window so the window is not a secret.
