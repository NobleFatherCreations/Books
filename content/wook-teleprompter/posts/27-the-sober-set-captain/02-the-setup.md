## The Setup

Here's the soundboard quote for this chapter. “I’ve been running this briefing for eleven years. In eleven years, my camp has had zero missing-friend situations, zero medical-emergency escalations that weren’t handled within six minutes, zero gate incidents involving my crew, and zero situations where someone woke up the next morning not knowing what happened. This is not luck. This is a printed sheet and a clipboard and the fact that nobody gets a wristband next year if they miss it. The magic doesn’t protect itself. You protect it. The briefing is how you protect it.” — Sister Lou Mantilla, fifty-eight, twenty-three years in recovery, eleven years running the Wharf Rats meet at three regionals, founding crew at two burns, and the person you want to be standing next to when something goes wrong, because if Sister Lou is there, something going wrong immediately transforms into something being handled. Has been described by five separate people as “the closest thing the scene has to a sworn officer of public safety” and has, every single time, corrected them: “I’m not an officer. I’m a person with a clipboard and eleven years of practice and zero patience for the idea that any of this is optional.” Recorded twenty minutes before doors at a regional in Northern California, clicking a pen, watching her crew disperse.

Here's what this chapter is actually about. This chapter does not have a Wook.

This chapter is the counter-architecture to every Wook in the book. It is the Encore’s first movement: the turn from what the scene needs protecting from to what the scene looks like when it protects itself. Five protocols. Not Tracks — protocols. This chapter does not name moves to watch for. It names the infrastructure that makes the moves impossible to run, or at least significantly more expensive. The 3-Tier Anchor System, the Pre-Show Crew Briefing, the Code-Word Protocol, the Anchor’s Bill of Rights, and the Anchor Backup Plan. Together, these five things are the boring meeting that prevents nine chapters.

The boring meeting is the chapter.

🎛️ THE TAPERS’ SECTION

Prevention Science · Systems Thinking · The Boring Meeting Principle

One paragraph each.

Prevention science is the public health framework that says: the most effective interventions are the ones that happen before the problem, not in response to it. Vaccines, not treatments. Seatbelts, not crash reconstruction. The Sister Lou briefing, not the four-hour search. Prevention is invisible when it works. It is only visible when it doesn’t, which is why it consistently underfunded in every domain that runs on it, including the one where the porta-potties are.

**Systems thinking says:** a system performs as well as its weakest single point of failure. One anchor who is not briefed is the entire system’s vulnerability. One crew member who doesn’t know the meet-up spot is the missing-Jess scenario waiting to happen. The briefing is what distributes the load so that no single person’s distraction, departure, or dead phone collapses the whole thing.

The Boring Meeting Principle is not a named theory but should be: the meetings that prevent the most catastrophic outcomes are the ones that feel, before any catastrophe occurs, the most like a waste of time. The fifteen-minute briefing feels like a waste of time at seven fifty-nine p.m. twenty minutes before doors when the music is about to start and everyone is excited and Sister Lou is clicking her pen. The four-hour missing-Jess search, the porta-line with gray lips, the beige RV in row 14 — those do not feel like a waste of time. They feel like exactly what they are. The boring meeting is cheaper.

You are in.

PROTOCOL 1 — THE 3-TIER ANCHOR SYSTEM

*(Inner / Outer / Default-World)*

The Architecture

Anchoring runs at three levels simultaneously. Most people run it at zero.

**Inner Anchor:** The person in your immediate crew who is closest to you and knows your current state, your dose plan, your location, and your code word. This is not your best friend in a general sense. This is the person who has been specifically briefed, specifically assigned, and who has agreed to the specific operational definition of the role. The Inner Anchor checks in every four hours at minimum. At higher-dose nights or larger festivals: every two hours.

**Outer Anchor:** The person in adjacent camp or social orbit who knows you are at the event and has the Inner Anchor’s contact information. The Outer Anchor does not need to know your dose plan. They need to know your name, your legal name, your Inner Anchor’s number, and the meet-up spot. If the Inner Anchor goes dark — which happens, humans are humans and phones die and the bass stage is very loud — the Outer Anchor is the backup node.

**Default-World Anchor:** One person not at the festival who knows where you are, who you are with (full legal name of at least the Inner Anchor), and at what time they should call for help if they have not heard from you. The Default-World Anchor does not need to be a crisis resource. They need to be a person with a functioning phone in a different time zone who will notice when the check-in does not arrive.

Why Three Tiers

Single-point-of-failure systems fail at the single point. The person who is your only anchor will, at some point, be at the bass stage when you need them, or will have a dead battery, or will have been pulled into a forty-five-minute conversation by someone with Tyler’s name in their mouth. The three-tier system is engineered to survive one tier failing. It cannot survive all three failing simultaneously, but the probability of all three failing simultaneously is orders of magnitude lower than the probability of one tier failing, which is nearly certain across a multi-day event.

Building It Before The Event

Day of the event, sober, before you leave the car:

Name the Inner Anchor aloud. The Inner Anchor says: “I’m the Inner Anchor.” Everyone hears it.

Name the Outer Anchor. Introductions are made if they do not already know each other.

Contact the Default-World Anchor. Text: “At [festival], [Inner Anchor’s full name] has my phone. Check-in every twelve hours. If no check-in for twenty-four hours, call [Inner Anchor’s number].”

That is the three-tier system built. It takes four minutes. It has never produced a four-hour search.

The Anchor Is A Job

**This cannot be said clearly enough:** the anchor is a job, not a hangout buddy who happens to be nearby. The job has specific requirements:

The anchor does not dose above mild-moderate during active anchor duty without handing off the role to a backup.

The anchor does not move to a different location without informing the person they are anchoring.

The anchor does not engage in conversations or activities that would prevent them from receiving the code word.

The anchor does not tell someone their person is “fine” without having verified that the person is fine.

The anchor stays close enough to be found.

The anchor is allowed to have a good time. The anchor is not allowed to disappear.

🔍 THE TEST

**One question, before every event:** “If my Inner Anchor’s phone died right now and they walked away from the music to find me, could they?”

**If the answer is yes:** the system is working.

**If the answer is maybe:** run the backup protocol.

**If the answer is no:** rebuild the system before the gate opens.

PROTOCOL 2 — THE PRE-SHOW CREW BRIEFING

*(The Sister Lou Template)*

The Eight Beats

This is the complete briefing. It takes fifteen minutes. It has been refined over eleven years. Nothing is optional.

Beat 1 — Government Names (2 minutes)

Around the group. Legal name. Person on the left repeats it back. No playa names in this beat. The medical tent doesn’t know your playa name. The county sheriff doesn’t know your playa name. The ICE contact form does not have a playa name field.

Beat 2 — ICE Contacts (3 minutes)

Phones out. ICE-FIRST and ICE-SECOND entered. Lock screen medical ID set. Two emergency contacts per person minimum. One must be reachable by call, not just text. Sister Lou watches until all phones are put away. This is not paranoia. This is the thing that turns “I think it’s Jess Something, maybe starts with C” into “Her name is Jess Okonkwo, her mother’s name is Marie, her number is in my phone right now.”

Beat 3 — The Physical Meet-Up Spot (1 minute)

One location. Described specifically. Visible from multiple points of the festival without requiring a map app. The backup for every dead phone scenario. Every crew member confirms they can picture it.

Beat 4 — The Interval (30 seconds)

Four hours, or two for high-stakes nights. Named. Agreed to. Specific time anchors rather than “whenever.” Specific means accountable.

Beat 5 — The Code Word (30 seconds)

One phrase. Says one thing: come get me, no questions. Selected from things nobody says accidentally. “Where’s my charger” if you never lose your charger. “Has anyone seen my sunglasses” if you wear contacts. “I need to find the restrooms” — never, because everyone needs restrooms and the word will activate unnecessarily at least twelve times per evening.

Beat 6 — The Four-Hour Escalation (1 minute)

Medical tent, then lost and found, then festival safety. In that order. With legal name and wristband number ready. The crew knows the sequence before they need the sequence, not during.

Beat 7 — Wristband Photos (2 minutes)

Every crew member photographs their wristband. Photos go to the crew group chat. The chat confirms receipt. This is the thirty seconds that turns the festival safety desk interaction from “we can’t find anyone by that description” into “yes, this wristband number matches our record for this person.”

Beat 8 — Buddy Assignment (2 minutes)

Named. Aloud. Explicit. “You are with her.” Not implied. Not assumed. Not “you two seem like you’ll stick together.” Named and confirmed. Both parties say yes.

The Critical Rule About the Briefing

The briefing happens before the gates open.

Not at the campsite while some people are still arriving.

Not after the first set.

Not “we’ll do it later, everyone’s here now.”

Before the gates open. That is the only window in which everyone is sober, everyone is present, and the information that the briefing provides is not yet urgently needed.

After the gates open, the briefing is the barn door after the horse has achieved freedom.

PROTOCOL 3 — THE CODE-WORD PROTOCOL

*(Extraction on demand, no questions)*

What It Is

**The code word is the sentence that means:** “I need extraction right now. No context. No questions. Just come.”

It activates four things simultaneously:

1. The Inner Anchor comes toward you at the fastest possible pace that does not create a scene.

2. The Outer Anchor is notified via text.

3. No explanation is required from you.

4. Nobody asks if you’re sure.

What It Is Not

The code word is not a sentence that requires you to justify the extraction, rate the severity of the situation on a scale of one to ten, or explain what is happening before help begins moving. Those requirements make the code word useless. If you have to explain in order to receive the extraction, you have not built a code word. You have built a reporting system. Reporting systems require capacity. The code word is for moments when capacity is precisely what you do not have.

Choosing It

Good code words are:

Things that sound normal enough to say in public without attracting attention

Things the specific person would not say in ordinary circumstances

Memorable under altered states

Short enough to text with one hand while walking

“Where’s my charger” — excellent if the sender never loses their charger.

“I think the weather is changing” — specific, unusual, text-friendly.

“Have you talked to Marcus recently” — if Marcus is a mutual friend who nobody normally asks about.

“I think I left something at the car” — decent, but common enough to occasionally be true, which blunts the signal.

Do not use “watermelon.” The scene has used watermelon as a code word long enough that it no longer functions as a code. It functions as a fruit at this point.

Practicing It

**Before the event:** say the code word out loud. Both people. So that the sound of it is familiar when it arrives under conditions that are not ideal for recognizing familiar sounds. The first time the code word should ever be heard in context is not at two a.m. in the middle of a crowd.

PROTOCOL 4 — THE ANCHOR’S BILL OF RIGHTS

*(Protecting the role, sustaining the function)*

The Anchor Is Not A Sacrifice

The anchor role exists to protect the crew. The anchor role also has a cost: reduced personal freedom during active duty, divided attention, the specific emotional labor of staying alert in an environment built to override alertness. None of these costs are unfair or unreasonable. All of them need to be acknowledged and managed, or the anchor role will be held badly, which is worse than not having it at all.

The Eight Rights

1. The right to a shift end. The anchor role has a duration. When the shift ends, it transfers. The anchor is not on duty forever. The specific end-time is agreed upon before the event. The transfer happens explicitly, not implied.

2. The right to their own dose plan. The anchor’s dose plan is their own, subject to the operational requirement that they maintain the capacity to run their role. What that requires is negotiated per anchor, per night, per crew. An anchor who cannot maintain capacity sober can have a mild dose plan. An anchor who cannot maintain capacity at any dose level is not the anchor for this night.

3. The right to not be skimmed without consequence. If someone attempts to pull the anchor away from their role, the anchor has standing to name it: “I’m on anchor duty. I’m not available for that right now.” No further explanation required. The crew backs this.

4. The right to name the hardest part. After an event, the anchor is allowed to debrief the moments that were difficult, draining, or frightening. The debrief is not complaining. It is the data that makes next season’s anchor rotation better.

5. The right to escalate without negotiation. If the anchor assesses that the situation requires escalation, the escalation happens. The anchor does not need the group’s consensus. The anchor does not need the person in crisis to agree. The anchor assesses and acts. The debrief happens later.

6. The right to be wrong. The anchor will sometimes escalate a situation that turns out not to require escalation. This is not a failure. This is the correct behavior. The cost of an unnecessary escalation is a briefly awkward interaction. The cost of a necessary escalation that did not happen is the chapter you do not want to be in.

7. The right to say they cannot do it. An anchor who is not in the condition to anchor — for any reason, physical or emotional or psychological — is entitled to say so and hand off the role without stigma. An anchor who does not feel able to anchor and does not say so is an anchor who will fail the role at its most important moment.

8. The right to be cared for. The anchor cares for the crew. The crew cares for the anchor. The role does not exist in one direction. When the anchor’s shift ends, someone brings them water. Someone checks in. Someone says thank you. The anchor is not the load-bearing wall that the rest of the structure builds against without ever reinforcing.

PROTOCOL 5 — THE ANCHOR BACKUP PLAN

*(One anchor is a person. Two anchors is a system.)*

Why Backups Exist

Anchors are human beings. Human beings get skimmed, get sick, get pulled into situations of their own, have their phones die at the exact moment the code word arrives, fall asleep in someone’s tent in family camp, or — in the specific scenario this book has been describing for nine chapters — run into the exact Wook whose forty-five-minute warm conversation is precisely calibrated to the anchor’s specific vulnerability.

The anchor backup plan is the system that assumes this will happen and builds around it.

The Three-Layer Backup

**Layer 1 — The Designated Backup Anchor:** one named person who knows they are the backup. They have the same information as the primary anchor: legal names, ICE contacts, meet-up spot, code word, escalation threshold. They are not on active duty unless the primary anchor becomes unavailable. When the primary becomes unavailable, the transfer is explicit and verbal: “I’m going off duty. You’re on.”

**Layer 2 — The Camp Lead or Safety Contact:** the person in the crew with the most scene experience and the clearest head. This is not always Sister Lou. At camps without a Sister Lou, this is whoever has most recently read this chapter and is willing to hold the role.

**Layer 3 — The Festival Infrastructure:** DanceSafe booth, harm-reduction tent, festival safety desk. These are not the ideal resolution to the anchor going dark — they are the last-resort layer. Know where they are before you need them. Walk past them at entry. Put them in your phone as Contacts, not as a map pin you’ll try to find at two a.m.

The Code for “My Anchor Is Gone”

In addition to the extraction code word, the crew needs a phrase that means: “My anchor has been skimmed / has gone offline / is unavailable. I need backup.”

This phrase is different from the extraction code word. The extraction code word means: “Come get me right now.” The backup phrase means: “The system has a gap, I am not yet in crisis, but someone needs to fill it.”

**Example:** “I can’t find [anchor name], have you seen them?” Said to a crew member, means: activate Layer 2.

The backup phrase can be the Inner Anchor’s name asked as a question. “Have you seen Hugo?” if Hugo is the anchor and you are not in crisis but the anchor is gone.

The Anchor Rotation

For multi-day events, nobody anchors for four consecutive days. The anchor role rotates across the crew’s available members. The rotation is planned in advance on the printed sheet. The rotation is not negotiated in real time at ten p.m. on day three when everyone is tired.

The rotation goes on the printed sheet.

The printed sheet goes to every crew member at the briefing.

The briefing happens twenty minutes before doors.
