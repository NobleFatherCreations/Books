## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV — NO PREDATOR*

Twenty minutes before doors.

This is the hour that most festival attendees spend doing one of the following: arguing with their tent zipper, eating a protein bar that has been in the bottom of the bag since the last event and which has developed the texture of compressed sawdust and the flavor of ambition, discovering that the bandana they planned to use as a dust mask is also the bandana they used as a plate at lunch, or standing in the pre-entry queue with the philosophical resignation of people who have learned that festival infrastructure runs on its own timeline and that timeline is not the one on the app.

Sister Lou Mantilla is doing none of these things.

Sister Lou is standing at the camp’s designated briefing spot — the gap between the two main shade structures, which has been, for eleven consecutive years at this specific festival, the location of the Sober Set Captain’s pre-doors crew meeting — with a clipboard, a printed sheet, and the particular energy of someone who has been here before and who knows, with the accuracy of eleven years of repetition, exactly how the next seventeen minutes will go.

She is fifty-eight. She has been in recovery for twenty-three years, which is long enough that her sobriety is not a recent achievement but a long-running infrastructure project — maintained, yes, with attention, but no longer fragile in the way new sobriety is fragile. She is the founding crew of the Wharf Rats at three regional circuits and has, over eleven years, run this specific pre-doors briefing at this specific festival without once missing it, which is a record she is simultaneously proud of and entirely unremarkable about in the way that people who do important things consistently are unremarkable about the consistency.

She has the kind of authority that comes from not needing to assert it.

People know she is going to run the briefing.

People know what the briefing costs to miss.

**What it costs to miss is:** you will not receive a wristband from Sister Lou’s camp next year. This is not posted anywhere. It is not in writing. It is simply known, the way the Bunk Police booth location is known and the way the porta-situation in the south quadrant is known — as ambient community information, distributed through the specific social network of people who have been coming to this festival for multiple years and who understand that the briefing is not optional, not because anyone makes it mandatory, but because Sister Lou has been running it for eleven years and the people in her camp have watched what happens to the people who did not run it.

Nobody flakes.

Her crew arrives between seven fifty-three and seven fifty-eight.

She knows this because she looked at her watch at the first arrival and at the last arrival and noted the spread in a mental file she keeps on crew punctuality, which is not a punitive file but a planning file — five minutes of spread is manageable, fifteen minutes of spread requires protocol adjustment. This crew, over five years of working together, has settled into a five-minute spread. This is a good crew.

There are sixteen of them.

Three she has worked with for the full eleven years. Eight she has worked with for three or more. Two are in their second year. Three are new this event — referred by existing crew, vetted through a single one-on-one conversation with Sister Lou that takes approximately twenty-five minutes and covers, in order: the person’s connection to the scene, their previous festival safety or anchor experience, their current sobriety or substance plan for the event, their emergency contact information, and a brief description of the worst situation they have ever seen at a festival and what they did about it.

The last question is the important one.

She does not evaluate the severity of the situation. She evaluates the action. People who say “I stayed with them and got help” pass. People who say “I walked away because I didn’t want to get involved” receive a longer conversation about what the role requires. People who cannot remember a situation of concern at any festival they have attended receive a different kind of longer conversation, which is about whether they have been paying attention.

The three new crew members tonight gave answers that passed.

They are here.

She begins at seven fifty-nine.

“Government names.”

The group goes around. Sixteen people say their government names to the person on their left. The person on their left repeats it back. This takes two minutes and forty seconds. She has timed it. The timing has gotten faster each year as the crew has gotten more practiced. In year one it took four minutes and twenty seconds and produced a conversation about why playa names weren’t sufficient that she does not need to have again because the answer has been the same for eleven years: “The medical tent needs the name on your ID, not the name you made up in 2018 that means something to you and nothing to anyone with a radio.”

“ICE contacts. Phones out. ICE-FIRST and ICE-SECOND. Names and numbers. Lock screens set to medical ID. Do it now. I’m watching.”

Sixteen phones come out. Fourteen people already have ICE contacts set from previous events. Two are setting them for the first time. She watches until the last phone is put away.

“The oak.”

**Everyone in the crew has a location:** the large oak tree at the south entrance to family camp. She can see two of the newer crew members glancing toward the festival grounds to orient themselves. She waits until their eyes come back.

“Every four hours. If your phone is dead, you walk to the oak. If you cannot walk to the oak, you send the code word before your phone dies. If you send the code word and nobody responds within ten minutes, you find the nearest harm-reduction vest. That vest is your secondary crew when the primary crew is unreachable.”

The code word this festival is “where’s my charger.” It was selected specifically because Sister Lou never loses her charger and therefore the sentence is conspicuous coming from her. This is a documented feature, not a bug.

“Your buddy.”

Pairs are assigned. She has done the pairing in advance, matching experienced crew with newer members, matching sober crew with crew who will be dosing, matching the people who are working specific areas of the festival grounds with the people who are most likely to be in those same areas. The pairings are on the printed sheet. Everyone takes a printed sheet.

“The sober captain is on duty from doors to close. That is me until three a.m. At three a.m. it transfers to Hugo. Hugo, you are on from three to dawn. If something happens and you need me, you call. I do not care if it is five-seventeen in the morning and I have been asleep for eleven minutes. You call.”

Hugo nods in the specific way of someone who has been the pre-dawn captain three years running and who has called at five-seventeen a.m. before and knows she picked up.

“Harm reduction is at the northwest corner of the main field. Medical tent is adjacent to the south entrance — same side as the oak, fifty yards east. If you cannot find either, find a vest. If you cannot find a vest, you are the vest. Act accordingly.”

“The four-hour rule. You know it. If something feels wrong: three asks and you escalate. Two contacts with no response and you go to the crew. One body in distress and you are already calling.”

She pauses.

“This briefing prevents nine of the previous chapters. You do not know which ones. You do not need to know. You do the briefing. The chapters do not happen.”

She says this every year.

She has said it every year since the third year, when a member of her crew pointed out that the briefing seemed to correlate with their camp never having a serious incident, and she said: “That’s because the briefing is what prevents the serious incident. You don’t see the bullet because the briefing is the thing that moved you out of the way.”

“Questions.”

There are no questions.

There are never questions at this point in the briefing. The questions have been answered over eleven years of running it and the answers have been absorbed into the crew’s operational knowledge and now live as practice rather than theory.

“Fifteen minutes. Every event. You’re welcome.”

She clicks the pen closed.

The crew disperses.

The festival opens its gates at eight-oh-two.

There is something the chapter needs to say about this moment, twenty minutes before doors, sixteen people with government names and ICE contacts and a printed sheet and a buddy:

Nothing dramatic happened here.

Nobody was rescued. Nobody was threatened. Nobody ran a play and nobody ran a counter. The chapter did not contain a crisis. The chapter contained a clipboard and a printed sheet and the specific quiet authority of a woman who has been doing this for eleven years because the years before she started it produced incidents that did not have to happen.

The boring meeting is the whole chapter.

Not because interesting things do not happen at festivals — the entire book has been twenty-six chapters of interesting things happening at festivals. But because the interesting things, the ones that cost people something, happen in the gap between “we’ll figure it out” and “we didn’t figure it out in time.”

The briefing is the gap closer.

The briefing is Sister Lou’s life’s work expressed in fifteen minutes.

The briefing is the thing that lets you go to the festival and come home and remember the set.
