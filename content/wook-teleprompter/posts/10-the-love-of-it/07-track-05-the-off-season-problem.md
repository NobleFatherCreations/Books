### TRACK 05 — THE OFF-SEASON PROBLEM
*(when leaving the work means losing the people)*

Here's the behavior. You cannot quit, and the reason has nothing to do with money.

Here is what happens to a person over six years of crew: your best friends are crew. Your partner is crew or met you through crew. Your winter plans are crew. The group chat that carries your daily life is a crew chat. The nineteen people who came to your mother’s funeral, eleven of them are crew. Your calendar, your identity, your competence, your nickname, and your entire sense of being good at something all live inside one organization that pays you in a wristband.

Now try to leave over a labor dispute.

You are not resigning a position. You are emigrating.

**That is the Off-Season Problem, and it is why the arrangement is so stable.** It does not need a contract, a non-compete, or a threat. It only needs your social world to be perfectly co-extensive with your workplace, which happens naturally, over years, because these are wonderful people and you love them.

And notice what it does to every smaller conflict along the way. You cannot push on the rate, the hours, the guy who yells, the scheduling, or the ladder policy — not because anyone forbids it, but because every dispute is with people who are also your entire life, which means every dispute is a potential exile, which means almost nobody starts one.

So the problems do not get raised. Not because they are not real. Because the cost of raising them is priced in a currency nobody wants to spend.

Here's the read on it. **Ask:** if I stopped doing this tomorrow, who would still be in my life in a year?

Count them. Actual names. People who would text you in February with no event attached.

If the number is healthy, then you are in a job you love surrounded by real friendships, which is one of the great outcomes available in a human life, and none of this Track applies to you.

If the number is two, or one, or zero, then the job is not a job and it is not a family either — it is the only room you have, and a person with one room will accept almost anything to stay in it, and that is true of everybody, and it has nothing to do with being weak.

The read is not “leave.” Most people who run this honestly do not leave and should not. The read is that a person with somewhere else to stand can ask a question at a production meeting, and a person with nowhere else to stand cannot, and the ability to ask the question is what keeps the whole organization honest.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Off-Season Test. Run it once a year, in the dead of winter, when no event is close enough to distort the answer.

**Step 1 — List the people you have seen in the last three months with no event, meeting, or crew obligation attached.** Just seen, because you wanted to.

**Step 2 — Mark which of them you met through this organization.** This is not a problem in itself; it is a map.

**Step 3 — Build one thing that is not this.** One. A climbing gym, a band, a Tuesday dinner, a class, an old friendship you have let go dormant since 2021. It does not need to be meaningful. It needs to exist.

**Step 4 — Keep one friendship that predates the crew, actively.** The person who knew you before the road name is the only one who can tell you whether you have changed in a way you would not have chosen.

**Step 5 — Notice what you can suddenly say.** This is the payoff and it arrives quietly. People who have one outside room start raising things at meetings within a season, without deciding to, because the sentence stops costing everything.

Quick vibe check. Having your whole social world inside one crew is not a pathology. It is what happens when you find your people, and for a lot of us — especially anyone who was lonely before they found this — it is the best thing that has ever happened. The Track is not about getting distance from people who love you. It is about not being structurally unable to disagree with them.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever watched someone quit a crew and then gone quiet on them? Not maliciously — just drifted, because the shared logistics ended and nobody scheduled anything. Every person who leaves a crew finds out very quickly how much of the friendship was infrastructure. If you want the family framing to be true, the test is not how you treat people at load-in. It is who you still text in February after they stopped coming.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Sober Folks:** If crew is your structure and your sober community and your purpose all at once, then a labor dispute is also a recovery risk, and that is a genuinely brutal position that almost nobody around you will understand. Build the outside room deliberately and early, with people who know that part of your life.

**Touring Artists:** Your version is the crew, the label, the management and the friend group being four names for the same eleven people. When the professional relationship ends, the question of what is left is answered all at once, and usually at the worst time.

This runs in every direction. The Off-Season Problem holds the forty-year-old rigger whose whole address book is this event, the young woman whose first real belonging was this crew and who will not risk it over a schedule, the queer kid for whom this is the first place that was ever safe and who will accept a great deal to keep it, the trans elder who built the department and cannot imagine being outside it, and the guy everyone calls a lifer as though it were a compliment. Total immersion is not a character flaw and it is not gendered. It is just the condition in which nobody can afford to say anything.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The workplace where everyone is also your friend group, your softball team and your landlord’s cousin. Great until the day you need to report something, at which point you discover that HR and Thanksgiving are the same nine people. The fix is never fewer friends. The fix is one room that is not that room.
