## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 WOOK POV*

I have been Sebastián. Last year I was Atlas. The year before, Phoenix. Costa Rica in 2023 I was Sebastián again — different context, same energy, different Sebastián. My real name is on a driver’s license my ex-wife is still trying to find in connection with a civil matter in Sedona that I have no interest in relitigating.

This weekend I am Sterling.

Sterling is a founder. Sterling is building something. Sterling is at this specific festival because this specific festival has a vendor row with forty-two booths of exactly the kind of small-scale makers I need for the retreat center concept I have been “raising capital” for since 2021. Forty-two booths. I estimate approximately twenty-eight of those makers will be financially stressed enough to be workable. Of those twenty-eight, I can realistically approach fifteen in one weekend without becoming conspicuous. Of those fifteen, I can probably close three to four conversations that go somewhere useful. That is my target: three to four relationships started, enough to provide materials, labor, inventory, or creative output to the retreat center concept over the next twelve months, in exchange for something that is not money.

The something that is not money is different for each person. That is the art of it. Finding the right non-money currency for each specific maker is what separates the Sterling approach from the guys who just walk up to booths and say “I have a platform.” Those guys are amateurs. Those guys get turned down in forty seconds because they have not read the room. Reading the room is everything. The row is its own ecosystem and you have to understand the ecosystem before you can work inside it.

I do one slow lap before I engage. Assessment only. No eye contact. Just reading.

First pass. Left side of the row.

Wire-wrap. Ten years at minimum — I can read tenure in a vendor’s setup the way a contractor reads a building. Her tray is hand-finished solid wood, not a bought display. The price cards are laminated, not handwritten. She has a Pelican case for her reader and her own small portable fan, which means she has been doing this long enough to know the heat problem. Her sign says WIRE WRAP. REAL SILVER. PRICED HONEST. CASH/VENMO/CARD. NO BARTER. NO “EXPOSURE.” The last two items in caps. She put them in caps because she has been asked for barter and exposure enough times that she decided to let the sign handle it so she doesn’t have to. I know exactly who she is. She is the Spool type. Spool types have seen enough of the world that their read on me will complete before I finish my opening sentence.

Skip.

**Second booth:** hand-poured soaps shaped like vintage tape decks. Twenty-eight, second year, the specific exhausted dignity of a person whose product is genuinely good and whose pricing is too low. She is currently being talked at by a man in a CONSCIOUS hat who is “exploring synergies.” She has the slightly fixed smile of someone who has been explaining that synergy does not pay her booth fee for approximately six minutes. That man is an amateur. He has led with the concept before establishing the rapport. She will end the conversation in the next thirty seconds and be slightly more guarded for the rest of the day.

This is the problem with the CONSCIOUS hat guys. They raise the defenses before I get there.

Workable anyway. Second year, still calibrating. I file her for later.

Live painter across the row. Four-foot canvas, acrylics, half-finished. Good composition. She is mid-brush, she has not eaten — I can read the metabolism state in the slight tension at the temple and the water bottle that has been empty for at least two hours. There is a man with a clipboard hovering for a break in her brush rhythm. I can see from here that the clipboard man is pitching the Tulum angle, which is the saturated approach in this circuit right now. Every second Wook on the row has the Tulum pitch. She has heard it twice this week already. Her jaw is set with the specific angle of a person deciding whether to let the clipboard man keep going or tell him to move.

Saturated. Skip.

Walking vendor, seventeen. Cooler, cold brew. He is here replacing his auntie who is at a medical appointment. I can read the delegation in the way he handles the cashbox — careful, slightly nervous, not the casual ease of someone who considers this theirs. He has the moral clarity of teenagers and accountants. He will not be moved by anything I am carrying. Also: the auntie network in this circuit is real, which means anything I try on the nephew gets back to the auntie and the auntie has been on this row since before I was Sterling.

Do not touch the kid.

Hooper. Mid-forties. I have seen this person — not this specific person, this type of person — at forty festivals across eight years. The child support energy is specific and legible: a man who is very good at what he does, who has built something real and independent through his craft, who is one month behind on a payment that is real and fixed and does not care about the calendar of the festival economy. He needs money. The art and the need are right there together in the way he sets the hoop down when he talks to people — carefully, deliberately, like someone who knows the hoop is his income and treats it accordingly.

This is my target.

I approach.

“Hey — I have been watching your work all morning. Incredible flow. Have you ever thought about anchoring a movement journey for a workshop dome experience? I’m curating something really special on Sunday. Pay is a beautiful basket of artisanal items and the chance to share your gift with a deeply receiving group.”

I watch his face.

I have seen this face do the math four hundred times.

**He says:** “What’s the cash rate?”

His voice is polite. His eyes are not. His eyes are the eyes of a man who has heard this pitch under every name it has ever been given — experience economy, gift exchange, sacred compensation, energetic alignment, collab — and who has arrived, through this specific iteration and the three hundred and ninety-nine before it, at the exact same arithmetic.

**I say:** “We’re operating from a gift framework.”

**He says:** “Cool. I’m operating from a child support framework. Have a great workshop.”

He picks up the hoop. His gear bag has a competition bib still zip-tied to the strap, faded, a name and a year on it: MATEO, 2019.

I smile. “Of course. Blessings.”

And I walk on. I do not register failure. Failure is the wrong frame for a no that was always going to be a no. Mateo was intelligence, not a prospect. Mateo told me something useful: child support is a real and fixed cost and it makes the gift framework non-operational. This is information I can use to refine my read on the next approach.

Eleven more booths.

The day is young.

I find Asha at booth thirty-one.

She is twenty-four. Wire-wrap, but not Spool’s level — three seasons in, still doing the work of building her style. Her display is beautiful and slightly inconsistent, which is the signature of someone who is still discovering what her work is. Her pricing is higher than it should be for her market positioning, which means she has been told her work is underpriced more often than she has been told it is correctly priced, and she has overcorrected upward without the sales volume to support the new price point. I estimate she is at about sixty percent of what she needs to cover her weekend.

Taped inside the lid of her cash box, facing her, where only she can see it: a school photo of a kid, maybe nine, gap in the front teeth, that maroon backdrop every school portrait in America has used since 1994. Brother, going by the age gap. She looks at it once while she is counting her float and her face does something that is not for anybody at this festival.

I note it. I will not use it. Using the kid would be crude and would also work, and the difference between me and the men in the CONSCIOUS hats is that I know both of those things and still do not do it. I would like credit for that. There is nobody here to give it to me, so I give it to myself, and then I go to work on her.

She needs the money, and she needs to believe in the thing.

I start with the thing.

I tell her I have been following the wire-wrap space for three years because I am building a retreat center in New Mexico that is going to do for craft what Marfa did for contemporary art — create the conditions for the work to be seen by the people who understand its value. I tell her that her work has the kind of intentionality that the retreat center is looking for. I tell her I have not said this to anyone else on the row today, which is technically true in the sense that I have not said it in this exact configuration.

She lights up. Of course she does. That is not a manipulation. That is a real human being receiving specific, genuine-sounding appreciation for work she has put her heart into in a setting that requires her to stand in the heat for twelve hours and ask strangers to value it. The lighting up is real. The connection it creates is real. The work she is going to do for my LLC over the next nine months will also be real.

I tell her about the equity.

Not money — equity. Twenty percent of the LLC, in exchange for becoming “a founding artist in residence.” The LLC will eventually generate revenue from the retreat center’s visual identity, which Asha’s work will be part of. Her contribution right now is labor and materials. The payoff is future and real and I have made it sound entirely reasonable.

**She says:** “Can I see the paperwork?”

This is the moment. The document I produce looks legal. It is not drafted by a lawyer. It was drafted by me in a Google Doc in a van in Joshua Tree in March, with the help of a template from a website that charges forty-nine dollars a month and is not a substitute for actual legal counsel. The terms are not technically illegal. They are also not fair. The key clause — the one that assigns Asha a percentage of a company whose valuation I control — means that if the company produces any revenue at all, Asha gets twenty percent of whatever I decide the company is worth in the moment I decide to pay her. Which, historically, is nothing, because the company never quite closes its current round.

She is reading the document.

She is not a lawyer.

She is going to say yes.

And I am going to leave this festival with a founding artist in residence who will spend nine months making work that I will photograph, post, use to attract the next round of investors and collaborators, and eventually move on from when she starts asking questions about the LLC’s actual financial structure.

Her work is real.

My appreciation for it is real.

The document has two meanings and she can only see one of them right now.

I sleep fine about this.

I sleep fine because in my accounting of events I am a founder who is building something and offering real opportunities to emerging artists in an economy that does not have enough of those opportunities, and the delay in compensation is a function of the stage of the company, not a design feature, and Asha will benefit eventually.

Asha will not benefit eventually.

Sterling will be at a different festival next year.

And the year after that.

And the vendor row is full of people who came to sell things they made, and the day is long, and the sun is hot, and somebody has to be the one who knows what the paper says.
