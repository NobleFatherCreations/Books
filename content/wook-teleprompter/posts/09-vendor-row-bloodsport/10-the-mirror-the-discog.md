## The Mirror + The Discog

HAVE YOU BEEN THE WOOK? Mirror Check At The Square Reader

Have you ever asked someone to do unpaid work for “exposure” while you got paid?

Have you ever pitched equity in an entity you did not yet legally have?

Have you ever signed a contract for someone in a hurry and not given them time to get a lawyer?

Have you ever paid someone back with a 🙏 note specifically to make the slow pay feel less like a debt?

Have you ever kept someone’s inventory or equipment longer than you needed to because possession was convenient leverage?

Have you ever told a maker their “scarcity mindset” was the problem when your budget was the problem?

Most of us have done at least one of these. The festival economy runs on a haze of small extractions that everyone participates in until someone names them. The Wooks at the top of the food chain depend on the rest of us imitating them in small ways downward.

Pay people in cash. Pay them on time. Pay them with plain notes. Sign nothing in the dust. Inventory every bin. Let your friend’s hard no be a hard no.

Repair what can be repaired. Pay back what is owed. Send the bin back. Apologize for the slow pay. Rewrite the contract. Drop the equity pitch. Cancel the LLC. Refund the deposit.

The festival economy heals when individual makers stop extracting from each other.

We can stop.

Here's the Wook Discog for this chapter. Studio Debut — The Baby Wook learning the moves

Twenty-six, running an “artist collective” for the first time. Genuinely excited about the vision. Has not yet learned that “we’re going to split the revenue once the concept is proven” is a sentence that has never produced the described outcome. Has not paid the first three contributors. Has told them this is normal for a pre-revenue stage company. The contributors are slowly becoming less available. The Studio Debut does not know it is running a Skim. It is running a Skim. The vision is real. The money is not in the plan.

Live Album — The Seasoned Operator

Sterling. Walking the row in white linen under a rotating name with a Google Doc contract on his phone and eleven more booths after Mateo. Has refined the pitch across six years and three names. Knows exactly which version of the Sacred Labor Skim lands on painters versus wire-wrappers versus healers versus hoopers. Has one signed agreement from the last festival that produced nothing and cost the maker nine months. Does not think of this as harm. Thinks of this as stage one of something that hasn’t quite materialized yet. He will be at the next festival. He will have a different name. Eleven more booths. The day is young.

Greatest Hits Compilation — The Institutional Scaled-Up Version

The festival itself. Booth fee: twelve hundred dollars. No electricity included. Booth electricity: four hundred dollars additional. Required permit: two hundred. Required insurance: three hundred. Labor to build and strike: three days. Revenue guarantee: zero — refundable only if festival cancels. The festival’s website shows 2019 attendance figures. It is 2026. The attendee numbers have declined. The vendor row has been moved to a less trafficked location. Three vendors asked for a refund when the move was announced. The refund policy, buried in section fourteen of the vendor agreement, says refunds are not issued for layout changes. Three vendors are owed money they will not receive. The festival posted beautiful photos of vendor row on its Instagram. The booth-fee Wook is not a person. It is a policy.
