### TRACK 06 — THE GIFT-FRAMEWORK GRIFT
*(labor extraction via reciprocity norms)*

Here's the behavior. “We don’t really do cash for facilitators. We’re operating from a gift framework.”

This sentence, said by a person charging participants a fee to attend the workshop where you are performing unpaid.

The Gift-Framework Grift is the selective application of gift-economy ethics to the people doing the work while non-gift-economy economics are applied to the people running the event. Participants pay. Vendors pay booth fees. The building is rented. The catering is invoiced. You perform for free because “the energy of the exchange is different when we move into gift.”

The energy of the exchange is different. Specifically: your energy pays for it and theirs doesn’t.

The Skim also runs in collaborative production contexts: the art project where everyone contributes labor while one person controls the brand and receives the revenue; the camp where three people do the structural work and the inner circle claims the credit; the workshop circuit where one facilitator’s brand grows through the labor of unpaid guest contributors.

Here's the read on it. Follow the money through the whole event. Who is paying whom for what? If participants pay, if vendors pay, if the venue charges a fee — and you are the only party not receiving compensation — the gift economy is applied to you specifically. That is not gift economy. That is wage theft with a crystal grid.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Full-Event Follow. Step 1 — Before agreeing to any unpaid or partially-paid contribution: ask who else in the event is receiving compensation and in what form.

Step 2 — Ask the organizer to name the compensation for each role, publicly, in the agreement.

Step 3 — Compare your compensation to the market rate for your specific work in your specific context. Keynote speakers, workshop facilitators, performers, and demonstrators all have market rates. Know yours.

Step 4 — If your compensation is below market rate: “I appreciate the opportunity. My rate for this kind of work is [number]. If that doesn’t work for the budget, I’m not available.”

Step 5 — Do not justify the number. State it and wait.

Quick vibe check. Real gift economies do exist in specific contexts where compensation is genuinely mutual, where the event itself runs on donated labor across all roles, and where no one in the operation is drawing a salary or keeping a margin. Those contexts exist and they are worth participating in when they are genuine. The test is: does the gift economy apply to everyone, or does it apply only to the people without leverage?

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever organized an event where some contributors were paid and others were not, without clearly disclosing that asymmetry upfront? Have you ever used “we’re a community event” to justify not paying performers while charging participants?

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **BIPOC and Queer Artists:** In festival circuits that lack diversity in their paid lineups, BIPOC and queer artists are disproportionately pitched the gift-economy slot while white and non-queer counterparts receive paid ones. This is not accidental. It is structural, and it compounds the financial exclusion that already characterizes the scene’s professional economy.

This runs in every direction. The Gift-Framework Grift is applied to every kind of maker and runs across every gender and identity. The application is heaviest on the people who most need the exposure — which is also the population most financially vulnerable to being underpaid for it.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The “passion project” that pays some team members and not others. The startup where engineering is compensated and design is “a contribution to the vision.” The Skim runs in every industry where creative labor is treated as intrinsically motivated and therefore not requiring compensation.
