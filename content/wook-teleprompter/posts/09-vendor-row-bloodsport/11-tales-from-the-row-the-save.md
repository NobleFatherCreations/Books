## Tales From The Row — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Asha Kowalczyk had been doing wire-wrap for three seasons. She set up at booth thirty-one on Friday morning with a wooden display tray she built herself, a Square reader on a Pelican case she bought secondhand, and a price list she had revised four times in the past month because everyone kept telling her to charge more.

She was charging more. She was not selling as much as she needed to at the new price point. It was Saturday at two p.m. and she was at sixty-two percent of what she needed to cover the weekend.

She had talked to Spool two hours ago during the mid-afternoon dead hour. Not about anything specific. Just: Spool had seen her display and walked over and said “nice weight on the coils,” which is a thing that costs nothing to say and means everything to someone who spent three years learning how to get the weight right. They had talked for twenty minutes about pricing, markets, the specific problem of a festival vendor charging more than she charged at farmers markets, and why that was actually correct and not overreaching.

**Spool had also said, before leaving:** “Someone’s going to come talk to you this afternoon in white linen. Ask for the cash rate first. If there’s no cash rate, there’s no deal.”

Asha had not known what this meant at the time.

At two forty-seven p.m., she found out.

Sterling’s pitch was good. She could feel it working on her in real time — the New Mexico land, the Marfa comparison, the founding artist framing, the specific language about her work’s “intentionality.” Something in her chest lifted when he said it. The intentionality was real. She had worked for three years to get the intentionality right.

This is the watermelon moment. Not dramatic. She is standing behind her display tray, feeling the thing in her chest — the real thing, the genuine recognition of work she has poured herself into — and she is simultaneously aware that the recognition and the pitch are happening in the same sentence, and that the pitch has a document attached to it, and that she has not yet asked the question Spool told her to ask.

She asks it.

“What’s the cash rate?”

Sterling’s face does the thing.

She watches it happen. The micro-recoil. The shift into spiritual register. “We’re operating from a gift framework. The founding equity is —”

“I understand,” she says. “What’s the cash rate for the kind of work you’re describing?”

“We don’t really have a cash rate for this. The value is in the equity —”

“Okay,” she says. “Then I’m not available. Have a good festival.”

She turns back to her display.

He stands there for approximately four seconds — the specific duration of someone recalibrating whether a different angle might work — and then he says “of course, blessings” and walks to booth thirty-two.

Asha watches him go.

She is aware that something just happened that she protected herself from without fully understanding the mechanism. She texts Spool: “The linen man just showed up. I asked for the cash rate. He didn’t have one.”

**Spool texts back:** “That’s it. That’s the whole move. Good.”

That evening Spool stops by with a cold brew from Beans’s cooler and they sit behind Asha’s booth during the post-sunset slow hour and Spool explains all seven Tracks without calling them Tracks. She just describes what she has seen, in the specific language of someone who has been on the row for ten years and has watched it happen to a hundred different makers.

Asha listens.

She sells four pendants during the conversation.

She drives home Sunday with her rent covered, her inventory intact, her bins in her own car, and one specific skill she did not have Friday morning: the cash-rate question.

She teaches it to the first-year vendor who sets up next to her at the next festival.

That is how the row stays alive.
