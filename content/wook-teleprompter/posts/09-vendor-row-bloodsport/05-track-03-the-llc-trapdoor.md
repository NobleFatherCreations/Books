### TRACK 03 — THE LLC TRAPDOOR
*(entity layering for liability evasion)*

Here's the behavior. The agreement looks legal. It was typed. It has sections. It has a section called “Compensation” and a section called “Term” and a section that uses the word “heretofore” which is the legal document equivalent of wearing a stethoscope to a party — it performs medicine without practicing it.

The LLC Trapdoor is the document designed to extract intellectual property, labor, materials, exclusivity, or money from a maker while giving them a percentage of something whose value the document’s author controls entirely. The key mechanism: the compensation formula references the company’s valuation, revenue, or “fair market value as determined in good faith by the founding board,” which is the Wook. Your twenty percent of something he decides is worth nothing is worth nothing.

**Secondary mechanism:** the exclusivity clause. You agree not to work with “competing ventures in the conscious goods and services space” for the duration of the agreement, which is typically “three years or until the company achieves its first funding round.” The company does not achieve its first funding round. Your exclusivity clause runs for three years. You have contractually removed yourself from competing opportunities while giving your labor for free.

**Third mechanism:** the IP assignment. Your work, once delivered under the agreement, becomes the company’s property. If the company produces anything using your work — a book, a course, a product line, a brand identity — you have no claim to it. Your twenty percent equity is your payment for the IP. The IP is worth more than the equity if the company ever produces anything.

Here's the read on it. Three red flags that survive a non-lawyer’s reading:

**One:** compensation formula that references the author’s own determination of value.

**Two:** exclusivity clause that covers your whole industry or practice for more than ninety days.

**Three:** IP assignment without a royalty structure.

If any one of these is present, the document is a Trapdoor.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Lawyer Minimum. Step 1 — Do not sign at the festival. Not ever. The festival is the worst possible environment for contract review: altered state, sleep deprivation, social pressure, time scarcity, flattery.

Step 2 — Ask for a copy to review “with your advisor.” You do not need to specify what an advisor is. Everyone knows what an advisor is.

Step 3 — Find a lawyer. Three options at low cost: a maker-friend’s lawyer who does this work, a flat-fee contract review on a freelance attorney platform, a friend who works at a firm who will look at it for a reasonable amount and dinner.

**Step 4 — Ask the lawyer:** “What does this give me? What does this take from me? If the company never produces revenue, what am I holding?”

Step 5 — If the lawyer flags problems and the Wook wants to “redline” instead of rewrite: walk away from the Trapdoor entirely. The whole document is the problem.

Quick vibe check. Real paperwork is good. Real partners want paperwork. Real paperwork protects everyone. Real partners give you time to review with counsel, welcome questions, and produce clean documents drafted by their own attorney that they can also defend in front of a lawyer. The Wook produces documents that cannot survive scrutiny and responds to scrutiny by saying you are “not trusting the container.”

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever drafted an agreement yourself using an internet template and presented it as a legal document? Have you ever used language that obscured what you were actually receiving from the other party? Have you ever framed a request for legal review as a lack of trust? If yes: the paperwork is the love letter, and yours is forged.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Makers and Vendors:** Anything granting “exclusive licensing” of your work or your IP without a clear, calculable, time-bounded royalty structure is a Trapdoor.

**Practitioners and Healers:** Anything assigning your method, your container design, your IP, or your customer list to a platform is a Trapdoor wearing sage smoke.

This runs in every direction. The LLC Trapdoor is used across every gender, identity, and orientation. The vocabulary shifts to match the scene. The clauses are remarkably consistent across the whole industry. The person who drafted the document knows what it says. The person who signs it, usually, does not.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The car loan with a prepayment-penalty clause that costs more than the interest it’s replacing, drafted by the dealership’s own finance office, reviewed by no one on your behalf, signed at hour three on the lot when you needed to leave too badly to read. Same document. Different font.
