## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

It is 1:47 a.m. and Jess has been gone for approximately four hours and nobody in the group can remember her last name.

This is going to be a sentence that takes a moment to fully absorb, so sit with it: a group of four people, three of whom have known Jess for three years, one of whom has known her for four, are currently standing in a circle around a picnic table at a four-day festival in Oregon trying to construct, from memory, a last name. Any last name. Her last name. The last name that appears on her driver’s license, her medical insurance card, her emergency contact form if she had ever filled one out, her Venmo which is just @jess with a cloud emoji, her group chat contact name which is Jess 🪐 because she once said she was a Saturn person and somehow the ring planet made it into the contact name permanently.

The group chat itself is named jess + the dirtbags and has been since 2022, which means the group chat is named after her, which means Jess is the most prominent member of the group, which makes it additionally extraordinary that nobody knows her last name.

Her name is Jess.

Jess what?

**Wes says:** “I want to say Chen? Or maybe Chan? It might start with C.”

**Noor says:** “I thought it was something with an M? Maybe?”

Devon says nothing because Devon is the group’s most catastrophically absent-minded member and knows better than to contribute name hypotheses that will later be scrutinized.

**Oliver says:** “It’s on her Venmo somewhere, right? No, wait — her Venmo is just @jess-cloud-emoji. I checked.”

He checked.

He has been checking his phone every four to seven minutes since 11 p.m. when the meet-up spot by the big oak near family camp produced nothing. Since then he has called twice. Both went straight to voicemail. The voicemail says: “Hey! Sorry I missed you — I’ll call you back when I can! If it’s urgent leave a message! If it’s not, maybe texts better!” The exclamation points are doing a lot of load-bearing work in terms of making Jess sound like a person who is fine, who is having a good time somewhere, who will call back when she can.

Oliver is not convinced.

The timeline, reconstructed:

Six forty-five p.m.: Jess leaves camp to find a phone charger. This is a normal and reasonable thing to do at a festival. Phone chargers are the modern equivalent of fire — everybody needs one, nobody has extras, the entire festival runs on a prayer that somebody’s portable battery has juice. She says she’ll be right back.

Seven-twenty p.m.: Nobody notices she hasn’t come back because the headliner announcement just dropped in the festival app and there was a brief collective detour into set-time math.

Eight-fifty p.m.: Wes, on the way to the second stage, sees Jess near the silent disco. She is talking to someone. He waves. She waves back. She seems fine. She seems great, actually — animated, laughing. The someone she is talking to is tall and has a beard.

Eight-fifty p.m. description inventory: Tall. Beard. That is the full description. Not “tall, beard, green jacket, distinctive scar, camp name, first and last name, where they are camped, what their vehicle looks like.” Tall and beard. In a crowd of approximately eight thousand people, of whom a conservatively estimated nine hundred have some variation of tall and beard, this is a description that is doing roughly the same work as saying “human person at the festival.”

Nine-forty p.m.: The group goes to the main stage for the headliner. Everybody assumes Jess is already there or will find them. This assumption is based entirely on vibes and the prior success rate of “we’ll find each other” as a festival coordination strategy, which is approximately 70 percent, meaning it has worked before, meaning the brain has filed it as reliable, meaning the 30 percent failure rate has been quietly absorbed into the category of “but it worked out fine eventually.”

This is the planning fallacy.

This is the chapter.

Eleven p.m.: The group realizes Jess is not at the main stage and has not been in contact. Wes sends a text. No response. Noor calls. Voicemail. They go back to camp to check.

She is not at camp.

Eleven-forty p.m.: Noor calls again. Voicemail. Oliver calls. Voicemail. Two calls go straight to voicemail which means either Jess’s phone is dead or Jess’s phone is off or Jess’s phone is in someone’s tent with Jess’s jacket and Jess is somewhere without it, which is possible, or Jess’s phone is off because she is in a situation where she cannot answer, which is the thought that is now occupying approximately seventy-three percent of Oliver’s available cognition.

**Midnight:** The group decides to search. This is the correct instinct. The searching begins. The searching is conducted without any information infrastructure because nobody in the group has ever run a missing-friend search before and has thus not learned any of the following things: where the festival’s lost and found is, what the medical tent’s protocol is for searching for a missing person, whether the festival has a safety desk, what information is needed to file a report, and — returning to the core problem — what Jess’s last name is.

Twelve forty-seven a.m.: Noor asks Oliver what Jess’s last name is.

Twelve forty-nine a.m.: This is when they realize.

The realization lands in stages, the way a bad situation always lands — not all at once, but in a cascade of smaller realizations that each generate a further realization that generates a further realization until you are standing at a picnic table at 1:47 a.m. cataloging everything you don’t know about your friend.

They don’t know her last name.

They don’t know her parents’ names. Wes thinks her mom is Karen. Oliver thinks her mom is Carol. They have been to three of the same festivals and they cannot agree on whether her mother’s name ends in an n or an l.

They don’t know her parents’ phone number. They don’t know where she is from, specifically — somewhere in the Bay Area, maybe Oakland, maybe Alameda, one of the places in that region, she has mentioned it. They don’t know her address. They don’t know the name of her emergency contact. They don’t know if she has medical conditions. They don’t know her doctor. They don’t know her blood type.

They don’t have a photo of her wristband. Wristband photos are the thing that nobody thinks to take until someone is missing and the wristband number is the only unique identifier that ties a specific person to a specific festival registration. If Jess is in the medical tent and they need to find her, the person at the medical tent will ask for a name. They have: Jess. And possibly C. Something. Or M. Something. This is the information equivalent of a treasure map that says “somewhere in Oregon.”

They don’t know if her phone is dead because she didn’t charge it or because something happened, and they cannot tell the difference between those two scenarios, and the inability to tell the difference is, at 1:47 a.m. in the dark with a missing friend and no last name, the specific texture of the worst feeling the festival produces.

**Devon says:** “We should have done the thing.”

Everybody knows what the thing is.

Nobody did the thing.

The thing was the fifteen-minute meeting that Noor had suggested in the car on the way up, the one where you say your real names out loud and put your ICE contacts in and pick a meet-up spot and pick a code word and establish the buddy system. The one that seemed like overkill on the way up when everyone was excited and the playlist was good and they were going to a festival, not a crisis situation.

The one that is not overkill at 1:47 a.m.

**Oliver says, very quietly:** “What is her last name.”

It is not a question. It is just the sentence that needs to be said.

Jess is fine.

The chapter is going to tell you this now, rather than at the end, because the chapter’s subject is not the question of what happened to Jess — the chapter’s subject is what happened to the group, and specifically what the group did not do in the car on the way up that it is now paying for at 1:47 a.m.

Jess is, at this moment, asleep in a stranger’s tent at the back of family camp. Her phone died at 9:14 p.m. because she found a charger but it belonged to someone who needed it back, so she got maybe thirty percent and then it died again. She was tired in the way you get tired at 9 p.m. on day two of a four-day festival when the adrenaline of day one has metabolized and your body is doing the math on what it has cost and the math is not great. She walked back toward camp, found a tent with its flap open, saw a sleeping bag, sat down for just a minute, and was asleep within four minutes in the way that sleep takes over when the body decides it is done waiting for permission.

The tent belongs to a seventy-one-year-old retired English teacher named Marlene.

Marlene is at this festival because her granddaughter is performing at the workshop stage on Sunday and Marlene drives six hours to see her granddaughter play. She has been to four of these. She finds them “extremely a lot” but loves her granddaughter with the fierce and uncomplicated devotion of a person who has had sixty years to figure out what matters, and what matters is her granddaughter’s set on Sunday.

Marlene found Jess asleep in her tent at approximately nine-thirty and did not wake her, because Marlene has taught ninth graders for thirty-seven years and has a well-developed sense of when a person needs sleep and when they need intervention, and Jess clearly needed sleep. She put a spare blanket over her. She went to the nearby communal area where the older attendees tend to congregate and had tea.

Jess wakes up at 5:14 a.m. to the sound of someone making tea.

She looks around at an unfamiliar tent.

She looks at the woman making tea.

**The woman looks at her and says:** “Good morning. I’m Marlene. There’s chamomile.”

And then, because thirty-seven years of ninth graders does not switch off: “You slept like someone who needed it. Don’t apologize for that. I’ve had two husbands and neither one of them could do it.”

Jess’s first thought is that she needs to call her friends. Her second thought is that her phone is dead. Her third thought is that she has no idea where her friends are camped exactly — she knows it’s near the main field, she knows there’s a blue shade structure, she knows they might be near what Oliver described as “the tree that looks like it’s judging you” — and that finding them at 5:14 a.m. without a phone is going to require walking, and Marlene’s chamomile is very good.

She drinks the chamomile.

Marlene asks about the weekend.

They talk for an hour about things that have nothing to do with festival culture and everything to do with what it means to show up for something you love even when showing up is expensive and complicated and occasionally involves sleeping in a stranger’s tent because your body decided before your brain did that it was time to stop.

At 6:28 a.m., Jess walks back to the group.

The group has not slept.

The group has spent four hours searching, texting, calling, visiting the medical tent (twice), visiting the lost and found (once), visiting the festival safety desk (once, where they learned that a missing person report requires a full name and ideally a wristband number, neither of which they had), arguing about whose fault the no-preparation situation was, having a separate argument about whether it was Karen or Carol, and sitting in a state of sustained low-grade dread that the chapter has been building toward since 1:47 a.m.

When Jess walks into camp, Oliver starts crying.

Not performing crying. The specific release of someone whose nervous system has been held at red alert for four hours and is now, very suddenly, no longer at red alert.

**Jess says:** “I’m so sorry, my phone died—”

**Oliver says:** “What is your last name.”

**Jess says:** “What?”

**Oliver says:** “Your last name. What is it.”

**Jess says:** “…Okonkwo?”

**Noor says:** “O.”

**Wes says:** “It starts with an O. I was close.”

He was not close.

The argument about Karen versus Carol will continue for approximately three more weeks, unresolved, until Devon accidentally finds Jess’s mother’s Facebook profile and the question is definitively answered, and the answer will not be Karen or Carol, and Wes and Oliver will look at each other and decide, mutually and without discussion, never to speak of it again.

The chapter’s discernment hook is not about a Wook.

There is no Wook in this chapter’s cold open. Jess is fine. Marlene is lovely. The night ended with chamomile and a conversation about what it means to show up for something you love.

The Wook in Chapter 21 is the cultural pressure that made the fifteen-minute meeting in the car feel like overkill. The Wook is the “we’ll find each other” lie that the scene has been telling itself for decades, the specific optimistic delusion that festival magic will handle logistics the way festival magic handles everything else. The Wook is chill, it’ll be fine, we always find each other — said by people who have always found each other, in the same way the guy at the batch said his source was solid, in the same way Jordan assumed the friend-of-a-friend was fine, in the same way Nadia took the bag because probably nothing.

The Wook in this chapter is the preparation that did not happen.

This chapter teaches the preparation that happens instead.
