### TRACK 04 — THE FOUR-HOUR MARK
*(acute search escalation protocol)*

Here's the behavior. Nobody in the group knows when “I’m a little worried” becomes “we need to go to festival safety right now.”

This is the timeline-ambiguity failure. In the absence of a stated threshold, the group negotiates the threshold in real time, under stress, with incomplete information, while simultaneously arguing about whose fault the preparation failure was and whether her mom is Karen or Carol. The negotiation produces a later escalation than the protocol would, because the group keeps waiting for certainty before escalating, and certainty is specifically what the group does not have.

In search and rescue practice, the escalation threshold is named in advance because naming it in advance prevents the negotiations at 1:47 a.m. The threshold is the four-hour mark. Not because four hours is a magic number — because four hours is the outer edge of “plausibly fine and phone-dead” and the entry point of “this situation requires institutional support.”

Four hours is the chapter’s protocol and it is also a rough guideline that should be compressed when: the person is a first-timer, the person has a medical condition, the person was last seen in the company of an unknown person, or the phone going to voicemail is unusual for them specifically.

The tall-beard description at the silent disco is a reason to compress the threshold. Wes saw Jess at eight-fifty in the company of an unknown person. That is specific information. That information should have produced an escalation at midnight, not at 1:47 a.m., because Jess was last confirmed to be with a stranger and the stranger has no name.

Here's the read on it. Name the threshold before you need it. Not in the moment. Before.

“If we haven’t heard from each other by [time] after last contact, the next step is [escalation step].”

That sentence, said once, in the car, before the gate, makes the 1:47 a.m. negotiation unnecessary.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Named Threshold Protocol. **Step 1 — Before the event:** name the threshold for each person. “If I haven’t heard from [person] in four hours and they’re not at the meet-up spot: medical tent.”

Step 2 — Compress the threshold when any of these apply:

First-timer

Known medical condition

Last seen with an unknown person

Phone-to-voicemail is unusual for them

The person mentioned being tired, unwell, or in a vulnerable state

Step 3 — At the threshold, escalate immediately and in this order: (1) medical tent, ask if they’re a patient; (2) lost and found; (3) festival safety desk. All three. In that order. Do not do them sequentially hoping number one produces the answer — do them in a thirty-minute sweep.

Step 4 — At the festival safety desk, have ready: legal name, approximate age and description, last known location, last known companion description, wristband number. These are the things the desk needs. These are the things the Emergency Card provides.

Step 5 — If the festival safety desk produces nothing and you have now crossed six hours since last contact: contact county services. This is the step that nobody wants to be the one to take. It is also the step that saves people.

Quick vibe check. Most four-hour missing-friend situations resolve in exactly the way Jess’s did — the person is asleep, or phone-dead, or lost in the magic of a particular set, and they show up fine at the meet-up spot at the four-hour mark looking well-rested and confused about why everyone is emotional. This is the desired outcome. The escalation protocol exists for the situations that don’t resolve this way. Running the protocol when it turns out to be unnecessary costs you thirty minutes. Not running the protocol when it was necessary costs considerably more.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever been the missing Jess — the person who let their phone die, missed the check-in, didn’t come to the meet-up spot, and came back four hours later to a group that had been running on adrenaline and guilt? If yes: the card and the battery and the meet-up spot are things you can give your group before the next event. The gift is in the fifteen minutes.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Camp Lead:** Your camp is a node for missing-person information. Build a protocol: any camp member who has information about another camp member’s location or safety status reports it to the camp lead, who maintains a running awareness of who is where. This is not surveillance. It is what “we’re a family” should actually mean operationally.

**DanceSafe and Harm-Reduction Volunteers:** You are often the first point of contact for a group conducting an informal search. Your awareness of whether someone has come through your tent is safety infrastructure. Know the festival safety desk’s protocol and be able to refer groups directly.

This runs in every direction. The four-hour mark applies the same way whether the missing person is the camp veteran, the first-timer, the sober anchor’s own partner, or the friend everyone assumed was with someone else. It applies equally regardless of the identity of the missing person or the group conducting the search. The escalation protocol works the same way across all demographics. The information needs at the safety desk are the same.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The employee who is a no-show, no-call and nobody knows what to do or when to call HR or how long to wait before calling their emergency contact — because that threshold was never named. Most organizations have a protocol. Most groups of friends don’t. The protocol should exist in both places.
