## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 WOOK POV*

The DanceSafe pin is on my snapback.

Not ironically. I want to be clear about that because the word ironically will occur to some people and I need them to understand that the word does not apply. I support DanceSafe. I read their materials. I update my Telegram harm-reduction FAQ after every festival based on what I observe at the medical tents, which is more field research than most of the people at DanceSafe’s informational table have ever done, with respect to those people, who are doing genuinely important work. I have Narcan in the Sprinter. Three units, not one. The Narcan is real. The pin is real. I am, by any meaningful operational metric, running one of the most harm-reduction-aware supply operations in the western US festival circuit.

My name is Wedge.

I am forty-seven. I have been doing this for nineteen years. My Sprinter is a 2022 high-roof cargo, custom interior, two locking compartments, refrigeration unit I installed myself because temperature stability matters for pressed pills and also because I bring very good cheese and the cheese deserves the same respect as the product. The Sprinter is clean in every sense of the word. It does not smell like what you would expect it to smell like. It smells like sandalwood car air freshener and the specific clean-chemical smell of the food-safe containers I use for storage, which I ordered from a restaurant supply company and which are, incidentally, NSF-certified, which is a certification that applies to food safety and which I find provides a certain aesthetic coherence to the operation.

It is 3:12 p.m. on Friday at a 40,000-person bass weekend in the Mojave.

My Telegram has been firing since eleven a.m.

The accounting, as of 3:12 p.m.:

Sixty-eight grams pressed molly. MDMA content verified via Marquis, fentanyl negative, pressed at a trusted lab on the West Coast, consistent yield across the batch. I test everything I move. I test it with the proper kits, I photograph the results, I keep the photos in a folder organized by batch number and date. If something were to go wrong — and in nineteen years, my product has not been the cause of a serious adverse event, which is a record I protect with genuine professional pride — I would have documentation. The documentation is part of the operation. The documentation is part of being one of the good ones.

Twenty-two grams clean K. Same protocol. Tested. Verified. I communicate the harm-reduction guidance with this one specifically because ketamine has dose-dependency concerns that MDMA does not, and I provide accurate dosing information per transaction. Not because I am legally required to. Because it is right, and because customers who are not in the medical tent are return customers, and return customers are good business, and good business and harm reduction are, in my operation, the same thing. People think these values are in tension. In my operation they are identical.

Fourteen grams cut K.

This is the one I do not discuss in the FAQ.

The word for it in the trade is stepping on it. You step on a thing to make it go further. Everyone in the supply chain above me steps on everything, which is the entire reason my testing protocol exists in the first place, and there is a version of that sentence where that fact does moral work on my behalf.

I have used that version of the sentence. I am using it right now.

This is the one going to four specific returning customers whose tolerance has climbed over the past two cycles to a point where the clean product is producing diminishing returns on their satisfaction, and where the cut product — it is cut with a common dissociative adulterant at a ratio that I have personally calibrated to produce approximately eighty percent of the subjective experience at sixty-five percent of the active compound — will, in my assessment, meet their needs at a margin that allows me to maintain pricing parity while managing my supply costs.

They do not know it is cut.

I know it is cut.

The ratio is safe, in my assessment, based on my nineteen years of experience and my specific knowledge of these four customers’ histories. I am not guessing. I have data. I have used this ratio before with customers at similar tolerance profiles. Nothing bad has happened.

I have not disclosed this to them because disclosure would require an explanation that would damage the relationship, and the relationship is what the long game runs on, and the long game is why I am still here at forty-seven with a perfect adverse event record and an operation that has survived nineteen years of the festival circuit. Disclosure would be, in a practical sense, self-defeating. The four customers would be upset not because of harm — the ratio is safe — but because of the framing. The framing would damage trust. The trust is the product, as much as the K.

I have made a professional calculation.

I make professional calculations every day.

This is one of them.

At 4:08 p.m. I take a smoke break outside the Sprinter.

I do not smoke. I take smoke breaks anyway because the smoke-break posture — outside, cigarette or vape in hand, slightly looking at nothing — is the universal signal for I am not conducting business right now, I am just a person standing outside a van. The smoke break is the visible inactivity that makes the invisible activity easier. I have a vape that I use for this purpose. It produces a vapor that smells like mango and contains zero nicotine. I am one of the good ones in this respect also.

Two women in their mid-twenties are sitting on beanbags fifteen feet from me. They are talking about someone they encountered last night. The description they are giving, in the specific warm-indignant tone of people recounting something that did not reach the level of reportable harm but that left a residue, is of a man who offered them something, would not take their first no, kept moving closer, suggested he had a “private spot” where they could “really talk.”

This is not me. My operation does not involve proximity pressure. If someone says no to what I’m offering, I note it as a preference and move on. I do not push. Pushing is amateur. Pushing is how you get flagged.

The description continues. The man was last seen near the art installations on the east side, they say. He has a specific look — they describe the look. I know the look. I know the person. He is one of three freelance operators I track as displacement risk in the Mojave circuit. He is, of all the freelance operators I track, the most visible and the most problematic. He operates without the discretion that nineteen years of professional practice produces. He makes scenes. He leaves residue. He is the reason harm-reduction organizations associate supply with predation, which creates a cultural environment that is bad for everyone including me.

I take out my phone.

I have a photo of him from an event last summer, taken when he did not know I was taking it, which I have been keeping in a folder labeled “competitors - general” for exactly this kind of situation. I have the parking location of his vehicle. I have the plate number. I have, because I maintain a reasonably thorough operational intelligence function, the number of a festival safety contact I have cultivated over three years of mutual professional courtesy — I tip them when I see something, they do not look too carefully at the Sprinter.

I compose a message. I am not reporting a competitor. I am not protecting market share. I am providing safety information about a documented predatory actor to festival safety personnel who have the authority and the means to act on it. These two things are simultaneous and they are not in conflict. They are, in my accounting of this moment, the same thing.

I hit send.

By Saturday afternoon, he will be removed from the festival.

The women on the beanbags, and every other woman at this festival who would have encountered him between now and his Sunday departure, will be safe from whatever he would have done.

The man who tipped security will be, in the festival safety team’s internal record, a concerned member of the supply community who helps keep the festival safe.

That concerned member is me.

I am one of the good ones.

I am a very good one.

I am, in the specific ledger I maintain in the part of my brain where the accounting happens, one of the best ones at being one of the good ones.

Here is what the chapter needs to say about Wedge before it says anything else:

Everything in his ledger is true.

The Narcan is real. The testing is real. The documentation is real. The harm-reduction FAQ is real and it is actually good and several people have cited it in their own event materials. The competitor he reported is genuinely predatory and is genuinely better off removed from this festival. The nineteen-year adverse-event-free record is real. The professional pride he takes in the operation is genuine. By the standards of most supply operations in most festival contexts, Wedge is running a materially safer, more harm-reduction-conscious, more operationally disciplined business than the alternatives.

None of that is the point.

**The point is:** he is downgrading four customers’ K without telling them.

**The point is:** the people who become dependent on him become dependent on him specifically because he has engineered the dependency. The “I got you” character he performs is not separate from the supply. The supply is the delivery mechanism for the character, and the character is the delivery mechanism for the dependency, and the dependency is what makes people return, and what makes people return is what keeps the margins working.

**The point is:** the warm, competent, DanceSafe-pin-wearing, Narcan-carrying, harm-reduction-FAQ-writing supply operator is the hardest version of this chapter to see because the 95% that is genuinely good makes the 5% that is predatory invisible. And the 5% that is predatory is not smaller in impact because it is 5%. It is larger, because the cover is the safety community’s own infrastructure.

You cannot test for the cut K with a Marquis kit. Marquis tests for the presence of the active compound, not the ratio. You can only find out the ratio is wrong when you buy from someone else and the same dose lands differently.

Or when Wedge decides you’re a high-trust returning customer whose tolerance has climbed to a point where the professional calculation says: cut it.

This is the discernment hook.

Not “is the plug good or bad” — the categories collapse. This plug is both. The discernment hook is: “what is this person’s relationship to my dependency?”

A real vendor wants you informed, capable, and free to buy elsewhere. A Wook plug wants you dependent, trusting, and unable to evaluate what you are actually receiving because the relationship has been specifically engineered to make that evaluation socially costly.

The DanceSafe pin is on the snapback.

The snapback is on Wedge.

Wedge is one of the good ones.

And he is building you a cage.
