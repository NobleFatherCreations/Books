## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

The Sprinter is a 2021 Mercedes passenger van, black, driven by a guy named Boyd who does not talk and does not make eye contact and has a cooler wedged between the front seats that nobody is supposed to touch. The cooler says FRAGILE on the side in tape. Whatever is fragile in the cooler is not being addressed.

Solenne Baptiste is twenty-six years old and she has been a DJ for four years and she has her first artist wristband on her left wrist and it is the plastic kind, not the paper kind, which means she is officially on the lineup and she can walk through the artist entrance and none of this has fully arrived in her body yet because she is sitting in a van on the way to a pre-party with five women she does not know, driven by a man who will not look at her, to a location she has a general sense of — hills, north of the festival site, big house — and that is all she knows.

Rex is not in the van. Rex sent the van.

Rex is the promoter who books two of the three festivals she needs to break the year. She met him at a warehouse show in Silver Lake in February. He stayed for her whole set, which almost nobody does on the second floor at a warehouse show where the main act is downstairs. He found her after and said: You’ve got something, I want to hear more from you. He gave her his card. She texted the next day. He took four days to respond, then called, talked for forty-five minutes about the scene, about what he was building, about how he had eyes for talent in a way that most promoters didn’t because most promoters were chasing plays instead of making them. He said he was thinking about putting her on the lineup at Meridian this summer. He said it like it was not quite certain yet, which made her want it more.

That is Lever One. She does not know it yet.

Her mother calls on Sundays at ten and Solenne has not missed one in four years, including the Sunday she had food poisoning and took the call sitting on a bathroom floor in Reseda.

She has been awake since five-thirty a.m. She drove three hours to the festival from her apartment in Echo Park, dropped her setup at the artist lot, found her camping spot, built nothing because she will deal with it after the party, and then the text from Rex came: pre-party at the house, sending a car, be at the artist entrance at 7. She had one hour. She ate half a protein bar, put on the outfit, said yes.

In the van, minute three, she looks at the other women’s wrists and does the math. She has the artist band. The others have general admission with a gold add-on. She is the only one with the plastic kind. She registers this — the way you register a word that doesn’t fit the sentence — and decides not to figure out what it means yet.

That is her first mistake. Not a catastrophic one. Just — the mistake of a smart person who wants something too much to ask the inconvenient question. She knows something is slightly off. She decides it can wait.

It can’t wait. But that’s chapter two. She’ll learn it here.

The hills house is the kind of place that exists in every music scene in every city with mountains and money adjacent to it: Mediterranean-ish, stucco, pool that someone is definitely not licensed to have this many people in, string lights, palm trees doing nothing wrong just standing there being palm trees while everything below them goes sideways. The kitchen has a marble island the length of a small runway. On the marble island, arranged in three neat parallel lines on a black slate tile like a catering display at an investor dinner, is cocaine. Not hidden. Not secreted away in a back bathroom like a normal guilty habit. Just: here it is, welcome to the pre-party, the coke is between the olives and the charcuterie.

Rex is at the door.

He is forty-three, which he presents as thirty-seven through the aggressive use of skin care and an asymmetric haircut. He has a Patagonia vest over a shirt that costs what Solenne makes in a week teaching Ableton to undergrads. He hugs her with one arm and a shoulder-press that says you belong here and I’m in a hurry simultaneously, which is the hug of someone who has perfected the hug as a tool.

“You made it. Good. Come on, there’s someone you need to meet.”

Someone you need to meet is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Rex uses it to create forward momentum. She is already following him before she has decided to follow him. The house has seven rooms visible from the entry and a hallway off the kitchen with a door labeled THE TALENT ROOM in a printed sign, laminated, like someone made it at an office supply store, which they did. She doesn’t ask about the talent room. She follows Rex to the pool deck.

Three people in the pool, two on sun loungers, a DJ on a small rig running a tech house set off a single laptop, not even a controller, just the laptop, which means either this DJ knows something the rest of the world doesn’t, or they are a placeholder while the real situation develops.

At the far end of the pool deck, in a chair that has been positioned at a slight remove from everyone else — not isolated, just elevated by furniture geometry — there is a man.

He is in his early fifties. Linen shirt open to the sternum, which is where all the consequential decisions happen. Tan that is not from the sun, the kind of even, maintained tan that comes from a booth or a trip to somewhere that is not Los Angeles. A ring on his right hand, middle finger, gold, heavy. He is holding a glass of something amber that he turns slowly while he talks to the person in the chair beside him, who is nodding.

Rex steers Solenne toward him with one hand on her elbow, which she allows because she is still in the social physics of the moment, still in the stream of it.

“Marcus, this is Solenne. She’s one of the artists I was telling you about.”

Marcus looks at her with the particular attention of someone who has looked at a lot of people this way and knows what the look communicates.

“I caught your set at Lightning,” he says. “You’re sharp.”

Solenne has never played Lightning in the Valley. She has never played Lightning anywhere. She has applied twice to Lightning and been rejected both times, which still stings a little in the small-hours part of her chest where she keeps her insecurities.

She does not correct him. She says: “Thanks.”

This is mistake number two. But honestly — honestly — what are you going to say? Actually I’ve never played Lightning, you may be confusing me with someone else, do you want to revise your opinion of me? No. You say thanks. You let the compliment land even though something inside you knows the footing is wrong. You let it land because you are twenty-six and you are hungry and some part of your brain has already done the math on what a Marcus saying you’re sharp could mean for the next three years of your career.

**Marcus says:** “Hungry is when you make moves. Working hard is just showing up. You hungry?”

It is not a question. It is a diagnostic. He is not asking if she wants food. He is asking if she will go along with the frame he is about to build: that she is hungry, that he has what hunger wants, that the relationship between her hunger and his resources is the context for everything that comes next.

**She says:** “Always.”

That is the first yes of the night that costs something. She does not know this yet.

Rex brings her a Topo Chico. It is already open. He sets it in her hand and says hydrate, baby in a way that sounds like affection but is actually a transaction — here, I am providing something your body needs, you are now slightly in receipt of my care.

She drinks it because she is thirsty.

The cocaine is still on the marble inside. Nobody has commented on it. The talent room door is still closed with the guy outside it. The other five women from the van are distributed across the party in the particular pattern of people who have been here before and are doing their jobs.

Marcus tells her about his curated artist development thing. He uses the word curated four times in six minutes, which is three too many, but Solenne is doing the math on the opportunity and not auditing the vocabulary. The development thing has a buy-in. It is twenty-five. He does not say thousand. The thousand is so assumed, in the architecture of this sentence, that to specify it would be like explaining to someone at the checkout that their total includes tax. Of course it includes tax. Of course it’s twenty-five thousand.

Two slots open, he says. He’s meeting with two other people tomorrow morning.

She has been here forty-seven minutes.

She has eaten nothing but half a protein bar and drunk one opened Topo Chico and had three sips of something amber from a glass someone pressed on her near the pool. She is not impaired. She is not high. She is just: in a house with a man who controls two-thirds of the festivals she needs, being told there are two slots and he is meeting two other people tomorrow morning.

The Sprinter that drove her here is in the driveway.

She does not have another ride.

The festival is forty minutes away.

**She thinks:** I will say I’ll think about it and get back to him. She thinks this while already calculating whether she can get the money together by Monday. She thinks: This is probably fine. She thinks: Why does it feel like the room is smaller than it was when I walked in?

The room is the same size. She is not. She has been compressed, very gently, through twelve levers that did their work so quietly she cannot identify where one ended and the next one started.

She does not have a ride that is not the Sprinter, and the Sprinter is Rex’s, and the festival is forty minutes of dark hills away.

She Venmos eight hundred dollars at 11:52 p.m. from the edge of the pool deck — a partial hold on one of the two slots, everything left in her checking account after rent — and tells Marcus the rest comes Friday, once a client payment clears. Marcus says of course, no rush, and he means it the way a landlord means no rush on an account he has already decided to collect.

Rex hugs her on the way out. Same hug. One arm, shoulder-press, *you belong here.*

Monday, nothing. Wednesday, nothing. Thursday she sends a plain *checking in on the timeline* and watches it sit on delivered for six hours and then stop saying delivered at all, because a number that has already paid does not need to stay reachable.

She never gets the eight hundred back. She never gets the slot. She plays four warehouse sets that summer, none of them Meridian, and she is a month late on rent twice before September. She misses three Sundays in a row and tells her mother it is a scheduling thing.

She does not tell the story for a long time, because the story she would have to tell is not the story she wants people to have about her judgment.

Solenne Baptiste is twenty-six years old and she is sharp and she is good at her job. None of that is what the twelve levers were built to test.

That is the chapter.

That is every chapter.

The twelve levers don’t announce themselves. They just make the room smaller. And they do it fast enough that by the time you notice the walls, you are already deciding what you owe.
