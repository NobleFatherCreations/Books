## Tales From The Booth — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist.

Camille Tran is twenty-seven. She DJs under the name Haze Station. She has one release on a small label out of Berlin, a Boiler Room set that performed better than expected, and a booking agent who is doing his best with what he has, which is a modest roster and a phone that works too well at 10 p.m. on weeknights.

**The offer comes through the agent:** pre-party before Meridian, Rex Kaufman hosting, someone wants to meet you. Camille knows Rex Kaufman’s name. She also knows two DJs who have done shows through Rex who she has been meaning to call about it. She has not called. She has been putting it off in the way you put off things that might give you information you don’t want.

She calls both of them before she gets in the van.

**DJ one:** Rex is fine, he’s chaotic but his events are real. Marcus Veld is a different story. If Marcus Veld is at the party, get the written terms before you get in the car. I made the mistake of not doing that. It cost me. DJ two: I don’t know any Marcus Veld. But Rex sends a lot of vans to a lot of pre-parties and not all of them are about music. Take a friend if you can.

She cannot take a friend on this one. But she has her information.

**She texts her agent:** Can you find out if someone named Marcus Veld is connected to this? Her agent texts back twenty minutes later: Marcus Veld — artist management and development, some music, some real estate. Couple of complaints on music forums, nothing formal. Do you want me to ask Rex what the ask is before you go? She says yes.

**Rex texts back:** No ask, just introductions. Her agent texts Rex: Great. Camille’s excited. What are the written terms for the evening? Rex does not respond.

Camille goes anyway — because she wants the Meridian slot and she is not going to let wariness cost her a legitimate opportunity — but she goes with three things in place: she has her own car, she has texted her roommate the address, and she has a thirty-minute exit ready at any point in the night (I have an early load-in, I have to be back by eleven).

At the party, Marcus says he caught her at Lightning.

**She says:** “I’ve never played Lightning. You might be thinking of someone else — I’ve been on a smaller circuit. Which Lightning was it?”

Marcus pauses. Smiles. Says: “You know, I see so many shows, I might have it mixed up. I’ve been following your work online.”

“Okay,” she says. “That makes sense.”

She stays warm. She stays engaged. She does not write him off. She asks questions. When the offer comes — twenty-five thousand, two slots, Monday deadline — she says: “I’m interested. Can you email me the written terms? My agent reviews everything before I sign.”

Marcus says there are no written terms, it’s an informal arrangement.

**She says:** “That’s fine. Can we put the informal arrangement in an email so we’re both clear on what we’ve agreed to?”

He says that’s not really how he works.

**She says:** “I understand. I work that way, though. If that’s a problem, no hard feelings.”

She stays for another forty minutes. She eats some of the food. She has a real conversation with a sound engineer who gives her two genuinely useful contacts and no invoice. She leaves at ten-fifty using her load-in excuse. She drives herself home.

The Meridian slot does not materialize through Rex.

The Meridian slot materializes nine months later through the sound engineer she met at the party, who passed her name to the booking team after hearing her set at a warehouse show in Oakland. She is paid for the slot. There is a written contract. Her agent reviewed it.

**The watermelon moment:** she is standing in the kitchen of the hills house eating something off a catering tray, and Marcus is looking at her across the pool deck with the expression of a man who has run this particular play a number of times and can tell when it is not going to land, and she feels it — in her chest, in the warm specific place below her sternum — the difference between wanting the thing and being willing to pay any price for the thing. The difference is not dramatic. It is just: a line she can see now that she looked for it.

She eats the food. She gets her contacts. She goes home. She keeps her career.
