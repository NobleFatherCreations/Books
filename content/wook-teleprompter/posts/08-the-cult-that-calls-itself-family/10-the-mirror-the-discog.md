## The Mirror + The Discog

HAVE YOU BEEN THE WOOK? Mirror Check At The Trailer

You have been in Lorelei’s camp, or you have been Lorelei, or you have been Marisol sitting on the cushions while Dinah was on her knees, or you have been Ash watching and staying quiet because the calculation said quiet was safer.

Have you ever called a newcomer “family” before they’d earned it, because naming the family was useful to you? Have you ever controlled a resource — the chat, the budget, the passes, the relationships — and declined to share it, not because sharing would hurt the camp, but because sharing would reduce your leverage? Have you ever felt the warmth toward a camp member diminish after they asked a question you found inconvenient, and attributed the cooling to something about them? Have you ever sat on cushions while other people did the work and told yourself you were contributing in other ways?

The Camp Cartel does not require a villain in linen. It requires a culture that has mistaken the performance of values for the practice of them, a power structure that accumulated quietly over years of competent caregiving, and a social environment where asking about the bank account is treated as a betrayal of the magic.

Most of the people running it are not bad people. They are people who found a structure that worked for them and stopped questioning whether it worked for everyone else.

The question is not “have you ever run one of these Tracks.” The question is: when you are next on cushions while someone is on their knees, do you see the twelve feet between you?

Do you walk it?

Here's the Wook Discog for this chapter. Studio Debut — The Baby Wook learning the moves

Lorelei in year one, 2016. She genuinely built something. She coordinated the storage unit and the truck and the organizer relationships and the build plan because she was the one who wanted it enough to do it. She genuinely called it family because she genuinely felt it. The control was functional, not political. The power was informal because it was too early to formalize. The first season was real. The studio debut sounds like exactly what it was: someone who loved the scene enough to build a thing and called everyone in it family because they felt like family. That is not a Track. That is a foundation. The Track started when the foundation calcified into architecture.

Live Album — The Seasoned Operator

Lorelei in year seven. The manifesto has been updated. The Instagram has a distinct visual brand. The first-season funnel is running with the efficiency of something that has been refined through repetition. The Soft Exile has taken three people. The budget has not been seen by the full camp in four years. The vocabulary is fluent enough that any concern can be managed, any question can be reframed, any discomfort can be held in the container until it stops being inconvenient. Lorelei has not made a single explicit decision to run a cartel. She has made a thousand small decisions to maintain her position, each one individually defensible, collectively constituting a structure that serves her and costs Dinah and Hugo and Tomás years. The Live Album sounds like community. It is governance.

Greatest Hits Compilation — The Institutional Scaled-Up Version

The festival within the festival. The camp that is large enough to have its own infrastructure: its own sound system, its own kitchen operation, its own healing village, its own volunteer coordination, its own social media presence, its own alumni network, its own physical infrastructure worth tens of thousands of dollars. The cartel at this scale is not run by one person. It is run by a board of four people who are all inner ring and none of whom have significant accountability to the three hundred people who constitute the camp’s middle ring and first-season arrivals. The budget is a nonprofit. The founder salary is real. The volunteer labor is classified as “contribution.” The wristband allocation is a staff meeting agenda item that the three hundred people have never been invited to. The Greatest Hits Compilation is profiled in scene media twice a year. The people who left are occasionally described as “not being ready for the container.”
