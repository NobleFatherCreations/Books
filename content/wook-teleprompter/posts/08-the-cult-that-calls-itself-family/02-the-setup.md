## The Setup

Here's the soundboard quote for this chapter. “Every camp that says ‘we don’t have a hierarchy’ has a hierarchy. You can tell because the people at the top say it the most, in matching outfits, while the people who actually own the trailer are still looking for the long bolts.” — Reggie “Wingnut” Okonkwo, fifty-two, build lead and owner of the actual truck, fifteen years welding shade structures across three states and two countries, current holder of the title “the person who knows where everything is” in every camp he has ever worked with and the one person in every camp whose opinion on the shade structure is sought entirely after it is built. Has worked with fourteen camps. Has identified the Camp Cartel pattern in eleven of them. Has left four camps, one after the conversation with the camp lead and three without. Currently co-running a small build camp called Service Road that has a four-sentence manifesto and a shared spreadsheet where anyone can see the budget. Recorded Sunday morning at strike while on his hands and knees under a frame looking for a clevis pin while three people in matching saffron discussed the “container” eight feet away.

Here's what this chapter is actually about. This chapter is about how camps — the thing this scene invented that is genuinely one of its most extraordinary gifts, the chosen family structure that has held people through divorces and deaths and sobriety milestones and gender transitions and thirty-year friendships — get turned into governance structures by people who would never tolerate being called a government. We are covering seven moves, and before we cover them we are saying clearly: camps are not the problem. This scene would not exist without them. The truck, the trailer, the kitchen, the shade, the generator, the coffee at six a.m. for a stranger — that is real and it is worth protecting. This chapter is for protecting it from the small, mean, quiet version that puts the manifesto on the outside and runs the cartel underneath.

🎛️ THE TAPERS’ SECTION

Janis · Whisper Networks · Power Without Process

Quick translation. Then back to the dust.

**Irving Janis named groupthink in the 1970s:** the thing that happens when a small cohesive group becomes so committed to its own harmony that it stops being able to process reality. In festival language: when your camp has lost the ability to hear a true sentence because it would inconvenience the vibe, the vibe is now governing. The vibe is the king. Everyone else is a peasant in tie-dye with a parachute to fold.

Whisper networks are informal information channels people build when formal accountability systems fail them. They are sometimes literally lifesaving. They are also, sometimes, run by the exact people who created the conditions that made formal protection impossible. Both can be true. The test is whether the whisper protects the vulnerable or protects the powerful. A whisper that warns first-timers about a known predator is sacred. A whisper that runs “Dinah has been intense” through the inner circle while Dinah is on her knees at seven a.m. is governance in a soft voice.

Power without process is the concept that describes what Lorelei has. She controls the chat, the budget, the wristbands, the relationships, and the narrative — and none of it is written down, transferred, or accountable to the camp’s stated values. The power is real. The process that would make it legible and contestable does not exist. That asymmetry is not an accident. Unwritten power is unchallenged power. The HOLD SPACE / NOT GRUDGES mug is the PR department of unwritten power.

That is enough theory. Put it under the truck.
