## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

It is three a.m. and Eli Torres is twenty-two years old and his friend Marco is in the porta line and Marco’s lips are going gray.

Not like a figurative gray. The actual color gray, in the specific way that color has no business appearing on a twenty-three-year-old’s lips in a porta line at three a.m. at a festival in the desert outside of Indio. The color that makes Eli’s chest do something it has never done before, something that is not fear exactly, because fear implies you still believe things might be okay, and what Eli’s chest is doing is something past that.

Marco is leaning against the side of the porta-potty with one hand and breathing wrong. Wrong meaning: shallow, fast, with a quality of mechanical effort, like his body is doing it manually instead of automatically. His eyes are doing something with their orientation that eyes are not supposed to do. Not rolled back — more like the eyes of a person whose internal operating system has rerouted to a back process and the front-facing interface is not currently being maintained.

The molly was cut with something. Eli does not know what. Neither does Marco. A guy at the bass stage — they had talked for maybe forty-five minutes, maybe an hour, seemed like a solid dude, super warm, offered the cap on the house, you’re my dude — and Marco said yes because the guy seemed cool and they had been talking for forty-five minutes and the conversation had been good and on the house means someone is being generous to you and generous people at festivals are the whole point.

The guy at the bass stage whose face Marco can no longer quite picture.

Marco grabs Eli’s arm. His grip is stronger than makes sense for someone who is currently gray.

**He says:** “Don’t get help. Don’t call them. They’ll arrest us. Please.”

His eyes are trying to find Eli’s eyes. Most of what he finds is slightly to the left of Eli’s eyes, but the intention is there. The intention is: I am scared. I do not know what is happening to my body. And I am more scared of what happens if you ask for help than I am of what is currently happening to my body.

Eli has ninety seconds before this information needs to produce an action.

He has no training.

He does not know the recovery position. He does not know if Good Samaritan amnesty applies in the state of California, which it does — California Health and Safety Code Section 11376.5, no prosecution for possession if you call for medical assistance in good faith — but he does not know this, because nobody told him before tonight, and tonight is not the ideal moment to learn it.

He does not know where the Zendo is. He was told at the gate that there was a harm-reduction tent but he did not write it down and his phone is at eleven percent and if he opens Google Maps his phone will be at eight percent.

He does not know what Marco took because Marco does not know what Marco took because the guy at the bass stage said just molly, we’re all good with the confidence of a man who had not tested anything and knew it didn’t matter because the moral exposure was Marco’s and not his.

He does not know any of this and he has ninety seconds and Marco’s grip is getting weaker.

Behind them in the porta line, a guy in a T. rex costume waits with the patience of a monk who has either achieved genuine enlightenment or has been wearing the T. rex costume long enough that patience is now load-bearing infrastructure. The T. rex is not small. The T. rex arms are comically inadequate. The T. rex’s overall presence is one of benign, tremendous, Jurassic equanimity.

Behind the T. rex, a couple from Tustin is running through the entire course of their marriage at full volume. I told you about the credit card, Greg. I told you in March, Greg. I told you in fucking March. Greg appears to have a view on this. Greg’s view is being treated as inadmissible.

The T. rex has decided to be the line, not in the line.

A girl in a unicorn onesie — the good kind, the one-piece with the hood and the horn, not the aspirational kind — breaks from approximately four people back in the line. She has been watching for thirty seconds. She looks at Marco. She looks at Eli. She looks at Marco again.

**She says:** “Do you need help?”

Eli looks at her.

He looks at Marco.

He looks at the T. rex, who has lifted one tiny arm in a gesture that is either a wave or an indictment or, possibly, the benediction of a creature that has survived two extinction events and knows something about the ratio of crisis to available response time.

The ninety seconds are almost up.

This is the moment the chapter has been building to. Not the crisis — the crisis was manufactured upstream by a man whose face Marco can no longer quite picture, who handed a cap to someone he had talked to for forty-five minutes with the warmth of a man who is genuinely warm and also does not test his product and also would like to not be named in any account of what happens next.

The crisis is downstream of the play. The bad trip is not the chapter’s subject. The giver-with-no-receipts is the chapter’s subject. The person who creates the condition and is gone before the condition manifests. The on the house, you’re my dude that costs nothing to say and costs someone else the gray lips at three a.m. in the porta line.

But the crisis is where you are. And the person in the unicorn onesie is asking if you need help. And you have one decision to make and it is very simple:

Say yes.

Here is what the chapter teaches you:

Say yes.

Say yes to the girl in the unicorn onesie. Say yes to the harm-reduction volunteer. Say yes to the ranger. Say yes to the medic. Say yes to the DanceSafe table. Say yes to the Zendo. Say yes to the person who asks if you need help, because the person who asks if you need help at three a.m. in the porta line at a festival in the desert is the immune system in a unicorn onesie, and the immune system is trying to save your friend.

Good Samaritan amnesty exists. Know it before tonight. Know which state you are in. Know the four words to say to the medic when you get there: “We don’t know what.” Know the recovery position. Know where the medical tent is. Know that the arrest you are afraid of is less likely than the harm you are watching happen and that the system you are afraid of is less dangerous, in this specific moment, than the color of your friend’s lips.

And know that the man whose face Marco can no longer quite picture — the warm, generous, forty-five-minute friend who handed over the cap on the house — is the one who put you here. His warmth was real. His generosity was real. And he did not test the batch. And those two things coexist without resolving, which is the chapter’s discernment hook and also the hardest part of the scene to name without sounding like you are attacking the culture:

Eli said yes.

Her name was Robin, she had a nursing-school ID in her fanny pack, and she got Marco onto his side and the Zendo onto the radio inside ninety seconds. He was in the medical tent by 3:24 and on a saline drip by 3:40 and he woke up angry and embarrassed and alive at nine in the morning. He does not remember any of it.

Eli remembers all of it. Eli specifically remembers the ninety seconds where he did not yet know what he was going to do.

The Free Cap Funnel is real. The warmth that operates it is also real. And you are standing in the porta line at three a.m. with the evidence of both.
