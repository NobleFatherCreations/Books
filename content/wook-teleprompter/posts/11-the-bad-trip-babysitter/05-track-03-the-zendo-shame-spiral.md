### TRACK 03 — THE ZENDO SHAME SPIRAL
*(shame-induced help avoidance)*

Here's the behavior. Marco’s grip is on Eli’s arm. Don’t get help. Don’t call them. They’ll arrest us.

This is the Zendo Shame Spiral. It is the most dangerous Track in the chapter because it operates at exactly the moment when the intervention is most needed, and it operates using the exact levers that are most powerful on someone who loves the person in crisis: their fear of consequences, their loyalty to the person asking, and their own knowledge that they are also holding something they do not want searched.

The Spiral is not always deliberate. Marco is not running a play on Eli. Marco is scared and the fear is finding its way out through the least-threatening-seeming sentence it can locate in a brain that is currently not running at full capacity. Don’t get help is the sentence that feels like the least loss in this moment because Marco is not calculating the alternatives clearly — he is calculating through fear with a mind that has been altered by an untested substance.

But the sentence is also wrong.

And the chapter needs you to know it is wrong with the same clarity you know the recovery position, so that when you are in the moment, when the grip is on your arm and the gray is on the lips, you do not have to calculate. You have already decided.

Here's the read on it. Good Samaritan amnesty exists in all fifty US states. The specific protections vary. The coverage, in most states, includes the person in crisis and the person who calls for help. Neither is prosecuted for possession when calling for medical assistance in good faith. This is not a guarantee — the law has limits and implementation varies — but it is the law, and the law is better than you think it is.

**The more important read:** the worst thing that happens at the medical tent is awkward. The worst thing that happens in the porta line is the word the Bunk Police volunteer says by looking at the ground.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Amnesty Override. One rule. Know it before you go in. Tattoo it somewhere readable at three a.m.:

When there is a medical emergency, call for help. The amnesty is real. The alternative is worse.

Step 1 — When the person in crisis says “don’t get help”: say “I hear you. I’m getting help anyway.”

Step 2 — Say it with love. Say it without apology. Say it the way you would say it to someone you love who is telling you something that is not true in a moment when they cannot assess the truth.

Step 3 — Get help. Medical tent. Ranger. Harm-reduction volunteer. The nearest person with a vest. The unicorn onesie who is already asking.

Step 4 — When you reach the medical resource: say the four words. “We don’t know what.” Those four words are the most important words in the chapter. They give the medic the information they need to respond correctly, and they give you the language to communicate quickly without having to reconstruct the full narrative.

Step 5 — Stay. Do not leave the person in crisis with the medical resource and walk away. Stay within earshot. Be present. Answer questions. Your loyalty is not in the porta line. Your loyalty is in staying.

Quick vibe check. The fear of legal consequence at festivals is not irrational. People have been arrested at festivals. Law enforcement does operate at festivals. The fear is real data. It is also, in most states, less likely to produce serious legal harm than the medical outcome of untreated toxic ingestion. Weigh the actual risks.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever been the person who said “don’t get help” when you were in crisis because you were afraid? Have you ever asked someone to protect you from consequences in a moment when the consequences were less dangerous than the situation? You were scared. The sentence was still wrong. And you are allowed to update it for next time.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **BIPOC Festie:** The fear of law enforcement is higher and more grounded in lived experience for BIPOC festival participants. Harm-reduction organizations including Zendo, DanceSafe, and MAPS have been building non-law-enforcement crisis response specifically because this fear is real and has historically prevented people from getting help. Know which harm-reduction resources at your event operate independently of law enforcement before you go in.

**Undocumented Festie:** The interaction with medical services when documentation status is uncertain is a real and specific fear. Harm-reduction tents and medical tents are not immigration enforcement. They are not required to report and most explicitly do not. Ask the harm-reduction volunteer about their protocol before you need medical help.

This runs in every direction. The shame that produces the Spiral can hit the first-timer worried her parents will find out, the touring musician worried about his reputation, the undocumented festie worried about anything involving a badge, and the sober crew captain who feels like she should have caught it sooner. The stakes attached to legal consequences are not equally distributed. Both are true. The Override still applies — with awareness of whose stakes are highest.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The employee who does not report a safety incident at work because they are afraid of being blamed for it. The fear is legitimate. The consequence of not reporting is usually worse. The system that created the fear needs changing. The reporting still needs to happen.
