## The Mirror + The Discog

HAVE YOU BEEN THE WOOK? Mirror Check At The Camp Stove

The camp stove mirror in this chapter is uncomfortable to write because it is the hardest one to contain.

Most of the Have You Been The Wook sections are about behaviors that run at a spectrum of harm. This one runs at one end of that spectrum and the chapter should not pretend otherwise.

But there is something smaller to say, too.

Have you ever used family language to describe an obligation that should have been a request? Have you ever framed someone’s departure from your crew as a betrayal of the crew rather than as their legitimate choice? Have you ever installed a vocabulary in a group that made concerns about your behavior harder to name?

Have you ever been the person who knew what the napkin said and stayed silent?

The chapter’s mirror is not “have you ever been El” — because the chapter expects that the vast majority of readers have not dosed anyone and run a nineteen-year coercive family. The chapter’s mirror is: “have you ever run any of the small versions of these seven Tracks in a context where the harm was real but smaller, and called it something else?”

The small versions accumulate.

The small versions, run consistently across a community, are what makes it possible for the full version to operate without being named.

The napkin is written by someone who knows.

The napkin gets written because not enough people used the plain language early enough.

Use the plain language.

Name the Track.

Write the napkin.

Here's the Wook Discog for this chapter. Studio Debut — The Baby Wook learning the moves

Twenty-eight, running a small crew, calling it family, genuinely meaning it, and gradually discovering that the family language produces a loyalty that is useful in ways he was not initially aware of. Has not installed a hierarchy. Studio Debut has a tent and a warm personality and the beginning of an architecture.

Live Album — The Seasoned Operator

El. Nineteen years. The bus, the Sprinter, the two Westies, the heeler named Pilot, the fire-circle history, the mason jars, the vocabulary, the Aged-Out Disappearance narrative, the managed exits, and the oatmeal at seven-thirty for fourteen people in a state park in Utah. El’s self-narrative would stand up in most conversations because most people do not ask the questions that make it fall apart, and the questions that make it fall apart are the ones that require the information the Aged-Out Disappearance removes. The Live Album sounds like a life’s work. It is. It is also a crime. Both of those things are true.
