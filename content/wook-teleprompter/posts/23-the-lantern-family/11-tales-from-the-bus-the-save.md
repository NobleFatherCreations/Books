## Tales From The Bus — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

The first thing Wren notices, on the night she arrives at the Lantern Family’s camp for the first time, is that El says “we’ve been waiting for someone like you” before he knows a single thing about her.

Not before he’s gotten to know her. Before he knows anything.

He does not know her name. He does not know where she is from. He knows that she is standing at the edge of the camp circle and that her friend has already started drifting toward the bus and that she has the specific look of someone who has been at the festival for two days and has been mostly fine and is looking for the thing that makes it more than fine.

“We’ve been waiting for someone like you.”

**The watermelon moment:** she hears it and something in her chest does not lift the way it should. Instead it cools — slightly, specifically, in the way that real recognition feels warm and manufactured recognition feels like the moment you notice the warmth is not quite body temperature. The sentence is beautiful. The sentence arrived too fast. Both things are simultaneously present in her sternum.

She files both.

Her name is Wren. She has spent three months since her friend mentioned the Lantern Family reading the scene’s informal information networks — the subreddit, a Discord server for the Dead & Company touring community, a Facebook group for traveling families that she found through a mutual friend. What she has found is not damning. There is no thread that says “El is dangerous.” There is no obvious red flag. There is something subtler: a consistent pattern of praise from current members and a notable absence of long-tenured former members. She has found four people who were with the Lantern Family for three or more years. Two of them did not respond to her DM. One of them responded with a message that said “great people, not for me, moved on to something else.” One of them called her on the phone and talked for forty-five minutes.

The forty-five-minute call was specific, behavioral, and consistent with the pattern she had assembled from the other information. The caller’s voice had the quality of someone who had been carrying something for a long time and was relieved to put it down, even briefly, for someone who might need to hear it.

She went to the Lantern Family’s camp with that call in her recent calls list. She went knowing the full setlist.

She stays for one night. El gives her a mason jar at ten p.m. She cracks her own bottle instead and hands the jar back. “I’m good, thank you.” El’s warmth does not cool. El’s warmth stays at its operational temperature. This is interesting information. Wedge’s source staying warm when she diversified was a green flag. El’s warmth staying warm when she declined the jar is either a green flag or a sophisticated operator who does not react to first-pass resistance.

She stays for dinner. The dinner is good. The family is warm, genuine in the specific way that people are warm when they genuinely believe in what they’re part of. She can see the warmth and she can also see the vocabulary running and she can see the Bus Hierarchy in the sleeping arrangements and she can see the Aged-Out Disappearance in the way nobody mentions the people who used to be here.

She leaves after dinner. She says she has to find her camp.

**El says:** “Our door is always open. Come back whenever you want.”

**She says:** “Thank you. I’ll think about it.”

She does not come back.

She passes the forty-five-minute call forward. She texts the person who called her: “I went. I saw what you described. I’m telling the next person who asks.” She texts a mutual friend who had been considering joining for the next run: “Call me before you decide. I want to tell you what I found.”

The information continues.

The thread continues.

The napkin, now digital, now the length of a phone conversation, continues to be passed from hand to hand across the circuit.

Wren drives home Sunday night. She is not in the bus. She is in her own car, with her own music, heading toward an apartment that is her own, in a city where she is making her own choices.

Nothing about her weekend was magic.

Everything about her weekend was hers.
