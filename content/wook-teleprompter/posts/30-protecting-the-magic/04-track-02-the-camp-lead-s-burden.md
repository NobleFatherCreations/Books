### TRACK 02 — THE CAMP LEAD’S BURDEN
*(role-based accountability holding)*

Here's the behavior. The camp lead knows.

This is the chapter’s most uncomfortable sentence and it has to be said clearly: in the vast majority of situations involving sustained harm inside a camp community, the camp lead knows something. Not necessarily the full account. Not necessarily the names and dates and documented behavioral history. Something. The routing around the missing stair. The “be careful with that one” said to two or three people while the caution was never formalized. The conversation that didn’t quite get followed up on. The person who left without explanation and whose departure was narrated as growth.

The camp lead’s burden is that the role that best positions a person to hold accountability is also the role that accumulates the most social cost for exercising it. The camp lead who names the missing stair risks the fracture of the social structure they have been maintaining. The camp lead who formalizes an accountability process risks the specific brand of community chaos that makes everyone long for the old way, which was the routing.

The burden is real.

The alternative — routing for nine years — is also real and has its own cost, distributed across every person who did not know the stair was missing and who needed to be protected from it.

Here's the read on it. The camp lead’s knowledge — however incomplete — carries a specific responsibility that the general community member’s knowledge does not. If you hold the role, you hold the responsibility. The responsibility cannot be discharged by routing.

**Ask:** “What is my specific responsibility given what I specifically know?” Not “what can I justify not doing” — what do I specifically need to do given the specific information I hold?

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Documented Concern Protocol. Step 1 — When you receive or develop a concern about a camp member’s behavior: write it down. Date, nature of the concern, source. Even if you cannot act on it immediately: write it down.

**Step 2 — Within seventy-two hours:** have a direct conversation with the person about whom the concern was raised. Not as an accusation. As a concern-based inquiry. “I received information about [specific behavior]. I want to understand your account of what happened.”

Step 3 — Document the conversation. Not verbatim — the substance, the date, the response.

Step 4 — Determine what accountability process applies. Your camp may not have a formal process. Most don’t. The absence of a formal process does not mean no process is available — community accountability practices exist and can be adapted. ICSA, the National Coalition Against Domestic Violence, and several scene-specific organizations have resources for this.

Step 5 — If the concern is above the camp’s capacity to address — if it involves legal harm, ongoing physical danger, or requires more structure than the camp can provide — refer to institutional structures: festival safety, county services, harm-reduction organizations with accountability experience.

Quick vibe check. Camp leads are not trained social workers, therapists, or investigators. The protocol is not asking them to be. It is asking them to document, direct-communicate, and refer when the matter is beyond the camp’s capacity. That is achievable for anyone holding the role.

Turn it around — the mirror set. **If you are a camp lead:** when was the last time you followed up on a concern about a camp member’s behavior rather than routing around it? When was the last time the camp’s routing around a person was examined as the accommodation it is rather than the solution it pretends to be?

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Small Camps Without Formal Structure:** The Documented Concern Protocol applies regardless of camp size. The absence of formal governance does not create an absence of accountability. It creates the necessity of building it.

**Camps With Elected or Rotating Leadership:** The documentation should survive leadership transitions. The concern documented under one camp lead is available to the next one. Build the document in a shared system, not in one person’s notes.

This runs in every direction. The Burden falls on the camp lead who’s fifty-four and has run this row for a decade, on the twenty-six-year-old who inherited the role because nobody else would, on the nonbinary organizer everyone calls “mom” whether she asked for it or not, and on the sober mentor who took the job because someone had to. It falls equally on camp leads of every gender, identity, and tenure. The role carries the weight regardless of who’s standing under it.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The manager who knows about the team member’s conduct issue and routes around it — assigns the person to the less exposed projects, briefs the new team members informally, never formalizes the concern. The routing is not management. The routing is the documentation failure that becomes the HR crisis two years later.
