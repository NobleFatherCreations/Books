### TRACK 05 — THE PLUR-LOCKED PLEDGE SYSTEM
*(pledge-based community contracting)*

Here's the behavior. The community accountability infrastructure that most festival spaces have been using is informal, undocumented, and consequently unenforceable. The routing around the missing stair is informal. The whisper network is informal. The “be careful with that one” is informal. The informal infrastructure produces informal outcomes: the Wook who is cautioned in private but never formally addressed, whose behavior continues in the same community or migrates to a different one, who is managed rather than resolved.

Some communities have been building something different.

The PLUR-Locked Pledge System is the emerging practice of community-level behavioral contracting: a set of explicit, named, agreed-upon norms with explicit consequences for violation, signed by community members or festival participants as a condition of participation, administered by a named conduct body with a defined process and defined outcomes.

This is not a radical concept. Professional communities have had codes of conduct for centuries. The scene has resisted them because codes of conduct feel like default-world bureaucracy in a space specifically built to be free of it. The resistance is real and the cost of the resistance is the nine-year routing and the forty-seven subreddit responses and the Lantern Family bus.

The pledge is the scene’s version of the code of conduct, built in scene language, with scene-appropriate mechanics, administered by scene-trained people.

Here's the read on it. Does your community have:

A named set of behavioral expectations?

A named process for receiving and addressing concerns?

A named body responsible for administering that process?

A named consequence structure with defined outcomes?

**If no to any:** the community is running accountability informally. The informal accountability is better than nothing. The formal accountability is significantly more effective.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Community Compact. The minimum viable community accountability infrastructure:

Step 1 — Named norms. In plain language. Not “we value consent” — behavioral descriptions of what consent looks like and what violation of it looks like. Specific. Nameable.

Step 2 — Named process. How does a concern get raised? Who receives it? What happens after it is received? Who follows up and in what timeframe?

Step 3 — Named decision-maker. One person or a small group responsible for the process. Not the entire community. Not the camp lead who has other roles. A designated person or team for accountability specifically.

Step 4 — Named consequences. What are the possible outcomes? Warning, behavioral agreement, suspension, ban? Named in advance. Not improvised in the moment.

Step 5 — Annual review. The compact is reviewed annually. Does it work? Has it been used? What needs to change?

Quick vibe check. The community compact does not have to be legalistic. The scene’s accountability practices are more effective when they are written in scene language, administered by scene-trusted people, and adapted to scene-specific contexts. The point is not to replicate HR. The point is to have named norms and named processes rather than informal routing.

Turn it around — the mirror set. **If your community has a compact:** does it work? Has it been used? Has a concern ever been raised through the formal process, and what happened? If the compact has never been used: it may be working because the community is genuinely accountable, or it may be that the informal routing is still handling things and the compact is a document rather than a practice.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Festival Organizers:** The festival-level compact includes performer agreements, vendor agreements, staff agreements, and attendee codes of conduct. Each of these populations has different leverage and different appropriate accountability mechanisms. Build each separately.

This runs in every direction. The community compact works the same way for the small camp writing its first agreement, the mid-size crew formalizing what’s always been unspoken, the touring circuit building something bigger, and any community regardless of composition. The mechanism adapts. The need is universal.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. Every professional community has a code of conduct and a professional ethics board and a defined process for addressing violations. The scene has had three or four of the components informally since 1969. The missing component is the documentation and the process. The documentation and the process are what make it a system rather than a culture.
