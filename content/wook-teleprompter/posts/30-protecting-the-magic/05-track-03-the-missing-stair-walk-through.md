### TRACK 03 — THE MISSING STAIR WALK-THROUGH
*(open-secret normalization audit)*

Here's the behavior. The missing stair is Cliff Pervocracy’s 2012 term for the person a community has learned to route around without formally addressing.

Every significant festival community has one. Often more than one. The missing stair has been there long enough that the routing is automatic, invisible, and appears to long-tenured members as just “how things are” rather than as the accumulated result of nine years of decisions not to name the stair.

The Walk-Through is the audit that makes the invisible visible.

It asks, for each person in your community about whom there is an implicit routing: what is the routing? When did it start? What behavior produced it? Who decided to route rather than address? What is the cost of the routing being maintained — to the people doing the routing, to the people who don’t know the stair is missing?

Here's the read on it. The stair is identified not by the behavior but by the routing. If your community has developed informal practices around any specific person — who they should not be alone with, which shifts they should not work, which newer members should be introduced to others before them — you have identified a possible missing stair. The next question is whether the routing is the solution or the accommodation.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Walk-Through Protocol. The Walk-Through is a structured conversation among camp leadership and long-tenured members, conducted annually, addressing:

Step 1 — Who in this community do we route around? Name them. Write the names. If naming them out loud is difficult: that difficulty is information about how normalized the routing has become.

**Step 2 — For each name:** what specifically is being routed around? What behavior produced the routing? When?

Step 3 — What accountability process has addressed the behavior? If the answer is “nothing formal” — the routing is the accommodation, not the solution.

Step 4 — What is the routing costing? Who is doing the routing labor? Who is at risk because they don’t know the stair is missing?

Step 5 — What is the appropriate next step? Formal accountability process, referral to festival safety, behavioral agreement, removal from camp. Named. Decided. Documented.

Quick vibe check. The Walk-Through is not a witch hunt. It is an audit. Some routings are appropriate accommodations for personality clashes or interpersonal conflicts that don’t rise to accountability concerns. The Walk-Through distinguishes these: the routing that is “these two people don’t work well together” is different from the routing that is “we learned to not have him alone with first-season women.” The second one is a missing stair.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever participated in routing someone without examining whether the routing was protecting people or protecting the community from having to address the behavior that produced the routing? The routing is comfortable. The Walk-Through is not. The Walk-Through is the reason the routing is sometimes a solution and sometimes a nine-year accommodation of a stair that should have been fixed.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Festival Organizers:** Your vendor row, your practitioner village, your artist community, and your staff all have missing stairs. The Walk-Through applies to every context, not just camps. The missing stair in the practitioner village with the credential problem costs attendees. The missing stair in the artist community with the contract problem costs performers.

This runs in every direction. The missing stair exists in the collective with the most explicit values statement, the crew that talks about accountability constantly, the touring circuit with a formal code of conduct, and the camp that’s never had to write one down. The communities with the most explicit accountability commitments may have the most deeply normalized stairs, because the accountability vocabulary gets used to explain why the routing is actually a values-consistent response.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The youth soccer league’s specific language for the annual coach evaluation, which makes it structurally difficult to name a problem as a problem because the word for the problem has been replaced with “growth area.” “Needs more patience with the younger kids.” “Communication style doesn’t always land.” All of these are the missing stair in a golf shirt.
