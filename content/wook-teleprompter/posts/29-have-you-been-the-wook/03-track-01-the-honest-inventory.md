### TRACK 01 — THE HONEST INVENTORY
*(moral inventory — AA-derived, applied scene-wide)*

Here's the behavior. The honest inventory is a list.

Not a confession. Not a public statement. Not a post. A private list, written on paper or in a note titled “camping gear” or “event logistics” or whatever you need it to be titled so that you will write it honestly and not perform it.

The list has four columns.

**Column one:** the person. Their name. If you cannot bring yourself to write their name, that is information about the column.

**Column two:** what you did. Specific. Behavioral. Not your interpretation of the event — what you actually did. Not “I was working through my own stuff” or “we had a complicated dynamic” — what you actually did. The move. Which Track from this book it maps to, if you can find it.

**Column three:** what it cost them. Not what it cost you. What it cost them. Their disruption, their loss, their adjustment, their subsequent experience. You may not know this fully. You can say “I don’t know the full impact” as part of the entry. The not-knowing is also information.

**Column four:** the status. Nothing done. Acknowledgment made. Behavior changed. Amends attempted. Amends completed. Mira two years ago unfiled under “doesn’t quite remember the name” becomes: Mira Sandoval, amber lantern light, kitchen dome, the thing I said, the text I sent to manage the narrative, the texts she stopped sending, the cost — don’t know the full extent, the two years I didn’t look — status: nothing done.

That is the honest inventory.

Here's the read on it. The inventory is honest when it costs you something to read back.

If you can read back your own inventory and feel fine about it, either you are more accountable in practice than most people or you have written the inventory that protects you rather than the one that reflects what happened. The honest inventory is uncomfortable. That is not a design flaw. That is the test.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The List Protocol. Step 1 — Make the list. Private. Not for publication. Not for the person you harmed. For you first.

**Step 2 — Four columns:** name, what I did, what it cost them, status.

Step 3 — Write one entry per person. No minimum number. No maximum. As many as it takes to be honest.

Step 4 — Read it back once. Note what you feel when you read it. The feeling is data about whether the list is honest.

Step 5 — Keep the list. It is a living document. You will add to it as you remember things and as you do things. The goal is not to complete it. The goal is to maintain it honestly.

Quick vibe check. Some people have short lists. Some people have very short lists because they have already done this work and their behavior has been consistently accountable over years. The short list is possible. The short list that contains only things you have already resolved and nothing new is less likely, because most people who have been in the scene for years have a longer list than they have been accounting for.

Turn it around — the mirror set. The entire Track is a mirror. The mirror set here is the entire Track. You are holding it right now.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Scene Veterans:** The longer you have been in the scene, the longer the honest inventory is likely to be, because the longer you have been here the more people you have been in relationship with and the more situations have occurred. Tenure is not an exemption from the list. Tenure is the reason the list has content.

**Camp Leads and Event Organizers:** If you have held a position of power in the scene, the inventory includes the decisions made from that position that cost other people things. The structural harm — the labor distribution, the access control, the exit narrative — goes on the list the same as the interpersonal harm.

This runs in every direction. The honest inventory belongs to the camp lead auditing his own six years, the twenty-three-year-old checking whether one bad night was a mistake or the start of a pattern, and the veteran of the scene who has more names on the list than she expected. The format applies to everyone. Nobody’s exempt because their intentions were good.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The performance review, self-assessment version. Most people write the version that supports the raise. The honest version includes the times the work caused harm to colleagues, the times the decisions were wrong, the times the impact was real and the intent was good and the two things are different. The honest inventory is the performance review your actual colleagues would write.
