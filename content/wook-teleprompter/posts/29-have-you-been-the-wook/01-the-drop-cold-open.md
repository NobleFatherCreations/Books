## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

He is forty-nine years old.

His name is David Reyes and he has been in the scene for fourteen years and he runs the build crew for one of the larger regional camps in the Pacific Northwest and he is the guy younger festies call when they need a couch in default world, the guy who has driven three people to the airport at four in the morning because they needed it, the guy whose camp has a policy about sober support and accountability and who has been, by every external metric the scene applies to these things, one of the good ones.

He is reading this book in a camp chair in his backyard on a Sunday afternoon in September.

He has been reading it since July. Not straight through. In the way you read a book that you know is going to be good and that you are also slightly afraid of, which means you pick it up for two chapters and put it down for a week and then pick it up again and then put it down because a chapter landed somewhere you needed it to not land. He has been reading it the way you eat something that is simultaneously too good and too hot — in careful bites, with time between them for the temperature to become manageable.

He is in Chapter 29 now.

He put the book down twice in Chapter 24. Once in Chapter 21. Once at the very beginning of Chapter 5, which he put down, and then picked up, and then read the full cold open without stopping, and then sat with for a while.

He is in Chapter 29.

He is reading the cold open.

He reads the first paragraph. The camp elder, the couch in default world. He reads the second paragraph. The way the recognition lands. He reads the third paragraph.

He goes very still.

He puts the book down.

There is a name.

He has been telling himself, for two years, that he doesn’t quite remember it.

The name is Mira.

He remembers it.

He has been telling himself he doesn’t quite remember it because “doesn’t quite remember it” is the specific internal formulation that has allowed him to file the situation in the category labeled “things that need revisiting but not today” for two years without revisiting them. The category is a real category. He has visited it occasionally. He has approached the edge of it and determined that the contents are not in a state to be engaged with productively and backed away. He has done this approximately eight times in two years.

He remembers the name.

Mira Sandoval.

She was twenty-three. She is twenty-five now. She joined his camp two and a half years ago as a first-season arrival — energetic, skilled, a wire-wrap artist who produced extraordinary work and who was immediately beloved by the camp, including by him, including in the way that was the beginning of the problem.

He remembers exactly where they sat.

The back of the kitchen dome, after the Saturday dinner cleanup when most of the camp had migrated to the main stage, in the specific low amber light of the camp lanterns that have been at every camp since 2014. He remembers the conversation. He remembers what he said. He remembers what she said. He remembers the moment she stopped speaking — not ran out of words, stopped, the specific quality of a person who has understood something about the conversation that they are going to need time alone with.

He remembers the text she sent three days later, the one he responded to with warmth and care and exactly the right words, the words that were also designed to move the narrative in a direction that protected him.

He remembers the texts that stopped.

He has told himself, for two years, that she drifted. That she found a different camp. That the connection was what it was and she moved on in the way people in the scene move on, season to season, camp to camp, no drama, no incident.

He has not asked anyone in her former social circle what she is doing.

He has not asked because asking would require him to receive an answer, and the answer might contain her name, and her name in someone else’s mouth would require him to respond to it, and responding to it would require him to be in the same room with what happened.

He picks the book back up.

**He reads:** A beloved camp elder — 49, fourteen years in, runs the build crew for one of the big regional camps, the guy younger festies call when they need a couch in default world.

He puts the book down.

He picks it up.

He reads the next paragraph.

He puts the book down again.

He looks at the backyard — the garden that needs weeding, the camp gear stacked against the fence because the last event ended three weeks ago and he hasn’t fully struck the camp yet, the coffee that is cold now, the September afternoon that is doing the thing September afternoons do in the Pacific Northwest where the light goes golden-horizontal and everything looks like it has been slightly improved by a filter it is not actually running.

He is forty-nine years old.

He has been in the scene for fourteen years.

He has run the accountability camp. He has run the briefing. He has been the person people call.

He also did a thing in a kitchen dome in low amber lantern light two and a half years ago, and he has been telling himself for two years that he doesn’t quite remember the name of the person it was done to, and the name is Mira Sandoval, and the book he is holding has the chapter that tells him what to do next.

He picks the book back up.

He reads the chapter.

He is going to read the rest of this chapter now, and so are you, and the chapter is going to say the things it has to say, and some of them he will recognize from the inside and some you will recognize from the inside, and the chapter’s job is not to be gentle about the recognition.

The recognition is what it is.

The recognition is the point.
