## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

Nadia Okafor writes memos about cognitive bias for a living.

Not metaphorically. Literally. She is the Director of Consumer Insights at a mid-size marketing firm in Chicago, and one of her recurring deliverables is a quarterly briefing for the executive team titled Predictable Irrationality in Digital Consumer Behavior, which she has been writing for four years and which includes, in its third section, a subsection called Anchoring, Priming, and the Architecture of Manufactured Urgency. She wrote that section herself. She wrote it carefully. She is good at her job.

She is also, right now, at four in the morning on day three of the Mojave Convergence, sitting cross-legged on a linen cushion in a yurt the size of a two-car garage, listening to a man named River explain the geometry of her aura.

River has good eyes. Not attractive eyes, specifically — good eyes. Calm. Interested. The kind that find yours and stay, which is a thing most people do not do, actually stay, and the staying feels like oxygen after a conversation at a networking event where everyone is looking past your ear for someone more useful.

Nadia has been awake for sixty-two hours. Not continuously — she slept Saturday afternoon for ninety minutes with her ear pressed into a yoga mat in the corner of the shade structure while something that might have been footsteps or might have been dubstep marched through her peripheral awareness. That was the sleep. That is all there was. Before the sleep there were sixty hours of: standing in dust, dancing on a dry lakebed under scaffolding that looked like it had a complicated relationship with OSHA, three conversations about breathwork with people whose conviction was genuinely moving even though her inner compliance officer kept raising its hand about the liability exposure.

**She has eaten:** half a Rx Bar (peanut butter flavor, the one she doesn’t like, it was the last one), three olives from a camp kitchen bowl at some point on Friday that she cannot fully reconstruct, one mysterious pancake from the communal breakfast that tasted like it had been made by someone who considered precision a form of control, and a piece of “adaptogenic mushroom chocolate” that a woman named Fable pressed into her hand near the tea tent at two in the morning with the instruction just let it open you and that tasted, genuinely, like a candle had made a specific threat.

There is dust in places dust had to fill out paperwork to reach. Her sinuses. Her waistband. The back of her throat when she talks. The crease of her right elbow where she has somehow developed a tan line through the dust, which is not a tan line but rather a dust line, a ring of fine Mojave silt that has decided to make a home between the tendons.

She has not had water in four hours. She has had cactus tea. The cactus tea was beautiful but cactus tea is not water and her blood right now is doing its best with the information available to it.

**In the yurt:** twelve people on cushions. String lights at low warmth. Incense — dragon’s blood, which smells gorgeous and is doing what incense does, which is make the air feel like a place where different rules apply. A singing bowl that someone struck at the beginning and that has been quietly reverberating in the low frequencies ever since, the way a bell becomes a policy if nobody stops it. A Bluetooth speaker playing something ambient and binaural, 40 Hz, which is — and Nadia knows this because she wrote a brief on sensory marketing last fiscal year — a frequency associated with enhanced suggestion receptivity in subjects with reduced prefrontal cortex activity.

Her prefrontal cortex has reduced activity.

River is explaining that her aura has a teal center with a compression around the throat chakra, which is interesting because in her default life she is known for directness verging on bluntness in board meetings, but here she is accepting the aura assessment with the meditative openness of someone who has decided, for the duration of this festival, to be the version of herself that is open, because she came here to be open, because she is not open enough in default world, because her last relationship ended because she was told she was not emotionally available and she is here, on this cushion, at four in the morning in the Mojave, being emotionally available at a person who is reading her aura.

Across the yurt, a woman named Solstice has a MacBook open on a low wooden table. The screen is oriented so that most people in the yurt can see it if they want to, though Solstice hasn’t made a point of directing attention to it. It has been there since the circle started, the way the incense has been there, the way the binaural tone has been there — just part of the environment. Not a presentation. Just: an ambient Stripe checkout page for a Founder & Creator Retreat, Tulum, March 14–21, $2,400 deposit to hold your place, limited to fourteen participants.

The deposit to hold a place was established two hours ago when Solstice mentioned the retreat while pouring tea. One person said it sounded beautiful. That was the anchor. Solstice said “yeah, it fills fast” in a way that had no pressure in it at all, which is its own kind of pressure — because if she were trying to sell it she would be trying to sell it, and she is not trying to sell it, she is just: noting. The way you note things. The Stripe page has been open since then. It has not been addressed again. It is just there, the way the singing bowl is there.

River finishes with Nadia’s aura. He says: “There’s a lot of light in you. I think you’ve been carrying this in your throat for a long time.”

She feels something. In her throat. Which is, to be fair, probably the dust.

**But also:** yes. There is something. She has been carrying something. That is actually true in the way true things are true, the way the best lies are.

Solstice says, from across the yurt, without looking up from a different conversation she is mid-: “The Tulum thing is a lot about that. The throat and expression. That’s actually one of the core themes.”

Not “you should come.” Not “I think this would help you.” Just: a connector. A joining of Nadia’s felt experience in her throat to the thing on the screen.

Nadia’s brain, which has been running on sixty-two hours of no sleep and one threatening candle, does what Day Three Brain does. It connects the dots. It feels the continuity of the moment — River, the throat, the insight, the Tulum thing, the throat and expression, yes, that is a through line, that is a narrative, and her brain, which has spent thirty-eight years turning information into coherent narratives because that is how human cognition maintains the fiction of control — her brain finishes the story.

Her hands are doing something she notices and files as cold. The yurt is sixty-one degrees, she has had no water since midnight and no food since the pancake, and her blood sugar has been running on cactus tea and a threatening candle for nine hours. The tremor in her fingers as she opens the app is not cold. She files it as cold. That is the last executive function she performs before the transaction.

By 5:47 a.m. she has Venmo’d $2,400 to an account called @ConvergenceCreatives. She has agreed to fly to Tulum on March 14th, which she has not verified against her calendar. She has posted a photo to her Instagram story — one of the string-light photos Solstice took of her and River during the circle, warm light, genuinely beautiful — tagging @MojaveConvergence and @TulumFounderRetreat and captioning it something is opening with a teal heart.

She does not realize, in the moment, that she has done any of this. She is aware of each individual action. She is not aware of the sequence as a sequence, because Day Three Brain does not experience sequences, only presents. Each decision was just: now. And now. And now.

She realizes it Thursday in her therapist’s office.

**She says:** “I’m the person who writes the memos.”

**Her therapist says:** “I know.”

**She says:** “I literally teach this.”

**Her therapist says:** “I know.”

Then she writes, in her notebook, the line that will become the central principle of this entire chapter: knowing the levers does not save you from the levers. Only structure saves you from the levers.

She stares at it. Then she writes: what is the structure?

That is the chapter. That is the whole chapter. What is the structure.
