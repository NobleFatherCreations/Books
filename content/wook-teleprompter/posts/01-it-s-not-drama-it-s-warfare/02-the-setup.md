## The Setup

Here's the soundboard quote for this chapter. “Everybody thinks the predator is gonna look like bad vibes. Rookie mistake. Half the time he’s carrying somebody’s cooler, wearing a crew shirt, and saying ‘family’ too much near the golf cart.” — Marlon “Bear” Tevaru, soundman, forty-four, thirty-two years between jam lots, desert burns, forest renegades, and one warehouse era nobody will admit happened. Has seen the whole evolution from tie-dye to tech bro. Currently labeling XLRs with painter’s tape at a regional while judging everyone’s cable management. Recorded at FOH during load-in, cracked iPhone 6, the cable snake coiled around his chair like a boa constrictor that had learned to be useful.

Here's what this chapter is actually about. This chapter is about the difference between conflict and predation. Conflict is messy. Conflict can repair. Conflict gets louder when two people slow down and listen. Predation is directional — it has a beneficiary and a cost center, and the cost center is you. Predation does not repair when named. It maneuvers. It upgrades the cover story. It survives because we keep calling it drama, personality, a bad match, a complicated situation, a thing that happened once, a misunderstanding between two people who both have their version. There are not two versions. There is a pattern. There is intent. There is the refusal to change when the pattern is named. Those three together are not drama. Those three together are a setlist, and the headliner has played this festival before.

🎛️ THE TAPERS’ SECTION

Greene · Goffman · Simon

You do not need to become a library goblin to clock a Wook. But a little theory in the fanny pack keeps you from mistaking a pattern for a vibe.

**Robert Greene gives us the ugly playbook:** power is often built sideways, through favors, access, timing, dependence, and social theater. In festival language, the Wook does not always kick down the tent. Sometimes he becomes the only person who knows where the mallet is.

**Erving Goffman gives us the stage:** every person is performing a role, managing an impression, using the room as part of the act. In scene language, the Wook is not just talking to you. He is talking to the camp, the green room, the group chat, the volunteer lead, the people watching from the tea tent, and the one person with the car keys. The performance is the weapon.

**George Simon gives us the closing argument:** some people know exactly enough to manipulate and exactly enough to deny it. His memory only fails when the receipt has his name on it. His version of events is a spreadsheet. Yours is “a lot of energy.” The cognitive sophistication required to execute repeated, targeted exploitation while maintaining plausible deniability is not accidental. It is a skill. And like all skills, it gets better with practice.

That is enough theory. Put it under the cooler.

You are in.
