## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 FESTIE POV*

The bag is heavier than she thought.

That is the first thing. Not the first thing she notices, but the first thing that matters — the first thing that is an actual fact about her actual situation, as opposed to a vibe she can talk herself out of. The bag is in the rear passenger footwell of the Subaru, and it is heavier than the conversation at the gas station made it sound.

**At the gas station, the conversation was:** just a little something for the camp, toss it in your purse, they don’t search girls’ stuff, it’s chill. The man saying this was the homie — she has met him twice, she does not dislike him exactly, she just does not like him, there is a difference, she likes almost everyone and she does not like him — and her boyfriend was nodding along in the way he nods along to things that are presented as established facts rather than questions, which is a nod she has seen before and which she has learned means I already know about this and I am not going to explain it to you rather than this is a good idea I want to share with you.

She took the bag because she did not know how to not take it in a way that would not require an explanation she did not have. That is the sentence she will spend a long time with later, in the processing tent and in her own head and eventually in a courtroom. She took it because she did not know how to not take it. Not because she wanted it. Not because she agreed to it. Because the no she would have needed to say was a different kind of no than the ones she knew how to say — bigger, more specific, requiring a confrontation with her boyfriend in front of his friend at a gas station about a thing she could not quite name precisely but that her body had already assessed as a problem.

She took the bag.

Her name is Nadia Chen. She is twenty-one. She is an art student. She is three months into a relationship with a person who she thought she was in love with, which she was, in the way you can be in love with someone while also not fully knowing them, which is a very common way to be in love at twenty-one and which is not a character flaw but which is, in this specific situation, a contributing factor.

The gate line at a multi-day festival is its own social ecosystem with its own temperature and its own unwritten laws.

**The temperature at the gate is:** anticipation collapsing gradually into frustration, collapsing gradually into resignation, occasionally spiking with excitement when someone’s playlist or someone’s outfit or some specific quality of the light through the eucalyptus trees reminds everyone in the line that they are about to be somewhere else. There are cars with roof racks that have never been used for anything except festivals and cars with camping gear strapped on with ratchet straps that have been there since the last event and cars that contain, inexplicably, one person and four inflatable flamingos. There is a man three cars ahead who has a dream catcher the size of a pizza hanging from his mirror, which is a choice.

The homie is in his own car, two cars ahead.

He waved as he drove through the gate. A casual wave, the wave of someone who is watching something happen to someone else from a safe distance, the wave of a man whose car did not contain anything the dog would be interested in because the thing that would interest the dog is now in Nadia’s rear passenger footwell.

She watched the wave.

She processed the wave.

She did not yet know what the wave meant, but her body had processed it before her brain caught up, and her body did something she will later identify as the same settling sensation described in Chapter 3 and Chapter 4, the cooling below the sternum, the weight of something being known before it is named.

She does not have time to name it before the dog does it for her.

The dog is a Belgian Malinois. His working name is Buster, which is a working name applied by the county sheriff’s department to all of their dogs and which does not reflect the individual personality of this particular animal, who is attentive and professional and has a very clean record. He reaches the rear passenger door. He sits down. His paws come up once to indicate what he has found and then he goes still in the specific posture of a dog who has completed a task and is waiting for the next instruction.

The trooper who approaches the vehicle is calm. He has done this many times. He is not aggressive. He is also not confused about what is happening.

**He says:** “Ma’am, I’m going to need you both to step out of the vehicle.”

Nadia gets out of the car.

Her boyfriend gets out of the car on the other side.

She looks at him across the roof of the Subaru. She looks at his face. She is looking for something — reassurance, maybe, or recognition, or the specific expression of someone who is about to say “I’m sorry, I should have explained, this is my fault, I’ll handle this” — the expression that would tell her that the thing her body has been knowing since the gas station is wrong, that she misread the nod, that the weight of the bag was something innocent.

His face does not produce that expression.

His face produces something else.

She watches it happen in real time. She watches him calculate. She watches the calculation complete. She watches him arrive at the answer and open his mouth.

He says — fast, loud enough for the trooper to hear, calibrated in pitch and timing for maximum official legibility:

“It’s hers, officer. I didn’t even know she had it. We’ve been having problems.”

The sentence is clean. The sentence is specific. The sentence is the sentence you say when you are attributing possession of a felony amount of a controlled substance to the person standing next to you in a way that includes a context-establishing explanation — we’ve been having problems — so that the attribution sounds like information being provided to help the investigation rather than like a man burning his girlfriend at the gate of a music festival because she was useful and is now not.

He rehearsed the sentence.

He rehearsed it before they got in the car. He rehearsed it because he has known since the homie asked at the gas station that this was a possibility and he arranged the distribution of risk so that the risk sat entirely with the person he told that they don’t search girls’ stuff, it’s chill. The reassurance at the gas station was not an assessment of actual likelihood. It was the final piece of social engineering. It was the thing that made Nadia take the bag and stop calculating about whether she should, because someone she trusted told her there was nothing to calculate about, and she believed him, and the believing him is what she will sit with in the processing tent at nine-forty-seven p.m. when she sees his Instagram story.

He is on the rail.

His hands are up. The drop is hitting. He has tagged her boyfriend — they have been friends since high school. He is having the festival. The festival that Nadia is not having. The festival Nadia is spending in a processing tent that smells like sweat and floor cleaner, sitting in a folding chair across from a sheriff’s deputy who is explaining, in the careful and not-unkind tone of someone who has had this conversation many times, the words felony amount and constructive possession and arraignment.

Her boyfriend bonds out at eleven p.m.

He calls her at eleven-seventeen p.m. She does not answer.

She stays in custody until Monday morning. Her parents arrive in the early afternoon. They drove through the night. Her mother sits next to her without saying anything for a long time, and then she says Nadia’s name in the way that means I see you and I know this is not your fault and I also do not know what to do with the magnitude of what has happened, and they sit together in the waiting area of the county courthouse while a public defender explains the timeline.

The boyfriend calls twice more from Saturday night through Sunday morning.

She does not answer.

**He sends a text on Sunday afternoon:** “I’m sorry. I panicked. I didn’t know what would happen.”

He knew what would happen. He rehearsed the sentence.

The chapter is about what happened before the gate. Not at the gate — the gate is just where the accounting happened. The chapter is about the gas station. About the nod. About they don’t search girls’ stuff as a sentence that functions as reassurance and as recruitment simultaneously. About the architecture of borrowed trust: the boyfriend vouches for the homie, the homie makes the ask, and the ask lands on Nadia because the architecture placed her at the end of the chain where the consequence lives.

**About the Unchill No:** the particular social gradient that makes “no, I don’t want to carry this” feel more costly in the moment than “yes, fine, whatever, it’s probably nothing” — where the no requires more words than the yes, where the no requires naming a thing nobody has named yet, where the no might start a conversation about the relationship, where the no might make her seem like the kind of person who is not cool about these things.

About the fact that the sentence he rehearsed is the evidence.

You do not rehearse a sentence before an event unless you have already decided you will say it.

He decided at the gas station, at the latest.

He may have decided before the gas station.

The bag was always going to be in Nadia’s footwell.
