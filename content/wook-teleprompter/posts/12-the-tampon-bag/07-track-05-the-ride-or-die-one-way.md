### TRACK 05 — THE RIDE-OR-DIE ONE-WAY
*(asymmetric reciprocity in romantic dyads)*

Here's the behavior. The ride-or-die relationship is real. Some of the most beautiful thing in this scene are the people who show up for each other across state lines, across crises, across the specific three a.m. moments that the scene specializes in producing. The friend who calls. The partner who stays. The person who answers at midnight when the weekend goes wrong.

The Ride-Or-Die One-Way is the version where the reciprocity flows in one direction.

He drove her to three events. He covered for her once at work. He has been her person for three months and the three months have felt real and she has genuinely loved being with him and she has also, if she is honest with herself, been the one who drives most of the time and the one who manages most of the logistics and the one who extends most of the benefit of the doubt when the nod at the gas station means I already know about this and I’m not going to explain it to you.

The Ride-Or-Die One-Way is the relational pattern in which one person’s loyalty is treated as unconditional — as ride-or-die in the full sense — while the other person’s loyalty is conditional on convenience. The ride-or-die person drives to the festival. The one-way person rehearses the sentence.

It is not always visible until the gate. Sometimes it is visible earlier and the person has been filing the signals in the “he’s complicated but I love him” folder. Sometimes it is only visible in retrospect, from a folding chair in a processing tent, watching his Instagram story update at nine-forty-seven p.m. with his hands up and the drop hitting.

Here's the read on it. Track reciprocity. Not as a score-keeping exercise. As information about the actual structure of the relationship.

**Ask:** When he needed something, did I provide it? When I needed something, did he provide it? When both of us needed something at the same time, whose need was prioritized?

**Ask:** Have I ever had my back covered the way I cover his?

The Ride-Or-Die One-Way is not always coercive. Sometimes it is just two people in a lopsided dynamic that both of them have been accommodating without naming it. But in the context of the gate line, the lopsided dynamic has a specific direction: the person who runs the risk is the person with the most to lose from the relationship ending. That asymmetry is the Borrowed Mule’s selection criterion.

The sentence “we’ve been having problems” is the tell. Not because it was said — because it was prepared. If your partner has already developed an exit narrative for your relationship that includes a reason you might have acted independently of them and against your shared interest — that narrative was not prepared for your benefit.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Reciprocity Audit. Not a dramatic conversation. A private note. A boring inventory.

Step 1 — Name five times he needed something and I provided it.

Step 2 — Name five times I needed something and he provided it.

Step 3 — Compare the quality and cost of the provisions. Not the count — the cost.

**Step 4 — Ask:** is this relationship building me or shrinking me? That question has a body answer before it has a brain answer. Trust the body answer.

Step 5 — The Ride-Or-Die One-Way does not require a dramatic exit. It requires a clear-eyed read. The read is the chapter’s gift.

Quick vibe check. Romantic relationships are not always symmetrical. Some people give more in some seasons and receive more in others. The asymmetry is not automatically the Track. The Track is the asymmetry weaponized at a moment of legal consequence — the moment when the one-way beneficiary uses the ride-or-die loyalty as a vehicle for their own protection.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever been in a relationship where you were the ride-or-die and the other person was the one-way? Have you ever noticed the asymmetry and filed it in the “complicated” folder? The folder is real. The filing is information.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. Young Women and Femmes in Romantic Relationships: The ride-or-die loyalty dynamic is socialized specifically into femmes in heterosexual-adjacent relationships from very early in life. The loyalty is real. The cost of it, in the context of a rehearsed sentence at the gate, is also real.

**Coercive Control Survivors:** The Borrowed Mule applied through a romantic relationship is a documented pattern in coercive control research. The partner is used as a tool, their loyalty is manufactured through the relationship, and their legal exposure is the outcome. If this pattern is familiar from past relationships: the resources in the Safety Appendix include organizations that address this specific intersection.

This runs in every direction. The Ride-Or-Die One-Way runs through the girlfriend who’s proven her loyalty for a year, the best friend who’s covered for him twice already, the queer partner whose devotion gets treated as infinite, and the camp family member who’s never once said no. Not just heterosexual dyads — same-sex partnerships, close friendships, camp families, and touring crews all produce it when one person’s loyalty is treated as a resource by someone who doesn’t match it.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The work friendship where you have covered for someone three times, defended them in a meeting, shared credit with them, and the one time you needed the same treatment they were in a different meeting. The asymmetry was there the whole time. The Tuesday version just has fewer consequences.
