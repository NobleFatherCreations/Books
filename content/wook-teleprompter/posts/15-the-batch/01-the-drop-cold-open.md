## THE DROP (Cold Open)

*📍 WOOK POV*

I’m not a dealer.

I want to be clear about that before anything else, because the word dealer has a specific set of associations that do not apply to me and I am not going to let those associations land on a situation that is, when described accurately, something different. I am a guy who knows a guy. I am a guy who has friends who want to have a good time and who happen to live in a city where the prices are higher and the quality is worse and who are going to be at this festival and who I am helping out. That is not dealing. That is friendship with logistical awareness.

My name is —

Actually we’re going to skip the name. Doesn’t matter. Call me what you want.

I am twenty-seven. I have been coming to this festival for four years. I know the layout. I know which stages run late. I know which porta quadrant smells the least like a moral failing. I know the Bunk Police booth is near the north entrance, I know the DanceSafe table is across from food court row, I know these things the way I know anything useful — through experience, through paying attention, through being a person who takes the scene seriously.

I fronted eight hundred dollars for eighty caps from a source I have worked with for two years.

My source has never burned me. Two years, a dozen transactions, consistent quality, nothing bad. I know people who have been burned by sketchy sources. I know what that looks like. This is not that. My source is a real person with a real reputation and two years of clean history and the only people in the scene who have a problem with him are people who don’t actually know him, which is a category of criticism that I do not take seriously.

I marked it up to forty a cap.

This is the part where I want to be careful about how I explain it, because the explanation matters.

**The markup covers:** my risk, my time, my fronted capital, my gas, the two hundred dollars my festival ticket cost that I am not asking anyone to share, the three text conversations I have had with my source this week managing the logistics of this transaction. Forty a cap is not gouging. Forty a cap is fair. You would pay sixty in the city and you would not know the person you were paying sixty and you would not have the two-year relationship I have with my source as a quality guarantee. Forty a cap is me absorbing the risk and passing the savings forward. The markup is the market being rational.

I have said this, or a version of this, to myself approximately eleven times since Thursday morning. The repetition is not a sign that I am unconvinced. It is a sign that I am thorough.

The Bunk Police line Thursday afternoon was long.

I am not going to act like the line was a surprise. The line was not a surprise. The Bunk Police booth at this festival gets busy on Thursday afternoon because everybody arrives Thursday and everybody has the same idea at the same time and the booth is one booth. The line was maybe twenty people. Twenty minutes at most.

I had things to do.

I had people waiting on me. I had thirty caps I was planning to move before sunset and they are not going to move themselves and the window between arrival and the first night is the best window and I was not going to spend twenty minutes in a line when I have a source I have been working with for two years who has never once given me reason to doubt him.

I’ll test the rest of it Sunday.

**The thought was:** I will test the rest of it Sunday, after the first night, once the product has spoken for itself, which it will, because it always has.

What I actually mean when I think “I’ll test it Sunday” is: I’ll test it when the information would be comforting rather than useful. I don’t know this about myself yet. I will know it by Saturday afternoon.

I just need to move some before tonight.

It is Saturday at 2:07 p.m. when the first text comes.

It is from Deon. Deon is twenty-three and he is at this festival because I told him it was the right festival to start with and I have been right about that every year for four years and he trusts my recommendations. He is a friend of a friend who became a friend and he came because I said it would be good and I gave him one of the eighty caps at cost, which is to say I gave him one at what I paid my source, because Deon is a friend and not a customer and there is a difference.

**The text says:** Bro something’s wrong with Maddie.

Maddie is Deon’s girlfriend. I have met her three times. She seems fine. She seems like someone who came here to have a good time and has been having one.

Something’s wrong with Maddie is not specific enough for me to know what it means yet. Something’s wrong could mean a lot of things. The festival produces a lot of things that look like something is wrong. I send back: You good? What’s happening?

Twenty-two minutes pass.

The second text is not from Deon.

The second text is from a different number, a number I recognize as Theo, who is at the festival with his crew and who I sold twelve caps to on Thursday night at forty dollars each and who I have known for about eight months. The text says: Dude, Cole is in the medical tent.

I look at this text for a moment.

Cole is in Theo’s crew. I have met Cole twice. Cole is fine. Cole is a twenty-five-year-old who goes to festivals and takes molly and is fine.

Cole is in the medical tent.

**I send:** What happened?

No response.

At 2:46 p.m., three texts arrive in the span of four minutes, from three different people, none of whom know each other, none of whom are in the same social circle:

Are you around? People are getting hurt. Was your shit clean?

Hey man you need to call me right now.

Where are you camping. Someone needs to talk to you.

I am sitting in my tent. My tent is a good tent — a four-season North Face that I bought with adult money and which has never once let in rain. I am sitting in this good tent and looking at my phone and I am still, at 2:46 p.m. on Saturday, in the mental space of probably something went wrong with one person and it probably has nothing to do with me because my source is solid and two years is a long relationship.

The screenshot arrives at 3:14 p.m.

It comes from a number I do not have saved. The message is a screenshot of a group chat I am not in, a chat that appears to have roughly eighty members, most of whom I do not know. The screenshot shows a single message with my name in it.

Don’t take anything that came from [name]. People are seizing at the bass stage. The Bunk Police booth has the batch. Stay away.

My name. In a screenshot. Going around a group chat of eighty people that I am not in, being shared by a number I don’t have saved, having presumably been shared out of the eighty-person group chat to other group chats, having presumably been shared out of those group chats to other group chats.

My name is not in one screenshot. My name is in a cascade.

I am deleting Telegram threads. I am doing this while knowing it will not work — the threads live on the other people’s phones, you cannot delete a sent message from someone else’s storage, the archive is distributed and you cannot unarchive it from your end. I am deleting them anyway because my hands need something to do that is not shaking.

My hands are shaking.

My source, who has been solid for two years, has apparently given me a batch that contains something that is making people seize at the bass stage.

The thought arrives very clearly, separate from the panic, like a text from a different part of my brain: I did not test the batch.

**The second thought:** I told people it was clean.

The third thought, which arrives last because it is the one my brain has been protecting me from since 2:07 p.m.: I told people it was clean and it was not clean and I told them it was clean because I did not test it and I did not test it because I told myself I was going to test it Sunday and Sunday was going to be fine and my source is solid and I have been saying my source is solid for two years and my source is not solid.

My phone buzzes.

The number is unfamiliar. The message is from the medical tent volunteer line — someone gave them my number, someone in the cascade, someone who has a name and a phone and a decision to make about what to do with the information they have.

The message says:

Hey. We have a young woman here who’s unconscious. She told her friend before she lost consciousness that the cap came from you. We need to know what was in it. We’re not cops. We just need to know what was in it.

I look up the name.

I know the name.

The unconscious girl is my ex-girlfriend’s little sister. She is nineteen years old. I met her at Christmas two years ago. She was wearing a Santa hat and arguing with her mother about veganism at the kitchen table in her mom’s house in Fremont, and she was funny about it, she was genuinely funny, she had a good sense of humor for a seventeen-year-old in a Santa hat who was losing an argument about cheese to her mother.

I did not know she was at this festival.

I did not know she was the kind of girl who took molly.

I did not know anything about her situation tonight except that she is nineteen and unconscious and somebody gave the medical tent my name as the person whose cap she took.

Two Rangers and a Bunk Police volunteer show up at my tent at 3:47 p.m. The volunteer has a kind face. The kind face has been doing this for a long time. The kind face has seen many situations like this one and has learned, through repetition, the specific expression that communicates I am not here to punish you, I am here to get the information that might help the person in the medical tent, and the speed at which you give me that information is the only variable that still has meaningful effect on what happens next.

I tell them what I know. Which is: I do not know what was in it. I do not know what my source cut it with. I did not test it. I have the source’s number. I give them the number.

**I ask:** Is she okay?

The Bunk Police volunteer looks at the ground.

That is the answer.

That is the whole answer.

Here is what this chapter requires you to know about the person sitting in the good tent at 3:47 p.m.:

He is not a villain in the way villains work in fiction. He did not intend this. He did not set out to hurt anyone. He did not know the girl in the Santa hat was going to be at this festival. He did not think “I will skip the test because I want someone to die” — he thought “I’ll test it Sunday” because “I’ll test it Sunday” is a sentence that preserved his access to the forty-a-cap window and cost him nothing until Saturday.

He is also responsible for what happened. Both of those things are true simultaneously, without canceling each other out, which is the hardest sentence in the chapter to hold.

The discernment hook for this chapter is not the source. Sources can be wrong. Sources that have been solid for two years can produce one bad batch. The hook is the test. The test was there. The test takes three minutes. The Bunk Police booth was twenty minutes away and the line was twenty people. The test would have answered the question that the Santa hat girl is now answering in the medical tent in a different way.

The test was skipped because the test was inconvenient and the convenience of skipping it was worth more, in the calculation, than the certainty it would have provided.

That calculation is the chapter.

That calculation is the Wook.
