### TRACK 02 — THE MY GUY IS SOLID
*(trust transitivity error / chain-of-trust fallacy)*

Here's the behavior. Your source is solid. You have two years of evidence. Nothing bad has happened in two years. Therefore: the current batch is solid.

This is the trust transitivity error. It treats the source’s historical track record as evidence about the current batch, when the current batch is a separate object with its own contents that your source’s history cannot speak to. Your source’s two-year record is real data about your source’s two-year period. It is not data about the batch you received on Thursday.

**The reason it is not data:** your source has a supply chain. Your source’s supply chain has unknown links above it. The chemistry of a batch can change based on factors that have nothing to do with your source’s intentions or character. Fentanyl contamination has been documented in MDMA, cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin, counterfeit pills, and multiple other substances that were previously considered clean. The contamination often comes from above the source in the chain — from the synthesis, the cutting, the pressing — and it is invisible to your source unless your source tests, which your source may not do for the same reasons you don’t: because the source’s source has been solid, and the chain of trust extends upward without interruption until the batch provides the interruption.

The My Guy Is Solid is not evidence about the batch. It is evidence about your relationship. Both are real. One of them is useful for predicting today’s product. That one is the test.

Here's the read on it. The test. Every time. Not sometimes. Not when you are suspicious. Not when you have a reason to doubt your source. Every time. Because the absence of a reason to doubt is not evidence that nothing is wrong — it is the default state that the test is supposed to update from.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Chain Audit. **Step 1 — Ask your source, every transaction:** “Have you tested this batch?” Not rhetorically. Literally: what kit, what result?

Step 2 — Verify with your own kit regardless of what they say. Not because you distrust them. Because the chain above them is unknown to both of you.

**Step 3 — Marquis for MDMA:** purple-black is correct. Yellow, orange, or no reaction: do not distribute.

**Step 4 — Fentanyl strip:** always. On everything. Regardless of substrate. The positive result is one line. One line: destroy the batch. Do not distribute a single unit of a batch that tests positive for fentanyl.

Step 5 — Document the test. Photo of the result. Timestamped. In your camera roll. So that “I didn’t know” can also be “and here is the test I ran.”

Quick vibe check. Good sources exist. Long-term reliable supply relationships exist. Trust, built through evidence over time, is real and useful. None of that replaces the test. The test is what turns trust into information.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever told someone something was clean based on your source’s reputation rather than a test? Have you ever passed on the “my guy is solid” reassurance as a substitute for the test result? If yes: you transferred the fiction of certainty without the substance of it.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Harm-Reduction Volunteers:** The most common sentence preceding a serious batch incident in your intake is “someone told them it was tested.” The sentence was not “here is the test result.” The sentence was “I trust the person who told me it was tested.” Those are different sentences.

This runs in every direction. The chain-of-trust error runs through the tour crew’s usual connect, the camp’s two-year relationship with a specific source, the femme distributor whose reputation has never once been questioned, and the friend of a friend everyone vouches for without having met him. It doesn’t care about the quality of the human relationship. It cares about the quality of the batch, which only the test can establish.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The audit firm that signed off on the financial statements because the client had been reliable for twelve years. The twelve-year track record was real. The fraudulent statements were also real. The audit was the test. The audit was not run with sufficient scrutiny. The twelve years became the permission slip. That is the My Guy Is Solid running in accounting.
