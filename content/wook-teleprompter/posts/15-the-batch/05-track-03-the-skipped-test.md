### TRACK 03 — THE SKIPPED TEST
*(availability heuristic override of risk assessment)*

Here's the behavior. The Bunk Police line was twenty people. Twenty minutes.

He had thirty caps to move before sunset.

The availability heuristic is the cognitive shortcut that treats the ease of imagining a bad outcome as a measure of its probability. The bad outcome — someone getting hurt from an untested batch — was not easy to imagine in Thursday’s context, because the context was busy and good and his source had been solid for two years and twenty minutes in a line is a real cost against a hypothetical risk.

The hypothetical risk was not hypothetical.

The hypothetical risk had a name and a Santa hat and a mother in Fremont who was going to receive a phone call.

The Skipped Test is not a single decision. It is the accumulation of smaller decisions — the line was long, the moment was busy, I’ll do it later, it’s probably fine, my source is solid — each of which is individually defensible in the moment and collectively produced the decision to distribute an untested substance to people who believed it was clean.

The Skipped Test is the chapter’s central safety content. Not because testing is complicated — because it is not, it takes three minutes, the kits are available, the protocol is simple. But because the decision to skip it is made under exactly the conditions that make every other shortcut in this book appealing: momentum, social pressure, the window, the belief that history predicts the future.

Here's the read on it. One rule. No exceptions.

Test every batch you distribute. Every time. Before you distribute. Not after. Not after a few units have gone out and nothing bad has happened yet. Before.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Three-Minute Gate. Step 1 — Before distributing any batch, test it. Marquis and fentanyl strip, minimum. Both.

**Step 2 — The three-minute gate is this:** if you do not have three minutes to run the test, you do not have the time to distribute safely. Stop. Wait. Come back when you have three minutes.

Step 3 — The window does not outrank the test. The social pressure does not outrank the test. The line being long does not outrank the test. The source being solid does not outrank the test. Nothing outranks the test.

**Step 4 — If you cannot test:** do not distribute. This is the only rule in the chapter that has no exceptions or qualifications.

Step 5 — Carry the kits. At every festival, in your own kit, separate from any supply. Marquis. Mecke. Simons. Fentanyl strips. They cost less than the ticket. They cost less than the phone call.

Quick vibe check. Testing does not prevent all harm. A fentanyl-negative result does not mean the substance contains exactly what is labeled. Unknown adulterants, dosing errors, and individual tolerance variations can all produce harm in tested substances. The test is necessary. It is not sufficient. What it is: the minimum accountability threshold for anyone distributing anything to anyone.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever distributed something without testing it? Have you ever told yourself you would test it “later” in a way that meant you would test it after the window for the test to be useful had closed? The gap between “I’ll test it later” and “I told them it was clean” is a sentence you may be living inside right now.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Camp Kitchen Leads:** If your camp has a supply that multiple people are accessing from, the camp kitchen lead is the de facto testing authority. Build the testing protocol into the camp’s pre-event briefing. Kits, results, documentation.

**First-Time Distributors:** The first time you distribute anything to anyone else is the time when the test matters most, because you have no baseline for your source and no experience with the downstream effects of distribution. Test obsessively in year one.

This runs in every direction. The Skipped Test gets skipped by the touring vet who’s done this a hundred times, the first-timer who doesn’t own a kit, the camp lead in a hurry before doors, and the distributor who’s never once had a bad batch. The reasons are identical regardless of who’s skipping it: inconvenience, momentum, trust in the source, belief that this time is probably fine. None of those reasons change the test’s function.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The safety inspection that was scheduled for next quarter because the plant was running at capacity and the shutdown for inspection would cost the week’s production. The risk was known. The window was inconvenient. The inspection was postponed. The batch failed in the field. The postponement was the Skipped Test.
