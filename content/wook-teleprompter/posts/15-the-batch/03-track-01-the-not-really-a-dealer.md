### TRACK 01 — THE NOT-REALLY-A-DEALER
*(identity laundering / role distancing)*

Here's the behavior. The internal narrative requires a specific self-image to function. The self-image is: I am not a dealer. I am a guy who helps out friends. I am a logistics person. I am a generous person with connections who happens to be able to bring good stuff at a fair price to people I care about.

The self-image functions as a permission structure. A dealer has responsibilities — to test, to disclose, to be accountable for the downstream effects of their distribution. A guy who helps out friends does not carry those responsibilities, because the relationship is defined as friendship and friendship does not have a liability protocol.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the fiction that the social definition of the transaction changes its legal and ethical content. It does not. Distributing a substance for profit — even at forty dollars, even to friends, even with a two-year source relationship — is distribution. The legal definition does not care about the friendship framing. The harm-reduction accountability does not care about the friendship framing. The girl in the medical tent does not care about the friendship framing.

The Not-Really-A-Dealer is the first Track because it is the one that makes all the other Tracks possible. If you are a dealer, you test. If you are just helping out friends, you skip the line.

Here's the read on it. **The question is not:** “Am I a dealer?” The question is: “Am I distributing a substance for profit or access?” If the answer is yes — the responsibilities of distribution apply regardless of the relational framing.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Role Audit. Step 1 — Name the transaction accurately. Not “helping out” or “bringing some for friends.” What is the transaction?

I am acquiring a substance from a source.

I am transporting it.

I am distributing it to people.

I am charging money.

**Step 2 — Ask:** what are the responsibilities of someone who does those four things? Testing. Accurate disclosure about contents. Capacity to address harms downstream. Those are the responsibilities. They apply regardless of what the relationship is called.

Step 3 — Decide whether you are willing to take those responsibilities seriously. If yes: run the protocols. If no: stop distributing.

Quick vibe check. Not everyone who gives a substance to a friend is a dealer in any morally meaningful sense. Sharing your own supply, at no markup, with full disclosure and testing, within a small group of people who all consented to the information — that is a different category from what this chapter describes. The Track is not “has ever given a friend something.” The Track is the systematic distancing from accountability through identity reframing.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever called yourself “not a dealer” specifically to avoid the responsibilities that come with that role? Have you ever used the friendship framing to skip a step you would have taken if you thought of yourself as running a distribution operation? The framing does not change the content of the transaction.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Scene Veterans:** The longer you have been doing this, the more convincing the “this is different from dealing” narrative becomes. The tenure makes the identity feel more real and the accountability feel less necessary. Both effects are inversely correlated with actual safety.

**Touring Artists and Crew:** The tour-bus supply situation has a specific version of the Not-Really-A-Dealer narrative: “we just bring enough for the run.” The run is distribution. Test it.

This runs in every direction. The Not-Really-A-Dealer shows up as the guy who “just knows a guy,” the femme distributor with the spreadsheet and the Venmo, the touring crew member who’s been doing this since he was nineteen, and the camp lead who fronts for the whole group. The narrative is gender-neutral. The batch doesn’t care who’s holding it.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The investment firm that does not consider itself a bank and therefore does not apply banking regulations to its deposit-taking activities. The regulatory arbitrage works until it doesn’t. The framing does not change the function.
