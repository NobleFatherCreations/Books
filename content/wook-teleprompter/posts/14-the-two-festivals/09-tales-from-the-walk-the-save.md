## Tales From The Walk — The Save

Festie POV. Same scenario. Counter-Drops live. Named protagonist. Watermelon Moment present.

Same festival. Year ten. Tee again.

The change happened in April, in a parking lot, over a text thread about who was bringing the canopy. A friend of six years named Hollis Yeun — twenty-nine, plays bass badly, has been at every one of these since 2021 — typed one sentence that had never been typed at Tee before by anyone in his life.

**Hollis wrote:** “random but — if something goes sideways out there, who do you want me to get? like which specific people.”

Tee looked at his phone for longer than the message warranted.

That is the watermelon moment in this Save, and it belongs to Hollis rather than to Tee, which is the whole design: the small internal note that a question exists which you have never asked, and that the not-asking has been costing somebody else something for six years.

The answer took four texts. Zendo, not security. The medic tent, not the gate. And a specific name: a woman named Dionne who runs volunteer coordination and is, per Tee, “the one who’s actually good.”

Hollis wrote them down. In his notes app. Under the heading GROVE, above a list of which friends do not eat dairy.

June. 12:52 a.m. Same walk. Different night — this time it is a guy from their camp named Petey, twenty-two, first festival, who has taken something at a dose that seemed reasonable to a twenty-two-year-old and has arrived somewhere he cannot presently get back from.

Tee has him by the elbow. Hollis is on the other side.

Two security staff angle toward them across the grass — the same angle, the same unhurried, entirely reasonable approach, the same two-and-a-half seconds in which eleven adjustments have historically been required.

Hollis steps forward.

He does not announce it. He does not look at Tee. There is no glance, no signal, no moment of meaningful eye contact, because the entire value of this is that it is not a moment. He just takes half a step diagonally so that he is the nearest body, and he starts talking before they arrive, which is the part that matters most:

**Hollis says:** “Hey! We’re taking him to the Zendo — he’s having a hard trip, we’ve got him. Can you radio ahead so they know we’re coming?”

Medical words. A named destination. And a task.

The staff member with the radio lifts it and calls it in, and in the four seconds it takes him to do that he stops being a person making an assessment and becomes a person on an errand, which is the one transformation this entire Track is built around.

The other one says, to Petey, kindly, “You’re good, brother, they’ve got you,” and they move on.

Eleven seconds, start to finish. Nothing happened.

And now the actual payoff, which is so small that it would be easy to skip past, and which is the reason this chapter exists:

During those eleven seconds, Tee was looking at Petey.

That is it. That is the whole Save. He had both hands on his friend and both eyes on his friend and the entirety of his attention on the twenty-two-year-old who was coming apart, and he noticed — because Petey’s jaw had gone rigid in a way that Blythe’s never did — that this was closer to a medical situation than a Zendo situation, and he said so, and they went to the medic tent instead, and the medic found a heart rate that mattered.

The protocol was off for eleven seconds and in those eleven seconds he caught something.

Nobody says anything about it afterward. Hollis does not mention it, that night or ever, because mentioning it would convert a piece of infrastructure into a favor, and a favor has to be acknowledged, and acknowledgment is work, and the work would land on Tee.

At 3:40 a.m. they are back at camp and Petey is fine and somebody at the fire says something about how long the line for water was.

Tee is not tired in the specific way. He notices that too.
