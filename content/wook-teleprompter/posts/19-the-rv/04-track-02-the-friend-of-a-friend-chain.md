### TRACK 02 — THE FRIEND-OF-A-FRIEND CHAIN
*(trust transitivity dilution)*

Here's the behavior. Your friend knows someone.

That someone is “solid.”

Your friend’s assessment of “solid” is based on an encounter fourteen months ago at a different event where everyone seemed solid because the event was good. Your friend cannot pick this person out of a lineup. Your friend has not verified anything about this person since the encounter. Your friend’s “solid” is not an assessment of current behavior, supply chain safety, propensity to run a stick-up operation, or general trustworthiness across contexts. It is a vibes-memory from a year-plus ago in a setting that produces positive vibes-memories almost universally.

This is not your friend’s fault. This is the trust-transitivity error from Chapter 3 applied to personal safety at two removes. Trust does not travel through two links unchanged. Trust is attenuated by each link. Your friend trusts them, and that trust is based on limited information and time passage, and you are now being asked to take that already-attenuated trust and stake your phone, wallet, car keys, four hundred dollars, and physical safety on it.

You are being asked to trust a stranger on the recommendation of a friend who barely knows them.

That is the same as trusting a stranger.

Here's the read on it. Map the chain. How many links between you and the person? When was the last contact between the linking friend and the source? Under what circumstances did the trust form?

Two links with a fourteen-month gap and a festival encounter as the basis: that is not a reference. That is a coincidence presented as a recommendation.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Direct Verification Protocol. Step 1 — Before following any friend-of-a-friend to any meeting: ask the linking friend: “How well do you actually know this person? When did you last interact? What’s their actual name and camp?”

Step 2 — Require specifics. Not “they’re solid.” Name. Camp. When last seen. How the original connection was made.

**Step 3 — If the answer is vague:** do not proceed with the referral. Your friend cannot vouch for someone they cannot describe.

Step 4 — If you can verify the source independently — through a camp network, a mutual connection who has more recent contact, a profile with actual traceable scene roots — verify before you walk to row 14.

Step 5 — If you cannot verify and you still want to proceed: do the transaction in the open, with a witness, with the geometry of a parking lot or a main thoroughfare rather than a beige RV with curtains drawn.

Quick vibe check. Friend-of-a-friend connections are the basis of most of how festival communities function. Most of them are fine. Most of the people at row 14 are not running stick-ups. The chain breaks most dangerously in contexts where the transaction requires either physical privacy or significant financial exposure, because that is where the gap between “vibes-solid” and “verified-solid” costs the most.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever passed along a friend-of-a-friend referral as “solid” based on limited contact? Have you ever said “just text them, they’re good” about someone you haven’t seen in over a year? You may have inadvertently constructed a chain that someone else is walking to the end of.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Long-Tenured Festies:** Your name carries significant vouching weight in the scene. The informal references people drop with your name attached — “[you] said they were solid” — may bear no resemblance to anything you have actually said. You have limited control over this. You can explicitly tell your network: “If anyone uses my name to vouch for a meeting or a transaction, have them check with me first.”

This runs in every direction. The chain dilutes the same way whether it passes through the tour crew’s usual guy, the camp’s newest addition vouched for by someone half-remembered, the festival hookup’s roommate, or the friend of a friend everyone agrees is “basically family.” The vibe-memory problem is universal. The verification protocol is also universal.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The babysitter referred by a neighbor who met her once at a block party two summers ago and describes her as “so sweet.” The “sweet” is a five-minute impression. The hire is unsupervised time with your kids. The gap between the data and the decision is the chain.
