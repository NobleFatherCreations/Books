### TRACK 02 — THE MASON JAR
*(non-consensual dosing / chemical consent removal)*

Here's the behavior. The jar is passed.

Communally. With social momentum. By someone before her who drank without comment. The communal passing converts an individual transaction into a community practice — the cultural meaning of the fire circle absorbed into the object being passed around it.

She does not know what is in it.

She was not asked.

This is not a gray area.

Giving a person a substance they have not agreed to take is not a gift-economy practice. It is not a scene tradition. It is not a form of care. It is the removal of another person’s right to decide what goes into their own body — the foundational decision from which all subsequent decisions on the night derive.

Everything that follows the undisclosed dose — the warmth, the trust, the elevated physical sensation, the three a.m. tent — happens to a person who is not operating with full information about their own state. Their consent to everything that follows is contaminated by the thing they did not agree to. The warmth is real. The contamination is also real. Both things are in the jar.

Cosmo does not experience himself as dosing someone without consent. He experiences himself as sharing a carefully prepared gift with someone he finds beautiful and believes will appreciate it. This interior experience is genuine. It is also precisely how the mason jar maintains its deniability across eight years of festival seasons — the genuineness of the intent is the cover for the operation.

Here's the read on it. You do not know what is in any container you did not fill and seal yourself.

This is not paranoia. It is the baseline. The scene’s genuine culture of sharing and passing and communal drinking creates the ambient trust that makes the mason jar possible. The mason jar needs that ambient trust to function. The function is the problem.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Seal Protocol. **Step 1:** You drink from things you opened yourself or watched opened in front of you.

**Step 2:** If someone offers you a filled container: “What’s in here?” — a complete question. Say it.

**Step 3:** If the answer is vague — “good stuff,” “you’ll love it,” “medicine” — that is a no. Not because vagueness is always predatory. Because you are the one who gets to know what goes into your body, and a person who won’t tell you has decided for you.

**Step 4:** If you have already drunk from something and you realize you don’t know what was in it: find someone. Your anchor, Mara Hot Water, a harm-reduction volunteer. “I drank something and I’m not sure what’s in it” is a complete sentence. Say it. The information protects you more than the silence.

Quick vibe check. Passed jars and shared drinks are real and mostly exactly what they appear to be. The test is the answer to “what’s in here?” A genuine gift answers directly. An invoice deflects, flatters, or creates social pressure to just drink.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever put something in a shared drink without telling the person who drank it? Have you ever told yourself the experience was worth more than their consent to have it? The warmth being genuine does not resolve this question.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Recovery Lane:** If you are in recovery and you drink something without knowing what is in it, you are in a different kind of crisis than the chapter’s primary subject. Find your anchor. Find harm reduction. The sobriety is the first thing.

**Ceremony Lane:** The “medicine” framing in ceremony contexts does not change the consent requirement. Ceremony holders who administer substances without explicit, informed, prior consent are running the Mason Jar in a robe. Consent is specific, verbal, and prior to administration. Always.

This runs in every direction. The Mason Jar gets passed by the ceremony holder who says the medicine knows what you need, the camp elder who’s done this every year since the beginning, the touring artist who trusts his own judgment more than yours, and the stranger who decides for you before you’ve decided for yourself. The person who decides what goes into someone else’s body without their knowledge is deploying a specific form of control that runs across every identity configuration. The seal protocol applies regardless of who’s holding the jar.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The medication added to someone’s food. The dosage changed without informing the patient. The thing put into someone’s system without their knowledge or consent. The clinical vocabulary exists for a reason. The festival version deserves the same clarity.
