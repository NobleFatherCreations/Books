### TRACK 04 — THE PHANTOM LIMB OF CAMP
*(group attachment loss)*

Here's the behavior. The camp was real.

The camp was not only the Wook. The camp was the people — the ones you love and the ones who are still there, including some of the ones who sided with him not because they chose him but because the camp is the structure of their social world and they did not know how to choose otherwise. The camp was the traditions — the specific way the kitchen ran, the specific lighting configuration during the burn, the Saturday afters, the particular quality of the Sunday morning when everyone was exhausted and the magic had settled into the bones.

The Phantom Limb is the loss of all of that along with the loss of him. The two are not separable in the nervous system even though they should be logically separable. He was the problem. The camp was the context. The context was real. The loss of the context lands like the loss of a limb — the nervous system keeps reaching for it, expecting it to be there, and finding absence where a familiar shape used to be.

“I miss the camp.” This is true. This is not the same as missing him.

“I miss the ritual.” True. Not the same as endorsing what happened.

“I miss the people.” True, and complicated, and not resolvable in four weeks.

Here's the read on it. Distinguish the grief for the camp from the grief for him. They are often experienced together and they are separate losses that require separate processing. You are allowed to mourn the camp without that mourning being a case for returning to the situation that produced the harm.

**Ask:** “What specifically do I miss?” Not him. The camp. The ritual. The people. The belonging. The specific quality of the Sunday morning. Name them. Name them specifically. Name them without him in the naming. When you can see them clearly, you can also see: which of these things is available somewhere else?

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Loss Inventory. Step 1 — Make the list. What specifically did you lose when you left? Not him — the camp, the people, the rituals, the belonging, the access, the identity.

**Step 2 — For each item on the list:** is this thing available somewhere else, in a different configuration that does not require his presence?

Step 3 — Build toward the available ones. Not immediately. Not as a replacement. As an acknowledgment that the thing you miss was real and worth missing and is not exclusive to that specific container.

Step 4 — Let the ritual loss be ritual loss. Grieve it specifically. The specific thing that happened at the Saturday afters. The specific way the kitchen ran. You are allowed to miss something that also hurt you. The two facts coexist.

Step 5 — Time. The Phantom Limb sensation diminishes over time as the nervous system builds new neural pathways to replace the ones that reached for the camp. The sensation does not disappear immediately. The timeline is longer than you want it to be. It resolves.

Quick vibe check. Missing the camp does not mean the decision to leave was wrong. Some decisions are correct and still cost something real. The Phantom Limb is not the nervous system telling you to go back. It is the nervous system grieving what was real. Grieving something is allowed. Returning to harm is not required.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever contributed to someone else’s Phantom Limb — by being the thing that someone had to lose in order to get safe? Have you ever been the camp someone had to leave? The loss they carry from that is your legacy in the scene. Name it.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. Long-tenured festies leaving a high-control group: The longer the tenure, the more the camp and the identity are fused, and the more the Phantom Limb is also an identity loss. Chapter 22 covers the Festie Hollowing. The Phantom Limb in this chapter is specifically about the loss of a specific group that was also load-bearing for identity. Both are real. Both require processing.

This runs in every direction. The Phantom Limb aches for the vendor who left a toxic row and still misses the smell of the coffee cart, the sound tech who quit a bad crew and still misses the specific joke that only worked backstage, the camp member who left the family bus and still misses Sunday breakfast, and anyone who left a community that was also their social world, identity context, and supply chain of belonging. It is not gendered. It is the cost of leaving any place that was genuinely home.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The job you had to leave because the manager was harmful, and two years later you still miss the team, the culture, the specific coffee machine in the corner of the fourth floor, the Tuesday lunch order, the inside joke that only worked in that building. The loss of the good things in a bad situation is real. It is not an argument for staying.
