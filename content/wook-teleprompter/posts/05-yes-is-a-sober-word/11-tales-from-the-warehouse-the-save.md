## Tales From The Warehouse — The Save

Wook POV. Same scenario. The play fails. The predator’s interior frustration as the target slips. This is the immune system working.

I bring an eight-ball to every party.

It is eleven-fifteen and I have already done the room. Twenty-two targets in a three-hundred-person warehouse in east Oakland, of which eight or nine are operational tonight depending on how the night develops. Standard distribution.

I find the two women from the earlier hour — the ones I helped through the come-up at nine-thirty, spent real time with, genuine care, that’s always real — and I get myself positioned in the sightline of the one who’s been on her phone. Black crochet top, silver rings, came alone or came with someone who has already migrated toward the main stage. Almost-leaving posture. Good. That’s the window.

She drifts over. Exactly on schedule.

Her name is Kezia. I find this out within four minutes. LA transplant, eighteen months in, works in illustration, came with a friend who “went to find water” eleven minutes ago and has not returned, which is information I file for the approach. She is open, a little unmoored, the particular quality of someone who would like to belong to what is happening around her and has not yet found the way in.

I pull out the bag.

I bump the two women first — always friends first — and then I look at Kezia and shrug.

You good?

She looks at the bag. She looks at me. She says: “What’s in it?”

This is not the first question I expect. Usually the first question is a nod, or a sure, or a laugh that means yes. What’s in it requires an actual answer and the actual answer is either the truth or a lie and the lie is checkable in ways that create downstream problems.

**I say:** “It’s good coke. Clean. Tested.”

**She says:** “Tested with what?”

I say reagent.

**She says:** “Do you have the kit?”

I do not have the kit on me right now. The kit is in the car. I start to explain this and she says “it’s cool, I have one” and reaches into a small crossbody bag and produces a Marquis reagent kit, which is not the right kit for cocaine but is the tell that this is a person who came prepared, and I am recalibrating in real time.

**I say:** “That’s for MDMA.”

**She says:** “I know, I have a cocaine reagent in my car. I can go get it, or I can skip it, but I just wanted to know if you had it.”

She is not hostile. She is entirely pleasant. She is asking factual questions in the same tone I use when I ask about a good spot for late-night food. She is just: equipped.

She takes the bump. She says thank you. She does not say you’re so nice. She says “thank you, that was generous.” The difference between those two sentences is not large. It is enormous.

By midnight I have bought her one drink — she asked what she could give me in return and I said “nothing, I like parties” and she said “okay” without debt in her voice, which means the Kandi Debt Racket is not running. She has texted someone twice. The text chain is with someone in the building — not outside, in the building — which means she has an anchor and the anchor is close.

At one-fifteen I suggest the back room, it’s quieter.

**She says:** “I’m good here. I like the energy.”

I say the back room has better sound isolation.

**She says:** “The sound is the point.” And then, without adjustment or performance, she turns back toward the stage.

At two a.m. a woman in a green jacket arrives next to Kezia and they hug and the green jacket woman looks at me and gives me a single long read and says “hey” in the tone that carries the word “I see you” inside it. The anchor. Kezia introduces me by name. The anchor knows my name already. That is the tell — Kezia texted my name at some point in the evening, which means someone outside this building has a record of where she is and who she is with.

By two-thirty I am at the bar talking to someone else.

Kezia will have a good night. She will go home at four, sober enough to make the decision to go home, and she will text her anchor when she gets there, and the anchor will send a thumbs up, and that will be the night.

She will not know that anything unusual happened. She will think she had a good conversation with a generous person and went home when she was ready. That is exactly what she will have done.

What she will not know is that the generous person ran his usual read and found, instead of a window, a wall — not a hostile wall, just: a wall. Test kit. Named anchor. Stayed where the witnesses were. Did not say you’re so nice. Did not produce the chemistry of gratitude that the frame requires.

The structure could not build because the foundation was already occupied by something else.

Her preparation.

I finish my drink. I check the room. Nineteen remaining targets. Eight or nine operational, same estimate.

I start again.

But not with her.

The immune system is the person who came prepared. The immune system is the anchor in the green jacket who knew his name before he said it. The immune system is the test kit in the crossbody bag, carried casually, the way you carry keys. The immune system is every person who reads this chapter and builds the perimeter before the lasers start.

The immune system is growing.
