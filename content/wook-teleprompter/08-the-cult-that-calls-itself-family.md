*📍 WITNESS POV*

It is Sunday at 11:14 a.m., which is the most honest hour at any festival.

Not because the people are honest. Because the circumstance is. Friday at eight p.m., everyone is their best festival self — intentions stated, outfits intact, the weekend still a blank canvas onto which they are painting the version of themselves they drove three hours to become. Sunday at 11:14 a.m., the canvas has been used. The weekend has happened to people. The party, as an entity, has filed for divorce and is already arguing about who gets the tent poles.

The eyelashes that were a whole personality on Friday night are now a small gray crescent on the camp table next to a half-empty bottle of LMNT, a dead vape pen still being inhaled hopefully like a sad little flute, someone’s kandi cuff that dissolved slightly in the rain on Saturday, and the camp cooler, which has become a small contained ecosystem with its own microclimate and possibly its own spiritual tradition.

One DJ is still going somewhere in the tree line, which is either dedication or a hostage situation.

The porta line smells like a war crime.

Strike has started.

This is when the camp tells the truth about itself. Not in its manifesto. Not in its annual photo in matching colors with good lighting and everyone present for the shot. In who is on their knees at 11:14 a.m. and who is on cushions, and what the distance between those two groups says about how the camp actually runs.

Out by the kitchen, three people are doing work.

Dinah is on her knees breaking down the propane stove. She is forty-one, a structural engineer in her default life, the person who read the load-bearing specs on the shade structure in 2017 and has quietly kept everyone under it safe from a windstorm every year since. She has been at this camp for eight years. She has been on her knees since seven a.m. Her water bottle has been empty for forty minutes. Nobody has refilled it. Not because anyone decided not to — the decision was never made. It just did not occur to the people who are not on their knees that the person on their knees might need water, which is itself a finding.

Hugo is folding the parachute cloth with the focused fury that only genuinely sober people can summon at this hour. Seven years clean. Six full builds. The single person in this camp whose Sunday morning is not also a hangover. He has been working since six-fifteen a.m. He can see the inner-circle cushions from where he stands. He is not looking at them. He is looking at the parachute and he is thinking something specific that he has not yet said out loud but that he will say, at eleven-fourteen a.m., to Dinah, when he puts down the parachute and walks the twelve feet.

Tomás is sorting bins. He joined the camp last year. He has not stopped doing labor since Thursday at nine a.m. He has lost four pounds. He has the specific two-thousand-yard stare of a man who was told this camp is family and is standing at the exact moment he realizes that family, in this camp, is a verb that means do the strike. He has a particular stillness that is not peace. It is the stillness of someone holding a very specific question very carefully and not yet knowing whether it is safe to put it down.

**The question is:** if this is family, why am I the one carrying boxes.

It is a good question. He will get an answer today. Not from Lorelei. From Hugo, at approximately eleven-fourteen and thirty seconds, when Hugo sits down next to Dinah and says the thing.

Eight feet away, in the last remaining patch of shade on the entire lot, the inner circle is having a Moment.

Five people on cushions. Not build cushions — actual brought-from-home meditation cushions in coordinated earth tones, which were carried to the festival in a dedicated padded bag and which have never touched a tent stake.

Lorelei the camp lead, who founded the camp in 2016, which she will tell you only when it is strategically useful. She is holding a hand-thrown ceramic mug that says HOLD SPACE / NOT GRUDGES in tasteful sans-serif. She does not technically own the truck. She does not technically own the trailer. She does, however, control the group chat — admin since founding, never transferred; the build list; the volunteer schedule; the wristband distribution, which she describes as “intuitive” and which has been the same intuition for nine years running; the relationship with the festival organizers; the camp Instagram; the password to the shared Dropbox where the budget allegedly lives. The budget has not been seen by anyone outside the inner circle in four years. Lorelei calls this “streamlined.”

Brett, her partner, is half-listening while rolling something with the unhurried hands of a man who has not done a meaningful camp labor task since 2019 and who is universally beloved because Brett is “the vibes.” Brett’s actual contribution this weekend was being charming at the Saturday afters and bringing two pints of mango sorbet that he ate himself in the trailer with the door closed. This is known. It is not addressed. It is the kind of thing that gets filed under Brett is Brett, which is the specific cognitive category for beloved people whose account is perpetually overdrawn but whose social credit is inexhaustible.

Marisol, three years in, is nodding emphatically. Marisol identified in year one that proximity to Lorelei is the actual currency in this camp and has been spending it carefully ever since. Marisol does not do dishes. Marisol “holds the emotional container,” which is one of those phrases that consistently means the same three other people will be doing the dishes. Marisol is on the camp’s annual poster, which has required an ongoing and gradually more creative rewriting of contribution history. Nobody has said this out loud. Dinah has said it in her head approximately forty times.

Ash, returning camper, is mostly quiet. Ash has been watching this camp long enough to know that quiet is the only safe posture when the inner circle is convening, especially on Sunday at eleven a.m. when the weekend has happened and the accounting has not yet been done. Ash has watched this exact type of conversation happen in 2022, 2023, and 2024 — always on Sunday morning, always about someone in the middle ring who had started asking questions, always ending with that person not being at the camp next year. Ash has not asked questions. This is not wisdom. It is the survival adaptation of someone who decided that belonging costs a certain amount of visibility and has been paying that toll for four years.

Pippa is first season. She is sitting slightly too straight and laughing slightly too hard at things that are not jokes. She is being onboarded. She thinks she is in. She is, in fact, being assessed. Lorelei noticed. Lorelei noted. The note is in a category that does not get written down but that has been translated, in previous seasons, into specific requests made approximately four months from now when the build planning starts.

The Moment they are having is about Dinah.

Lorelei is saying, in a soft and worried voice, that Dinah has been “a little intense” this weekend. She noticed Dinah seemed to be “carrying a lot of energy” during the build. She is “wondering if maybe this camp isn’t quite the right fit for her next year.”

Lorelei has not spoken to Dinah about this.

Lorelei will not speak to Dinah about this. The conversation will happen in the inner circle. The inner circle will arrive at a consensus. The consensus will be deployed, eventually, as a gentle and regretful and loving message from Lorelei that uses the word “container” twice and frames Dinah’s departure as a personal growth chapter rather than a managed exit.

Marisol nods. “Honestly, I’ve been feeling that too.”

Brett does not know what “hot” means in this sentence. Brett does not know what most sentences mean. Brett is, however, agreeing, which is his only structural function in the camp, and he performs it reliably.

Ash says nothing. Ash is staring at a fly on a cushion with the philosophical intensity of someone who is watching a small terrible thing happen and has decided not to be the person who names it, at least not today.

Pippa feels her stomach do something. Pippa is new. Pippa is not going to say anything. The math of her social capital in this camp is still negative — she has spent the weekend building credit and she does not yet have enough to spend it on a public disagreement with the camp lead. This is a rational calculation. It is also exactly how the cartel persists from season to season: by recruiting people who are in the calculation phase before they have enough information to know the math is wrong.

Hugo’s phone buzzes.

He reads it.

It is the camp group chat.

**Lorelei has posted:** “Just want to hold space for checking in on each other during strike. How’s everyone’s energy? Feeling some intensity in the field and want to make sure we’re tending to each other.”

Hugo reads it once. He reads it again.

He puts his phone in his pocket.

He does not move for a moment. Nine years at this camp and he has watched this conversation happen from a distance three separate times and every time he has told himself it was not his to interrupt. The ninth year is heavy. The parachute is right here in his hands and folding it is a task that would keep him honestly busy for another twenty minutes.

The twelve feet is not the hard part. The twelve feet takes four seconds.

The hard part is the second before it.

He puts down the parachute. He picks up his water bottle, which is still half-full because Hugo planned ahead, which is a habit of people who have been doing the actual work for long enough to know the water runs out.

He walks twelve feet.

He sits down next to Dinah.

**He says:** “Dinah. Stop. I’ll finish the stove. Here’s my water.”

She takes the bottle. She drinks half of it. She does not say anything for a moment.

**She says:** “Are you leaving too?”

**He says:** “Yeah. I just decided.”

Tomás is six feet away, still holding a bin. He is looking at them. He is doing the math. He has had enough information now.

**Hugo looks at him and says:** “Come sit down.”

Tomás puts the bin down.

This is how the cartel ends. Not in lightning. Not in confrontation. Not in a public reckoning or a manifesto rebuttal or a scene in the group chat. It ends in twelve feet and a water bottle and the sentence that has been true for two years being said to the person it needed to reach.

Twelve feet.

That is the distance of the entire chapter.

## 🎬 VIDEO 3 — Track 01 — THE CAMP MOM POWER GRAB
*~876 spoken words · est. 5:50 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 01 — THE CAMP MOM POWER GRAB
*(informal authority capture)*

Here's the behavior. She — or he, or they, but let’s stay with she for this instance because the Camp Mom archetype has a specific gendered flavor in most scenes — started as the person who just “took care of things.” The person who made sure the kitchen was stocked. Who remembered birthdays. Who texted when someone seemed off. Who knew where the extra tent stakes were. Who had the festival organizer’s cell number. Who handled it. The care was real. The competence was real. The centrality that resulted was also real and also, gradually, structural.

The Camp Mom Power Grab is the conversion of informal care-role into informal governance authority. She controls the group chat because she set it up and never transferred admin. She controls the wristband distribution because she handled the relationship with the festival contact and knows the allocation system. She controls the build list because she’s the one who’s always done it. She controls the narrative because she’s been there the longest and people default to her read on things.

None of these controls were seized in a coup. They accumulated through years of competent caregiving in an environment that never developed formal processes to distribute the power the competence generated. The problem is not the competence. The problem is what the concentration of power begins to do: it becomes the mechanism for excluding people who ask inconvenient questions, for managing exits, for controlling the camp’s self-image, for ensuring the cartel’s longevity.

“Lorelei has always handled that” becomes “Lorelei is the only one who can handle that” becomes “questioning Lorelei’s handling is questioning the camp’s ability to function.”

That last one is governance.

Here's the read on it. **Ask:** can Lorelei — or whoever holds the informal authority role — leave the camp for a season and have it run without crisis? If the answer is no, the camp has built a throne instead of a structure. Ask: who inherits the keys, the relationships, the accounts, the permissions if the Camp Mom steps back? If there is no answer, the succession was never planned because succession was never supposed to happen.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Formal Distribution Ask. Step 1 — Make the ask in the context of care, not confrontation: “I want to take some of the load off you. Walk me through the trailer system and the organizer contact so I have it too.”

Step 2 — Ask for the budget. Not accusatorially. As a participant: “Can the full camp see this year’s financials? I want to understand how we’re resourced.”

Step 3 — Request that admin access be shared on the group chat, the cloud folder, the social accounts. Formally. As a proposal.

Step 4 — If all three are refused or deflected: the power is not informal. It is intentional. You now have information about the camp’s actual structure.

Quick vibe check. Some camp leads are just good at their jobs and have earned their central role through decades of genuine competence. Centrality is not automatically a Grab. The test is: does the central person try to distribute their power over time, or do they consolidate it? Do they cross-train, or do they make themselves indispensable?

Turn it around — the mirror set. **If you are the central person:** when did you last try to give away a piece of power you didn’t have to give away? When did you last cross-train someone in a function you control? If the honest answer is “I haven’t, because it’s easier to handle it myself” — you may have built a throne without noticing. Examine the floor.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **BIPOC Camp Leads:** When the central authority role is held by a BIPOC person in a predominantly white camp, the dynamics of accountability become additionally complex — questioning authority can be racially coded as attack, and the Camp Mom herself may be using her position to protect the community she has built. The structural problem is still the structural problem. Accountability and care for the person can coexist.

**Disabled Leads:** If the central person also carries a disability that makes certain kinds of labor impossible and for which the camp has no protocol — the authority and the access question are entangled with accessibility. Untangle them separately.

This runs in every direction. The Camp Mom Power Grab runs in camps led by femmes, mascs, nonbinary elders, sober mentors, queer organizers, and anyone who started doing care work and was never asked to give the keys back. The grab is available to anyone who is competent enough, present enough, and patient enough to become indispensable before the camp develops the structures that would make indispensability impossible.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The employee who has been doing the job long enough that only they know the password to the critical system, the institutional memory of the vendor relationship, and the location of the files. When they leave, there is a crisis. That is not a tribute to their dedication. That is a management failure that the organization tolerated for years because the crisis was always future.

## 🎬 VIDEO 4 — Track 02 — THE WRISTBAND HIERARCHY
*~689 spoken words · est. 4:36 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 02 — THE WRISTBAND HIERARCHY
*(credential gating / access stratification)*

Here's the behavior. The festival gives the camp a number of artist, staff, or volunteer passes. The camp distributes them. Lorelei decides who gets the upgrade. The decision is made on an “intuitive” basis that, examined over nine years, has produced the same outcome: inner-circle members and new recruits being assessed get the access. Middle-ring members who have been doing structural labor for years get general admission.

The wristband is not just a wristband. At a festival, access is status, belonging, and logistical freedom. The artist pass means you can be where the action is. The staff band means you can navigate the site at three a.m. without being questioned. The volunteer credential means your labor is legible to the festival’s infrastructure as real. The difference between the bands is the difference between being inside the camp and being of the camp.

When the band distribution is controlled by one person and never explained, it becomes a loyalty metric. The people who ask questions get general. The people who do extra labor and don’t ask questions sometimes get access. The people who align with the inner ring get the upgrade. The bands are the camp’s visible reward system for invisible compliance.

Here's the read on it. **Ask:** what is the process for access distribution? If the answer is “Lorelei handles it” or “it’s intuitive” or “it’s based on what the camp needs” — ask who made the decision about need. If nobody can name the criteria, the criteria are not organizational. They are personal. Personal criteria in access distribution are always, eventually, loyalty criteria.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Transparent Access Proposal. Step 1 — Propose a written access-distribution process before the festival: roles, hours, criteria. Transparent to all camp members.

Step 2 — Ask for the camp’s access allocation from the festival in writing. Who has how many of what kind.

Step 3 — Compare the distribution to the labor log. If the people doing the most work don’t have the most access, name that discrepancy specifically: “Dinah ran the build for eight years and gets general admission. The inner circle gets artist passes. I want to understand the criteria.”

Step 4 — If the criteria cannot survive being named out loud, they are not criteria. They are preferences.

Quick vibe check. Some camps have genuinely complex access logistics driven by their actual festival function — sound camps need staff bands for their sound crew, art car camps need vehicle passes for their drivers. Functional access is real. The test is whether the function explains the full distribution, or whether the function explains some of it and the rest is relational.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever advocated for someone’s access upgrade based primarily on how much you liked them? Have you ever withheld the information about how access gets distributed because knowing the process would empower the people asking about it? The hierarchy is not always conscious. But it always has an architect.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **BIPOC and Queer Artists:** When access distribution in predominantly white scenes follows patterns that consistently favor white inner-ring members over BIPOC and queer artists doing structural labor, the wristband hierarchy is also a racial and identity hierarchy. Name both layers.

**First Season:** You don’t yet know what the baseline access distribution looks like. Ask before you accept what you’re offered as normal. “What access do returning middle-ring members typically get?”

This runs in every direction. The Wristband Hierarchy is managed by whoever controls the relationship with the festival and whoever controls the camp. Both can be any gender or identity. The hierarchy itself is structural and runs regardless of the identity of the person managing it.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The office where some people get the conference room booking system and some people have to ask someone else to book it for them. The functional difference is small. The status signal is large. Access to access is always partly about belonging.

## 🎬 VIDEO 5 — Track 03 — THE “WE DON’T DO THAT HERE” SOFT EXILE
*~756 spoken words · est. 5:02 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 03 — THE “WE DON’T DO THAT HERE” SOFT EXILE
*(norms enforcement as ostracism)*

Here's the behavior. Nobody is kicked out. Kicking people out is messy and documentable and creates the kind of incident that gets named. Instead, the Soft Exile is the withdrawal of the things that make belonging feel like belonging: warmth, the easy inclusion, the invitation to the inner-circle conversations, the access to information that other people seem to have, the sense that the camp is glad you are in it.

Hugo asked about the bank account in year five. He did not accuse anyone of anything. He asked: “Can we all see the camp financials?” The question was reasonable. It was the question of a person who had built the camp’s infrastructure for five years and wanted to understand how the camp was resourced.

The response was not a refusal. A refusal can be contested. The response was a shift in temperature. Lorelei became slightly more formal with Hugo. The invitations to the inner-circle conversations started going to different people. Hugo’s input at the build planning meeting was received and then not referenced. Nothing was said. The exile had no incident. It was conducted entirely through the management of warmth, and by the time Hugo could clearly name what was happening it had been happening for three years.

Here's the read on it. Watch the temperature gradient. After a question, a concern, a boundary, or a no — does the warmth level stay the same? Does information still flow to you as easily as before? Do you still get the invitation to the things you used to get invited to? The Soft Exile runs on the hypothesis that you will attribute the cooling to your own behavior — that you will decide you did something wrong and self-correct, or that you will decide the environment is unwelcoming and leave. Either outcome serves the cartel.

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Temperature Check. Step 1 — Name the temperature shift directly and early: “I’ve noticed things have felt a little different since I raised the financial question. Is there something I should know?”

Step 2 — If the response is “I don’t know what you’re talking about” or “you might be reading into it” — that is information. The exile is confirmed.

Step 3 — Build lateral. The Soft Exile works by making the inner ring’s warmth your primary source of belonging. If you have strong relationships with middle-ring members independently — built without going through the inner ring — the exile has less power.

Step 4 — If the exile continues after the direct conversation: name it to the middle ring. Not dramatically. Factually: “I asked about the budget and the temperature changed. Has anyone else had that experience?”

Quick vibe check. Sometimes people genuinely become more distant after a hard conversation, not because they’re running a cartel, but because the conversation stirred something that needs processing time. The test is duration and proportion: does normal warmth return within a reasonable time, or does the cooling become a permanent new baseline?

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever gotten colder toward someone after they asked a question you didn’t like? Have you ever stopped including someone in the inner conversations after they raised a concern? Have you ever withheld warmth as a governance tool without calling it that? The Soft Exile is the most passive of the cartel Tracks and the one most likely to be run without full awareness.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Veteran Festies in Middle Ring:** If you have been around long enough to notice the pattern across multiple seasons, you are also in the position to name it to newer members before they lose years to the calculation phase. That conversation — “here is what I’ve seen happen to people who ask that question” — is one of the most useful things an experienced person can offer.

This runs in every direction. The Soft Exile can be run by camp moms, camp dads, nonbinary elders, beloved femme organizers, and quiet male camp leads who never say anything explicitly but are the thermometer everyone else reads. The exile is conducted through social temperature. Temperature has no gender.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The work team where someone who raised a safety concern in a meeting is no longer included on the project updates email. Nothing was said. The exile happened in the cc field.

## 🎬 VIDEO 6 — Track 04 — THE GROUP CHAT FLYING MONKEY SQUAD
*~760 spoken words · est. 5:04 at 150wpm*

### TRACK 04 — THE GROUP CHAT FLYING MONKEY SQUAD
*(proxy harassment / coalition manipulation)*

Here's the behavior. Lorelei does not run the concern about Dinah herself. She raises it in the inner circle. The inner circle distributes it. By Sunday evening, three camp members have independently checked in with Dinah in ways that feel personal and caring but that are carrying the same information — “is everything okay, you seemed like you were carrying a lot of energy this weekend” — because the information was seeded at eleven a.m. on cushions while Dinah was on her knees.

The group chat runs the same way. A message doesn’t have to be an attack to be a governance tool. “Just want to hold space for checking in on each other during strike. How’s everyone’s energy?” is a message that means multiple things simultaneously: a genuine check-in, a soft reframe of the labor situation as an emotional situation, and a signal to the inner circle that the camp lead is observing.

The Flying Monkey Squad is the deployment of the social network as a distributed enforcement and narrative-management tool. Individual members of the squad often do not know they are in the squad. They have been seeded with a read on a situation and they are acting on it in good faith, which is what makes the pattern so hard to contest from the inside.

Here's the read on it. When multiple people deliver the same message independently within a short time window — especially after a question was raised or a concern was named — ask: where did this message originate? Did these three people arrive at this concern simultaneously and independently, or is there a source? If there is a source, what is the source’s interest in the message being distributed?

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Origin Audit. Step 1 — When you receive the third version of the same message from three different people: “I notice a few people have said something similar this week. Where is this coming from?”

Step 2 — If the source names themselves, go to the source directly. “It sounds like there’s a concern about me. I want to hear it from you directly.”

Step 3 — If the source does not name themselves, treat the distributed message as a signal about the camp’s governance, not as three independent data points about your behavior.

Step 4 — Refuse to respond to the indirect version. The indirect version is the mechanism. “If there’s a concern, I want to be in the room when it’s raised. I’m not going to process it in a side channel.”

Quick vibe check. Communities do genuinely check in on each other. Multiple people caring about one person simultaneously is not automatically a squad. The test is whether the check-ins are carrying the same content — the same framing, the same concern, the same read — which suggests a shared origin, or whether they are varied and independently derived.

Turn it around — the mirror set. Have you ever seeded a read on someone and then watched it travel through the camp’s social network? Have you ever been the person who “just wanted to check in” on someone because someone you trusted told you something about them before you had your own experience? The Flying Monkey Squad does not require malice. It requires people who trust the source and act on the information.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Harm-Reduction Crew:** You are particularly susceptible to being recruited as a Flying Monkey because your role legitimizes welfare checks. If you are sent to check on someone by a camp lead, ask yourself what the camp lead’s interest in that check is.

**Queer Scene:** In small queer scenes, the Flying Monkey dynamic can run with devastating efficiency because social networks are dense and trust is high. A seeded read travels further and faster.

This runs in every direction. The Squad is assembled by camp leads, inner-circle members, and anyone with enough social capital to make their read on a situation spread as ambient community concern. The assembler can be any gender or identity. The squad members are usually acting in good faith.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The manager who mentions to three different team members separately that they’re “a little worried about how X has been handling the project,” before X has been given any direct feedback. The performance conversation has been seeded in the culture before it happens in the room.

### TRACK 05 — THE FIRST-SEASON FUNNEL
*(newcomer lovebombing)*

Here's the behavior. Pippa arrives on Thursday and is immediately, intensely welcomed. “We’ve been waiting for someone like you.” She is included in conversations that feel like the inner circle. She does not know that she is being assessed, not welcomed. She does not know that the warmth is calibrating her labor capacity, her social compliance, her potential as a future middle-ring member. She does not know that the word “family” in this camp’s usage is a verb that means “you have been earmarked.”

By Sunday morning she has done two generator fixes, one shovel intervention, and the full kitchen prep for eighty people. She has not once been brought water by an inner-ring member. She has brought water to inner-ring members twice.

The Funnel converts the genuine longing for belonging — which is most powerful in new arrivals, who have not yet built lateral connections in the scene and are dependent on the camp’s warmth for their entire festival experience — into a labor and loyalty pipeline. The lovebomb is the open valve. The work starts flowing before the person understands the structure of the pipe.

Here's the read on it. How fast did you get called family? How much labor have you done versus how much access have you received? What happened to the previous first-season people — are they still here, or did they leave after one season, and what does the official story say about why?

Here's your counter-drop — run this. &rarr; The Season Pace Protocol. Step 1 — Receive the welcome warmly and genuinely. The warmth may be real.

Step 2 — Decline the “family” frame for at least one full season: “I’m really glad to be here. I want to earn that over time.” Watch how this lands.

Step 3 — Observe the labor distribution before you contribute at an intensive level. Who else is doing what work? Are the people who have been here for years doing proportionate or less labor than first-season arrivals?

**Step 4 — Ask about the people who left:** “Who was in camp in year six? Where are they now?” The answer to this question is camp archaeology.

Step 5 — Talk to someone who has been in the camp for three or more years who is not inner-ring. Middle-ring perspective is the most accurate map of the actual terrain.

Quick vibe check. Some camps genuinely are family from the first day and mean it, and the warmth and the structure and the labor and the access all flow in roughly proportionate ways. The test is symmetry: do first-season members have proportionate labor, access, and voice, or is the labor disproportionate to the access and voice while the “family” language is disproportionate to the actual duration and depth of relationship?

Turn it around — the mirror set. **If you are an established camp member:** have you personally made sure the first-season arrivals this year had what they needed, or did you observe their labor from a distance while being impressed by their contribution? Have you checked in on their workload as you would check in on a family member who is new to a physically demanding situation? If not, the family framing was one-directional.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **New to Scene:** Your first camp invitation is the most powerful social experience you will have in your first festival year. The intensity of the welcome is not proportionate to the depth of the relationship. Pace yourself. Build lateral connections outside the camp.

**Live Painters, Glovers, Wire-Wrap Artists:** If a camp recruits you because your craft makes their camp look beautiful in photos, they are also recruiting your unpaid contribution as a brand asset. You are not just a camper. Negotiate your role accordingly.

This runs in every direction. The Funnel happens in every kind of camp across every identity composition. Mutual aid camps, sober camps, queer family camps, burner mega-camps, touring crews. The job that starts with two weeks of your new manager being incredibly warm, enthusiastic about your potential, and then in week three quietly redirecting approximately forty percent of someone else’s workload onto your desk as a “growth opportunity.” The warmth was real. The mask is the manifesto’s perfect evil twin.

Quick vibe check. Beautiful manifestos are not the enemy. The Track is when the language is consistently used as a weapon against the people the language was supposed to protect. If the camp’s manifesto would be embarrassed by the camp’s actual Sunday morning behavior, the manifesto is mask, not mirror.

Turn it around — the mirror set. **If you wrote the camp’s manifesto:** when was the last time the manifesto was applied to a hard situation in a way that made you — not an outsider, not a dissident, you — uncomfortable? The Gift Economy Skim is the engine of cartel longevity.

That is real and the camp should accommodate it. The test is whether the accommodation applies equally or whether it applies selectively based on inner-ring status.

Refractions — this lands differently depending on who you are. **Artists, Live Painters, Glovers, Hoopers:** If your craft is being treated as your camp contribution while you are also being asked to do structural labor, name that. **First Season:** You are most vulnerable to the Skim in your first year because the social equation is not yet clear and the lovebomb is still active. The labor cap set before the event is the only protection that is active before you need it.

This runs in every direction. The Gift Economy Skim requires people doing the structural labor and people not doing it and a values framework that prevents the discrepancy from being named. All three elements run across every camp identity. The mechanism is available to any camp that has values language, a labor-intensive existence, and an inner ring.

And Sober Tuesday — the morning-after truth. The team where “we’re a startup family” is the reason nobody is paid overtime. The gift economy of startup culture is the Tuesday version of the same skim, with equity instead of artist passes and “mission alignment” instead of “being of use to the magic.”

## 🎬 VIDEO 13 — The Soundcheck — The Camp Cartel Audit

Run this on the camp you are in, thinking of joining, or thinking of leaving. Seven maps. One session. A boring note titled “Camping Checklist” or “Gear List.”

2. The Labor Map. Last full season: who did the build, the kitchen, the strike, the storage run, post-event cleaning, conflict management, chat management. Compare to the Key Map. If the labor names and the key names are different people, you have a cartel.

3. The Voice Map. Last season: who got the loudest public thank-you, who got named in the year-end post, whose face was on the Instagram. Compare to the Labor Map. If credit names and labor names are different, you have publicity laundering.

5. The Onboarding Map. Newcomers last two seasons: how were they welcomed, how fast were they called family, how much labor did they do in season one, how many are still in year two. High-intensity welcome plus low year-two retention is a funnel.

6. The Vocabulary Map. Last season: how often was manifesto language used to enable accountability versus used to disable a concern. If the language disables more than it enables, the manifesto is a mask.

7. The Plain Sentence Test. Pick one concern. State it in plain language. Name. Behavior. No vocabulary. Observe the response. If the response is vocabulary management, Public Cry Coup, Concern Hostage, or Soft Exile: you have your answer.
