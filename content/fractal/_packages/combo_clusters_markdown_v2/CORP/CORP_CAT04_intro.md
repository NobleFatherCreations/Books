# CAT04: Reality Distortion & Perception Control Composites — Corporate & Consulting

## Category Introduction

In this sector, the thing everyone eventually fights over is the record. What was promised in the meeting, what the email actually said, what the cap table showed in March, what the review meant, whether the scope ever included that deliverable. This category covers the sequences that make a person doubt their own memory and judgment, until they defer to someone else's version of events. It often runs alongside conditioning and accountability evasion, and it tends to sustain itself: once you start second-guessing what you heard, no one has to say anything more.

It lands hardest where the paper is thin and the conversations are many. A founder who was told equity would be papered "once the cleanup is done." An executive in a negotiated separation, handed a summary of a meeting that does not match the one they attended. A consultant told that the scope was always understood to include the extra work. An employee whose feedback is described in the review as something quite different from what was said aloud. Institutional memory in a company is whatever gets written down and kept, and the person who controls the minutes, the deck, the shared drive or the official summary holds a great deal of quiet power.

The sector has soft words for it. It is alignment, narrative, messaging, "what we actually agreed," "that's not how I remember it," "let's not relitigate the past," or a gentle suggestion that you are tired, sensitive, or reading too much into things. Sometimes people truly do remember events differently, and stress, overload and poor note-taking are ordinary. A shifting explanation is not proof of design. What stands out is repetition: reasons that change each time for the same event, previously stated facts denied, documents called formalities, and the person raising the issue gradually described as the problem.

The protective practice is unglamorous and powerful. Keep contemporaneous written records. After any important conversation, send a short summary the same day, such as "To confirm what we agreed today," and see whether it is corrected or ignored. Seek independent witnesses and outside readers. Avoid arguing settled facts out loud when a written record can hold them. Keep the observable events separate from your guesses about why. If your confidence in your own perception is badly shaken, talking with a trusted person or a qualified professional is a sound step, and for high-stakes agreements, qualified counsel can read the paper with fresh eyes.
