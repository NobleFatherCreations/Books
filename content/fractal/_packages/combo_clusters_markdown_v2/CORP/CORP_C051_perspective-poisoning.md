# C051: Perspective Poisoning — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a habit of seeing new people and news through a frame you were handed before you met them, and a belief that those borrowed opinions are your own careful thoughts.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of judging a new person by what you were told about them before you have spoken to them.
- A pull to trust the first number or version you hear, and to measure everything after it against that one.
- An automatic sentence, "I already know what they're like," spoken about someone you have never met.

**A point in a life.** Arriving in a new senior role.

**Where it happens.** The finance office of a mid-sized company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a chief financial officer, forty-six, and you have spent two decades learning to form your own view of a set of books. That is why the company wanted you. It is a mid-sized business with a founder-era chief executive, a board that wants institutional discipline, and a finance function everyone describes as "in transition." The offer was clean. You said yes in late spring and your start date was the first of June.

**Eleven days before day one, a flight**

The chief executive sent you a transition briefing that morning. It was only seven pages and it was beautifully written. He said he wanted you to walk in with everything he knew, so you would not lose a month to politics. You read it somewhere over grey sea, with a plastic cup of tea going cold. Page two describes the controller, a woman of nineteen years' tenure, as "deeply loyal, occasionally territorial about the close." Page three says the audit committee chair "likes to be seen asking hard questions, and may test you early." You do not think of yourself as someone easily influenced. You make notes in the margin. You underline "territorial." You write, in your own hand, the word careful. Your shoulders settle.

Page four lists three open issues, a reconciliation backlog, a vendor dispute and a revenue-recognition memo, and tells you that you "should treat these as legacy." Page five says the previous CFO left because "he could not let go of how things used to be done." You promise yourself, quietly, that you will let go. Each of the three issues has a single sentence of context, and no more. It is a short page, kind to a reader on a plane, and you are grateful for it.

**Your first days, your new office**

The desk is still bare and the window looks onto a car park. In your first days you ask for the controller's working files and for time with the audit chair. "Let me set those up," the chief executive says. The files arrive in one folder he has arranged, in his order. The introductions, he says, he would rather make himself. Everything you learn about the building comes through the man who wrote the pages, and you do not mind. It is faster.

**The second week, the senior team meeting**

The reconciliation backlog comes up under item three. The chief executive says, easily, "That's the legacy item we discussed." Ten minutes later, of an unrelated invoice, he says it again: "More legacy." The room nods. The controller says nothing. Two executives repeat the word legacy. You catch yourself typing it into a note to the team on your laptop: "Please send status on the legacy items by Friday." Nobody in the room asked you to.

**Week six, your office**

Only now do you place the first morning. The controller greeted you warmly and a little formally, with a hand on a folder while she spoke. You remembered page two, and you did not ask what was in the folder. At lunch someone mentioned that the audit chair had asked for time with you in your first week, and the page tightened in your mind: may test you early. Her three plain questions, about the close calendar and the control environment, you answered cautiously, then wondered whether you had answered like a person being tested.

Today the controller puts the folder on the table between you. It holds a binder of unresolved intercompany items she has been trying to raise for a year: dates going back fourteen months, initials, her handwriting in the margins. Raised in March. Raised in June. You do not feel lied to, exactly. You feel slow.

**A year later, a small meeting room**

You are about to interview a candidate for treasury analyst. The chief executive's note on her is three lines long and you have read it twice. Before she sits down you have decided what sort of person she is. She names an expected salary in her first minute, and for the next forty you hear everything she says as higher or lower than that figure. When she says she has a question about the close, you glance at the note and let it pass.

Afterwards a colleague asks what you made of her. "I'd read the note," you say. In the evening you open your margin notes from that flight. The word careful looks like someone else's handwriting. Last week, in a meeting, you could not say what you yourself had noticed on your first day.

## The Name

That's **Perspective Poisoning**.

## Definition

Perspective Poisoning is the sequence where a lens is placed on a person before they meet the information it is meant to colour, using Anchoring, False Framing, Selective Disclosure, Information Control, Repetition as Truth, Social Proof and Priming to convert the tendency to read new facts through an already-set frame into the loss of neutral, independent interpretation. In corporate settings it concentrates in handovers, transition notes, onboarding packs, and briefings to boards or new executives. It is hard to detect because each line is plausible and often sincerely meant, and because a briefing is a normal courtesy. The pattern does not require design; a rushed or biased handover can have the same effect, and intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Anchoring** — The seven-page briefing, read on a flight eleven days before you start, calls the controller "occasionally territorial" and the audit chair someone who "may test you early," and you underline "territorial." This functions by giving the reader a reference point that later facts are measured against. Concern rises when the descriptions are about temperament rather than conduct and arrive before you have met anyone.

2. **False Framing** — Page four tells you to "treat these as legacy," and page five says the previous CFO left because "he could not let go of how things used to be done." This functions by assigning a meaning to events that the evidence has not yet supported. Concern rises when labels discourage inquiry instead of describing a status.

3. **Selective Disclosure** — Each of the three open issues gets a single sentence of context; the page does not say who raised them or how often. This functions by presenting an incomplete picture as the full one. Concern rises when what is left out would change how the item is read.

4. **Information Control** — You ask for the controller's files and time with the audit chair, and the chief executive sets both up himself; the files come in one folder, in his order. This functions by keeping the corrective evidence at a distance. Concern rises when introductions and records come only through one person.

5. **Repetition as Truth** — "That's the legacy item we discussed" turns up in a budget call and a status email until the backlog has no other name. This functions by letting familiarity pass for fact. Concern rises when the term is repeated by people who have not reviewed the matter.

6. **Social Proof** — The room nods, the controller says nothing, two executives repeat "legacy," and you write it yourself in a note to the team. This functions by making the frame look shared. Concern rises when agreement appears without anyone citing the underlying records.

7. **Priming** — You read the controller's hand on a folder and the chair's request for time through the briefing, do not ask about the folder, and wonder whether you answered like a person being tested. This functions by making neutral observations confirm the memo. Concern rises when you notice that you are interpreting before you have asked.

## Why This Order

**Anchoring** comes first, because the earliest information has the greatest weight. A description received before arrival becomes the baseline against which the person's own observations are compared. It has to come before the word "legacy," because that label only sorts the controller and the three open issues the way it does once page two has told you who she is.

> You ask the price of a used bike and hear "forty." Even when you haggle down to thirty, the thirty feels like a win. Flip it: if the seller first said "Serious buyers don't haggle," then named forty, you would still ask what the bike is worth and name your own price.

**False Framing** then supplies the categories. "Legacy," "territorial," "tests early": these are not facts, but they sort facts. It has to come before the one-line context on each issue, because "legacy" holds only if the page names the problems and never says who raised them, or how often.

> A friend calls their lateness "being relaxed" and your annoyance "being uptight." Now you are arguing about your mood, not the hour. Flip it: if your friend first said, truthfully, "I was only late once this week," and only then called your annoyance "being uptight," you would list every day you waited.

**Selective Disclosure** is what makes the sorting stable. If the controller's year of escalations were on the page, the label would not hold, so the page must be short on exactly that. It has to come before the single folder arranged in his order, because that folder only seems complete if the briefing has already left out who raised the issues and how often.

> A housemate tells you, truthfully, that a neighbour once complained about noise. They leave out that it was one evening, years ago. Flip it: if your housemate first said you should leave all talk with the neighbour to them, then mentioned that the neighbour once complained, you would knock and ask the neighbour yourself.

**Information Control** is what keeps the lens in place after the first week. If the new executive had independent access to people and files, they could check the labels. When introductions and records flow through the same sponsor, the frame is hard to test, and the person's own diligence runs through that channel. It has to come before the word "legacy" at the senior meeting, because that word becomes the backlog's name only once the files and introductions that could give it another pass through the man who says it.

> Picture a new school where one pupil shows you round, tells you who to sit with and hands you the timetable. You never get the chance to find out anything by yourself. Flip it: if the pupil who showed you round first told you three times that the quiet boy by the window is "the grumpy one," and only then handed you the timetable, you would say hello to him yourself.

**Repetition as Truth** and **Social Proof** are the maintenance layer. A word said in three meetings becomes a name. It has to come before the nodding room, because two executives can only repeat "legacy" once the chief executive has said it enough times for it to sound like the item's name.

> Someone calls a quiet colleague "the grumpy one" in three chats, and by Friday you are thinking of her that way yourself. Flip it: if everyone in the first chat you joined called her "the grumpy one" at once, before you had heard it anywhere else, you would take her a coffee and judge for yourself.

**Social Proof** follows: a name that others repeat becomes a consensus. This is what weakens independent evaluation: disagreeing now means disagreeing with a room. It has to come before your memory of the controller's folder, because you only read her warm formality as territorial once a room of colleagues has echoed the word you were handed.

> Everyone around the table calls a film boring, and your own enjoyment of it gets harder to say out loud. Flip it: if, before the film started, someone said "you'll find this one dull," and then everyone at the table agreed it was boring, you would still say which scene you liked.

**Priming** closes the loop. By the time the person sees ordinary behaviour, they have already been told what to see. It also supplies plausible deniability, since the briefing was only a courtesy and the interpretation was theirs. It has to come last, because your cautious answers to the audit chair only look like being tested once the page, the word and the room have taught you what to watch for.

> Before you meet a neighbour's dog, you are told it bites. Then every wag of its tail looks like the start of something. Flip it: if you were told the neighbour's dog bites before you met it, and only then everyone on the street said the same, you would ask the owner to introduce you and watch its tail yourself.

The key conversion point is the first time the new executive repeats the frame in their own voice. What would weaken the sequence is early, independent contact: a conversation with the controller and the chair before forming a view, a request for the original documents, and a habit of asking, "What evidence supports this description?"

## Why the System Produces This Order

A new executive arrives with a handover. The company wants them to start fast, the outgoing or sponsoring executive has the history, and the first weeks are crowded: introductions to arrange, files to find, meetings to attend. A briefing note is the quickest way to hand over what someone knows, and the person who wrote it is the person best placed to make the introductions. Nobody has to design a sequence for what follows: these facts produce it.

Anchoring comes first because the briefing arrives before the first day, when the newcomer has no view at all. Whatever is on page two is the first description they will hold, and every observation afterwards is compared with it.

False Framing comes second because a short note needs short categories. "Legacy" and "territorial" are efficient words, and they sort the controller and the open issues without anyone having to argue the case. Nothing here needs a villain; brevity does the work.

Selective Disclosure comes third because a seven-page note cannot hold a year of escalations. What goes in is a matter of what the writer finds worth a line, and a true sentence about three issues can sit on the page with nothing about who raised them.

Information Control comes fourth because the writer is also the person arranging the files and introductions. Access follows the sponsor, and the new executive's diligence runs through the same channel that drew the first picture.

Repetition as Truth comes fifth because meetings are where shared words are made. A word said under item three and item five becomes the item's name, whoever first meant it as shorthand.

Social Proof comes sixth because a room of senior people tends to follow its chief executive. When two executives pick the word up, disagreeing with it would mean disagreeing with the table.

Priming comes last because by now every ordinary behaviour of the controller and the audit chair arrives pre-labelled. A guarded answer to a plain question looks like being tested because the note said it would be.

Put it together, and the order is the order this setting needs: a first picture, its labels, a short page, one channel, a repeated word, a nodding room and an expectation. It turns an experienced reader of books into someone who judges new people by what he was told first.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A handover note, a busy chief executive and the habit of routing everything through a sponsor shaped the whole thing, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ulises Haverford had been hired as head of procurement. Eight days before she started, Rupert Quarles, the operations director, sent her a five-page note. Page one gave the supplier budget as ninety thousand a month. Page two called the senior buyer "loyal, a little possessive about contracts." Page three told her to treat three open disputes as "legacy," each with a line of background and nothing more. When she asked for the contract files and for time with the finance director, Rupert said he would arrange both. The files arrived in a single folder, in his order. In her second week she heard "legacy" in three meetings, once about an unrelated invoice. Two colleagues repeated it back, and the room nodded. Before her first coffee with the finance director he mentioned that the man "tends to test newcomers early." She answered the man's plain questions with great care, and afterwards wondered whether she had passed.

Rupert was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that Ulises had settled in nicely and already sounded like one of the team.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The new executive may find themselves watching a colleague for the traits they were told about, and discounting contrary signs. Over time, they may defer to the sponsor on people decisions, while believing the views are their own. They may feel an unease they cannot name, a sense of having started late. When they finally see the full file, they may feel embarrassed, and may be reluctant to correct the frame because they have spoken in it.

## Institutional Echo

Handovers, onboarding packs and board briefings can operationalize this pattern without anyone intending it, since they are written by someone with a stake and read by someone without context. Executive transition notes that characterise colleagues, and committee preparation notes that characterise a chair, may serve as lenses in this way. Talk of "legacy" issues can also be a real status label in a turnaround, though it can function as a reason not to look. Whether disclosure duties to a new officer exist depends on the firm and jurisdiction.

The honest reading is that sponsors are trying to be helpful and that opinions about colleagues are normal. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether material context was known to the author and omitted, and whether the same lens was given to others.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good briefing saves time, and that a wise leader trusts a predecessor's read. "Hit the ground running" and "no politics, no surprises" make independent inquiry sound like a delay.

The value being exploited is the natural wish to be helped at the moment you are most uncertain. The public story is a generous handover. The private loss, when this pattern is present, is the chance to see people and facts before they were described.

## Observable Indicators

1. A briefing characterises colleagues by temperament before any meeting.
2. Open issues are labelled as legacy, settled or not worth revisiting.
3. Context is minimal for the items most likely to need scrutiny.
4. Introductions to key people are arranged and attended by the sponsor.
5. Access to files and committees is mediated by one person.
6. A single word describing a problem spreads across meetings.
7. People repeat the description without having reviewed the matter.
8. A predecessor's departure is explained in one disparaging sentence.
9. Questions about the labels are met with "we covered that."
10. The new executive starts to judge colleagues by the memo's terms.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The transition memo and briefing in every version, with dates received.
- Notes of first impressions you recorded before and after meeting the people described.
- Records of which introductions and files were arranged by whom.
- Original documents behind each "legacy" item, where you lawfully hold or are entitled to see them.
- Dated notes of when a label first appeared in meetings and who used it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Most transition notes are sincere, and candid views of colleagues can be useful. A predecessor's departure may genuinely be about differences of method, and some items are legacy in the true sense.

The discriminator is whether the frame survives contact. A helpful briefing is revised when new facts arrive and invites checking. This pattern resists it.

Concern strengthens when: the memo omits context that the author knew; checking the labels is discouraged; the same descriptors are used about several people who raised concerns.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The First-Impression Test:**
Ask: "Did I form my view of this person before I met them?"
Concern rises if: the memo gave me the view first.

**SPOT IT — The Label Test:**
Ask: "What evidence sits behind the word 'legacy'?"
Concern rises if: nobody can point to a document.

**SPOT IT — The Channel Test:**
Ask: "Can I reach the people and files without the sponsor in between?"
Concern rises if: I cannot.

**SPOT IT — The Echo Test:**
Ask: "Who repeating this has reviewed it?"
Concern rises if: nobody has.

## Chain Breakers

- **Read the memo as opinion.** Mark every line that is a judgement, not a fact.
- **Meet the people described** before you form a view, and listen first.
- **Ask for the original documents** behind each open item.
- **Ask the audit committee chair, the controller and the previous owner of each item** for their own account, separately.
- **Write down your first impression** and revisit it at ninety days.
- **Ask what evidence supports a label** whenever it is used in a meeting.
- **Avoid repeating the sponsor's terms** until you have checked them.
- **Obtain independent advice** if the issues touch controls, reporting or your own liability.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A label is repeated without a document; key access runs through the sponsor; your questions are met with "we covered that."

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A sidelined or labelled colleague turns out to hold material information on controls or reporting; you are discouraged from speaking to the audit committee; your own sign-off is requested on items you have not been allowed to review. Consider independent counsel, the audit committee chair, a compliance officer, or an independent auditor.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, evidence of fraud in progress, or statements of self-harm by anyone involved. Do not confront; preserve records lawfully, tell someone you trust, and contact counsel, law enforcement or emergency services as appropriate.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Thank you for the briefing. I would like to read the underlying documents myself before I form a view."
- "Could we set up one-to-ones with the controller and the chair, without anyone present, so I can introduce myself?"
- "What evidence do we have for the reconciliation issue being a legacy item?"
- "I would like to hear the previous owner's account of this item."
- "Can you point me to the file behind that description?"
- "I will hold off on repeating that characterisation until I have reviewed it."
