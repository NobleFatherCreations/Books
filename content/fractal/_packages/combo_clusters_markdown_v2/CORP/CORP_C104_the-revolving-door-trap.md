# C104: The Revolving Door Trap — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a quiet belief that every new plan is temporary, a reflex to nod along while waiting it out, and the sense that no effort lasts long enough to matter.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of giving any fresh start only half your effort, keeping the rest back for when it is quietly dropped.
- An automatic thought at every big announcement, "this one won't last either," before anyone has said what it is.
- A pull toward not asking who is responsible, because you expect that person to be gone before the answer comes.

**A point in a life.** The middle years of a career.

**Where it happens.** A large corporate strategy office.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a senior programme manager, forty-two, and you have been at the company for nine years, which means you have outlasted four heads of strategy. You keep the kickoff deck from each of them in a drawer at home. You are not sentimental about it. You keep them the way a sailor keeps charts of waters that have since changed.

**Monday morning, the all-hands**

The fifth head of strategy has been in the building eleven weeks. She is thoughtful, and she has listened. The auditorium smells of coffee and carpet glue, and four hundred people sit with their laptops shut, which almost never happens. A calendar invitation went out on Friday with a title you have already read twice: "Initiative 2.0: A Fresh Start." She walks out without notes and stands on the carpet, not the stage.

"You have told me what has been missing," she says. "A clear direction. A plan you can see. I'm here to give you one."

She talks for a minute about "honouring what came before," and then for forty minutes about what is next. Your shoulders drop an inch. You had wanted someone to say it. Behind you a woman whispers, "Finally," and the whole row nods. You write one word on your pad, direction, and underline it.

**Wednesday, a sixth-floor conference room**

The kickoff deck is crisp. Slide four shows seven workstreams, each named with a verb, and under them twenty-three initiatives. There is a governance calendar and a launch event at the end of the month. You count, because counting is what you do, and you are halfway down the list before you realise you could not say which of them you would ask about first. A director at the end of the table asks how many of them have a dedicated lead. "Everyone leads something," the head of strategy says, and people laugh, warmly.

You look for the old ones. The onboarding redesign appears as a bullet inside a box called "Experience Foundations," with no owner and no date. The data quality effort is not on the page. When you ask, someone says it has been "folded in." Then you are asked to build the transition plan, mapping "legacy initiatives" into the new structure by Friday. Your name is on none of the old work now, and neither is anyone else's.

Before the meeting ends, a vice president reads out one more line. The onboarding pilot, two weeks from its first results, stops on Friday because it does not map to a workstream. Nobody objects. You write the date in the margin of your own copy.

**Wednesday evening, your study at home**

That evening you open the drawer to year six. The supplier consolidation is there: one chart, one bar for the first savings, which showed the month before it was stopped. There was never a second bar. The paper has gone soft at the corners. You stand with the deck in your hand until your partner calls up the stairs to ask if you are all right.

"Fine," you call back. "Looking for something."

You put the chart back on top of the pile and shut the drawer with your knee, gently. Four decks, four heads of strategy, and a fifth already waiting in your bag.

**Thursday, your desk on the fourth floor**

Your team of delivery leads gather around your desk after the stand-up. Nobody says anything cynical. A product owner says she is excited. A senior analyst says he will "make sure the data fits the new taxonomy." A programme coordinator pulls her laptop around to show you her new template, already filled in for the first week. Behind the template is a second window, a private tracker with the old work still in it, in case. She does not close it, and you do not ask. They have all done this before, and they adopt just enough to be compliant. You have one too.

Later you open the last page of the deck, and it is only a date. The first value checkpoint is fourteen months away, which, you note, is about how long the last four held her job. She stops by your desk, coat on, on her way to the airport. "And please include a slide on lessons learned," she says warmly, "so we really honour the last cycle." You open a blank page and type the title: "What we learned." Under it you could write the same four bullets that sat in the last three decks.

**Two years later, a Sunday in October**

Two years later it is a Sunday in October, and your phone shows an invitation for Monday: the sixth head of strategy, a launch called "Momentum." You read the title and turn the phone face down on the counter. By the time you have rinsed your cup you have already counted the months until the corner office changes hands. You do not open the attachment.

Your niece, six weeks into her first job at the company, texts you. "Who runs Momentum? Who do I send my questions to?" You type, "Send it to the shared inbox," and you do not look at the name on page one, because you will not know it by spring.

On Tuesday you will give the Momentum workstream two tidy hours a week. The rest of your best work lives in a spreadsheet on your personal laptop, with your name on every row, where nobody can reorganize it. Last month a recruiter asked what you were proudest of building here. You said, "It was a team effort," and heard how fast it came out.

The drawer needed a second shelf last spring. Your partner asks what you want from the year, and you cannot name one thing you finished and would sign. The last thing you built that anyone remembers is a chart with one bar.

## The Name

That's **The Revolving Door Trap**.

## Definition

The Revolving Door Trap is the sequence where leadership turnover is used as an institutional reset, using Leadership Arrival Narrative, Initiative Proliferation, Predecessor Erasure, Implementation Interruption, Teacher Insulation, and Accountability Reset to convert hope that new leadership will bring real improvement into the loss of continuity and the ability for any initiative to be fairly evaluated. It may benefit leaders who need a visible mandate and a clean slate, and it can also benefit institutions that prefer not to be judged on long-run results. It exploits the optimism that greets every arrival. It is hard to detect because each leader's choices are defensible, and because those with the longest memory have the least authority. What may be lost is cumulative learning, staff trust, and the chance that any single effort will be allowed to mature. The turnover itself may be driven by market conditions, not intent; the pattern is in how accountability resets.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Leadership Arrival Narrative** — The story of what was missing: the fifth head of strategy tells the all-hands what the company has lacked, promises a clear direction under "Initiative 2.0: A Fresh Start," and talks of "honouring what came before." This functions by creating an authorised account of the past in which the new leader is the turning point. Concern rises when each arrival narrative describes the previous era in similar terms.

2. **Initiative Proliferation** — The count: seven workstreams with verb names, twenty-three initiatives and a launch event at the end of the month. This functions by displaying leadership through the volume of new activity. Concern rises when the number of initiatives grows faster than the capacity to deliver them, and none is tied to a measurable outcome.

3. **Predecessor Erasure** — The pages that are gone: the onboarding redesign is an unowned, undated bullet inside "Experience Foundations," the data quality effort is absent, "folded in," and you are asked to map "legacy initiatives" into the new structure by Friday. This functions by removing the baseline against which the new leader would be judged. Concern rises when previous results disappear from the narrative, including those that were working.

4. **Implementation Interruption** — The bar with no successor: the year-six supplier consolidation, in your drawer, showed its first savings the month before it was stopped, and there was no second bar. This functions by ensuring no effort lasts long enough to show its results, good or bad. Concern rises when programmes are replaced before the first evaluation point.

5. **Teacher Insulation** — The corridor: the delivery leads say they are excited, fit the data to the new taxonomy and show new templates, while keeping the old work alive in private trackers. This functions by protecting the people who deliver from the instability above them, at the cost of engagement. Concern rises when compliance and belief diverge and staff speak of "waiting it out."

6. **Accountability Reset** — The distant date: the first "value checkpoint" is fourteen months out, about as long as the last four people held her job, and you are asked for a "lessons learned" slide. This functions by starting a fresh clock for every leader. Concern rises when checkpoints fall after the average tenure of the people who set them.

## Why This Order

The **Arrival Narrative** must come first because it authorises everything else. A leader who arrives to continue what exists has no mandate for change; a leader who arrives to rescue the company from its past has a mandate for almost anything. The narrative is also sincere, which makes it persuasive: new leaders often do see real problems. It has to come before the slide of twenty-three initiatives, because the room only reads that slide as a plan after it has cheered her promise to give you one.

> Picture a new neighbour who says "this street has needed someone like me" and gets cheers at the first meeting. Nobody asks yet what the street was missing. Flip it: if the new neighbour handed out a long list of ten street projects at the first meeting before saying anything about what the street needed, you would ask what all of it was for.

**Initiative Proliferation** follows because the narrative needs a visible form. A new leader is judged early on by evidence of movement, and the quickest evidence is a larger portfolio. It also builds a structure that only the new leader can navigate, which raises the cost of replacing the leader for a time. It has to come before the old work vanishing from the deck, because the data quality effort can only be left off a page that is already crowded with new items.

> Think of a new coach who adds ten drills to Monday's practice. Everyone is busy and out of breath, and nobody can say which drill is helping. Flip it: if the new coach first told everyone to forget every drill used before, and only then added ten new ones on Monday, you would have nothing to compare them against and could not tell which helped.

**Predecessor Erasure** is the step that makes the next step safe. If the earlier work remains visible, its results can be measured and compared. Once it is "folded in," the baseline is gone, and no one can say whether the new approach is better than the old one. This is the point where the pattern weakens independent evaluation the most. It has to come before the onboarding pilot being stopped, because with no owner or date left on the page, nobody at the table can say the pilot is close to results.

> Picture a hallway of family photos repainted before the new partner moves in. With the old pictures gone, there is nothing left to compare the new house against. Flip it: if the repainting of the hallway stopped halfway, with the family photos still hanging on the unpainted wall, you would still have pictures to compare the new house against.

**Implementation Interruption** is the operative mechanism, because a result that never matures cannot be tested. The supplier consolidation stopped one month before its benefit is not an example of malice; it is an example of the structural problem that nothing finishes. It has to come before the team's quiet compliance, because the pilot stopping two weeks from its results shows the delivery leads that old work will not be allowed to finish.

> Think of a cake taken out of the oven at minute twenty because someone wants to try a different recipe. You never learn whether the first one would have risen. Flip it: if you had already nodded at the new recipe and tucked the old one into your apron pocket when someone reached for the cake at minute twenty, you would simply carry on with the first recipe.

**Teacher Insulation** explains why the institution keeps functioning: the delivery layer protects itself with surface compliance and private trackers. This gives plausible deniability to everyone, since the new templates are in use and no one can show disengagement. It has to come before the checkpoint date, because the filled-in templates and private trackers let everyone look compliant, so no one objects when the first checkpoint is fourteen months away.

> Picture a child who nods at every new house rule and keeps the real homework list in a notebook under the bed. Flip it: if the family announced that every earlier missed chore no longer counted, and only then handed you the new house rules, you would leave your real homework list out on the desk.

**Accountability Reset** closes the loop: the first checkpoint arrives after the likely departure, so the next leader inherits the story. It has to come last, because the lessons-learned slide and the fourteen-month date only land once the team has stopped expecting any result to be finished or counted.

> Picture a lease whose first inspection is booked for after you expect to have moved out. Whatever it shows is for the next tenant to deal with. Flip it: if the lease already put the first inspection after you expect to move out, and only then did you start nodding along to each new clause, you would see little reason to read any of them.

The key conversion point is the lessons-learned slide, which is repeated and never acted on. What would weaken the sequence: a persistent register of commitments that survives leadership changes; a rule that previous initiatives are formally closed with results, not folded in; and evaluation dates fixed independently of who holds the post.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A large company is built around a few facts that have nothing to do with anyone's character. Senior leaders stay in a post for a short stretch, often shorter than a project takes to finish. A new leader is expected to show a direction within weeks, to a board and to staff who are waiting for one. And the thing that gets counted in those weeks is activity: how many initiatives are running, how many teams are involved, how many launches are on the calendar. Results arrive later and belong to whoever is sitting there by then.

The Arrival Narrative comes first because a new leader has no history in the building and needs a reason to be listened to. Saying that the company has been missing a clear plan gives them one, and it is usually sincere, since new leaders often do see real gaps. Nobody has to invent it; the first all-hands needs a story.

Initiative Proliferation comes second because a story needs something to show. A long list is the quickest visible proof of movement, and a list of twenty-three can be put on one slide before any one of them has done anything.

Predecessor Erasure comes third because the new list needs room. Earlier programmes have owners, budgets and baselines, and they compete for the same attention. Tidying them into a legacy category is a reasonable-looking way to manage a crowded calendar, and it also removes the numbers anyone could use to compare.

Implementation Interruption comes fourth because a portfolio that big cannot be funded or staffed in full. Something has to stop, and the projects nearest to producing a result are the easiest to see and to cut, since stopping them frees people at once. Nothing finishes, so nothing can be tested.

Teacher Insulation comes fifth because the delivery layer has to keep the company running through all this. People who have seen several cycles learn to adopt enough of the new template to be safe and to keep their own records for the old work. Nobody coordinates it; each person is protecting a job.

Accountability Reset comes last because review dates are set by the planning calendar, and the planning calendar is longer than a leader's stay. The first checkpoint lands after the likely departure, and the next person inherits the story along with the slide on lessons learned.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a story, then a list, then a clean page, then a cut, then quiet compliance, then a date in the distance. What it turns the person in the middle into is someone who does the visible part of the work and keeps the real part in a drawer.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A short tenure clock, a quarterly review and a count of launches did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Tomas Ibarra had been a client delivery lead for three weeks when the new director, Lionel Lanton, took him to lunch. Lionel said Tomas was the first person in the division who had told him the truth about what was missing, and that the division had been waiting years for someone with a plan. Tomas went back to his desk standing taller.

By the next Monday there were twelve new workstreams on a slide, and Tomas had been given two of them. The dashboard he had built in his first week was no longer on the shared page; when he asked, the folder said "archived." The client reporting pilot he had started was due to show its first numbers on the fourteenth. On the eleventh Lionel told him it was being paused, with thanks, to keep everyone focused.

Tomas noticed his teammates thanking Lionel in meetings and then, afterward, keeping their own notes of the old work in files with plain names. He started one too. When the first review was announced, it was set for the following September. "That gives us time to really see results," Lionel said.

Lionel was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that September would belong to whoever came next.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may find that hope has turned into a practised courtesy. Over time, they may stop investing deeply in any new programme, protect their own records, and treat each launch as weather. They may be admired for their resilience while quietly losing belief.

They may feel responsible for a continuity that nobody has asked them to hold. They may notice that they have become the institution's memory without authority, and that the role is both a source of pride and a quiet drain. Younger colleagues may learn from them to adopt lightly, and the pattern passes down.

## Institutional Echo

Organisations with frequent senior turnover can slide into repeated resets, particularly where leaders are evaluated on the launch of initiatives and not on long-run results. Consulting engagements, which often bring a new framework with a new leader, can reinforce the cycle. Planning calendars, annual budgets, and incentive schemes that reward initiation may make it more likely.

Tenure averages for senior roles are often cited, but figures vary and should not be assumed. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether evaluation dates were consistently set beyond a leader's likely tenure and whether previous results were measured before being retired.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a new leader is a new beginning: that organisations change by changing the person at the top, and that the past is a burden to be shed. The language is "fresh start," "reset," "next chapter," "new operating model."

The value being exploited is hope, which is a fine thing and is often justified. The public story is a company renewing itself. The private cost, when this pattern operates, is that the people who deliver have learned to wait, and the thing that was working is not allowed to finish.

## Observable Indicators

1. Each arrival comes with a story of the previous era's shortcomings.
2. The number of workstreams or initiatives grows with each cycle.
3. Earlier initiatives are "folded in" without owners or dates.
4. Programmes are stopped or replaced before a defined evaluation point.
5. Delivery teams keep private trackers alongside official ones.
6. Staff adopt new templates quickly and without visible commitment.
7. First results checkpoints fall well beyond the likely tenure of the sponsor.
8. Lessons-learned slides recur with similar content and no recorded follow-up.
9. Budget moves from earlier programmes to new ones without a written closure.
10. Institutional memory resides with a few long-tenured individuals who lack decision rights.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Each kickoff deck and roadmap from every leadership cycle, with dates.
- Charters, benefit cases, and success measures for programmes that were stopped or folded in.
- Records of results achieved by stopped programmes before closure.
- Budget and resourcing changes between cycles.
- Your own dated record of what each programme had delivered when it ended.
- The evaluation dates set for each new initiative and their relation to leadership terms.
- Lessons-learned documents from each cycle, side by side.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Strategy genuinely changes. A new leader may find a failing programme and close it, and market conditions can force a new direction. Some resets are healthy.

Benign lookalikes specific to this pattern:

- **Real strategic shift.** A merger, a market shock, or a regulation can justify new priorities.
- **Honest sunset.** A programme that failed its measure should be closed.
- **Portfolio consolidation.** Folding initiatives together can reduce overlap.
- **Normal turnover.** Senior roles change for personal and market reasons.

Concern strengthens when: programmes with positive early results are closed without evaluation; baselines and owners disappear; and accountability checkpoints repeatedly fall after the sponsor's likely departure.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Baseline Test:**
Ask: "What happened to the results of the last programme?"
Concern rises if: nobody can say, and the work is absent from the deck.

**SPOT IT — The Checkpoint Test:**
Ask: "When will this be evaluated, and who will hold the role then?"
Concern rises if: the date falls beyond the sponsor's likely tenure.

**SPOT IT — The Closure Test:**
Ask: "Was the earlier initiative formally closed, with results recorded?"
Concern rises if: it was "folded in."

**SPOT IT — The Tracker Test:**
Ask: "Do delivery teams keep their own private trackers?"
Concern rises if: yes, "in case."

## Chain Breakers

- **Keep a commitments register.** A short, dated record of what each programme promised, spent, and delivered.
- **Ask for formal closure.** "Could we record the results of the supplier work before it moves into the new structure?"
- **Request an evaluation date.** "What date will we assess this, and what will we look at?"
- **Pair each new initiative with a measure.** One line: the outcome, the baseline, and the review date.
- **Offer continuity, not obstruction.** Propose that work with early results continue to its checkpoint inside the new structure.
- **Do not refuse the new plan outright.** Engage honestly and put your concerns in writing.
- **Share the register with the sponsor and a neutral party.** For example, an internal audit or a transformation office.
- **Look after your own career.** Keep a record of your results and build relationships beyond the programme.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A programme with demonstrated benefits is stopped without evaluation; baselines or owners disappear; or evaluation dates fall beyond the sponsor's likely tenure.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Material spend is written off without a recorded result; records of earlier commitments are altered or deleted; or you face consequences for raising the pattern. Routes include internal audit, an audit committee, independent directors, an employment adviser, and counsel if contractual commitments to customers or suppliers are affected.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats or retaliation after raising concerns; any self-harm statement by anyone involved. Prioritise safety, contact local emergency services or crisis lines, and consult qualified counsel. Do not confront anyone. Do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we record what the supplier work had delivered before it moves into the new structure?"
- "What date will the first checkpoint be, and who will own it then?"
- "I'd like to add a one-line outcome and baseline to each workstream. Is that workable?"
- "Could we formally close the onboarding redesign, with results, so we can see what we learned?"
- "I'm glad to help translate the roadmap. I'd like the translation to keep the owners and dates."
- "May I share a short register of past commitments with the programme office?"
- "What would tell us in six months that this is working?"

## Reflection

If no initiative is ever allowed to finish, who has the right to say that any of them failed?
