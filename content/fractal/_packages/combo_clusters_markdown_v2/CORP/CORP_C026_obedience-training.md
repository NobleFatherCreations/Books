# C026: Obedience Training — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a reflex to follow any rule, however small or pointless, and a belief that a single slip calls for a large apology, because questioning a rule once brought trouble for everyone.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of apologising many times for a tiny mistake, and offering to make up for it far beyond what anyone asked.
- A pull toward settings with strict rules, where obeying feels calmer than deciding which rules actually matter.
- An automatic thought, "just do it, it's not worth the trouble," that silences a question before you ask it.

**A point in a life.** The first year of a career.

**Where it happens.** A fund-owned company's portfolio operations team.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're twenty-five, an analyst in your first real job, on the portfolio operations team of a company owned by a private equity fund. You got the role by being careful. You are the sort of person who proofreads a two-line email, and you took the offer believing that carefulness was what they wanted.

**Your second day, the operations floor**

Your manager puts a single sheet on your desk, next to the laptop still in its plastic. It is called Team Standards. It is tidy and confident. Slides must use the approved template, with titles at twenty-eight point and no more than three bullets per slide. Status updates go in at four-fifteen, not four-twenty.

You read it twice. It seems a little fussy, and also reassuring. Rules mean you can get it right.

The last standard reaches past the slides: messages from the manager must be answered within ten minutes during working hours. Below it is a space for your signature, "acknowledging that you have read and will uphold these standards." Everyone has signed; he shows you the folder. You sign. It feels like joining something.

· · ·

For three months it is fine, though you notice that he reads your slides closely, and that his comments are about spacing, not substance. You notice he keeps a spreadsheet. Then one afternoon you reply to one of his messages in eleven minutes, because you were in the lift. The next morning there is a printed note on your desk, with a date, a reference to Standard Four, and a line that says "pattern of delayed responsiveness." You apologise. You tell him it will not happen again, and it does not, because you now keep your phone in your hand on the way to the toilet.

**May, his glass office**

A slide goes out with a title at twenty-six point. You did not see it. He calls you in. The blinds are half down and the air conditioning is too cold. He does not raise his voice. He projects the slide on the wall, and the two points of difference look enormous at that size. He asks you to explain what the standard is for.

You explain. He nods slowly. Then he asks you to rebuild the entire deck, all forty-one slides, by the morning, and to send him a one-paragraph reflection on "why attention to standards matters."

You stay until midnight. The cleaners vacuum around your chair. You send the paragraph at four minutes past twelve and reread it in the taxi, worried about a comma. He replies with one word: "Noted."

The Monday team meeting is in the same office, eight chairs around his glass table. A senior colleague mentions, laughing, that he sent last week's deck on the wrong template, and the manager thanks him for the numbers. When it is your turn, you ask, gently, whether the four-fifteen deadline could move to four-thirty, because the market data only lands at four. He smiles. "It's good to hear from people who still need the standards explained." The others look at their laptops. Your cheeks go hot. You say, "Of course," and write something meaningless in your notebook so your hands have something to do.

**June, your desk on the floor**

For two weeks you do everything on the sheet, and nothing comes: no note, no projector. Every message answered in four minutes. Every title at twenty-eight point. Your shoulders ache from the way you sit, ready.

On the third Friday he stops at your desk and taps it once. "See? Easy." Something in your shoulders lets go, and you are more grateful than you have ever been for being left alone. You go home almost happy. On Monday you work to feel that again.

By the fifth month the team has learned it together. Before anyone sends a deck, someone leans over to check the title size. Tom at the next desk checks yours without asking, and you check his. Nobody raises anything at the Monday meeting any more. A colleague whispers, "Just don't give him a reason," and you realise that is exactly what you have been doing all day. You catch yourself reformatting slides that nobody has asked you to reformat.

**Fourteen months later, a new firm**

Fourteen months later you are at a different company. You chose it, if you are honest, for its handbook: eighty pages, a policy for everything, a laminated card about meeting etiquette beside every screen. Reading it in your first week, you felt your breathing slow.

On a Wednesday a colleague you like, Ana, mentions that the client's name is misspelled on page six of your report. One letter. You apologise. You apologise again at the coffee machine, and a third time by email, and you offer to recheck all ninety pages over the weekend and send her a list of every change. She looks at you oddly. "It's one letter," she says. "I already fixed it." You spend the weekend rechecking anyway.

In a planning meeting someone explains that every expense now needs three sign-offs, even for printer paper. A question rises in you, ordinary and sensible. You feel it reach your throat. Then something older says leave it, keep your head down, and the question is gone before you have decided anything. You nod with everyone else.

You notice that you check the clock whenever you leave a room. Your mother asks how the new job is, and you say, "Fine, they're very organised," and ask about her garden.

On Friday you realise you have not asked anyone why about anything in over a year. You used to be careful because you cared about the work. Now you are careful because, somewhere along the way, you stopped trusting yourself to decide what matters.

## The Name

That's **Obedience Training**.

## Definition

Obedience Training is the sequence where small, arbitrary rules are enforced with disproportionate consequences until compliance becomes a reflex, using Enforcing Trivial Demands, Behavior Control, Overcorrection Demands, Punishment of Boundaries, Negative Reinforcement Traps, and Group Consolidation to convert a desire to avoid punishment for minor infractions into the loss of independent judgement about which rules matter. It is hard to detect because the rules are individually reasonable, formatting and responsiveness standards are normal, and the consequence is framed as professionalism. The concern is not discipline; it is discipline whose content is irrelevant and whose product is submission.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Enforcing Trivial Demands** — A one-page sheet policed down to the point size: titles at twenty-eight point, no more than three bullets, status updates at four-fifteen not four-twenty, and slide comments about spacing rather than substance. This functions by training attention on rules whose content does not matter, so that following them becomes the skill being rewarded. Concern rises when enforcement of the trivial exceeds attention to the substantive.

2. **Behavior Control** — A clock in your pocket: messages from the manager answered within ten minutes, a signature acknowledging every standard, and a dated note in your file for replying in eleven. This functions by extending oversight to small habits, including time away from the desk. Concern rises when you restructure ordinary behaviour to meet a standard that has no stated purpose.

3. **Overcorrection Demands** — A response far larger than the slip: a title at twenty-six point brings the projector, a forty-one-slide rebuild by morning, and a written reflection on "why attention to standards matters." This functions by making the cost of a small lapse disproportionate. Concern rises when the remedy is far larger than the error.

4. **Punishment of Boundaries** — A question answered with a remark about you: when you gently question one standard, he says it is good to hear from people who "still need the standards explained," and the others look at their laptops. This functions by making the questioning of rules costlier than obeying them. Concern rises when questions about rules attract reputational comment.

5. **Negative Reinforcement Traps** — Relief as the reward: after two weeks of doing everything on the sheet with no note, he stops at your desk and says "See? Easy," and your shoulders let go. This functions by making avoidance of discomfort the motive for compliance. Concern rises when the only positive experience is not being criticised.

6. **Group Consolidation** — A team that watches itself: colleagues check each other's title sizes, nobody asks anything at the Monday meeting, and a colleague whispers "just don't give him a reason." This functions by making the group enforce conformity. Concern rises when peers warn each other instead of questioning the rules.

## Why This Order

**Enforcing Trivial Demands** comes first because it is the cheapest to defend. Nobody can argue against tidy slides. A signed standards sheet establishes that the rules were agreed, so any later enforcement is merely the application of something you consented to. It has to come before the ten-minute reply rule, because the signed sheet about title sizes is what makes a rule about your minutes look like one more tidy standard.

> A shared flat has a rule that the spoons face up in the drawer. A tidy house rule is ordinary on its own. You laugh and agree, and what matters is what happens the first time one faces down. Flip it: if your flatmates first asked you to report when you leave and come home, before any rule about spoons, you would find it strange straight away and could say no.

**Behavior Control** extends the reach once the trivial is accepted, from the product to the person's minutes. It has to come before the forty-one-slide rebuild, because that punishment needs a written note already in your file showing the standards cover you, not only your slides.

> A relative expects a reply to every text within five minutes. You start carrying your phone into the shower. Flip it: if the relative made you write a long apology for one slow reply before ever asking for five-minute answers, you would see how far out of scale it was and could tell them so.

**Overcorrection Demands** then sets the price. A harsh response to a small lapse communicates that the margin for error is thin, and only needs to happen once or twice. It has to come before your question about four-fifteen, because the midnight rebuild has already shown you what a small slip costs, so his smile at the table lands harder.

> You arrive two minutes late to a family dinner and are made to wash everyone's dishes and write a note of apology. The slip was small, and you remember the dishes. Flip it: if the family laughed at you for asking why dinner starts at seven sharp, before any fuss about two minutes late, you would notice the laughing on its own and could ask someone else at the table.

**Punishment of Boundaries** teaches what questioning costs, so that the analyst stops testing. It has to come before the quiet Friday, because the relief of "See? Easy." only works once questioning has been made costly and you have stopped testing anything.

> You ask your coach why a drill is done this way, and she says, "Some players need things explained." Nobody asks again. Flip it: if the coach was sharp all week and only eased off when nobody asked anything, before she ever mocked a question, you would see the easing off as a reward for silence and keep asking about the drill.

**Negative Reinforcement Traps** does the work from inside: the analyst now behaves to avoid discomfort, and relief feels like approval. It has to come before the team checks each other's title sizes, because colleagues only police each other once each of you has felt that quiet compliance is what brings relief.

> A teacher is sharp all week and relaxes on Friday if the room has been quiet. You work for the relaxing, not for the maths. Flip it: if the class started shushing each other before the teacher had ever relaxed on a Friday, you would notice the shushing came from nowhere and could ask a classmate why.

**Group Consolidation** closes it. When peers police each other, the manager no longer needs to. It has to come last, because the team only leans over to check title sizes once each of you has felt the Friday relief and wants to keep it.

> In a class where one pupil was told off for chatting, the others soon shush each other before the teacher turns round. Flip it: if the pupils shushed each other first, and only later the teacher began relaxing on quiet Fridays, you would see the quiet came before any reward and could ask why everyone was so careful.

The conversion point is when the analyst starts enforcing the standards on themselves, unprompted.

Whether this reflects deliberate design or an anxious manager's attempt at rigour is not obvious; intent would require additional evidence. What weakens it: asking what each standard is for, comparing treatment across colleagues, and seeking outside benchmarks for what is reasonable.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A company owned by a private equity fund runs on a few structural facts. Everything flows upward into reports: the fund's partners read decks, not people, so a page that looks uniform is a page that looks controlled. A middle manager is judged on whether those decks ever embarrass him, which makes surface errors feel more dangerous than weak thinking. And junior analysts arrive eager, replaceable and without a benchmark, with no way of telling whether their team's rules are normal.

Enforcing Trivial Demands comes first because templates are the cheapest control a reporting chain can buy. Nobody objects to tidy slides, and a signed sheet turns them into an agreement. Once a small rule has been accepted in writing, enforcing it looks like fairness.

Behavior Control comes second because a manager answerable for every deadline wants his people within reach. A rule about reply times is a natural extension of a rule about formats, and it moves the standard from the product to the person's minutes.

Overcorrection Demands come third because, in a setting where one wrong page can travel upward to the fund, the manager's own fear is out of scale. The response to a small lapse takes on the size of that fear: a whole deck rebuilt, a reflection written. It only needs to happen once to set the price.

Punishment of Boundaries comes fourth because standards that exist to protect a manager cannot easily be explained. A question about why the deadline is four-fifteen exposes that, so the question is treated as a gap in the asker. A tired room watches, and learns.

Negative Reinforcement Traps come fifth because pressure in a reporting chain is constant, and its absence feels like a gift. When the notes stop for a fortnight, relief arrives, and relief is the most powerful reward the setting has to give.

Group Consolidation comes last because everyone on the team shares the same exposure. When one person's slip can bring the projector out for anyone, checking a colleague's title size becomes a kindness. The manager no longer needs to look.

Put it together and the order is the order a reporting chain needs to make a team produce uniform pages without anyone asking why. Nobody has to plan it. It turns a careful, thoughtful analyst into someone who follows every rule and can no longer tell which ones matter.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A reporting chain that reads decks instead of people, a manager judged on uniform pages and a team with nothing to compare itself against did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a new associate on a client site. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Sam Adeyemi joined a consulting team on a long client project as its newest associate. On his first morning the engagement manager, Clare Voss, gave him a list: every file named in a set pattern, every email signed off the same way. Sam liked the order of it. A week later the list grew a line: everyone logs their location in a shared sheet every hour. When Sam named one file with a dash instead of an underscore, Clare asked him to rename all two hundred files on the shared drive that night and write her a note on why naming mattered. At the next team call he asked, politely, what the hourly log was for. Clare said some people needed more structure than others, and the call moved on. For the next three weeks nothing happened, no notes, no corrections, and on a Friday she told him, "Good week." Sam felt light all weekend. By the second month the associates were checking each other's file names before anything went into the shared drive, and nobody asked about the log.

Much later, Sam saw a forwarded email in which Clare told another manager that her teams "run themselves by the second month."

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The analyst may begin to check the clock, hold a phone at all times and over-format work no one asked to be reformatted. Over time, they may lose the habit of suggesting improvements and start to judge their own worth by compliance. They may feel pride in meeting trivial standards and shame at small lapses, and be unable to tell friends why the job feels so tense when the work itself is simple.

## Institutional Echo

Performance standards, templates and response-time norms are common in investor-backed companies, and many are sensible quality and responsiveness controls. Signed acknowledgments can help clarity, and can also function as a record against the employee. Written notes in files can be fair documentation or selective documentation, depending on whether they are applied evenly. What counts as lawful monitoring or discipline varies by place. Intent would require additional evidence: whether standards are applied equally, whether the consequences are proportionate, and whether substantive feedback accompanies the formatting feedback.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that attention to detail is character, and that people who resist small rules will resist big ones. Many cultures prize discipline, and early-career workers are told to earn trust through small things. The language that makes it sound noble is professionalism, standards and excellence. The public story is a rigorous team that expects the best. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a person who stops asking which rules matter.

## Observable Indicators

1. Written standards cover formatting and response times in granular detail.
2. Employees sign acknowledgments for the standards.
3. A small deviation draws a file note or written reflection request.
4. Remedies are far larger than the error, such as a full rebuild for one formatting lapse.
5. Colleagues are treated unequally under the same standards.
6. Questions about a rule are described as needing "explanation" or as a sign of attitude.
7. The manager maintains a log of small lapses.
8. Peers warn one another to avoid giving the manager a reason.
9. Substantive feedback is rare compared with formatting feedback.
10. Employees begin to self-police and over-comply.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The Team Standards sheet, every version, and your signed acknowledgment.
- Every written note or write-up, with date, standard cited and the facts as you recall them.
- Instances where the same lapse by others drew no response, with dates, as far as you lawfully know.
- Requests to rebuild work, reflection requests and the time they took.
- Messages showing reply-time expectations and any out-of-hours demands.
- A dated list of substantive feedback you received compared with formatting feedback.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Templates, response standards and file notes are normal in investor-backed companies, and a new analyst can need firm calibration. The discriminator is **proportionality and consistency**: a genuine standard has a stated purpose, applies to everyone, and brings a remedy sized to the error.

Benign lookalikes:

- **Real quality control.** Template compliance matters in investor materials.
- **Client-facing responsiveness.** Fast replies can be genuinely needed.
- **Legitimate performance management.** Notes can document real issues.
- **Onboarding intensity.** Early months often involve strict calibration.

Concern strengthens when: remedies are far larger than the error; the same lapse is treated differently across colleagues; questions about a standard draw reputational comment.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Proportion Test:**
Ask: "Is the consequence sized to the error?"
Concern rises if: one formatting lapse costs a night's work and a written reflection.

**SPOT IT — The Purpose Test:**
Ask: "What is this standard for, and who benefits from it?"
Concern rises if: nobody can say.

**SPOT IT — The Equal Treatment Test:**
Ask: "Does the same lapse get the same response from someone else?"
Concern rises if: it does not.

**SPOT IT — The Question Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I ask why?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a comment about me.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask what each standard is for,** in writing and without heat.
- **Keep a dated, factual log** of notes received and what was said.
- **Compare treatment calmly** where you lawfully can.
- **Ask for substantive feedback** alongside formatting feedback.
- **Seek an outside benchmark** from a mentor or peer in another firm for what is reasonable.
- **Request your file** through HR and read what is in it, within your rights.
- **Speak to HR or a mentor early,** before a pattern has formed in your file.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Remedies are larger than the errors; you are given written notes for minor lapses; standards are applied unequally.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The notes are used to justify a performance plan or dismissal, or you are penalised for raising a concern. Relevant routes may include HR or employee relations, an employment attorney, a union or employee representative where one exists, and an employment or labour regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, or a mention of self-harm by you or anyone involved. Contact local emergency or crisis services, a trusted adult or counsellor, and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you help me understand what this standard is for?"
- "Could I get feedback on the substance of the deck as well as the formatting?"
- "I want to meet the standards. Could we agree what a reasonable remedy is for a small error?"
- "I'd like to see what's in my file, please."
- "Is the ten-minute reply time for client-facing work only?"
- "I'd like to talk to HR about how the standards are applied."
