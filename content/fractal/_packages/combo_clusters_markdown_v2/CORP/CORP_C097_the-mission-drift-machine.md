# C097: The Mission Drift Machine — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the numbers are the work, and a weary habit of trading your own judgment for checklists while the purpose that drew you in quietly fades from view.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of judging your day by boxes ticked rather than by whether anyone was actually helped.
- A pull to join one more working group about the problem, hoping it will bring the purpose back.
- An automatic sentence, "it's not what I signed up for, but it's what counts now," said with a shrug.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, a decade into a calling.

**Where it happens.** A fast-growing fintech's customer service floor.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a head of customer experience at a fintech whose founding story is on the lobby wall. "We exist to make money feel less frightening." It was written by the founders eight years ago, after one of them watched a parent get lost in a wall of fees. You joined because of that sentence. You were thirty-one, you had worked in a bank call center, and you knew exactly what a frightened customer sounded like at 4 p.m. on the last day of the month.

**Year one, the all-hands town hall**

Every town hall begins with the CEO reading it aloud, and in the first year it is how you make decisions. The CEO's voice catches on the last word, the way it has every year, and nobody minds. Today you stand at the front with the clicker and tell two hundred people what your team did this month: when a customer calls who is about to miss a payment, your agents can extend a deadline, waive a fee, stay on the line. You have a phrase for it, "calm first, process second," and from the third row a young agent says it back to you before you finish. Others laugh and say it with her. You feel what you came here for.

**Year two, the call floor**

Then the company grows, and the board wants what boards want: something it can see. A consultant is brought in to help with "operational excellence." You stand at the back of the floor with a paper cup of coffee while she, in a very good jacket, explains each number twice. The first thing installed is a dashboard with two numbers at the top: handle time, in minutes, and a one-question satisfaction score. They are reasonable numbers. You helped choose them, and you are pleased to have something to show. The dashboard goes up on the big wall screen at the end of the floor, and the agents look up at it between calls.

**Year three, the call floor**

Over the next year, a compliance layer grows around the dashboard. Every call requires a structured disposition code, a scripted verification, and a note. Handle-time targets are reviewed weekly. A senior agent who once spent forty minutes helping an elderly customer is coached, gently, on "efficiency." You start to see your agents glance at the timer in the corner of the screen. You glance at it too. On a Thursday you watch a young agent decline to stay on the line with a caller stuck on a form, because her timer has turned amber. She says sorry in the voice she uses when she does not mean it to be heard.

The knowledge-base article for hardship cases is replaced by a decision tree with four branches, and the fifth, the one your agents used when the tree did not fit, is removed to reduce inconsistency. Within a month you hear it for the first time: "The system won't let me." Nobody is choosing anything anymore.

**Year four, the quarterly business review**

Today is the quarterly business review. In the boardroom the CEO opens, as always, by reading the mission aloud. Heads nod. Then the dashboard comes up. Handle time is down by more than a tenth. Satisfaction is up by two points. The room applauds. A slide beneath it says: Our mission, measured. Your hands go cold on the table.

You raise your hand and ask about the escalation queue, which has doubled since the spring, and about repeat contacts, which the dashboard does not show. The CFO says, with respect, that repeat contacts are not a board-level metric. The COO says it is a great point and suggests the working group take it up. It is the working group you were asked to join once before, when you objected to the fifth branch and were thanked for your candor. It meets monthly. Your question is now on its agenda, with the others.

When the room empties, an agent from your team, there to present the call stats, waits by the door. She says a customer called last week, in tears, about a bounced payment. She had four minutes to resolve it or lose her bonus tier. She resolved it in three and a half. The customer said thank you. The satisfaction survey was never sent because the call code was "resolved."

"Is this what we said we'd do?" she asks.

You do not answer her question that day, or the next.

**Two years later, the call floor**

Two years later it is a Tuesday, and you walk the floor with the week's printout in your hand. Green, green, amber. Your team no longer quotes "calm first, process second"; they quote their handle times. A new agent, three weeks in, has kept a customer on the line for twenty-two minutes. The customer was crying, and the agent did not hurry her. Your eyes go straight to the number beside her name. You hear yourself say, "Over target," and then, gently, as if it were a kindness, you coach her on keeping calls short. She apologizes twice, and you tell her it is fine, and you mean the opposite of fine.

In your inbox is an invitation to the Customer Care Standards Group, a new working group on "restoring the human touch." You accept before you have read the agenda. This one, you tell yourself, will be the one that matters. You stopped taking the hardest calls yourself, the ones you used to ask for. On Sunday evenings you open the dashboard before you open anything else.

You look at the lobby wall as you leave. The sentence is still there, in a lovely typeface. You realize you could recite the dashboard from memory, and you hesitate over the exact words of the mission.

## The Name

That's **The Mission Drift Machine**.

## Definition

The Mission Drift Machine is the sequence where an institution's stated purpose is gradually replaced by its operational metrics, using Mission Declaration, Metric Installation, Compliance Infrastructure, Autonomy Displacement, Metric-Mission Substitution, and Dissent Absorption to convert trust that a stated mission still governs daily decisions into the loss of the connection between institutional purpose and institutional action. It may benefit leaders who need visible performance for boards and investors, and it can operate with no one deciding to abandon the mission. It exploits a good faith assumption: that numbers are a faithful proxy for purpose. It is hard to detect because every step is defensible, and because the mission is still recited in public. What may be lost is the judgment that once served customers, the staff who held to the mission, and the institution's ability to say what it is for. Intent would require additional evidence; metrics often substitute for mission without any plan.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Mission Declaration** — The founding sentence is everywhere: "We exist to make money feel less frightening" hangs on the lobby wall and the CEO reads it aloud to open every town hall. This functions by establishing a shared moral reference that people trust and later hold the institution to. Concern rises when the mission is recited more often than it is used to settle a decision.

2. **Metric Installation** — A dashboard chosen as stand-ins for care: average ticket handle time and a one-question satisfaction score sit at the top, and you helped choose them. This functions by making something measurable stand in for something that is not. Concern rises when the proxy is introduced without a plan to check whether it still tracks the mission.

3. **Compliance Infrastructure** — Constant checking: a disposition code, a scripted verification and a note on every call, handle-time targets reviewed weekly, and a senior agent coached on "efficiency" after forty minutes with an elderly customer. This functions by filling the working day with required steps so there is no time for the unrecorded work the mission depended on. Concern rises when time per task shrinks while required documentation grows.

4. **Autonomy Displacement** — A tree in place of judgment: the hardship article becomes a four-branch decision tree, the fifth branch is removed "to reduce inconsistency," and agents begin to say "The system won't let me." This functions by moving discretion from the person who sees the customer to a standardized process. Concern rises when staff are coached against discretion they used to be praised for.

5. **Metric-Mission Substitution** — The numbers become the proof: handle time down eleven percent, satisfaction up two points, applause, and a slide beneath them that offers the figures as the purpose itself, measured. This functions by redefining the goal as the score. Concern rises when improving the score and serving the customer diverge and the score is chosen.

6. **Dissent Absorption** — Critics invited inside: the COO calls your question about escalations and repeat contacts "a great point" and sends it to the working group you joined after being thanked for your candour. This functions by routing objections into committees where they are acknowledged and not acted upon. Concern rises when concerns are welcomed warmly and nothing changes.

## Why This Order

The **mission** has to be declared first, and sincerely, because it is the source of trust. People join because of it, customers choose the company because of it, and later its authority will be used to cover the very changes that hollow it out. If the declaration were insincere, the pattern would be easier to see. It has to come before the dashboard, because the numbers are only welcomed as proof of the sentence on the lobby wall while everyone still believes in it.

> A neighborhood bakery has "Baked with love" painted over the door. You trust it before you have tasted a thing. The sign is why you walk in. Flip it: the bakery owner shows you a chart of loaves sold per hour, and only then paints "Baked with love" over the door; you read the chart first and ask what it leaves out.

**Metric Installation** is next because a board can only govern what it can see. The proxy is chosen for good reasons, and that is its power: handle time does track something real, and a satisfaction score is better than nothing. The important thing is that these numbers do not measure the mission, and once they are on a dashboard they begin to be managed. It has to come before the disposition codes and timers, because a layer of rules only gets built around each call once handle time and satisfaction sit on a screen the board watches.

> A teacher is told to track "pages read" because pages can be counted. Reading for joy is hard to count, so the pages become the thing that matters. Flip it: the teacher must file a short reason each time she changes a lesson, and she notices how every pause starts to feel watched; she asks who reads the forms.

**Compliance Infrastructure** then consumes the available time. It does not forbid care; it simply makes care slow, and slow now looks like failure. This is also what makes the sequence hard to resist: each requirement is individually sensible. It has to come before the decision tree, because agents only accept losing a branch after the timer and the codes have already made every call feel watched.

> Your phone asks "Are you sure?" before every action and wants a reason. You can still do everything. It all just takes longer, and every pause feels watched. Flip it: your phone simply makes the choice for you, with no question asked, though you know better, and you stop and ask it to show you where its advice came from.

**Autonomy Displacement** is the step that actually moves the mission out of the room. Once judgment is replaced by a tree, the mission lives in the document, not the person. It has to come before the slide that says the mission is measured, because the numbers can only stand in for the mission once nobody on the floor is still choosing what a good call is.

> A satnav says, "Turn left." The road is flooded. You turn left anyway, because the machine knows the route and you do not. Flip it: the satnav announces a high route score as proof it is right, and only then says "Turn left" into the flooded road; you look out of the window and ask what the road is like.

**Metric-Mission Substitution** follows naturally: with no one entitled to judge, the number becomes the judge. At that point plausible deniability is complete, since the company can honestly say it is following its own measures. It has to come before your question joining the working group's agenda, because the room only invites a critic in once the slide has given it a number it can point to as the answer.

> A school announces proudly, "Our results speak for themselves," and begins to teach only what the test asks. Flip it: a parent complains that the school teaches only what the test asks and is invited onto its committee, thanked every month, and she asks what has changed before she says yes.

**Dissent Absorption** closes the loop and supplies the exit cost. Anyone who objects is invited in, thanked, and given a monthly meeting. The key conversion point is the moment the dashboard is presented as "our mission, measured," because it turns a proxy into a definition. It has to come last, because inviting a critic in only holds once the dashboard has been declared the mission, so that every complaint is sent to a group that cannot change it.

> You complain about the bins and are invited onto the residents' committee. You are thanked every month, and the bins stay the same. Flip it: you complain about the bins and are invited onto the residents' committee, and only then does the committee announce proudly that its attendance figures speak for themselves; you ask where the bins appear in them.

What would weaken the sequence: a mission-linked metric that is not easy to game, such as repeat contacts or hardship outcomes; discretion that is protected, with audit; and a standing review of whether each metric still serves its original purpose.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A growing fintech is built on a few plain facts. A board governs what it can see, and what it can see is what a screen can count. A founding sentence is easy to repeat and hard to audit. A call center runs on shift lengths and queues, so every minute has a price. And a regulated company must show its work, which means forms, codes and trails. Those facts alone produce the sequence you lived through.

Mission Declaration comes first because the sentence on the wall is what recruits people, what customers trust, and what later changes will lean on. It has to be sincere, because an insincere one would be easy to see through. Its authority is spent afterward on the very changes that hollow it out.

Metric Installation comes second because a board needs something to read. Handle time tracks something real, and a satisfaction score is better than nothing, so both are chosen for good reasons. Once on a dashboard, they begin to be managed.

Compliance Infrastructure comes third because a company that must show its work builds a layer of codes and scripts around every call. None of it forbids care. It makes care slow, and slow starts to look like failure.

Autonomy Displacement comes fourth because a decision tree is cheaper to audit than a person's judgment. Removing the branch that did not fit moves the mission out of the room and into a document.

Metric-Mission Substitution comes fifth because once nobody on the floor is entitled to judge, the number is the only judge left. A slide that says the mission is measured simply states what has already happened.

Dissent Absorption comes last because a company that wants to hear critics has a place for them: a working group, a monthly meeting. Objections go there to be thanked, and they stay there.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a sentence to trust, a number to watch, a process to follow, a tree to obey, a score to defend, and a committee to hold the doubt. What it turns you into is a person who keeps the purpose in a frame on the wall and the work on a dashboard.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A board that wanted something visible, an audit trail and a busy call floor did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a manager. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Nadia Petrovic ran support at a small lending company when a new chief operating officer, Calvin Hartigan, arrived. At his first town hall he read the company's founding sentence aloud, then turned and said Nadia was the reason he had taken the job. Within a month a scorecard went up on the floor screen with two numbers on it: minutes per call and a one-question rating. Soon every call needed a code, a script and a note, and her agents began watching a small clock in the corner of their screens. In the spring the hardship guide was replaced by a flowchart, and the last option, the one agents used when nothing else fit, was gone. At the quarterly review Calvin read the founding sentence once more, put up a slide titled "Our mission, measured," and the room applauded. Nadia asked about repeat callers. Calvin said it was an excellent point and added it to a monthly working group, chaired by himself, which thanked her at its first three meetings and did not change anything.

Calvin was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that the people who care most are the easiest to keep busy.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to speak in the language of the dashboard while privately feeling that something has been lost. Over time, they may stop recommending exceptions, coach their own team toward the numbers, and then notice that they are teaching others to do what they once resisted.

They may feel quiet moral fatigue: the sense of taking part in something they cannot name. They may avoid going to the working group because it feels pointless, and then feel guilty for avoiding it. They may leave, or stay and soften, and in either case lose the confidence that the mission is theirs to keep.

## Institutional Echo

Companies that scale a mission-driven product often introduce dashboards, targets, and objectives-and-key-results systems to give boards visibility. Where metrics drive bonus tiers or team reviews, they tend to shape behavior, sometimes in ways nobody intended. Mission and values language is also used in corporate responsibility and ESG reporting, where the gap between statement and practice can be wide.

Working groups and standards committees can be genuine channels for change, or they can function as places where concerns are held. The difference turns on whether they have authority, a timeline, and a report to someone who can decide.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that what gets measured gets managed, and that anything worth doing can be shown in a number. Beside it sits a second belief: that professionalism means standardization, and that discretion is a risk. The language is "operational excellence," "consistency," "data-driven."

The value being exploited is real. Boards do need visibility; customers do deserve consistency; measurement does expose real problems. The public story is a company growing responsibly and still living its mission. The private cost is that the people closest to the customer can no longer act on what they see.

## Observable Indicators

1. A founding mission is quoted at meetings but rarely cited in decisions.
2. Dashboards headline speed or score measures, while outcome measures are absent.
3. Requested metrics, such as repeat contacts or escalations, are called out of scope.
4. Required documentation per task grows while time per task shrinks.
5. Staff are coached away from discretion they were once praised for.
6. Decision paths are reduced and exceptions removed "for consistency."
7. Bonuses or reviews are tied to the headline measures.
8. Dissenting staff are invited to working groups with no decision rights.
9. Improvement in the dashboard coincides with rising complaints elsewhere.
10. Staff describe a gap between what is said and what is rewarded.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The mission statement and values documents in force when you joined, and later versions.
- Each version of the dashboard, KPI definitions, and the date each metric was introduced.
- Handle-time targets, coaching notes, and bonus-tier criteria that apply to your team.
- Data on repeat contacts, escalations, and complaints that sit outside the headline view.
- Records of your objections to metric choices and the responses.
- The working group charter, minutes, and any decisions it made.
- Examples, anonymized, of a case where the metric and the mission diverged.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Metrics are a normal and sometimes essential part of running a service. Handle time may reflect real queues; compliance steps may reflect genuine regulatory obligations.

Benign lookalikes specific to this pattern:

- **Growth discipline.** A larger customer base may truly need consistent processes.
- **Required regulatory steps.** Verification and disposition codes may be mandatory.
- **Interim measures.** A dashboard may be a first step with outcome metrics planned.
- **Working groups that work.** A group with real authority and timelines is genuine consultation.

Concern strengthens when: outcome metrics are repeatedly declined; staff are penalized for discretion that serves the stated mission; and working group recommendations are deferred without decision.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Decision Test:**
Ask: "When did the mission last decide something?"
Concern rises if: nobody can name a recent decision it settled.

**SPOT IT — The Proxy Test:**
Ask: "Which result would the metric hide if it got worse?"
Concern rises if: the answer is the mission's own outcome.

**SPOT IT — The Time Test:**
Ask: "Do staff have time to do the unrecorded work the mission requires?"
Concern rises if: required steps leave none.

**SPOT IT — The Committee Test:**
Ask: "What has the working group changed?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a list of discussions.

## Chain Breakers

- **Propose a paired metric.** Ask for one outcome measure beside each speed measure: "Can we add repeat contacts next to handle time?"
- **Write a short case.** One anonymized example showing the gap between score and purpose, with dates.
- **Ask what each metric is for.** "Which part of the mission does this number stand for, and how will we know it still does?"
- **Protect documented discretion.** Propose a defined exception path with audit, so judgment is visible, not hidden.
- **Make the working group decisional.** Ask for a charter that names a sponsor, a deadline, and a report.
- **Raise it with someone who can decide.** A board member, an independent director, or the audit committee may want to hear it, in writing.
- **Do not game the number to prove the point.** Keep your own conduct clean and accurate.
- **Look after the team.** Acknowledge the strain staff feel and do not ask them to take risks on their own.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Outcome metrics are repeatedly declined; coaching penalizes discretion tied to the mission; or complaints rise as the dashboard improves.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Customers in vulnerable circumstances are being harmed or misled by the standardized path; staff report being pressured to record outcomes inaccurately; or raising concerns draws consequences. Routes include a compliance officer, an independent director or audit committee, an employment adviser, and, where customer treatment or reporting is in question, a financial services or consumer-protection regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** A customer or colleague is in immediate danger or expresses intent to self-harm; threats or retaliation follow a report. Prioritize safety, contact local emergency services or crisis lines, and consult qualified counsel. Do not confront anyone. Do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Can we add repeat contacts alongside handle time, so we see whether the call actually resolved the problem?"
- "What part of the mission does this number represent, and how will we check it still does?"
- "I'd like to propose a documented exception path for hardship cases, with review."
- "Could the working group have a sponsor, a deadline, and a report to the executive team?"
- "I want to flag that the survey isn't reaching customers whose calls are coded as resolved."
- "Could we review whether the bonus tiers reward what we want to be known for?"
- "I'd like to present a short case to the leadership team next quarter."
