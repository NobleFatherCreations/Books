# C065: The "Both Sides" Equalizer — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that when you are hurt you must share the blame equally, and a habit of agreeing to "mutual" fixes that leave unclear who did what to whom.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of starting any account of harm with "of course, I wasn't perfect either," before saying what happened.
- A pull to accept a joint meeting with the person who hurt you, because it is offered as fair to both.
- An automatic thought, "maybe we were both to blame," that blurs a memory you once held clearly.

**A point in a life.** Midcareer, after filing a complaint.

**Where it happens.** The product team of a services company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a product lead, thirty-five, at a software-enabled services company, and for four months you have been quietly documenting a pattern. A peer product lead, whose roadmap overlaps yours, rewrites your slides after you present them. He restates your ideas in leadership meetings as his own and corrects your numbers in front of others, including, twice, with figures that turned out to be wrong. In March he forwarded a screenshot of your draft to a group of senior people with the line, "Is this what we're calling a strategy?"

You don't go to HR lightly. You keep a dated list, in plain sentences: date, meeting, what was said, who was present. You raise it with him once, privately, and he says you're being sensitive.

**April, your desk**

You submit a written complaint through the HR portal, with six dated examples and the screenshot. The confirmation page shows a green tick. Two days later you get an invitation to a "facilitated conversation." The subject line reads, "Supporting two colleagues." You notice that it says two. You tell yourself it is only politeness, and your jaw tightens, and you let it go. Asking would sound petty.

**April, a mediation room**

The room has a round table, a box of tissues and a printed agenda, the same for each of you. The HR business partner is warm and fair-sounding. She explains that the process will be "balanced," that you have each "shared your perspectives," and that she wants to "move us from positions to partnership." A page is slid across the table: he has also filed a complaint. It says you have created "a climate of mistrust" by documenting his behavior and by "escalating without first attempting resolution." You did attempt it, in March. That is not on the page. You ask whether the facts in your complaint will be looked at. She says that is not the purpose of this stage. You are each given the same questions and twenty minutes, and each asked what you would like to see "going forward." You answer clearly. He answers charmingly. She thanks you both for your courage.

**Late April, your desk**

A week later a document arrives, titled Working Agreement. It has six numbered commitments, each beginning "Each of us agrees": to communicate respectfully, to raise concerns directly before escalating, to avoid "characterizing the intent of colleagues," to refrain from "discussing the matter with others," and to treat the process as closed once signed. There is a line for each signature, and a note that the agreement "is not an admission by either party." A friend who is a lawyer might call it neutral. You read it four times. The sentence about not characterizing intent forbids the account you have been keeping. "Once you've each signed," the HR partner says brightly in her email, "we can all move on."

You ask what will happen to the complaints. The two case files, the reply says, have been "aligned." Your file now contains his complaint. His now contains yours. They are the same length, and each carries the same summary: "Interpersonal conflict between two senior colleagues."

You ask what was found and whether anything was done. The answer comes back that, in the interest of fairness, outcomes cannot be shared with either person. No one has said what happened to the slides, the screenshot, or the figures that were wrong.

On Thursday evening the portal changes your case status to Closed, with the resolution "resolved by agreement." You have not been asked whether it was resolved, and you have not signed. Friday morning, the signed copy is due. Your manager stops at your desk and says it is a good result for everyone. Another colleague says he heard that things between you two have "settled down." You nod. You were never in a position to say otherwise.

**Two years later**

It is a Sunday in November, and you are telling your sister about the contractor who left your kitchen half finished and kept the deposit. You start with the three emails you forgot to send. You tell her about the day you were short with him on the phone. You do not get to the deposit for ten minutes. She says, "It sounds like he did the main thing wrong," and you say, "Maybe, but it's complicated."

His office has offered a call "with the two of you, to clear the air," and you have said yes. In the shower you have been rehearsing what you will apologize for. The kitchen has gone soft at the edges in your memory. You can no longer say for certain which of the cabinets were his fault.

The dated list is still on your drive, last changed in March. You have not opened it since.

## The Name

That's **The "Both Sides" Equalizer**.

## Definition

The "Both Sides" Equalizer is the sequence where one-directional harm is recast as a bilateral conflict, using Conflict Reframing, Procedural Symmetry, Mutual Agreement Fabrication, File Equalization, Privacy Weaponization and Closure Manufacture to convert trust that a resolution process weighs facts, and not only process, into the loss of recognition of who was actually harmed. In corporate settings it appears in HR mediation, employee relations and partner disputes, where an even-handed form can feel like fairness. It is hard to detect because every step can be defended as neutrality, and because the person harmed is asked to sign. Intent would require additional evidence; the pattern is identified by outcome and sequence, not motive.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Conflict Reframing** — A subject line with two names: your complaint about one person's conduct becomes a "facilitated conversation" headed "Supporting both colleagues." This functions by changing the question from what happened to how the two of you get along. Concern rises when the reframing precedes any look at the facts alleged.

2. **Procedural Symmetry** — Everything twice: each of you gets the same questions, twenty minutes and the same agenda, and you learn he has filed his own complaint calling your documenting a "climate of mistrust." This functions by giving the appearance of fairness while ignoring that the starting positions are not alike. Concern rises when equal treatment is applied before it is established whether the complaints are equal.

3. **Mutual Agreement Fabrication** — A paper with no direction: the Mutual Conduct Agreement lists six commitments each prefaced "both parties agree," including not characterizing intent and not discussing the matter, with a note that it is not an admission by either party. This functions by turning one person's grievance into a shared fault and making their signature the proof. Concern rises when the agreement restricts describing events or intent and contains no finding.

4. **File Equalization** — Matching folders: your file now holds his complaint, his holds yours, they are the same length, and each carries the summary "Interpersonal conflict between two senior colleagues." This functions by making the records indistinguishable, so a later reader sees symmetry. Concern rises when a counter-complaint appears only after the first and the summaries are identical.

5. **Privacy Weaponization** — A closed door at the end: the reply says outcomes cannot be shared with either party because that would compromise confidentiality for the other, and no one says what happened to the slides, the screenshot or the wrong figures. This functions by making consequences unverifiable, so no one can say whether anything happened. Concern rises when privacy is invoked to refuse even a confirmation that the facts were examined.

6. **Closure Manufacture** — A status you did not choose: the portal marks the case closed, "resolved by mutual agreement," before you have signed, and your manager calls it a good result for everyone. This functions by ending the matter in the record without a finding, a remedy or a named owner. Concern rises when closure is declared before the harmed party has been asked whether the issue is resolved.

## Why This Order

**Reframing** comes first because everything else depends on the category. Once the complaint is a "conflict," symmetry is the natural remedy, and symmetry would look wrong if the matter were still called misconduct. The word both is doing the work before any procedure exists. It has to come before the same questions and twenty minutes, because equal treatment only reads as fair once your complaint has been renamed a conflict between two senior colleagues.

> A neighbor dumps rubbish on your drive. When you speak up, it becomes "a dispute between neighbors," and nobody is asking who dumped what. Flip it: if you and the neighbor were first given the same short slot to talk, you would use your turn to say plainly who dumped the rubbish.

**Procedural symmetry** then makes the reframing feel earned. Equal time, equal questions, equal tone are experienced as fairness, and the person harmed is poorly placed to object, since objecting sounds like asking for unequal treatment. It also prevents anyone from asking whose facts are better supported, since the process has no stage for that question. It has to come before the Working Agreement, because a page of matching commitments feels reasonable only after you have each answered the same questions and thanked the partner.

> A teacher gives two children five minutes each to explain a broken toy, whoever broke it. Equal time is ordinary, but now you can't ask for more without sounding unfair. Flip it: if the teacher had both children sign "We'll both take more care" before anyone explained, you would ask first who actually broke the toy.

**Mutual Agreement Fabrication** is the third step. The agreement is where the sequence moves from process to record. It requires a signature, so the person harmed becomes a co-author of the conclusion. Its commitments (do not characterize intent, do not discuss) quietly remove the vocabulary for describing what happened. It has to come before the matched case files, because your signature on a neutral-looking agreement is what lets the two files read as one story.

> You are handed a note that says "we both agree to be kinder." You sign because it sounds reasonable. It says nothing about who was unkind. Flip it: if two matching complaint files already sat side by side before any note was handed over, you would ask to add a page saying who was unkind.

**File equalization** then ensures any future reader sees two matching files and draws the reasonable inference that the parties were equally responsible. It has to come before the refusal to share outcomes, because identical summaries leave only one thing to ask about, what was decided, and that is what the answer then withholds.

> Two folders sit on a shelf with the same label, each holding a complaint about the other. Anyone who glances at them sees a tie. Flip it: if you were told "The outcome stays private" before anyone had looked in either folder, you would ask what you, at least, are allowed to know.

**Privacy weaponization** is what makes the result durable, because the one thing that would show the sequence is a visible consequence, and it is withheld "for the other party's confidentiality." It has to come before the portal showing Closed, because a case can be marked resolved without your say only once you have been told that you may not see what was decided.

> "We can't tell you what was decided, because that wouldn't be fair to the other person." There is nothing left for you to check. Flip it: if your case was marked finished before anything had been decided, you would ask who finished it and what was actually decided.

**Closure manufacture** supplies the plausible deniability: a case marked resolved, with a signed document. It has to come last, because the Closed status only reads as a fair ending after the renaming, the matched questions, the agreement, the matched files and the withheld outcome.

> A notice pops up: "Resolved." Nobody asked you. Flip it: if the notice said "Resolved" and then nobody would explain what had been settled, you would reply that you were never asked and request the outcome.

The conversion point is the signature. What weakens the sequence is a pre-signing request that the complaint's facts be addressed, a request for a finding or a written statement of what was and was not examined, and independent advice on the agreement before signing.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A workplace process has to be fair to every named person, so it treats a complaint and a counter-complaint alike. A conflict category triggers a mediation track, and legal wants documents that admit nothing and take no side. Privacy rules limit what either person can be told about the other's outcome, and the portal needs a status that ends in closed. A rule of even treatment, neutral wording and a case counter produce the order below.

Conflict Reframing comes first because every later step depends on the category. Once a complaint is a conflict between colleagues, symmetry is the natural remedy, and it would look wrong if the matter were still called misconduct.

Procedural Symmetry comes second because equal time and the same questions feel like fairness. The person harmed is badly placed to object, since objecting sounds like asking for unequal treatment, and no stage exists for asking whose facts are better supported.

Mutual Agreement Fabrication comes third because the process needs a record, and a signed page is the neatest one. It makes the person harmed a co-author of the conclusion, and its commitments quietly remove the words for describing what happened.

File Equalization comes fourth because a future reader sees two matching files and draws the reasonable inference that each party was equally responsible.

Privacy Weaponization comes fifth because the one thing that would show the sequence is a visible outcome, and outcomes are withheld for the other person's confidentiality.

Closure Manufacture comes last because the portal needs a finished case. A status of resolved, with a signed document behind it, is a complete record.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a new category, even treatment, a signed page, matching files, a withheld outcome and a closed case. It turns a person who was harmed into a party to a conflict.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A process built to treat every complaint evenly, a privacy rule and a status field on a portal, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a junior designer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Corinne Abasi, a designer of two years, filed a complaint that a senior colleague had been presenting her mock-ups as his own. Two days later the HR partner, Yusuf Vandermeer, invited her to a "conversation to support two colleagues." In the room the senior colleague had filed his own page about her. Yusuf gave each of them the same questions and twenty minutes, and said the facts of her complaint were not the purpose of this stage. A week later she was handed a working agreement in which each of them agreed to avoid describing the other's intent and to treat the matter as closed once signed. Her file and his now held each other's complaint, with the same summary: "workplace friction." When she asked what had been found, Yusuf said that outcomes could not be shared, out of fairness to the other person. On Thursday the portal showed her case as resolved. Nobody had asked her.

Yusuf was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that when everything was symmetrical, nobody could say you had picked a side.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt whether they were, in some measure, partly responsible. Over time, they may rehearse the mediation, wondering if a better answer would have changed it. They may become careful in writing and silent in meetings, because the agreement has made their own account a risk. They may feel gratitude for being treated "fairly," then a slow anger they cannot place. They may find that the colleague's conduct continues, and that anything they say now sounds like a breach.

## Institutional Echo

Mediation-first policies and "restorative" frameworks can serve genuine purposes, and can also operate as a way to avoid findings. Template mutual-conduct agreements, standard closure codes such as "resolved by agreement," and policies that treat all complaint parties identically can make symmetry the default outcome without anyone aiming for it. Privacy rules that bar sharing outcomes with a complainant exist in many organizations, often for sound reasons. What protections or rights a complainant has to information about findings varies by jurisdiction and by policy.

The honest reading is that many real disputes are in fact mutual, and that HR teams are often under-resourced and trying to avoid escalation. Intent would require additional evidence: for example, whether comparable complaints in similar circumstances were routinely resolved by agreement without findings, and whether a counter-complaint reliably followed an initial one.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that fairness means treating everyone identically, and that the neutral party is the one who takes no side. It sits beside the idea that it takes two to conflict, and that anyone who insists on a distinction is being rigid, or "not a team player."

The value being exploited is real and good: the wish for peace, for reconciliation, for a workplace where people can move on. The public story is two professionals who shook hands. The private record is a person whose account was never weighed, and a colleague whose conduct was never examined.

## Observable Indicators

1. A conduct complaint is answered with an invitation to a "joint" or "supporting both" conversation.
2. A counter-complaint appears shortly after the first.
3. The process offers identical time, questions and agenda to both parties, with no stage for testing facts.
4. The agreement uses "both parties agree" for commitments that address only one party's alleged conduct.
5. The agreement restricts describing events, characterizing intent or discussing the matter.
6. Each person's file contains the other's complaint and a matching summary.
7. Outcomes, findings or remedies are withheld on privacy grounds.
8. The closure note says "resolved by mutual agreement" with no finding.
9. Specific evidence submitted, such as a screenshot or figures, is not mentioned.
10. The harmed party's later account is treated as a breach of the agreement.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Your original complaint, as submitted, with its date, exhibits and the portal confirmation.
- Every invitation and agenda for the "joint" conversation, with the subject lines.
- The counter-complaint, if you are lawfully given it, and the date it was filed.
- All drafts of the mutual agreement and the version you were asked to sign, with notes on what changed.
- Your own same-day notes of the mediation: who spoke, what was asked, whether facts were examined.
- Case-file summaries or closure codes, where lawfully visible.
- Your written requests for a finding or a statement of what was examined, and the replies.
- Later incidents of the same conduct, dated, set against the agreement's signing date.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Many workplace disputes are genuinely mutual, and good mediation helps colleagues repair working relationships. Equal process is not suspect in itself, and a counter-complaint may be honest.

Specific lookalikes:

- **A real two-way conflict** where both people behaved poorly and each account was heard.
- **Mediation chosen by the complainant** as the preferred way to resolve, with a finding available if requested.
- **Privacy limits** applied consistently, with the HR team explaining what can be said.
- **A counter-complaint with its own specifics**, examined on its merits.

Concern strengthens when: a counter-complaint arrives only after yours and mirrors it; the agreement bars describing events but contains no finding; confidentiality is cited to refuse even confirmation that your facts were examined.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Direction Test:**
Ask: "Does the agreement say who did what, or only that both parties agree?"
Concern rises if: the document describes no conduct and assigns no direction.

**SPOT IT — The Facts Test:**
Ask: "Were the examples in my complaint examined at any stage?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that the process was not designed to look at them.

**SPOT IT — The Mirror Test:**
Ask: "Did a complaint about me appear after I filed mine, and does it match mine in form?"
Concern rises if: yes, and the two files now read the same.

**SPOT IT — The Outcome Test:**
Ask: "Can anyone tell me, even in general terms, what was done?"
Concern rises if: nothing can be shared and the case is marked resolved.

## Chain Breakers

- **Read the actual policy before the first meeting.** Ask which stages exist, whether a finding is possible, and how to choose an investigation over mediation.
- **State your preference in writing.** "I'd like my complaint investigated, with a written finding. I'm willing to join a conversation after that."
- **Ask what the process is for, in advance.** If it will not look at facts, you can decide whether to take part.
- **Do not sign a mutual agreement on the day.** Take it away, read it with a second reader, and ask counsel what each clause would restrict.
- **Ask that the agreement name the conduct,** or that it state it is not a resolution of your complaint.
- **Send a short confirmation after each meeting** of what was asked and what was not.
- **Keep dated notes of any recurrence,** separate from anything you send.
- **Do not discuss the matter publicly or in group channels** before you have advice.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A counter-complaint mirrors yours; the process will not examine facts; an agreement arrives with no finding and restrictions on describing events.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** You are asked to sign a mutual agreement with a closure and non-discussion clause; the conduct continues after signing; outcomes are refused; or you are treated as in breach for describing what happened. Relevant routes may include an employment attorney, an independent director or compliance officer, a union or employee representative where one exists, and an employment or labor regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, stalking, intimidation, discriminatory or sexual conduct, any self-harm statement, or a risk of physical harm. Prioritize safety and emergency support. Do not confront anyone, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before this goes further, can you confirm whether my complaint will be investigated and whether there will be a written finding?"
- "I'd like to understand how the process treats the specific examples I submitted."
- "I'm happy to take part in a conversation. I'd like to do so alongside, not instead of, a review of the facts."
- "I'd like to take the agreement away and review it with my own adviser before I sign."
- "Could the agreement state what conduct it addresses? As written, it treats both of us the same."
- "Can you confirm in writing what has been decided and by whom, within what confidentiality allows?"
- "I'm not asking to see his file. I'm asking whether mine was resolved."
