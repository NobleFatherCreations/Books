# C090: Divide and Conquer — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that people who should be on your side are against you, and a habit of seeing them as all bad based on the pieces of the story you were given.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of judging a whole group by one story you heard about them, without ever meeting them.
- A pull to feel flattered when someone says your side is better than the other, and to stop asking questions.
- An automatic thought, "they'd never understand people like us," that keeps you from talking to natural allies.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, speaking for other people.

**Where it happens.** A company works council and its management.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a works council member, you are forty-four, and you were elected by your colleagues to stand between them and decisions made two floors up. You have spent seven years on the council. You know the corridors, the people in payroll and the way a union steward sounds when they are running out of patience. Six weeks ago the company announced a restructuring. You were told it was "under review." What you want this week is one straight answer you can carry back to them.

**Monday, a small boardroom**

You are meeting with management on Monday. The head of people greets you warmly in a small boardroom with good coffee and a plate of shortbread that nobody touches. She says the company values the council's role. She says employees bring their worries to her, and that she is glad to carry the council's replies back to the canteen, so nobody has to sit through a difficult exchange.

She says, with visible regret, that employees are "very anxious and, frankly, some are being quite difficult about the process." She mentions, in passing, that there is "a small group causing a lot of noise" and that she hopes the council can "help bring the temperature down." She slides a restructuring FAQ across the table. It is clean and reassuring. It says roles in your division are "expected to remain stable," that consultation will "begin shortly," and that no decisions have been taken. You fold it into your folder.

**Tuesday, the canteen**

On Tuesday you sit with employees in the canteen. They are tired. "There are the sensible ones and there are the noisy ones," a man by the window says. "You have to pick." One says management told them the council was "more interested in procedure than people." Another says they heard the council had been "told everything and isn't sharing." You have no good answer to that, and you can feel how it would sound.

Then someone passes you a different FAQ, printed from an internal portal. It is the same title, the same logo, and a different document. This one says that "reductions in your division are under consideration," that "selected roles will be consolidated," and that employees should "prepare for transition conversations." You hold the two documents side by side on the canteen table. You do not need to read them twice. They describe different companies.

**Thursday, the corridor**

On Wednesday you asked the head of people about it. She was surprised. "The portal version is a working draft for managers." She said the version she gave the council is the formal one, and it was unfortunate that something unfinished got out. She looked sincerely troubled.

That afternoon, in the corridor, she stops you, touches your sleeve and says warmly, "Between us, you are far more reasonable than the others on the council." Your shoulders drop. For a second it is nice to be the sensible one. You do not ask who the others are. When you mention how angry the canteen was, she tilts her head. "The councils at the other sites never seem to have this trouble with their people." Your face goes hot, as if the anger were yours to have prevented.

**Friday, the council room**

The council meets on Friday. Its members have all seen only one version of the FAQ, and they begin to quarrel about which one is true. The steward says the portal copy is a rumor. The payroll representative says the formal one is a lie. You put your two copies on the table, and nobody reaches for them. The hour passes in careful voices.

**Three months later, a regional gathering**

Three months later it's a Thursday in March, and you're at a regional gathering of works councils in a hotel conference room, a lanyard around your neck and a plate of small sandwiches in your hand. A delegate from another site, a friendly woman with a laptop sticker, says hello and asks how your restructuring went. You say "fine, thanks," and you look past her for someone from your own table. You have decided what her council is like. You have never met it.

Mid-morning the head of people, who is here as a guest speaker, finds you by the coffee urn and says you did very well on the panel. Warmth goes through you before you can stop it. You don't ask what she thought of the others on the panel. You nod.

At the lunch break you sit with the two people from your own site and say, "They've got it easy over there," about a council you did not speak to. Nobody asks what you mean.

You were elected to stand between two groups, holding both documents. You have not stood between anything since.

## The Name

That's **Divide and Conquer**.

## Definition

Divide and Conquer is the sequence where natural allies are kept apart by an institution that controls what each group hears about the other, so that each acts on a selectively edited picture and no one sees the whole, using Triangulation, Smear Campaigns, Information Control, Splitting, Priming, Comparative Devaluation, and Selective Information Sharing to convert trust that allies see the same full picture into the loss of a shared, complete view of the situation. It may benefit whoever would face a united response. It exploits the structure of consultation, which often involves separate meetings, separate documents and separate audiences. It is hard to detect because each group receives something plausible. In this sector it can cost employees and their representatives the chance to form a common position before decisions are made.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Triangulation** — Messages pass through a go-between: the head of people says employees bring their worries to her and she will carry the council's replies back to the canteen. This functions by placing the institution between the groups, so that each hears about the other from it. Concern rises when what each group believes about the other came from management, not from direct contact.

2. **Smear Campaigns** — Unflattering descriptions circulate: employees are "quite difficult" and "a small group causing a lot of noise" to management, and the council is "more interested in procedure than people" to employees. This functions by lowering trust between potential allies. Concern rises when the same adjectives appear in both groups' accounts of each other.

3. **Information Control** — The company decides which papers go where: the council gets a clean FAQ promising stable roles, the canteen holds a portal version saying "selected roles will be consolidated," and the difference is called a working draft. This functions by making the institution the only holder of the full picture. Concern rises when you learn of a document only because someone passed it to you.

4. **Splitting** — Two camps with opposite aims: management talks of emotional, uninformed employees, employees talk of management hiding things, and each side talks as if the other wants the reverse. This functions by encouraging binary thinking. Concern rises when you are pressed to choose a side in a conflict you did not know existed.

5. **Priming** — Each side arrives already holding the other at a distance: someone always mentions what management has said about them, and the same happens in reverse, before anyone has met. This functions by shaping meetings in advance. Concern rises when people arrive at a shared meeting already wary and already certain.

6. **Comparative Devaluation** — Comparison in the corridor: "you are far more reasonable than the others on the council," then, when you mention the angry canteen, "The councils at the other sites never seem to have this trouble with their people." This functions by flattering each group at the other's expense. Concern rises when praise for your group always implies criticism of the other.

7. **Selective Information Sharing** — Different documents for different rooms: council members have each seen only one version of the FAQ and quarrel over which is true, while you alone have held both. This functions by allowing each version to be accurate within its limited frame. Concern rises when each document is defensible and the difference only becomes visible when they are laid side by side.

## Why This Order

**Triangulation** comes first because the structure of the communication has to be set before any content moves. If the groups spoke directly, the rest of the sequence would be easy to expose. It also looks like an ordinary arrangement: consultation really does involve separate meetings. It has to come before the remark about a noisy group, because she can only describe each side to the other once replies are going through her instead of across a table.

> Two friends who have fallen out stop speaking, and a third friend carries messages between them. It feels helpful, and nobody gets to answer directly. Flip it: if the third friend told each of the two that the other was being nasty before any falling out, you would ring the other friend yourself and ask.

**Smear Campaigns** follow because the channel needs content. Negative characterizations are small and easy to place, and they build a foundation of mistrust. It has to come before the FAQ she slides across, because a reassuring document is only believed once you have been told the employees are anxious and difficult.

> At school you are told the other class thinks you are stuck-up, and the other class is told you think they are loud. Flip it: if the other class's own message to you was held back with just one line shown, before any remark about you, you would ask to read all of it.

**Information Control** then ensures the groups cannot check what they are told, since the institution holds the documents. It has to come before the canteen dividing into sensible and noisy, because the camps only form once each side holds just the page it was handed and cannot check the other.

> A landlord keeps the lease in his own drawer and shows you only the page he wants. Flip it: if the landlord had already sorted the tenants into "good ones" and "troublemakers" before hiding any page, you would ask to see the whole lease anyway.

**Splitting** turns the mistrust into a structure: two camps. It has to come before the stories each side has heard about the council, because those stories only land once the canteen is already divided into sensible and noisy.

> In a family argument it suddenly becomes the sensible ones against the dramatic ones. Nobody is allowed to be in between. Flip it: when a cousin had been warned about you as "the dramatic one" before any argument even started, you would ask who said it and why.

**Priming** makes the structure automatic, so that the next meeting begins with each side already holding the other at a distance. It has to come before her calling you more reasonable than the others, because the compliment only feels like a prize once the canteen has already cast the council as procedural and secretive.

> Before two new neighbors have even met, each has been told the other is "a bit of a nightmare." Flip it: if you were told "you're far nicer than the last people on this street" before any warning came, you would smile and knock on the new neighbor's door yourself.

**Comparative Devaluation** adds a reward: flattered groups are less likely to question the arrangement. This is also where the pattern most resembles real relationship management, since leaders do speak differently to different audiences. It has to come before the Friday quarrel, because you only quarrel about which FAQ is true once each member holds just one version and has heard that other sites cope better.

> A teacher says quietly, "You're much more sensible than the rest of the class." You feel proud, and a little less likely to question the rules. Flip it: if the teacher handed you only half of the class rules sheet before any praise, you would ask the teacher for the other half.

**Selective Information Sharing** closes the loop and supplies the deniability. Each document may be accurate for its audience, so nothing is a lie. The institution can say each group got what it needed. It has to come last, because it needs every earlier step: the quarrel over which FAQ is real only happens among people who were each given one page and told something about the rest.

> Two flatmates each get a different half of the landlord's letter. Both halves are accurate, and neither is the whole. Flip it: if you and your flatmate each already held half the letter when the landlord called you the easy tenant, you would sit down together and read both halves.

The conversion point is the moment the two versions are compared. The sequence works only while that has not happened. What would weaken it: sharing documents across groups early; a joint meeting with a shared agenda; a written request for the single authoritative version; and agreement among representatives to exchange every communication they receive.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A works council sits between two groups that rarely meet. Management holds the documents, employees hold the worries, and the council is meant to carry information between them. Consultation is run in separate meetings, restructuring plans exist in several drafts, and everyone wants to avoid a difficult room. Nobody had to design a way to divide a workforce. The arrangement already keeps people apart.

Triangulation comes first because it is efficient to route replies through one person. A head of people offers to carry messages back to the canteen, and for the company this saves a difficult exchange.

Smear campaigns come second because a go-between describes each side to the other, and people describe the anxious as "difficult" without much thought. Each remark is mild, and together they set the temperature.

Information control comes third because a restructuring has many drafts and one formal version, and the person who holds the documents decides which one is handed over. Nothing needs to be hidden; only one page needs to be offered.

Splitting comes fourth because camps simplify a hard situation. The sensible on one side and the noisy on the other is a story everyone can follow, and nobody has room for a middle position.

Priming comes fifth because each group meets the council already carrying a story about it. The council arrives at every table with a reputation it did not earn.

Comparative devaluation comes sixth because praise is cheap and flattering. Telling a representative that they are better than their colleagues, or than their counterparts at other sites, is both a compliment and a quiet question about whether to push.

Selective information sharing comes last because by now each group holds only what it was given. Both documents may be accurate for their audience, and nobody has to say anything false for the council to quarrel over which is real.

Put it together and you get the order this setting needs: a messenger, a mild smear, a single formal page, two camps, a cautious welcome, a compliment, and two partial truths. Nobody had to plan it. The structure rewards the party that holds the documents, and it turns a person who was elected to stand between two groups into one who stands with only one.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A restructuring, several drafts of one plan and a wish to avoid difficult rooms did the work, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a team spokesperson. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Dariusz Kowal was the elected spokesperson for the drivers at a regional depot. The manager, Roberta Finch, offered to carry his replies back to the drivers' room herself, so nobody had to sit through an awkward exchange. She said some of the drivers were being difficult, and that a small group was causing noise. She handed him a one-page summary of the new rota that said nothing had been decided. In the drivers' room, men told him the manager had said he was more interested in rules than people. A day later someone gave him another summary, printed from the planning system, with a list of shifts to be cut. When he asked Roberta, she said that was a working draft. In the corridor she told him he was much more reasonable than the others, and that the other depots never had this trouble. On Friday the drivers met, and each person had seen only one version of the rota, and they argued about which was true. Roberta told a friend that a depot with two camps never gave her any trouble.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may feel pulled in two directions, and unsure whom to trust. Over time, the person may start to act as a translator, softening messages in each direction, and be accused of favoring each side.

They may find their own judgment doubted by both groups. They may stop sharing documents out of caution, which feeds the problem. They may feel an exhausted loneliness at being the only one who has seen both.

## Institutional Echo

Restructuring processes often involve separate consultation with representatives, managers and affected employees, each with its own document set. When draft and final versions circulate to different audiences, or when management briefings differ from employee communications, the groups can form incompatible pictures.

Consultation duties, information rights and notice requirements for works councils and similar bodies vary by jurisdiction and agreement. The honest reading is that drafts do leak, that managers do receive working versions, and that sensitive restructuring plans are legitimately staged. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the difference between versions was explained, whether the groups were kept apart on purpose, and whether the same adjectives were applied to each group.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that organizations speak with one voice, and that those who ask for the same information as everyone else are being difficult. Alongside it sits the older idea that order comes from managing factions.

The social value being exploited is the hope that consultation is real. The language that makes it sound normal includes "working draft," "need to know," and "help bring the temperature down." The public story is a company consulting carefully. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is a workforce whose representatives never saw the same document.

## Observable Indicators

1. Different groups receive documents with the same title and different content.
2. A "working draft" and a "formal version" exist and are given to different audiences.
3. Management describes one group to another in negative terms.
4. The same adjectives appear in each group's account of the other.
5. Direct contact between groups is discouraged or routed through management.
6. Each group is praised for being more reasonable than the other.
7. You learn of a document only through an informal source.
8. Meetings begin with participants already wary of each other.
9. The representative is described differently to each side.
10. Requests for the single authoritative version receive an explanation but not the document.
11. Disagreements between groups intensify while the restructuring details remain unclear.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every version of the restructuring FAQ, with dates, source, audience and the way you obtained it.
- A dated, factual side-by-side of the differences between versions, as far as you can lawfully see.
- Notes of each meeting with management and with employees, written the same day.
- Statements made about one group in front of another, with wording and witnesses.
- Written requests for the authoritative version, and the replies.
- Consultation timetables, notices, and information provided to the council.
- Communications between you and the other representatives, and who received what.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Restructurings are legitimately staged, with different information appropriate to different audiences at different times. Drafts do get out, and management may truly be unsure. Groups can disagree without any outside influence.

Specific lookalikes include:

- **Phased disclosure.** The difference between versions is explained and limited.
- **Real draft leakage.** A working version was circulated in error and is corrected openly.
- **Genuine group disagreement.** Employees and management hold different interests.
- **Confidentiality of a live process.** Some detail is withheld for legal or market reasons that are stated.

Concern strengthens when: the same title carries different content for different audiences; negative adjectives about each group circulate on the other side; the authoritative version cannot be obtained; and direct contact between the groups is discouraged.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Side-By-Side Test:**
Ask: "If I laid every version next to each other, would they agree?"
Concern rises if: they differ in substance, not just detail.

**SPOT IT — The Adjective Test:**
Ask: "Are the same words used about each group by the other?"
Concern rises if: "difficult" and "uninformed" appear on both sides.

**SPOT IT — The Source Test:**
Ask: "Did I learn what the other group thinks from them, or from management?"
Concern rises if: from management.

**SPOT IT — The Flattery Test:**
Ask: "Am I being praised for being more reasonable than someone else?"
Concern rises if: yes, and I am being asked to keep it quiet.

**SPOT IT — The Authority Test:**
Ask: "Which document is the formal one, and who says so?"
Concern rises if: the answer changes.

## Chain Breakers

- **Compare documents early.** Ask every representative and group for the version they received, as lawfully shared.
- **Ask management in writing for the single authoritative version** and for the date each audience received which document.
- **Ask for a joint meeting** with a shared agenda and the same documents on the table.
- **Agree an exchange rule** among representatives: each shares everything received from management.
- **Check characterizations directly**: speak with the other group about what they actually said.
- **Keep a dated log** of what each audience was told, by whom, and when.
- **Do not repeat management's characterizations** of either group. Neutral wording protects trust.
- **Check your information rights** and consultation process with your own adviser or union, as applicable.
- **Do not leak documents you are not authorized to hold.** Use proper channels to request them.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Two versions of a document circulate; management characterizes one group to another; you learn of a document informally.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The authoritative version cannot be obtained; consultation proceeds on differing information; decisions that affect jobs are taken before the groups see the same facts; or representatives are pressed to take sides. Relevant routes may include a union or works council federation, your own attorney, an independent director, a labor or employment regulator, and mediation where the process allows.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation of representatives, stalking, blackmail, retaliation that endangers safety, fraud in progress, or self-harm statements by anyone involved. Prioritize safety and emergency support, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you confirm in writing which version of the FAQ is the formal one?"
- "I've seen two documents with this title. Could you explain the differences?"
- "Could we hold a joint session where everyone has the same documents?"
- "I'd like to hear from employees directly, and to share what I hear with you."
- "I'm not going to characterize either group. I would prefer we all see the same facts."
- "Can you tell me when each audience received which version?"
- "Please send the consultation timetable, so the council can plan."
