# C133: Competence Undermining — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that you cannot manage on your own, a sense that praise always hides a catch, and a reflex to hand your achievements to others before they are counted.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic sentence when praised, "it was really a team effort," even when you did it alone.
- A habit of checking your work with someone else first, because you no longer trust it is good enough.
- A pull to compare yourself with whoever seems most capable in the room, and to come off worse every time.

**A point in a life.** A few years into a product career.

**Where it happens.** A software company's product team.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a senior product manager, you're thirty-two, and for two years you have been the person who actually knows how the pricing engine works. Your director hired you. He said so at your first one-to-one, warmly: "I didn't hire you to execute. I hired you because you think." The first quarter was the best of your career. You rebuilt the onboarding flow, got the metric moving, and presented it to the leadership group, and afterward he walked you back to your desk and said, "That's why I brought you in."

**Two quarters in, the leadership meeting**

It is the quarterly review. The deck on the wall shows your onboarding work under his name, in the first person plural: "we shipped." You assume it is shorthand. Ten minutes later he says, "I've been working on a rethink of pricing tiers," and you have the working document open on your laptop, with your comments in it. You say nothing, because you are not someone who fights over a pronoun. The room smells of whiteboard marker and burnt coffee. Your face stays warm for an hour.

**Fall, your desk**

When you ask for a larger scope, he says he wants to "protect your bandwidth." When you want to present to the executive team yourself, he offers to "carry it up," so you don't have to "deal with that room." He rewrites your emails before they go out, kindly, for "tone." You find yourself running every decision past him, including ones he has told you in writing are yours. You stop drafting the replies you would have sent.

He stops at your desk after the launch review. "That was really strong, especially for someone who hasn't done this at scale." On Friday, "Honestly, I didn't expect that from you, but good." You thank him each time. You replay it at your desk, and what you keep hearing is the second half.

He is also always unavailable at the moment a hard decision needs making. He forgets to sign off on the vendor change. He needs you to "just take a first pass" at the budget he owns. He asks you, again, how the approval workflow works, because "you're so much better at it than I am," and you explain it, again, and it becomes your job to run it. You eat lunch at your desk to keep up with both jobs.

**Winter, your desk**

When your onboarding numbers hit target, the target moves. When you propose a launch, a dependency on his team quietly disappears from the roadmap, and the launch stalls with nothing wrong in it. Each win comes apart quietly, and you start to wait before you tell anyone about the next one.

The success metrics in your quarter plan are set at a level you privately think nobody could meet: growth up, support tickets down, no new headcount, both before the end of the quarter. You miss one. It goes in your file. Your jaw is tight when you read the line.

**Thursday before promotion season, your desk**

He says: "I've drafted your packet so you don't have to. I want to make this easy for you." He shares it with you at five, and you read it at nine that night. It is two pages and well written. Your name is on it. The pricing engine isn't. The onboarding rebuild appears as "contributed to initiatives led by the product organization." Under development areas it says you are "still building executive presence" and "would benefit from continued coaching on ownership." In the same email thread, a colleague on another team has a packet attached as an example. Her bullets are specific, with numbers. Yours has the word "supportive" twice.

You sit with the document open for a long time. In your own folder you find the pricing proposal, with your name and a date, from fourteen months ago. You do not feel angry yet. What you feel is a small, cold doubt about whether it was always a little more his than you thought. Down the hall his light is on. You have his email open, and you are composing a reply that thanks him.

**Two years later**

You are a group product lead at a company across town. It is a Friday in June, in the big room with the folding chairs, and the head of the business stands at the front and says your launch was the best the company has seen. People clap. Someone says your name. You feel yourself turn toward the room with a smile you can feel from the inside, and the first thing you do is point at someone else.

"Honestly, it was Jonah's team," you say. It was not. Jonah's team ran the support schedule. You wrote the plan, the pricing, the sequence and the date. Your hand is still pointing at Jonah's row when the clapping stops.

You take your seat, and while the next speaker begins, you open your phone and send the launch memo to a friend from your old company, who has never seen it, with the message "Does this read okay? Is it good enough?" It has been out in the world for three weeks. Nobody has complained. She writes back "It's great!" within a minute and you do not believe her. You open a second document and send that one to someone else.

You look along the front row at the senior leads, the ones who seem to move through meetings without apologizing for taking up the air. You count how many of them are better than you. It comes out the same every time: all of them. Your calendar has six slots this month for "review" with colleagues, and nothing booked for the thing you are actually good at.

## The Name

That's **Competence Undermining**.

## Definition

Competence Undermining is the sequence where a person's accomplishments are taken or reassigned, their capability is treated as limited, and their confidence is steadily lowered through small remarks, burdens, obstacles and unwinnable standards, until they doubt they can function without the person above them. It can use Credit Theft, Infantilization, Negging, Weaponized Incompetence, Sabotaging Success, Impossible Metrics and Comparative Devaluation to convert the wish to be seen as capable into the loss of credit for their own work and of demonstrated ability. It is hard to detect because each act has a benign reading, such as shorthand, protection, coaching, delegation or stretch goals, and because the pattern changes the target's own record and self-assessment. Poor management, unconscious bias and genuine mentoring can look similar; intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Credit Theft** — The pronoun that moved: your onboarding work appears under his name as "we shipped," and in the meeting he announces "a rethink of pricing tiers" while your working document, with your comments in it, is open in front of you. This functions by deleting the evidence of ability from the record that decides promotion. Concern rises when the omission repeats, and your authorship is clear in the files.

2. **Infantilization** — The offered shelter: "protect your bandwidth," "carry it up" so you need not "deal with that room," his rewrites of your emails for "tone," and every decision run past him. This functions by narrowing your visibility and authority while sounding like care. Concern rises when you are routed away from decision-makers who would see your work.

3. **Negging** — The compliments with hooks: "strong, especially for someone who hasn't done this at scale" and "I didn't expect that from you, but good," each one thanked for. This functions by pairing approval with doubt, so that reassurance and confidence loss arrive together. Concern rises when the barb recurs and you find yourself seeking his approval to feel steady.

4. **Weaponized Incompetence** — The help he keeps needing: he forgets the vendor sign-off, asks you for "a first pass" at his own budget, and asks again how the approval workflow works until running it becomes your job. This functions by moving his work onto you while his unavailability makes you responsible for outcomes without his authority. Concern rises when the gaps always land on the same person and the same direction.

5. **Sabotaging Success** — The wins taken apart: the target moves once your onboarding numbers hit it, and a dependency on his team vanishes from the roadmap so your launch stalls. This functions by keeping you from a visible accomplishment that would be hard to deny. Concern rises when obstacles appear near milestones and nobody can explain them.

6. **Impossible Metrics** — The quarter plan that cannot be met: growth up, support tickets down, no new headcount, all before quarter end, with one miss entered in your file. This functions by guaranteeing a recorded miss that later documents can cite. Concern rises when goals are not benchmarked against peers or resourced.

7. **Comparative Devaluation** — The packet beside another packet: his draft leaves out the pricing engine, calls you "still building executive presence," and sits next to a colleague's packet full of numbers while yours says "supportive" twice. This functions by making the doubt feel factual and not personal. Concern rises when comparisons are used in documents but the comparators and criteria are unavailable to you.

## Why This Order

**Credit Theft** must come first because the other tactics only work on a person whose record can be questioned. If your accomplishments are visible, praise, support and doubt are hard to sustain; once they are attributed elsewhere, the evidence of competence is gone, and the rest of the sequence rests on a thinner, more negotiable record. It has to come before his offers to carry your work up, because those offers only keep the executives from your name once the deck already says "we shipped."

> Picture a friend who tells the story of the trip you planned and says "we found this great place," as if you were not there. You let it go, because correcting a pronoun feels petty. Flip it: if your friend first said, "Let me call the hotel for the trip you planned, you've got enough on," you would say you will make the call yourself, and thank them.

**Infantilization** makes the first step durable. By limiting your exposure to senior people, it keeps the people who might notice the missing credit from seeing your work directly. It has to come before the backhanded praise, because a line like "for someone who hasn't done this at scale" lands harder on a person who now runs every decision past him.

> Picture a parent who says "let me handle that phone call" every time you could make it yourself. After a while it stops occurring to you to dial. Flip it: if your parent first said, "You sounded quite good on that call, for you," and only then offered to handle the next one, you would notice the second half and say you will make it.

**Negging** then enters the relationship, and the sequence of approval plus doubt matters: it makes you dependent on his judgment to feel steady, which makes the next steps easier to accept as feedback. It has to come before his unavailability on hard decisions, because the doubt left by the second half of that praise is what makes you say yes when he asks about the workflow.

> "Nice haircut, it really suits you now." You say thank you, and spend the evening on the word "now." Flip it: if the person first asked you to book their haircut again because they never get it right, and later said "it really suits you now," you would still notice the word "now."

**Weaponized Incompetence** and **Sabotaging Success** change the facts and not only the story. Weaponized Incompetence transfers workload and responsibility while keeping authority, which produces both exhaustion and plausible deniability: a person who "just can't" is hard to blame. It has to come before the vanishing dependency, because by the time your launch stalls you are already running his workflow and have no spare evening to chase the cause.

> Picture a housemate who can never work out the washing machine. You end up doing every wash, and being cross would seem unfair. Flip it: if your housemate first put your drying rack away "for tidiness," and only then said they can never work out the washing machine, you would take the rack back out and ask them to learn the machine.

**Sabotaging Success** then removes the wins that would break the narrative. It has to come before the quarter-plan targets, because the stalled launch leaves you with no win to point to when the targets are set.

> You finally get your plant to flower, and someone moves it to the dark corner "for tidiness." Flip it: if someone first said the plant had to flower by Friday, which no plant could manage, and later moved it to the dark corner, you would ask for a fair date and keep it in the light.

**Impossible Metrics** turns this into paper, because a goal designed to be missed produces a record of failure that looks neutral. It has to come before the packet comparison, because the missed target in your file is what the packet's thin bullets then point to.

> Picture a coach who tells you to beat a record nobody has beaten, in shoes that do not fit, and then writes your time on a clipboard. Flip it: if the coach first said, "The others all hit that time on their first try," and never said who, you would ask to see their times on the clipboard before you started.

**Comparative Devaluation** completes the picture by supplying the contrast: you appear less than your peers in a system whose criteria you cannot see. It has to come last, because the colleague's numbered bullets only make yours look thin once the file holds a missed target and a stalled launch.

> A teacher says "the others got this first time," and never says who the others are. You can't argue with people you can't see. Flip it: if the teacher first said "the others got this first time," and then set you a task no one could finish in the time given, you would ask who the others were and how long they had.

The conversion point is the promotion packet. By then the record, the self-image and the colleagues' impressions have all been shaped, and the packet is the document that makes it official, delivered as a favor. What would weaken the sequence: your own contemporaneous record of authorship, early written confirmation of ownership and scope, and a sponsor or skip-level who sees your work directly.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A product organization produces this order without a villain. Three facts about the setting do the work. Credit travels up one reporting line, so a director is the only person leadership hears from. Promotion slots are few and the packet is written by the manager. And metrics are set by the same person who reads the results.

Credit Theft comes first because every other step needs a record that can be questioned. When a deck says "we shipped," the executives who could notice authorship see a team's result, and the author's trail is already thinner.

Infantilization comes second because the same reporting line that carries credit up also screens who is seen. Offering to carry your work to the executive room is a kindness, and it keeps the people who might notice the gap from seeing the work directly.

Negging comes third because a manager who has become the only route to the room gives feedback, and feedback is where approval and doubt are easiest to combine. "Strong, for someone who hasn't done this at scale" is read as a review.

Weaponized Incompetence comes fourth because a director who owns more than he can do has to move work somewhere. He asks the person who is good at it, and every explanation turns into a job.

Sabotaging Success comes fifth because a stalled launch costs nothing visible: a dependency drops off a roadmap that someone else owns, and no single change looks like a decision.

Impossible Metrics come sixth because a quarter plan is where expectations become paper. Targets that cannot all be met are not recognized as a design until the missed one is in your file.

Comparative Devaluation comes last because a packet is read against other packets. A colleague's specific bullets turn your general ones into a ranking you cannot see the basis of.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a system where credit, scope, feedback and measures all route through one person ends with a packet that reads like a favor. It turns the person at the center into someone who checks everything twice and claims nothing.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A single reporting line that carries credit up, a promotion packet written by the manager, and targets set by the person who reads the results did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ines Kowalczyk, a data analyst in her first job, built a churn model that her manager, Desmond Fenley, called "exactly why I hired you." In the next monthly deck the model appeared under "our work," with his name on the title slide. He began presenting her analyzes upward himself, "so you can stay focused." After the next one he said, "That was good, honestly, for someone straight out of school." When a report needed running, he asked her to show him again how the data pipeline worked, because he "never quite got it," and then asked her to run it each week. A dashboard she had built was switched to read from an older table, and it broke the week before her demo. Her quarterly goals were set at a pace three analysts could not have met, and she missed one. On the Thursday before review season he sent her a draft of her own evaluation, "to save you the effort." It called her "supportive" twice and was shorter than the one he had attached for another analyst, whose bullets had figures in them. Desmond was never unsure what he was doing. He told a friend that Ines would be grateful for the help.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to second-guess work they know is good, and to rehearse thanks for criticism that stings. Over time, they may stop claiming credit even in rooms where they could, and become reluctant to ask for visibility. They may over-prepare, double-check decisions that are properly theirs, or defer to the director on matters of their own remit. They may find they cannot summarize their own accomplishments without hesitation, and may attribute the shift to personal weakness, not to a pattern in the environment.

## Institutional Echo

Promotion cycles that rely on a manager-drafted packet can concentrate control over a person's record in one reader. Calibration processes in which a case is summarized by someone other than the person can reduce the visibility of work the summarizer did not attribute. Goals set without peer benchmarks or resourcing, and shared with different detail to different people, can generate unwinnable targets with no one designing them.

The honest reading in many cases is that directors are busy, packets are written quickly, shorthand is common, and genuine development feedback is useful. Intent would require additional evidence: whether omissions run one way, whether peers' packets are specific while yours is not, and whether the pattern recurs with other direct reports.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that good work speaks for itself and that credit follows merit. It hides the fact that credit has to be recorded, and that the person who records it holds power. Alongside it sits the belief that gratitude and humility are professional virtues, so a person who corrects the record can be cast as petty or not a team player.

The value exploited is a good one: wanting to be seen as capable, and trusting a mentor. The public story is a leader who develops people. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a person who comes to believe their own competence was always borrowed.

## Observable Indicators

1. Your authored work appears in decks or packets under collective or someone else's name.
2. You are routed away from senior audiences who would see your work directly.
3. Praise is regularly paired with a remark about what you were not expected to do.
4. Decisions and sign-offs that are his are left to you, while responsibility for outcomes stays with you.
5. Dependencies or approvals disappear shortly before your milestones.
6. Targets are changed after they are met, or are set so they conflict.
7. Your goals are not benchmarked against peers or resourced.
8. Development feedback in a packet or review has no dated examples.
9. Comparisons to peers are used, but the comparators and criteria are unavailable to you.
10. Your own record shows work that the formal record does not.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Dated drafts, comments and version history showing your authorship of the work that was attributed elsewhere.
- Decks and reports with attribution as presented, next to your originals.
- Your written scope, goals and metrics for each period, and any changes made after they were set.
- The promotion criteria and the packet drafts, with the date you received each.
- Records of requests for visibility, scope or decision rights and the replies.
- Peer examples of packets or reviews that you have been lawfully shown, noting that you should not access documents you are not authorized to see.
- A dated note of remarks that paired praise with doubt, recorded as quoted words and not interpretations.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Directors compress work into shorthand, packets are written in a hurry, and a stretch goal or a candid note on executive presence can be sound.

The discriminator is **direction and consistency**. Ordinary compression omits credit at random and is corrected when pointed out. This pattern omits it in one direction, repeatedly, and meets correction with a development note about you.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Genuine coaching.** Feedback comes with specific examples, a path and growing autonomy.
- **Real team attribution.** A shared project may be reported as "we," and your part may be small or large.
- **Resource limits.** A shifted target may follow a real budget change.

Concern strengthens when: omissions run one direction across several documents; correction is answered with a negative development note; and peers describe similar treatment.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Authorship Test:**
Ask: "If I laid my files next to this document, would my work appear in it?"
Concern rises if: your authorship is clear in the files and absent from the record.

**SPOT IT — The Barb Test:**
Ask: "Does the praise I receive carry a comment about what I was not expected to do?"
Concern rises if: the doubt recurs and you rely on the approval to feel steady.

**SPOT IT — The Moving Target Test:**
Ask: "Was the goal changed after I met it, or set so two parts conflict?"
Concern rises if: the target moves without a written reason.

**SPOT IT — The Packet Test:**
Ask: "Can I see and respond to what is written about me before it is submitted?"
Concern rises if: you see it late, and cannot correct the record.

## Chain Breakers

- **Keep a running record of your work** with dates: what you did, what was decided, what you handed over.
- **Confirm ownership in writing.** After a meeting: "To confirm, I'll own the pricing tier proposal and present it on the 14th."
- **Ask for the promotion criteria and the packet in advance,** and respond in writing to what is incomplete.
- **Add your own summary** for the packet or calibration: a short, factual list of accomplishments with dates.
- **Build a second reader.** A skip-level, a peer in another team or a sponsor who sees your work directly.
- **Ask for examples.** "Can you give me two examples of where I should show more ownership?"
- **Use policy channels.** Ask HR or the promotion process owner how a packet can be amended.
- **Do not publicly accuse or rework others' files.** Stay with documents you are authorized to hold.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** One or two attribution gaps; a barbed comment; your sign-offs routinely handed to you; a moved target. Begin the record and ask for written scope.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A packet or review that omits documented work and carries negative development notes without examples; peers with comparable work described differently; pay, promotion or standing at stake. Relevant routes may include an independent senior sponsor, HR or the promotion process owner, an employment lawyer or union representative where available, and an ombuds or compliance channel.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, retaliation for raising the issue, harassment, or anyone describing self-harm because of the pressure. Prioritize safety and emergency or crisis support, and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "To confirm what we agreed, I'm owning the pricing proposal and presenting it to the leadership group on the 14th."
- "Could I see the promotion packet before it is submitted, and add a short summary of my work?"
- "The onboarding rebuild isn't listed. Can we add it, with the result?"
- "Can you give me two specific examples behind the 'executive presence' note?"
- "What are the criteria and the comparator group for this rating?"
- "I'd like these targets confirmed in writing, including how the conflict between ticket volume and speed is resolved."
- "Who is the right person to see my work directly this quarter?"
