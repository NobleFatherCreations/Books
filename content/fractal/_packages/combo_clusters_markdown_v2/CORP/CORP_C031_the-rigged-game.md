# C031: The Rigged Game — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the scoring is fair and any loss must be your own fault, and a quiet sense that good results belong to someone else while the bad ones stay with you.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of playing down your part in anything that went well, and owning every part of anything that went badly.
- A pull to keep trying harder where the rules keep changing, sure that next time you will finally get it right.
- An automatic sentence, "it was probably my fault," before anyone has looked at what actually happened.

**A point in a life.** The first years of a finance career.

**Where it happens.** A mid-sized private equity firm.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a deal team associate at a mid-market private equity firm, twenty-eight years old, and you found the company. Not "supported the process." Found it. Eleven months ago you were reading a trade newsletter on a train, noticed a founder's name in a footnote, and spent three weekends building a one-page screen on a business nobody at the firm had looked at. You sent it to the vice president on your deal team: "Think this fits our thesis. Happy to take a first pass at outreach." He wrote back within the hour. "Great catch. Let's run with it. I'll handle the founder relationship, you build the model." That sounded like how it works. You were glad to be trusted with the model.

**January to June, the vice president's office**

Your annual goals sheet says you will "source a minimum of four actionable opportunities per quarter." You count what the platform put in front of the whole deal team last year: five names that fit the thesis, shared by four associates. Four a quarter, from you, is sixteen a year. You have sourced one real one, the one everybody is now working on. You sign the sheet in his office. The pen feels heavy.

When you ask what counts as actionable, he says, "Something that gets to investment committee." In the next review it becomes "something that gets to a signed letter of intent." In the review after, "something with a proprietary angle," which turns out to mean no banker involved, and is not in the sheet at all. Each new definition is delivered kindly, in passing, as if it had always been the definition. You start writing the dates down in a paper notebook in your bag, in case you are the one who is wrong.

**November and December, the deal floor**

Two weeks before investment committee he stops at your desk and tells you to cut the model's downside case, because the partners find it "negative." A week later he stops again and says that you need to show more initiative, and that anything leaving the team goes through him first. You do both. You are asked to be proactive and to wait. You open a draft email to the principal, close it, and open it again.

That night, at your desk, in the deal-team channel, a principal asks why the downside case is so thin. The vice president replies: "Good question, I'd have liked more rigor there. Taking that to the associate." You read it with the floor empty and a cleaner's cart rattling past the glass. Nobody in the channel answers. Three people react with a thumbs up. When you raise it quietly at his desk the next morning, he says, "I hear you, but let's not litigate who said what. I need you to own the model. Owning it means owning it."

Then comes the allocation sheet. In December the finance lead circulates a worksheet, and the final slide of the investment memo feeds it. Sourced by: him. Lead: him. Execution: him and a principal. Supporting analysis: a column of initials, and yours is third from the end. Where the form has a box for "Originator," one name appears, and it is not yours. The deal is approved, and the congratulatory email to the whole firm names him and the principal.

You stop him as he passes your desk and say, as evenly as you can, that the company came from your screen. He smiles. "Of course. And that's why you're on the team. But everyone sources on this firm's platform. If we start splitting hairs on attribution, it gets political, and political isn't a look that works for associates." Heat climbs your neck. "Sorry," you say. "Of course."

**February, the vice president's office**

At your review, you are told you are "strong technically, but need to show more initiative on origination." You score a two out of five on that line. You ask to see the sheet, and he says the firm is "not a spreadsheet culture about this." Then he reads out one number from it: four a quarter, against your one. You point to the line that credits analysis on approved deals, and he says that is not how the firm reads that line. Then he opens the review form and, going by the worksheet, types your sourced deals for the year as zero. "I can only score what's written."

**Two years later, a Thursday in October**

Two years later it is a Thursday in October, and you are in a conference room at a software company where you now work. A launch you built has gone well. Your new manager asks who wrote the integration, and you say, "The team, mostly. I just put a few pieces together." It was you. It was almost all you.

Two weeks ago a release slipped by a day. Before anyone had opened the logs, you wrote to forty people that it was probably something you had missed, and offered to stay late. The logs showed a vendor outage. You stayed late anyway. When your manager changes how the quarter is scored for the third time, a colleague groans and you quietly rebuild your whole plan over the weekend, certain that the right shape for it is out there somewhere.

On the train home you open your sent folder and find an old email with a one-page screen attached. You read it slowly, and for a moment you cannot say whose idea it was.

## The Name

That's **The Rigged Game**.

## Definition

The Rigged Game is the sequence where a person is measured against targets they cannot meet, then blamed for the shortfall, while whatever they do achieve is credited to someone else, using Impossible Metrics, Moving Goalposts, Double Binds, Scapegoating, Credit Theft, Shame Installation, and Malicious Compliance to convert the belief that an evaluation system claiming to be fair actually is into lost trust in evaluation and a lost willingness to keep trying. It is hard to detect because every mechanism looks like ordinary management and the system looks fair from outside. What the person can lose is career progress, a share of economic upside, a professional record that accurately reflects their work, and the confidence that effort leads to outcomes. Whether any of this is deliberate would require additional evidence, since the same surface can come from disorganization, unclear criteria or ordinary credit-sloppiness.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Impossible Metrics** — A target that cannot be hit: four actionable opportunities a quarter, when the platform put five fitting names in front of four associates all last year, and you sign the sheet anyway. This functions by setting a bar the person cannot clear, so that failure is guaranteed and looks like their own shortfall. Concern rises when no one can explain how the target was derived.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — A definition that keeps changing: "actionable" becomes "gets to investment committee," then "signed letter of intent," then "proprietary angle," none of it written down. This functions by letting the person do what was asked and still be told it was not enough. Concern rises when the definition changes only after the work is delivered.

3. **Double Binds** — Demands that cancel out: cut the downside case because the partners find it "negative," show more initiative, and take everything that leaves the team through him first. This functions by making every available choice a failure. Concern rises when two instructions cannot both be satisfied and the person is judged on whichever one they missed.

4. **Scapegoating** — Blame placed publicly: in the deal channel he answers a principal's downside question with "Taking that to the associate," and in his office adds "Owning it means owning it." This functions by routing a decision made upward into a deficiency attributed downward. Concern rises when responsibility moves to the lowest-ranking person and the original instruction is not in writing.

5. **Credit Theft** — The allocation worksheet shows him as Sourced by and Lead, one name in the Originator box, and the congratulatory email to the firm names him and the principal. This functions by transferring the visible, economic and reputational value of the work to someone with more power. Concern rises when attribution is decided in a form the contributor never sees before it circulates.

6. **Shame Installation** — A line that turns a fair question into a flaw: "Political isn't a look that works for associates," followed by "need to show more initiative on origination," and your own "Sorry." This functions by turning a legitimate question into a character flaw. Concern rises when ordinary questions are met with remarks about attitude or fit.

7. **Malicious Compliance** — The form followed to the letter against you: the analysis credit line set aside as "not how the firm reads that line," then, going by the worksheet, he types your sourced deals for the year as zero and says "I can only score what's written." This functions by providing procedural cover for a result. Concern rises when rules are applied rigidly to one person and flexibly to others.

## Why This Order

**Impossible Metrics** has to come first because the entire sequence needs a standard that cannot be met. Without it, the later steps would need individual justifications. With it, failure is pre-installed, and every later act can simply refer to "the numbers." It has to come before the changing meaning of "actionable," because the meaning can only slide beneath a sheet whose sixteen-a-year number you have already signed.

> Picture a gym that sets you a target of a thousand sit-ups a day. You never reach it, and nobody asks why it was a thousand. Flip it: at the gym you are told to do "some" sit-ups, then that it means a hundred, then that it means a thousand; only then is a thousand fixed as the daily target, and you notice the number kept moving.

**Moving Goalposts** makes the metric uncatchable. A fixed impossible target can eventually be challenged. A shifting one cannot, because by the time the person gathers evidence, the question has changed. The person also starts to doubt their own memory of what was asked. It has to come before the two orders to cut the downside and show initiative, because you only try to obey both once you have learned that no answer stays fixed for long.

> You are told to "tidy your room," then that it means "spotless," then that it means "the way I like it." By the third version you are not sure what the first one said. Flip it: before you tidy your room, you are told to be quicker and to take your time in the same breath; only then does "tidy" become "spotless," and you ask for the list in writing.

**Double Binds** remove the last route to proving competence. Once any action can be recast as the wrong one, performance stops being the variable that matters. It has to come before the channel reply about the thin downside case, because that reply blames you only after both orders have been given to you privately and not in writing.

> Your parent says "stop being so quiet" and "don't answer back" in the same week. Whatever you do next is wrong. Flip it: first your parent tells the whole family that the quiet in the house is your doing, and only then says "stop being so quiet" and "don't answer back"; you notice the blame arrived before the rules.

**Scapegoating** then converts the structure into a public record. By this point the associate is presumed deficient, so a channel message assigning blame lands without resistance. It has to come before the allocation worksheet, because the worksheet lists his name and not yours without any objection only after you have been named in the channel as the person behind the thin model.

> A group chat asks why the picnic was a mess, and the organiser answers, "I'll take that up with Sam." Sam wasn't in charge of the food, but now nobody asks who was. Flip it: before anyone asks about the mess, the organizer posts that all the food was their own work; only afterward, when the mess comes up, do they say they will take it up with Sam.

Losses are attached downward; **Credit Theft** attaches the wins upward. The two work as a pair: the core is an asymmetry in who carries what. It has to come before your talk at his desk, because you only raise the Originator box with him after you see it filled with one name and the firm-wide email naming two others.

> You bake the cake and a friend carries it in. Everyone thanks the friend. Flip it: a friend first tells you that only petty people count who baked what; then you bake the cake, a friend carries it in, everyone thanks them, and you notice you have already stopped yourself from mentioning it.

**Shame Installation** raises challenge costs without further effort from the power holder: if the associate absorbs the idea that asking about attribution is unsophisticated, they start policing themselves. It has to come before the review, because the zero he types for your sourced deals reads as fair only after you have apologized and dropped the question about attribution.

> A relative says, "Only people who are petty count who paid for dinner." You stop mentioning the bill. Flip it: you offer to pay your share of the dinner and are told the bill can only be split item by item, by you, with a pen, on one napkin; only then does a relative say only petty people count, and you stop mentioning the bill.

**Malicious Compliance** closes the loop and supplies plausible deniability. No one has done anything but follow the sheet. The outcome looks like the system working. It has to come last, because "I can only score what's written" closes the matter only after the sheet, the redefinitions, the orders, the channel reply, the worksheet and your apology have all been entered.

> A sign says "no refunds without a receipt." You hand over the receipt, and they say it is the wrong kind of receipt. Flip it: you hand over the receipt and the clerk says it is the wrong kind of receipt; then the clerk says people who ask for refunds are the difficult sort, and you notice you have stopped asking.

What weakens the sequence is written definitions, same-day summary emails, sight of the allocation worksheet before it circulates, and an independent reader who can see the pattern across the deal team. The key conversion point is the first moment the associate apologizes for asking a fair question.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A firm's annual goals sheet is written from a template, with numbers set before anyone checks how many names the platform really produces. Deals are approved through a form that finance circulates, and a person's year is scored from what the form says. And a deal team is small, so one senior person stands between the associate and everyone else. A template, a worksheet and a gatekeeper: those three facts produce the order below.

Impossible Metrics comes first because a goals sheet is signed before the year starts, and its numbers come from what the firm wants rather than from what the platform can supply. Sixteen names a year from a pool of five is a mismatch written down in January and discovered later.

Moving Goalposts comes second because the sheet uses words that need interpreting. "Actionable" means whatever the reviewer says it means at the review, and each new meaning is given in passing, so nothing in writing ever changes.

Double Binds comes third because a gatekeeper gives instructions to one person in private, and the instructions do not have to agree. Cut the downside case, show initiative, send nothing out without him: each is reasonable alone.

Scapegoating comes fourth because a deal team answers questions in a shared channel, and the quickest answer to why something is thin is the name of the person who built it. By now the associate is presumed to be the weak point, so the reply meets no resistance.

Credit Theft comes fifth because the worksheet lists roles in one line each, and the person who handles the founder relationship is the easiest to list as the source. The form has one box for the originator, and the form is what the partners read.

Shame Installation comes sixth because a question about attribution touches how the firm describes itself. Calling it political is a short step from the worksheet, and an associate who wants to be seen as sophisticated stops asking.

Malicious Compliance comes last because the review has to be filled in from what the worksheet says. Scoring only what is written is the form working as built, and the sourced deals read as zero.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a number before the work, a word that moves, a gatekeeper who cannot be satisfied, a record that assigns blame and credit, and a form that closes the matter. It turns an associate who found a company into one who apologizes for asking who did.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A template goals sheet, a shared channel and a worksheet that finance circulates were enough, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a junior consultant. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ingrid Solano was a junior consultant at a mid-sized firm when she found a prospective client, a small manufacturer whose name she had noticed in a trade newsletter. She built a one-page screen and sent it to her senior manager, Everard Pike, who wrote back that it was a great catch and that he would handle the client relationship while she built the model. Her goals sheet asked for six leads a quarter, though the firm's whole pipeline produced a handful each year. When she asked what counted as a lead, he said one that reached a proposal; at the next check-in, one that reached a signed engagement; at the next, one with no outside referral. In the weeks before the pitch he told her to cut the risk section because it read as negative, then said she needed to show more initiative and to send nothing outside the team without him. When the client's lead asked in the shared channel why the risk section was so thin, Everard answered that he would take it up with Ingrid. The proposal named him as originator. In the corridor Ingrid said the client had come from her screen, and he told her that attribution fights got political. She apologized. At her review he entered her originations for the year as zero. "I can only score what's written," he said. Everard told a friend that the only reliable form was the one you filled in yourself.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may begin to over-prepare, carrying more and more work to guard against a standard they cannot see. They may stop proposing ideas, because ideas that succeed are credited upward and ideas that fail are theirs. They may reread old emails to check what they really said, and wonder whether the problem is simply them.

Their explanations to friends may sound petty when spoken aloud: a box on a form, a thumbs up in a channel. The smallness of each event can make the whole feel unreportable. Some leave the field. Others stay and stop trying, which can then be cited as proof the earlier assessment was right.

## Institutional Echo

Firms that rank people against each other, allocate bonus or carry by contribution, and rely on a small number of senior sign-offs can reproduce this pattern without anyone designing it. Where credit and economic participation are recorded in a document the contributor does not see before it circulates, the originating person has little means to correct it. Where evaluation criteria are communicated verbally and revised each cycle, a target that cannot be met is easy to produce by accident.
How carry, bonus and sourcing credit are allocated differs between firms and is often set by private agreements, so no claim here should be read as a statement about any firm's practice. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Civilizational Echo

The concealing myth is meritocracy: that results speak for themselves, that the system sorts people fairly, and that anyone who is passed over simply did not deliver. It is a deeply attractive story, because it promises that effort is legible, and people who believe in it work hard inside it.

Around it sits the belief that credit flows up the chain by nature, and that it is unseemly to keep score. The person who raises attribution is called political, which is exactly the label the pattern needs. The public story is a firm that rewards talent. The private loss, when this pattern is present, is that the one person who most believes in the fairness of the game becomes the one it is built to defeat.

## Observable Indicators

1. A written performance target cannot be traced to any stated method, volume or role.
2. The definition of a key term changes between reviews without a written revision.
3. A request for the written standard is deflected, delayed or answered verbally.
4. Two instructions are given that cannot both be followed, and the person is assessed on one.
5. A public channel message assigns responsibility to a junior person for a decision made higher up.
6. An attribution, origination or allocation form omits or misstates a contributor, and the contributor first sees it after circulation.
7. Congratulation messages name senior people only, although the work history shows who did it.
8. A request to correct credit is met with remarks about politics, maturity or optics rather than with the records.
9. Rules from the goals sheet are applied strictly to one person and loosely to peers.
10. Ratings fall after the person raises a question about a metric or credit.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Your original sourcing email, screen or memo, with dates and attachments, and any reply acknowledging it.
- Every version of your goals sheet, target list and review forms, with dates, and a running note of each change in a definition.
- The deal-team channel messages that assign responsibility to you, preserved by screenshot with date, channel and sender visible.
- The credit memo, allocation worksheet or originator field as you lawfully received it, and any request you made to correct it with the reply.
- Same-day written summaries you sent after verbal instructions, and whether they were confirmed or disputed.
- Calendar entries showing who attended meetings where credit or targets were discussed.
- Names of colleagues who saw the originating work or heard the instructions.
- A dated timeline of targets set, definitions changed, ratings given and attribution decisions.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Origination credit is genuinely hard to apportion in deal teams. An associate may find a company, but a vice president may do the relationship work that makes it real, and carry allocation can rest on formulas the associate does not see. A target can be hard but fair, goals can move because the fund's strategy changed, and a senior person may truly believe credit follows the lead role. Feedback on initiative may be sound.

The discriminator is whether the standard is written, consistent and applied to everyone, and whether credit and blame are recorded in the same way. Concern strengthens when: the same pattern of upward credit and downward blame appears across several juniors; requests for written criteria or a view of the allocation are refused or punished; and ratings fall after questions are asked rather than after performance changes.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Written Standard Test:**
Ask: "Where is the target written down, and who set it?"
Concern rises if: "It's understood," or the answer changes with each asking.

**SPOT IT — The Two-Direction Test:**
Ask: "Who is named when it goes well, and who is named when it goes badly?"
Concern rises if: the success column and the failure column never contain the same names.

**SPOT IT — The Impossible Pair Test:**
Ask: "Can I satisfy both instructions at once?"
Concern rises if: any reasonable choice leaves me falling short on one of them.

**SPOT IT — The Question Cost Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I ask what a number means?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a remark about my attitude rather than an explanation of the number.

**SPOT IT — The Same Sheet Test:**
Ask: "Do peers get held to the same sheet, applied the same way?"
Concern rises if: the rules are strict for me and flexible elsewhere.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the standard in writing, early.** "Could you confirm in an email how actionable is defined for this year's goals?" Calm, specific and dated.
- **Send a same-day summary after any verbal change.** "To confirm what we agreed today, the target is now X, measured by Y." If it is not disputed, you have a record. If it is disputed, that is informative.
- **Record origination neutrally at the start.** An email to the deal team stating "I sourced this company; here is the screen" is a normal professional record, not an accusation.
- **Ask to see the credit and allocation inputs before they circulate.** "Could I review how my contribution is recorded before the memo goes to committee?" Lawful, polite and hard to refuse without explanation.
- **Seek a second reader.** A mentor outside the line or a finance or HR contact can read the sheet without the same frame.
- **Keep a one-page timeline.** Dates, targets, definition changes, ratings.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A target or definition changes without written notice; your sourcing work is credited elsewhere in a draft memo or announcement; a channel message assigns you responsibility for a decision you did not make; a request for the written standard goes unanswered.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A signed or circulated allocation, memo or review omits your work despite written evidence you supplied; ratings or access fall after you asked a fair question; several colleagues describe the same sequence; a carry, bonus or promotion decision turns on the disputed record. Consider a qualified employment or contract lawyer, an independent senior person, the firm's compliance contact or HR where they are independent of those involved, and your own written agreements on participation, which need qualified review.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to your career or references if you pursue the matter, intimidation, pressure to sign a release, or statements of self-harm by you or anyone involved. Prioritize safety, contact local emergency or crisis services, and seek counsel. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you confirm in writing how an actionable opportunity is defined for this review period?"
- "To confirm what we agreed today: the target is X, measured by Y. Please tell me if I have that wrong."
- "I sourced this company in March, and the screen is attached. Could you help me understand how origination is recorded on the allocation sheet?"
- "I would like to see how my contribution is described before the memo goes to committee."
- "Two things have been asked that I cannot do at the same time. Which should take priority?"
