# C091: Social Puppeteering — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that you cannot control what others hear about you, and a wariness of kind-sounding concern, since warnings once reached you through friends carrying someone else's message.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of wondering who is really behind a friend's "worried" comment, and quietly pulling back from them.
- A pull to explain yourself to everyone in advance, because stories about you always seem to arrive first.
- An automatic thought, "I can't prove where it came from," that stops you from naming the pattern you see.

**A point in a life.** The prime of an executive career.

**Where it happens.** A fund-owned manufacturing company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a vice president of operations at a private equity backed company, you are forty, and you have run the plants for six years through a sale, a refinancing and one hard winter. You report to the chief executive. The fund that owns the company sends an operating partner to the board and to the monthly operating review, and a chief of staff sits between that partner and you. She is friendly. She is the one who sends the notes. After each review she circulates a "meeting digest" to the executive team, the operating partner and two portfolio contacts at the fund: two tidy pages headed "Key points and next steps." Nobody argues with a digest.

**March, your office**

Last month, in the review, you said the new maintenance schedule is aggressive and that you would want one more quarter of data before committing. You said it plainly and politely. The digest recorded: "Operations expressed reservations about the schedule and requested additional time." It was accurate, and also slightly different.

Now the March digest is open on your screen, at your desk, with the afternoon light on the keyboard. One line reads: "VP Ops indicated unease about team capacity." You did not say that. You write to her. She answers in four minutes. "Oh, I may have summarized. Happy to adjust." She does not adjust it. Your face goes hot, and you decide it is not worth a second email.

**April, the plant office**

In one week, three colleagues ask, independently, whether you are "doing okay." The chief executive, in your doorway with a coffee, says "I've been hearing" and leaves it there. A plant manager says he thought you looked tired in the recording of the review. A man from the fund, on a call, mentions that he has "a good coach if you ever want one." None of them seems to know they are carrying anything.

The chief financial officer comes in the next morning, closes the door and lowers her voice. She says the digest made her "a little worried." "The chief of staff mentioned she is worried about you too, so I said I would keep an eye out." She says it as a kindness. You thank her. The door clicks behind her, and you sit with your hands flat on the desk.

**May, your kitchen**

By May the same three phrases keep reaching you. You are "overextended." You are "carrying a lot." You are "someone we need to look after." You hear each phrase come back to you from a different mouth, each a little more tired than the one before. When you asked the plant manager where he heard it, he said everyone was saying it.

On Sunday evening, at the kitchen table, you try to tell your spouse. You list what was said. It is a short list of kind sentences, and none of them is a complaint. Your spouse asks, "But what did anyone actually say?" You answer, "Nobody has said anything about me." You hear it as you say it.

On Monday morning the operating partner calls your cell while you stand at the counter with your keys. He is cordial. He says he is just checking in. You say the digest has it wrong: you said the schedule was aggressive, not that your team lacked capacity. "Of course," he says, kindly. "Anyway, I only heard from a couple of people that you might need more help." You ask who. He says it was nothing formal.

**June, the boardroom**

Outside the boardroom doors the operating partner stops you. "I worry you are taking on too much," he says. "I'd hate for you to burn out. Maybe the new schedule should be someone else's to carry." You say you are fine. He says, "Of course, of course," and looks at you a beat too long. A plan you presented in the winter is revised without you, and the notes call it "supporting Ops."

When you walk in, everyone's voices go soft and careful. A chair is already pulled out for you, and someone asks if you would like water before you have said a word. You have not spoken. You sit, and the meeting opens with a kind look from every side of the table.

**Two years later**

You are the chief operating officer of a smaller company now, and it is a Thursday in October, at a dinner for a friend's fortieth. A woman you have known for years touches your arm and says, "You look like you're doing a lot. Are you okay, really?" She means it. You can see that she means it. You smile, and your first thought is not her face but a name: who sent her.

You step away to the coat rack and stand there a minute. You are already composing the paragraph you will send around on Monday: how busy the quarter has been, what you chose to take on, why it is all fine. You have sent four of those this year. Nobody asked for any of them. You are not sure when you began to hear a question in every kindness.

On the drive home you pass the exit for your old office and keep both hands on the wheel. There was a pattern, you are nearly sure of it. You have drafted the sentence that would name it. But a sentence like that wants a source, and you have none: no email, no line you could quote that anyone would call unkind. You delete the draft and look at the road.

At home you tell your spouse the party was lovely, and you hear how smoothly it comes out. The woman at the party texts a heart. You do not answer. She has been your friend for fifteen years, and you cannot say which of you has moved away.

## The Name

That's **Social Puppeteering**.

## Definition

Social Puppeteering is the sequence where influence is exercised entirely through other people, so that no hand appears on any string, using Triangulation, Flying Monkeys, Proxy Recruitment, Information Cascades, Plausible Deniability, Concern Trolling, and Priming to convert trust that one's own words are being conveyed accurately into the loss of control over one's own message and relationships. It may benefit whoever prefers that a person be seen in a particular light, and it exploits the fact that messages in layered organizations pass through intermediaries who summarize, soften and interpret. It is hard to detect because every handler is sincere and every summary is nearly accurate. In this sector it can cost an executive their credibility with a sponsor, their authority over their own agenda and their ability to be heard as themselves.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Triangulation** — Your words reach the decision-makers only through the chief of staff's digest: it records "concern about team capacity" you never voiced, and when you ask, she says "I may have summarized. Happy to adjust," and does not. This functions by placing someone else's words between yours and the listener. Concern rises when direct contact with the decision-maker becomes rare and the intermediary's version is the one the decision-maker has.

2. **Flying Monkeys** — Others bring you impressions on someone's behalf: the chief executive says "I've been hearing," a plant manager says you looked tired in a recording, and a fund colleague mentions a coach, none seeming to know they carry anything. This functions by moving influence out of sight. Concern rises when people who do not usually discuss your work begin to ask how you are.

3. **Proxy Recruitment** — People enlisted through care: the chief financial officer says the digest made her "a little worried," that the chief of staff is worried about you too, and that she said she would keep an eye out. This functions by making the recruited feel they are helping. Concern rises when sincere colleagues repeat the same worry in similar words.

4. **Information Cascades** — The phrase repeats: you become "overextended," "carrying a lot," "someone we need to look after," each person saying what the last said, a little more tired each time. This functions by turning a single digest line into a widely held view. Concern rises when you can find no original statement, only a chain of people who have heard one.

5. **Plausible Deniability** — No one has said or instructed anything damaging: the chief of staff relays the partner's questions with care, the partner relays the digest with care, and the board relays its care back. This functions by leaving no one responsible. Concern rises when attempts to correct the record are met with warmth and no change.

6. **Concern Trolling** — Worry that questions your capacity: the operating partner says "I hear you are feeling stretched," and answers your correction with "Of course... with so much on your plate." This functions by turning support into evidence of weakness. Concern rises when expressions of care consistently reduce your responsibilities, not increase your resources.

7. **Priming** — Rooms made ready before you arrive: meetings open with a kind look, your plan is revised without you and called "supporting Ops," and a chair and a glass of water wait before you speak. This functions by shaping the meeting before you speak. Concern rises when you are treated as fragile in meetings you have not yet joined.

## Why This Order

**Triangulation** comes first because the whole pattern depends on a channel in which your words are not heard directly. A chief of staff relaying notes is an ordinary part of the structure, and that is exactly why it is hard to question. It has to come before the colleagues who start checking on you, because they can only repeat a worry that the digest has already put in writing under your name.

> You can only talk to your landlord through the letting agent. What you say arrives in the agent's words, and the answer comes back in them too. Flip it: if the letting agent's staff kept asking whether you were coping before any message had passed to the landlord, you would ask the agent straight out what had been said.

**Flying Monkeys** follow because a channel with only one voice is easy to spot. When several others begin to relay, the message appears independent. It has to come before the chief financial officer's offer to keep an eye out, because she volunteers only once several people have been asking after you.

> Three different friends ask you, in the same week, whether you are all right. None says why. You start to wonder what they know. Flip it: if you overheard someone asking one friend to "check on you" before any of them had asked after you, you would ask that friend what the worry was.

**Proxy Recruitment** explains why they do it: they have been enlisted through care, so none experiences themselves as taking part in anything. It has to come before the phrases returning from different mouths, because each phrase starts with someone, like the plant manager or the chief financial officer, who believes they are helping.

> A neighbor says, "I'm just worried about her, could you look in?" You say yes because you care. You never think of yourself as having been sent. Flip it: if word that she was struggling was already being repeated up and down the street before your neighbor asked you anything, you would ask her yourself how she was.

**Information Cascades** turn the first digest line into a shared belief. By the third retelling, no one remembers where it began. This is the stage where the pattern most resembles ordinary organizational life, since summaries do drift and colleagues do notice stress. It has to come before the operating partner's "nothing formal," because by then the phrases come from so many people that he can truthfully say he only heard it around.

> Someone says the bakery is closing. The next person says it as fact, and the third adds a date. Nobody has seen a sign. Flip it: if the first person to say the bakery was closing told you "oh, I only heard it somewhere," you would go and ask the baker.

**Plausible Deniability** sits here because the cascade has made responsibility diffuse enough that nobody can be held accountable. It has to come before his remark about you taking on too much, because a worry sounds kind only after he has made clear that no one in particular told him anything.

> A message passes from person to person, each adding only a kind word. When it reaches you, there is no one to ask, "Why did you say that?" Flip it: if the first person in the chain had told you to your face that they doubted you, before any kind words got passed along, you would ask what they had seen.

**Concern Trolling** then gives the story its moral cover: it is care, so objecting looks ungracious. It has to come before the soft voices in the boardroom, because the chair, the water and the lowered voices only make sense once he has said he fears you are doing too much.

> A friend says, "I worry you're taking on too much with the new job." It sounds like care, and it also quietly says you can't cope. Flip it: if your friend was already treating you gently, like glass, before saying one word about your new job, you would ask your friend what was going on.

**Priming** completes the work by making every new room a rehearsal of the same impression. It has to come last, because the soft voices and the pulled-out chair in the boardroom only land on someone who has already been told, kindly, that they are taking on too much.

> You walk into a room and everyone's voices go soft and careful. You have not said a word, and you are already being treated as fragile. Flip it: if the room went soft and careful around you first and only afterwards someone said they thought you were overloaded, you would ask them what they had seen.

The conversion point is the moment you start to correct the record and are told, kindly, that you seem stressed. What would weaken the sequence: a direct, regular conversation with the decision-maker; asking for the digest to be circulated for correction before it goes out; and putting your own position in your own words, in writing.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A fund-owned company produces this order without a plan. Three facts about the setting do the work. Information about an executive travels up through intermediaries: a chief of staff writes the notes, a partner reads them, a board hears the partner. A digest is the only record most people ever read, and nobody argues with it. And a small executive team talks constantly, so a phrase heard once is repeated by Friday.

Triangulation comes first because the whole pattern needs a channel in which your words are not heard directly. A note-taker between you and the decision-maker is an ordinary part of the structure, and that is why it is hard to question.

Flying Monkeys come second because one voice on one channel is easy to spot. When several colleagues ask the same question in the same week, the message looks independent, though all of them are drawing on the same two pages.

Proxy Recruitment comes third because people who are asked to look out for a colleague take the job as care. Nobody who agrees experiences themselves as taking part in anything, and the chief financial officer says yes the way anyone would.

Information Cascades come fourth because a phrase that is safer to repeat than to question spreads on its own. By the third retelling no one remembers where it began, and summaries do drift while colleagues do notice stress.

Plausible Deniability comes fifth because by now responsibility is spread across enough people that a question about origin has no address. "A couple of people" and "nothing formal" are the honest answer.

Concern Trolling comes sixth because worry gives the story moral cover. Objecting to care looks ungracious, and a manager who hears "I worry you are doing too much" has few ways to answer that do not prove the point.

Priming comes last because a story told in every room is finally told by the rooms themselves: a pulled-out chair, a lowered voice, a glass of water offered before you speak. Each new meeting rehearses the same impression.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs. A company that routes everything through summaries and kind intermediaries ends with a story no one owns, and it turns the person at the center into someone who rehearses every sentence before saying it.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A reporting line that ran through summaries, and a team that checks on one another out of habit, did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a peer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Uriel Rasmussen, a regional operations manager in her second year, said in a staff meeting that the rollout date was too early. The meeting notes, written by Gideon Norley, the director's deputy, said she "was anxious about the timeline." When she asked him to correct them, he said he would, and did not. Within a week three colleagues she barely worked with stopped her in corridors to ask if she was all right. A friend in finance said, "Gideon mentioned he's worried about you, so I told him I'd look out for you." Soon the same words came to her from different mouths: "stretched," "a lot on her plate." When she asked her director where it came from, he said he had only heard it around, and it was nothing official. At her next review the director said, "I worry you're taking too much on. Maybe hand the rollout to Gideon." The following week, when she walked into the planning room, voices dropped, and someone pulled out a chair for her and offered water. Gideon was never unsure what he was doing. He told a friend that Uriel would be on leave by fall.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to feel watched through warmth, and to find it difficult to object to kindness. Over time, the person may start rehearsing how they will sound, and may soften their positions to avoid being read as stressed.

They may become hesitant to speak in meetings. They may find they are managing everyone's impression of their health. They may also lose faith in colleagues who seem sincere, and then feel guilty for doubting them.

## Institutional Echo

In investor-backed companies, communication between a fund, its operating partner, the executive team and the board often runs through chiefs of staff, digests and portfolio contacts. A digest written by one person, and circulated without review by those it describes, can become the record on which others base impressions.

Where a fund's representatives also have influence over compensation, role and replacement, a persistent impression of an executive may carry consequences. Whether a given summary is accurate, or whether any statement creates liability, depends on the facts and jurisdiction. The honest reading is that chiefs of staff summarize imperfectly, and that colleagues often express genuine concern about stress. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether summaries consistently drift in one direction, whether corrections are made, and whether the same phrases appear across sources.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that good leaders are surrounded by people who look out for them, and that care cannot be a form of control. Alongside it sits the belief that sophisticated people communicate through trusted intermediaries.

The social value being exploited is genuine concern for colleagues. The language that makes it sound normal includes "just making sure you're supported," "I've been hearing," and "we are all behind you." The public story is a supportive investor and a caring team. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is an executive who is gradually described out of authority.

## Observable Indicators

1. Summaries or digests record your remarks in slightly different words from what you said.
2. Corrections are accepted warmly but not made.
3. Several people ask whether you are all right in a short period, using similar phrases.
4. A decision-maker refers to what he has "heard" rather than what you told him.
5. The source of a concern is described as informal or unnamed.
6. Offers of help, such as a coach, arrive when you have not asked.
7. Your plans are revised without you and described as support.
8. Meetings begin with an unusual degree of sympathy or caution.
9. Direct access to a decision-maker is replaced by access through an intermediary.
10. The phrases used about you become more general over time.
11. Responsibility for the digest or the concern cannot be placed with any one person.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every digest and summary, with the date, author, distribution list and your remarks as you recall them.
- A dated, factual note of what you said in each meeting, written the same day, to compare with later summaries.
- Written corrections you asked for, and the replies.
- Notes of each conversation in which someone asked how you were, with date, words, and who raised it.
- The reporting lines, distribution lists and communication protocols between the fund, the board and the executive team.
- Any change to your authority or plans that followed a digest, with dates.
- Names of anyone who heard the same phrases.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Chiefs of staff condense hundreds of words into a few lines, and some drift is inevitable. Colleagues often notice stress and care. A fund's representatives may legitimately ask about workload and offer coaching.

Specific lookalikes include:

- **Honest summarizing.** Errors are random, and corrected when raised.
- **Sincere concern.** One person asks once, and respects your answer.
- **Standard coaching offers.** Offered to several executives, with consent.
- **Normal channel use.** A relay exists for logistics, and direct contact remains open.

Concern strengthens when: summaries drift consistently toward making you sound strained; corrections are acknowledged but not made; several people use the same phrases about you; and direct access to decision-makers narrows.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Digest Test:**
Ask: "Does the summary say what I said, or something close to it?"
Concern rises if: it shifts, in a consistent direction, toward strain or reservation.

**SPOT IT — The Correction Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I ask for a correction?"
Concern rises if: I am thanked, and nothing changes.

**SPOT IT — The Chorus Test:**
Ask: "Are different people using the same words about me?"
Concern rises if: yes, and none can say where they heard them.

**SPOT IT — The Direct Line Test:**
Ask: "Can I still reach the decision-maker without an intermediary?"
Concern rises if: the relay has become the only route.

**SPOT IT — The Support Test:**
Ask: "Does the care I'm offered increase my resources or reduce my role?"
Concern rises if: it reduces my role.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write down what you said** in each meeting the same day, and compare it with the digest.
- **Send a short written correction** the same day: "A small clarification on the digest: my point was that the schedule is aggressive and I would like one more quarter of data."
- **Ask for the digest to be circulated for correction** by those it describes before it goes out.
- **Restore direct contact** with the decision-maker: a regular one-to-one, with your own notes.
- **Put your position in your own words**, in a short email to the relevant people.
- **Ask for the source of any concern** that is described as something heard, calmly and in writing.
- **Respond to care with specifics**: "Thank you. I'm fine. I'd value resources on the maintenance schedule, not a change in my role."
- **Do not accuse the intermediaries** or the sponsor of manipulation without evidence. Doing so can create exposure.
- **Consider independent advice**, from your own counsel or a trusted mentor outside the fund, before any change to your role.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Digest lines misstate your remarks; corrections are accepted but not made; several people ask after your wellbeing in the same period.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Your authority, objectives or compensation change on the basis of impressions you cannot see; a decision-maker relies on summaries and declines direct conversation; coaching or performance steps are proposed without a stated reason; or others describe the same pattern. Relevant routes may include your own attorney, an independent director, the company's audit or compliance committee, HR, and an employment regulator where relevant.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, blackmail, stalking, fraud in progress, or self-harm statements by you or anyone involved. Prioritize safety and emergency support, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "A small clarification on the digest: what I said was that the schedule is aggressive, and I'd like another quarter of data."
- "Could the digest be shared with attendees for corrections before it goes out?"
- "Thank you for checking in. I'm fine, and I'd value a direct conversation about the schedule."
- "Who raised the concern about my capacity? I would like to respond to it."
- "I'd like a regular one-to-one so you hear my views from me."
- "I'd appreciate help with the plants, not a change to my scope."
- "Could we agree that anything about my work is raised with me first?"
