# CAT06: Social Warfare Composites — Corporate & Consulting

## Category Introduction

Everything before this category happened between you and one person: an advisor, an investor, a senior partner. This is the phase where the group gets involved. It tends to start the moment you do something independent. You ask for the cap table. You decline the exclusivity clause. You mention a second advisor, or hint that you might not renew. Somewhere in the next few weeks the temperature changes, and not in a conversation you were part of. An introduction goes unanswered. You're dropped from a thread. A board observer asks, in a careful voice, whether "everything is okay with you and the team."

In this sector, reputation is the currency that moves capital, clients and references, and it travels through a small number of channels: the mutual friend who made the introduction, the investor group chat, the partner network, the reference call, the conference dinner. Most of that traffic is invisible to the person it describes. That is what makes the category so effective. You rarely see the sentence that is said about you. You see the silence that follows it. The pattern shows up as a hub person who holds most of the relationships, a story about you that reaches you third-hand, and a request, polite and warm, to "make sure we're all aligned" before anything is put in writing. Observable behavior here is mostly absence: fewer copies, fewer invitations, answers that now come through someone else.

The sector disguises all of this as professionalism and discretion. It sounds like protecting the founder's reputation, managing the investor narrative, keeping the family quiet, or "handling it in private like real executives do." People who raise concerns about fees, scope or equity are called difficult, not a team player, or not the type who litigates, and those labels carry weight because a mutual friend vouched for both sides and everyone fears burning that trust. Many of these moments have ordinary explanations. Investors really do talk to each other, partners really do protect client confidences, and a reference call can be honest and fair. Concern rises when the shift follows an act of independence, when the claims about what "everyone thinks" cannot be traced to anyone who will say it, and when you are asked to declare loyalty, publicly or quickly, before you have seen anything in writing.

What protects you here is structural, not heroic. Keep relationships that do not run through the hub: your own counsel, a peer group, a co-investor you speak to directly, the introducer you check in with yourself. Verify "what everyone thinks" by asking the specific people, one at a time, in writing where you can. Decline to take sides on a deadline someone else set. Keep a dated record of who said what to whom and how you learned it, separate from what you send. The fourteen composites in this category cover isolation, reputation attacks, proxies, triangulation, manufactured consensus and replacement. Read each one for the sequence, not for a verdict on any person, because intent would require additional evidence that this sector's paper trail can often supply, if you have kept it.
