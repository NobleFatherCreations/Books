# C008: Bait and Switch — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that once you have put money, time or hope into something you must stay until it pays off, and a habit of trusting the promise over what you can see.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A pull toward offers that sound almost perfect, and a reluctance to look closely in case the shine fades.
- A habit of agreeing to one small extra step, then another, long after the picture stopped matching what you were told.
- An automatic sentence, "I've come too far to quit now," whenever you count what is actually being delivered.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, a decade into building.

**Where it happens.** A founder's family office with a long boardroom table.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a founder with a real portfolio, and a family office that you built from nothing over a decade. Not a vanity project: real holdings, real reporting lines, a boardroom with a long table that you had to grow into. What you do not have is a chief operating officer, and you have said so aloud, more than once, to the friend who has watched you carry it.

That friend vouches for an adviser. "Very structured. He'll bring the discipline you keep saying you want."

**A Wednesday in March, your boardroom**

The adviser comes to your boardroom on a Wednesday as a structured operator, in a pressed jacket, with a sleeve of slides: a quarterly roadmap, a risk framework, an investor coordination plan, a capital deployment oversight model. His credentials are real enough to pass a search. He says "execution" the way you say "legacy." Your shoulders ease, because here is the discipline you keep asking for.

He stands at the end of the long table and describes your office as it could be in eighteen months: the roadmap running, the framework in use, the investors coordinated, you at this table with time to think. The picture is so close to what you've imagined that you catch yourself nodding before he has finished the sentence. When you ask, afterwards, when the roadmap will exist, he smiles and says the schedule is a formality, "we'll work in an agile way, the roadmap will emerge." It sounds reasonable. It is also what you hoped. You sign on the Friday, at the same table.

· · ·

The first week he calls every day, three-hour sessions, ideas flowing, and you take them in the boardroom with the door shut. You think: this is what I've been missing. In the second week the calls continue, but something shifts. He starts talking about a digital training platform he has been developing. He wonders if your team could pilot a module. He floats how it might fit your brand. In week three he sends an invoice for the discovery phase and a note: "Great progress on alignment."

**April, the long table, week five**

By the fifth week you lay the pitch deck beside the letter and the letter beside what you have received. Three piles on the polished wood. There is no roadmap. There is no risk assessment. No investor targets, no pipeline, no quarterly plan. There are notes from a dozen calls, a good number of which were about the platform.

You ring him from the head of the table. He is warm and unbothered. The brainstorming is the strategy work, he says. The conversations are the roadmap. The platform is a monetization lever. Each time you reach for the original promise, the promise has been defined again, and your own words come out thinner each time.

So you read the letter as it is, line by line, a pen in your hand. A fixed monthly retainer, a revenue share on transactions, a performance bonus linked to capital raised, and a term that renews automatically unless written notice is given sixty days before it ends. Page six has a milestone schedule: a "discovery and alignment phase," a "framework development phase," a "knowledge transfer phase" and an "ongoing advisory phase." No roadmap is dated. No risk assessment is named. The pitch described a chief operating officer. The schedule describes a series of conversations, and a notice window you had to find in a clause.

· · ·

You tell him, on the next call, that you are thinking of ending it. He is gentle. "We're five weeks in. The retainer is paid, your people have already moved onto my calendar. It would be a shame to start from zero now." You do the sum on the back of page six: the invoices, the two people whose responsibilities you restructured around his calendar, what you told your board and your network when you said you finally had a partner. Ending it would mean explaining that your judgment, the one thing you built the portfolio on, misread him. Continuing is cheaper this month.

You put the letter in the drawer beside your chair.

**Two years later**

Two years later the term has renewed twice, both times inside a window you meant to put in the diary. The platform module is running for your team. The roadmap is still described as emerging.

In spring a software firm gives you a demonstration so smooth that you feel your chest lift. Your finance lead suggests calling two of their existing clients first. You don't. You tell yourself you can always check later, and sign before the lift wears off.

The builder doing your kitchen at home asks for one small change to the plan, then another, then a third that moves the finish date by a month. Each one sounds fair. You agree to each before you have looked at the drawing.

On a Friday your assistant leaves a single sheet on the long table: two years of invoices on the left, everything delivered on the right. The right-hand column is short. You read it, fold it in half, and say aloud to the empty room, "We're nearly there."

You have paid for two years of conversations. What it cost you most is your first read of a person, the one you used to trust.

## The Name

That's **Bait and Switch**.

## Definition

Bait and Switch is the sequence where what is delivered bears little resemblance to what was represented, but the substitution is staged so that by the time the gap is visible, leaving costs more than staying — using Catfishing, Future Faking, Moving Goalposts, Commitment Traps and Sunk Cost Exploitation to convert trust in stated representations into money, time and opportunity committed before the truth was visible. In consulting it concentrates in engagement letters where the pitch describes execution and the milestone schedule describes process. It can benefit the advisor, it exploits the trust of busy principals, and it is hard to detect because every element can have an innocent explanation: agile scope, discovery phases and emergent roadmaps are real. What may be lost is retainer money, months of management attention and the chance to hire someone else. Intent would require additional evidence; a poor engagement can come from overreach or changed circumstances.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Catfishing** — A presented identity that is not what turns up: a structured operator in your boardroom with a sleeve of slides, a roadmap, a risk framework and investor coordination, who says "execution." This functions by creating a picture of capability that the paper does not require him to be. Concern rises when the capabilities pitched are not tested through examples, references or sample work.

2. **Future Faking** — A described future: your office in eighteen months with the roadmap running, the framework in use and investors coordinated, and "the roadmap will emerge" when you ask for dates. This functions by selling outcomes at the moment of maximum imagination and minimum specification. Concern rises when the pitch lists deliverables that the engagement letter does not name or date.

3. **Moving Goalposts** — Redefinition of what was promised: brainstorming is the strategy work, conversations are the roadmap, and a training platform is "a monetization lever." This functions by changing the meaning of the commitment each time you reach for it, so that nothing is ever clearly missed. Concern rises when the description of the same deliverable changes between pitch, letter and status calls.

4. **Commitment Traps** — Terms that make leaving costly: a fixed retainer, a revenue share, a bonus tied to capital raised, a sixty-day renewal notice and a milestone schedule counting conversations as delivery. This functions by ensuring that exposure grows faster than information. Concern rises when payment is tied to time rather than delivery and when the notice mechanism is narrow.

5. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — Costs counted aloud: "We're five weeks in. The retainer is paid, your people have already moved onto my calendar," and you tally invoices, restructured roles and what you told your board. This functions by turning the principal's own investment of money, attention and reputation into the mechanism that keeps the arrangement alive. Concern rises when each month of discussion makes it harder to ask for a stop.

## Why This Order

**Catfishing** comes first because the picture of the advisor must be established before the terms are read. People read a contract as a description of the person they have just met. If the person seemed to be a chief operating officer, the schedule is read as a COO's plan. It has to come first, because "the roadmap will emerge" only sounds like an operator's method once the pressed jacket and the sleeve of slides have made him look like one.

> Imagine hiring a tradesperson who arrives in a smart van with a confident manner. You read their quote as the work of a professional, and you read it kindly. Flip it: if the tradesperson's quote said "we'll sort the details out later" before you had seen any smart van or confident manner, you would read the quote closely and ask for dates.

**Future Faking** is the temporal condition that makes the first step safe. Nothing has to be false at signature: the roadmap really is intended, in some form. This is why intent is so hard to establish. A future is described, not delivered, and a described future cannot be breached. It has to come before the redefinitions in week five, because "the conversations are the roadmap" needs a roadmap that was only ever promised, never dated, before it can bend.

> A friend says, "We'll definitely sort out your garden next spring." You can't hold them to it today, and you can't object to it either. Flip it: if the friend's plans for your garden kept shrinking, from planting to weeding to one pot, before they ever said "next spring", you would see the shrinking and stop waiting for spring.

**Moving Goalposts** is the operative mechanism. Where the pitch said "roadmap" and the schedule says "framework development," the vocabulary is already soft enough to be reinterpreted. Each reinterpretation is plausible alone; together they empty the original promise. It has to come before the renewal clause and the notice window, because those terms only hold you once the deliverables have been redefined until a breach is hard to show.

> You ask your flatmate to "tidy the kitchen." First it means the dishes, then it means only the sink, then "I wiped a counter." Each answer sounds fair, and the kitchen is still not tidy. Flip it: if the flatmate first had you agree to a year-long kitchen rota you could not leave, before "tidy" started shrinking, you would refuse the rota and ask what tidy means.

**Commitment Traps** then convert disappointment into captivity: the retainer is guaranteed, the notice window is narrow, and because the deliverables have been redefined, a claim of breach is hard to prove. It has to come before "We're five weeks in", because that appeal only weighs on you once the paid retainer and the renewal clause make leaving look costly and slow.

> Think of a gym membership that renews itself unless you cancel in a thirty-day window you did not know about. You are unhappy, and you are also tied in. Flip it: if the gym told you "you've already paid for months, don't waste it" before you knew of any renewal window, you would notice you were not going, and cancel while it was easy.

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** closes the loop and provides the deniability. By the time the gap is visible, you have paid, restructured, announced and invested. Stopping feels like confessing a mistake in judgment, and most people choose to wait. It has to come last, because "a shame to start from zero" only works once the renewal clause and the paid retainer have already made walking away feel expensive.

> You have been forty minutes in a queue when someone says, "You've waited this long." The line has not moved, and leaving now feels like wasting the forty minutes. Flip it: if someone said "you've waited this long" in your first minute in the queue, before any rule that stepping out loses your place, you would laugh, step out and come back later.

What weakens the sequence is anything that fixes the promise in time: a dated, named deliverable list in the letter, payment linked to acceptance, a short initial term, and a second reader before signature. The key conversion point is the moment you sign a milestone schedule that does not match the pitch because the person you trust vouched for him.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A founder's family office has three structural facts behind this. There is no chief operating officer, so the founder approves every engagement alone and is actively looking for someone to fill the gap. Advisers reach offices like this through trusted referrals, which vouch for a person rather than a contract. And advisory letters in this market are built from retainers, revenue shares, phase-named milestones and renewal clauses that run on unless someone gives notice.

Catfishing comes first because the referral sells a person, not a document. The founder meets the pitch, the jacket and the slides before the paper, and whoever appears to be a structured operator sets the lens through which the letter will be read.

Future Faking comes second because advisory work is sold before it exists. A roadmap is genuinely intended at signature in some form, and "agile" and "emerging" are normal words in the trade. A future described in good faith and a future never meant look the same on day one.

Moving Goalposts comes third because the letter names phases, not products. "Framework development" and "knowledge transfer" can cover almost anything, so when the work drifts toward the adviser's own platform, each new definition still fits inside the old words.

Commitment Traps come fourth because the letter is standard for its market. Automatic renewal, a guaranteed retainer and a notice window on a later page are ordinary terms, and once the deliverables have been redefined there is nothing clear left to call a breach.

Sunk Cost Exploitation comes last because by week five the founder has paid, restructured two people's jobs and told a board. Every one of those is visible to others, and ending the engagement would mean naming the misjudgement out loud. Waiting is the quieter choice the structure offers.

Put it together and the order is what a referral-led, phase-scoped, auto-renewing engagement produces on its own: a picture first, a promise next, then soft words, firm terms and a cost of leaving. It turns a founder who wanted an operator into a client who keeps paying rather than admit the operator never arrived.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A referral, a standard letter and a founder approving alone did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to an operations director. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ruth Calder ran operations for a mid-sized distribution company and had been asking for a scheduling system for years. Simon Pell's firm pitched it beautifully: a deck of case studies, a team of named specialists with photographs, and Simon himself, calm and exact. He told her she would have a working system by the summer, and that the detailed plan would take shape once they started. By summer, "working system" had become "a pilot," and then "a proof of concept," and each time she asked, the new word sounded reasonable. None of the specialists in the photographs ever joined a call. When she read the contract again, she found a three-year minimum term and an exit fee on a page she had not been shown in the pitch. When she told Simon she was thinking of stopping, he reminded her, kindly, that half her data had already been moved and two of her supervisors retrained; stopping now would waste all of it. She renewed the next quarter.

Simon was never confused about what he was doing. He told a colleague afterward that none of the specialists in that deck had ever worked on one of his projects.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may find themselves unable to prove what was promised, because the pitch was spoken and the letter says something different. Over time, they may start to re-narrate the engagement as a long discovery process, since that is easier than holding the gap. They may keep paying while hoping the next phase brings the work. They may feel embarrassed in front of a friend who vouched for the advisor and reluctant to raise it with the board. They may also doubt their own reading of the letter, since it described the work in its own words.

## Institutional Echo

Consulting engagements can reproduce this shape structurally. Proposals that describe outcomes in the sales stage while the contract describes phases and activities create a gap between the pitch and the paper. Fixed retainers, auto-renewal clauses and short notice windows can shift the cost of ending an engagement onto the client. Discovery or alignment phases that expand can substitute for the named deliverables. Where the person who pitches is not the person who delivers, the representation may be hard to attach to anyone. The honest reading is often benign: strategy work is genuinely iterative, scope can truly evolve, and early phases are real. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same gap between pitch and schedule recurs across clients, and whether the advisor's own projects take priority over the client's.

## Civilizational Echo

The concealing myth is that the relationship is the deliverable, and that a good advisor works in an agile, emergent way that cannot be pinned to a schedule. Beside it sit the beliefs that sophisticated principals do not need to haggle over milestones, and that a vouching friend settles the question. The value being exploited is real: trust in referral, and the wish for structure from someone who can supply it. The public story is a partnership built on a shared vision. The private cost, where the pattern is present, is a retainer paid for a process.

## Observable Indicators

1. The pitch lists deliverables that the engagement letter does not name.
2. A milestone schedule describes phases and activities rather than outputs with dates.
3. A guaranteed retainer is not tied to acceptance of deliverables.
4. Auto-renewal with a narrow notice window.
5. Revenue share or bonus attributed by the advisor's own account.
6. Early calls turn to the advisor's own projects or products.
7. The description of a deliverable changes between pitch, letter and status calls.
8. Status reports describe activity rather than outputs.
9. Invoices are issued for phases without an acceptance step.
10. The advisor describes the letter as a formality or the schedule as flexible.
11. Questions about deliverables are met with warmth but no dated answer.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The pitch deck, slides and any presentation notes, with the date presented.
- The engagement letter in all drafts, with the milestone schedule and the notice and renewal clauses.
- A side-by-side list of each pitched deliverable against the contract language and against what was received.
- Status calls and notes with dates, including the topics discussed.
- Every invoice, with the phase named and the acceptance, if any.
- Messages where the advisor redefines a deliverable or calls the schedule a formality.
- Messages involving the advisor's own products, platforms or projects.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Real consulting work is often iterative. Discovery phases do matter, scope genuinely evolves, and an engagement can be legitimately re-planned by agreement.

The discriminator is **direction and documentation**. Genuine change is agreed in writing, is bidirectional, and keeps named outputs. This pattern redefines the promise orally, moves consistently downward, and keeps the fee fixed.

Other lookalikes:

- **Agile engagements.** The roadmap may legitimately emerge from discovery.
- **Real change orders.** Scope may change in writing by agreement.
- **Overstretched advisors.** A poor engagement can come from capacity or inexperience.
- **Fair milestone language.** "Framework development" can describe real work.

Concern strengthens when: the same pitched deliverables are never named in any later document; the advisor's own project replaces the client's work; requests for dated outputs are answered with phrases like "it will emerge."

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Side-by-Side Test:**
Ask: "If I lay the pitch next to the milestone schedule, do they list the same outputs?"
Concern rises if: the pitch describes a roadmap and the schedule describes meetings.

**SPOT IT — The Output Test:**
Ask: "What can I hold in my hand from the last invoice?"
Concern rises if: the answer is notes from calls and no dated output.

**SPOT IT — The Redefinition Test:**
Ask: "Has any deliverable been described in a new way when I asked about it?"
Concern rises if: brainstorming has become strategy and conversations have become the roadmap.

**SPOT IT — The Notice Test:**
Ask: "When must I give written notice, and who would have to cooperate?"
Concern rises if: you can't find the clause or the window is already closing.

## Chain Breakers

- **Compare the pitch to the paper before you sign.** List each promised output, find it in the letter, and ask for what is missing in writing.
- **Tie money to acceptance.** Ask for payment on delivery of named outputs, or a short first term with a review.
- **Date the deliverables.** "Could the schedule list the roadmap and risk assessment with dates?"
- **Calendar the notice date.** Put the renewal window and notice date in your diary on day one.
- **Send a written summary after each call.** "To confirm what we agreed: X will be delivered by Y." Note the response.
- **Raise gaps in writing, early and calmly.** "Noting the roadmap has not yet been delivered. Could you confirm the date?"
- **Take the contract to your own counsel before renewal.** And speak to the friend who vouched, so that the introduction is not the end of the checking.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** The pitch and the schedule list different outputs; no dated deliverable has appeared; status calls turn to the advisor's own projects.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Material fees have been paid with no named deliverables received; the renewal or notice window is closing; the advisor disputes what was promised; revenue-share or bonus claims are attributed without records. Relevant routes may include a contract attorney, a forensic accountant for fees and attribution, mediation or arbitration where the contract provides it, and, where the advisor is licensed, the relevant licensing body.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, blackmail or extortion; harassment or stalking; fraud, including misuse of your accounts or authority; any self-harm statement by anyone involved (contact local emergency or crisis services immediately). Prioritize safety, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "The milestone schedule lists phases. Could it also list the roadmap and risk assessment with dates?"
- "To confirm what we agreed today: the roadmap will be delivered by the end of the month. Is that right?"
- "I'd like payment tied to acceptance of each deliverable. Can we amend the letter?"
- "Can you send me an example of a comparable roadmap with details removed?"
- "I've noted the renewal date. Could you confirm in writing when notice would need to be given?"
- "I'd like my counsel to review this before we renew."
