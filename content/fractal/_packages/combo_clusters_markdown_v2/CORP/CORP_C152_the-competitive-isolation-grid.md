# C152: The Competitive Isolation Grid — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that there is never enough recognition to share, and a reflex to treat the people beside you as rivals, checking with one central person before speaking to them.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of keeping what you know to yourself, in case sharing it helps someone get ahead of you.
- An automatic thought when peers gather without you, "they must be talking about me," instead of joining them.
- A hesitation to compare notes with colleagues or friends, and surprise when someone simply offers to help.

**A point in a life.** Midcareer, working for several companies at once.

**Where it happens.** A venture fund's portfolio program.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a fractional CMO, you're forty-three, and you are one of four fractional executives in an operating partner's portfolio program, which means you share a logo, a team chat workspace you barely use, and a vague sense that the three other people are your rivals.

You joined with enthusiasm. The operating partner, a warm and fast-moving person who remembers your children's names, said on your first call: "I curate a small bench. You're one of four. I only work with people I really believe in." The talent pool sits on a page in the fund's internal portal, with headshots. Yours is in the second row. It's a good arrangement. Portfolio companies need what you do and cannot afford a full-time hire, and the operating partner makes the introductions. Each placement comes with a standard fee and, at the end of the year, a share of a "bench bonus pool" for those who "delivered the most value to the portfolio."

**The early months, your home office**

You do what any sensible person does. You ask the others for a call. The partner says, "Of course, but let's do it when I can be on it, so I can help you all see the picture." The call is rescheduled twice. You message one of the others, a CFO whose headshot is in the top row, and get a friendly reply and nothing after. When you do speak, it is mostly to him, over video, with the dog asleep under your desk. He mentions what one colleague said about your last launch plan, "just so you know," and asks you not to bring it up.

He tells you privately that a colleague is "struggling with the finance side." A portfolio founder asks you for help with a launch, and a week later he mentions that someone else "was very keen." You feel a chill that you cannot name, followed by a surge of determination. You find yourself studying the other headshots for signs of struggle.

**Month six, your home office**

The bonus pool is mentioned often. It is "tight this year." It is "going to be a close call between a couple of you." He says it kindly, like news he wishes were better.

Then a one-page document appears in your folder: the Talent Pool Roster and Bonus Pool Allocation List. Four names. Four columns: Placements, Value Delivered (partner assessment), Collaboration, Allocation. Your Collaboration cell reads "Developing." Nobody else's row is visible to you, because the sheet is set to show only yours. The partner tells you your pipeline looks strong, then adds: "I wouldn't mention it to the others. It might be demotivating."

One of the bench messages you: "How's your pipeline looking? Mine's thin." You write: "Steady, can't complain." The relief when you hit send is physical. That Friday the partner archives the bench channel in the chat workspace. "Let's route everything through me," he writes. "Cleaner that way." You stop asking the others for help. You start watching their output on the portal. You find yourself rehearsing what to say about your own wins.

The week after you keep the pipeline quiet, the partner calls to say a portfolio founder needs help with a launch. "I'm putting you forward for this because I trust you most," he says. An introduction follows, then another, and his voice is warmer than it was when you asked for a call. Your shoulders drop an inch.

**December, the annual dinner**

The tablecloth is white and the candle is down to a stub. At the annual dinner the four of you sit together for the first time in months. You discover, quickly and with a pit in your stomach, that the other three have each been told they are the front-runner, that each has a pipeline they were asked to keep quiet, and that each was told, in warm tones, that you were struggling a little. Nobody says anything for a moment. The operating partner arrives with a toast to "the bench." The glasses are raised. You raise yours. You have not called any of them since. You sleep badly, and you check the portal before you check your messages. You wonder how many of your opinions of those three were ever really your own.

**Two years later**

It is a Wednesday in October, and you are at a coworking space where you now rent a desk, and a woman at the next desk, a freelance designer, has noticed you wrestling with a pricing spreadsheet. She says, "I did one of those last month, want to see how I set mine up?" You say, "No, I'm fine, I've got it." She shrugs and goes back to her screen.

Across the room, three people you do not know are laughing with their heads close together in front of a whiteboard. Your pulse picks up, and you are already listening for your own name. You open your notes, which contain the name of a client who would be a good fit for a former colleague from the bench, and you close the file. That one stays with you.

At six she is packing up. She stops, puts a sticky note on your monitor with a phone number on it, and says, "That's my accountant. She's good. Take her." You stare at the number after she leaves. You cannot work out why she would do that, and you keep waiting for the catch. The note is still on your monitor on Friday, and on the Friday after that. You have not called the number.

## The Name

That's **The Competitive Isolation Grid**.

## Definition

The Competitive Isolation Grid is the sequence where potential allies are turned into competitors by routing every relationship, piece of information and signal of approval through one central person, so that solidarity becomes structurally difficult. It can use Triangulation, Comparative Devaluation, Manufactured Scarcity, Information Control, Isolation and Conditional Love to convert the need for peer connection and mutual support into the loss of solidarity and direct, uncontrolled relationships with peers. It is hard to detect because each person's experience is private, each conversation sounds supportive, and competition for a limited pool is a real feature of many programs. Intent would require additional evidence: a gatekeeper may simply be overloaded, and scarcity may be genuine.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Triangulation** — The middle seat: the call with the others is "when I can be on it" and rescheduled twice, a peer's friendly reply goes nowhere, and he relays what one colleague said about your launch plan and asks you not to mention it. This functions by making the central person the channel for every relationship. Concern rises when direct contact between peers is delayed, supervised or discouraged.

2. **Comparative Devaluation** — The remarks about the others: he says a colleague is "struggling with the finance side," and a week later that someone else "was very keen" for the launch. This functions by raising each person's standing at someone else's expense, creating anxiety and rivalry. Concern rises when what you hear about a peer cannot be verified and you hear the opposite from them.

3. **Manufactured Scarcity** — The thin pool: the bonus is "tight this year" and "going to be a close call between a couple of you." This functions by creating a zero-sum frame in which helping a peer feels like losing. Concern rises when the size and the rules of the pool are not shown, and the scarcity is mentioned more often than it is measured.

4. **Information Control** — The sheet with one row: a roster and allocation list that shows only your row, with "Developing" in your Collaboration cell, and "I wouldn't mention it to the others. It might be demotivating." This functions by preventing comparison, so each person is unable to test what they've been told. Concern rises when you are asked not to share good news with peers, or when the only view you have of the data is your own row.

5. **Isolation** — The shared channel closes: you answer a peer with "Steady, can't complain," then the partner archives the bench channel, writing "Let's route everything through me. Cleaner that way," and you stop asking the others for help. This functions by removing the people most likely to notice the pattern. Concern rises when you find yourself watching peers' output and not asking for help.

6. **Conditional Love** — The warmth that follows: "I'm putting you forward for this because I trust you most," then introductions and a warmer voice than when you asked for a call. This functions by tying approval to staying inside the grid. Concern rises when your standing changes after you contact peers or ask about the pool.

## Why This Order

**Triangulation** comes first because it builds the structure: if every conversation passes through one person, he controls what each person learns. Before anything is withheld or distorted, a central position has to exist, and a bench of four who speak to each other is not one. It has to come before the private word about a struggling colleague, because he can only tell each of you something about the others once every conversation runs through him.

> Picture a family where every message goes through Mum. "Don't tell your brother I said." The siblings never quite talk. Flip it: if Mum first told you quietly that your brother "is really struggling," before she carried any message between you, you would phone your brother yourself and ask how he is.

**Comparative Devaluation** then puts a charge into the lines. Telling each person that another is struggling makes the others look like rivals and not as peers. It is most effective when it is plausible, small and kind. It has to come before the talk of a tight pool, because a close call among a couple of you only bites once you are already studying the other headshots for signs of struggle.

> A teacher quietly tells you another pupil "is really struggling." You start watching them instead of the work. Flip it: if the teacher first said there is only one place on the team, and only later said the other pupil "is really struggling," you would ask that pupil how they are.

**Manufactured Scarcity** gives the rivalry a reason: if the pool is limited and the others are strong, every success of theirs is a threat to you. This is the step that makes competition feel natural. It has to come before the hidden roster, because a sheet that shows only your row matters once the pool has been called a close call and the others look like rivals.

> Picture two children told there is one cookie left. Suddenly each other's every move matters. Flip it: if someone first told the two children, "Don't tell each other what you plan to do," and only then that one cookie is left, you would break the cookie in half and say so out loud.

**Information Control** stabilizes the arrangement. The hidden roster, the single-row view and the request to keep a pipeline quiet prevent the only act that would break the structure, which is comparison. It has to come before the archived channel, because with only your own row visible and a pipeline you were asked to keep quiet, the bench channel has nothing left to carry.

> A friend says "keep our plans between us, it might upset the others." With nobody's plans in the open, nobody can compare them. Flip it: if your friend first said the others were "a bit odd" and best left alone, and only then said "keep our plans between us," you would ask the others how their plans are going.

**Isolation** is then not an act but an outcome: people stop reaching for each other because it feels risky and pointless. It has to come before the praise that you are trusted most, because he only needs to put you forward as the favorite once you have stopped asking the others for anything.

> Think of a street where everyone has been told the neighbor is "a bit odd." Nobody knocks. Flip it: if someone first said, "You're the one I trust most, as long as you keep this quiet," and only then called the neighbor "a bit odd," you would knock and say hello.

**Conditional Love** closes the loop by rewarding the behavior that keeps it in place: the person who stays discreet is "trusted most." It has to come last, because trust given as warmth only holds while you keep your pipeline quiet and stay away from the others, which the archived channel has already settled.

> Picture the favorite child, "the one I trust most," who has also learned that the warmth stays only while the secrets do. Flip it: if you were the favorite child and first heard "I trust you most, as long as you keep secrets," and only then that your sibling is "a bit odd," you would ask your sibling yourself.

The conversion point is the first time you decline to tell a peer something because the partner asked, and you feel relief. What would weaken the sequence: a direct, peer-to-peer line, a shared sheet of the rules for the pool, and a standing practice of comparing notes, which is exactly what the structure is shaped to prevent.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A portfolio program with four fractional executives produces this order without a plan. Three facts about the setting do the work. Placements come only through one operating partner, so he is the only route to work. A bonus pool is fixed, so what one person receives another does not. And the bench is small and lightly connected, with a chat workspace that nobody feels owns.

Triangulation comes first because a central position has to exist before anything can be withheld or shaped. If the four spoke freely, there would be nothing to hold in the middle, so every call is simply routed through the one person who makes the introductions.

Comparative Devaluation comes second because a person in the middle has to give each of them something about the others. A kind remark that one of them is struggling with the finance side is small, plausible and easy to pass on.

Manufactured Scarcity comes third because a fixed pool turns that remark into a threat. If there is one bonus and the others are strong, their every success becomes a loss, and the rivalry feels natural rather than arranged.

Information Control comes fourth because the one act that would break the arrangement is comparison. A roster that shows only your row and a request to keep a pipeline quiet make comparison impossible.

Isolation comes fifth because by now reaching for a peer feels risky and pointless. Peers who are not talking have no reason to knock, and archiving a chat channel only finishes what the quiet had started. Nobody has to forbid contact.

Conditional Love comes last because the structure is held in place by rewarding the person who stays discreet. "I trust you most" is the warmth that stays only while the secrets do.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a program that presents four people as a bench and pays one of them most. It turns the person in the middle into someone who checks with the partner before speaking to a peer.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A fixed bonus pool, a single route to work, and a lightly connected bench did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a freelancer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Tamsin Okello, one of four freelance analysts that a research director, Barnaby Wenson, kept on call, asked if the four could meet. He said they would, once he could be there. The meeting was moved twice. In private he told her another analyst was "struggling with the modeling." He mentioned that the year-end bonus pool was small and "going to a couple of you." He sent her a one-page summary of how the pool would be divided, showing only her own line, and asked her not to mention her pipeline to the others because it might be demotivating. When a fellow analyst asked how her workload was, she wrote, "Fine, can't complain." The same week Barnaby closed the shared analyst channel, saying everything was "cleaner through him." A few days later he gave her a large client and said, "I trust you most." Barnaby was never unsure what he was doing. He told a friend that none of the four had ever asked each other anything, and that he liked it that way.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may start to monitor peers, rehearse achievements and hold back help. Over time, they may feel alone in a program that markets itself as a community. They may find themselves doubting colleagues they would have trusted, and craving the central person's approval more than is proportionate. They may see their own work only in relative terms. When the structure becomes visible, they may feel foolish, and angry at themselves and at the peers they distanced.

## Institutional Echo

Portfolio talent pools and bench programs are sometimes run by one person who controls introductions, assessments and bonuses. Bonus pools with discretionary allocation and no published criteria can create zero-sum dynamics regardless of intent. Roster tools that show each participant only their own row can protect privacy and also prevent comparison.

The honest reading is that a single curator may be overloaded, that bonus pools are in fact limited, and that privacy about compensation is a common norm. Intent would require additional evidence: whether the different messages to different people were inconsistent, whether the pool rules existed in writing, and whether peers describe the same sequence.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that excellence emerges from competition, and that a good curator knows each person's strengths. The related belief says that peers who compare notes are gossiping, and that discretion is professionalism. Divide-and-rule is an old story, but in modern settings it sounds like "I manage each relationship individually."

The value exploited is the wish for genuine peer connection. The public story is a curated bench with a generous sponsor. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is four capable people who might have been each other's best resource, and instead were each other's audience.

## Observable Indicators

1. Peer contact is routed through, or scheduled only with, the central person.
2. Different people receive different assessments of the same peer.
3. Bonus or recognition pools are described as scarce, without published rules or sizes.
4. You are asked not to share news or pipelines with peers.
5. Rosters or allocation sheets show each person only their own row.
6. Assessment columns, such as "Collaboration," use undefined terms.
7. Introductions are framed as a favor to you over others.
8. Peers who talk directly are described as unhelpful or gossiping.
9. Your standing shifts after you contact peers or ask about the pool.
10. Peers appear to know nothing of what you were told about them.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The roster, bonus pool rules and allocation sheets you are lawfully shown, with dates.
- Any written criteria for the pool, including "value delivered" and "collaboration."
- Messages in which the central person described a peer, or asked you to keep something quiet.
- Dated records of placements, introductions and who made them.
- Records of your attempts to arrange peer calls, and the responses.
- Notes of any comparisons you later make with peers, taken openly and with their agreement.
- Your own placement and fee records.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Bonus pools are often truly limited, curators are often overloaded, and keeping compensation private is a legitimate norm. A bench program can also be competitive without being manipulative.

The discriminator is **whether information and contact are restricted in ways that benefit the center**. A transparent program shares the rules, welcomes peer contact, and tells everyone the same thing. This pattern restricts contact and tells different stories.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Privacy norms.** Compensation may be confidential for reasons that apply to everyone.
- **Curatorial judgment.** Candid assessments may differ because the work differs.
- **Practical scheduling.** The partner may truly be the bottleneck for calls.

Concern strengthens when: different people hear conflicting stories; peers are discouraged from direct contact; and the pool rules are not stated.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Channel Test:**
Ask: "Can I speak to my peers directly, without the central person present?"
Concern rises if: contact is delayed, supervised or discouraged.

**SPOT IT — The Story Test:**
Ask: "Would my peers describe my standing the way I've been told it?"
Concern rises if: they have been told something different.

**SPOT IT — The Pool Test:**
Ask: "Are the pool's size and criteria written somewhere I can see?"
Concern rises if: scarcity is mentioned more often than it is explained.

**SPOT IT — The Secrecy Test:**
Ask: "Have I been asked not to share good news with peers?"
Concern rises if: the request is framed as protecting someone's motivation.

## Chain Breakers

- **Contact peers directly and openly.** "I'd like to set up a call among the four of us, without anyone needing to be present."
- **Ask for the rules in writing:** pool size, criteria, definitions for each assessment column.
- **Compare notes lawfully and with consent,** limited to what each person is comfortable sharing. Do not access sheets or files you are not authorized to see.
- **Ask for the same message in writing** for every participant: "Can you confirm how the pool will be allocated for everyone?"
- **Decline to keep secrets from peers** that are not confidential by contract.
- **Keep your own records** of placements, fees and assessments.
- **Maintain clients and relationships that do not run through the center.**
- **Do not spread speculation about the partner or peers,** which could cause its own problems.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** One peer-contact delay; different remarks about a peer; a pool described as tight without numbers. Ask for the rules.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Material money allocated by undisclosed criteria; conflicting accounts confirmed by peers; reduced access after you ask about the pool or contact peers. Relevant routes may include an independent attorney, the fund's compliance function or an independent partner, a mediator under any agreement, and a regulator if an offering or finder's arrangement is involved.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to your reputation or career, blackmail, intimidation, or anyone describing self-harm. Prioritize safety and emergency support, contact qualified counsel, and do not confront the person or negotiate under threat.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we set up a call among the four of us this month?"
- "Can you share the bonus pool rules and how the 'collaboration' rating is assessed?"
- "I'm happy to keep fee details private, and I'd like to share introductions openly."
- "Could you confirm in writing how the allocation will be decided?"
- "I'd rather not keep my pipeline from the others. Is there a reason it's confidential?"
- "I'd like to see the roster as the others see it."
- "Who decides the final allocation, and is there an appeal?"
