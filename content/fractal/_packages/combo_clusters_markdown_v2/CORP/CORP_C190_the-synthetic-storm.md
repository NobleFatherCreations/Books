# C190: The Synthetic Storm — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that no picture, voice or record can be trusted anymore, and a reflex to give up checking because the flood of versions feels too big to sort.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic shrug at any piece of evidence, "it could be fake anyway," whether it is or not.
- A strong body reaction to a scene that seemed real, and a lingering doubt about where it came from.
- A habit of trusting how something makes you feel more than whether it can be checked.

**A point in a life.** Nine days before a financing closes.

**Where it happens.** A listed services firm.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a head of external affairs, forty-five, at a listed services firm, and in nine days the company closes a refinancing that took fourteen months. You have written the investor materials, briefed the lead banks and kept the story steady through a hard year. Your talent is calm. In a crisis, you're the person who writes the holding statement while everyone else is still shouting.

**Tuesday, 7:40, your office**

At 7:40 on a Tuesday morning your phone fills up and doesn't stop. You're at your desk, the lights on only over your side of the floor, a tea you haven't touched. By 8:15 there are four versions of the same call. One has the chief executive's name instead. One has an extra paragraph. One has a different date and a different set of numbers. Then a post from a stranger claiming to be a former employee. A chart. A "leaked" email thread with the company's logo that you've never seen. Soon there are fourteen messages in one chat from people you cannot identify, each quoting a different item and none quoting the same one. You try to answer each and fall behind. Every denial you write is a new thing to screenshot. Your coffee goes cold beside the keyboard.

Inside the pile is the item that arrived first. A lender contact sent a PDF with the single line, "Is this real?" It's a transcript of an investor call, headed with the company's name and a date you remember. In it the chief financial officer says, in her own cadence, that covenant headroom is "tighter than we told you" and that the board had discussed delaying the refinancing. She didn't say it. You were on that call. At 8:30 the board chair's assistant forwards one page of board minutes, the right typeface and the right footer, recording a vote to "pause the refinancing pending review." No such vote occurred. You drafted the summary for the investor file, and the page in your hands has a line that isn't in yours. Your jaw is tight. You read the line until the words stop being words.

**Tuesday, 10:00, your office**

At ten, IT sends two sentences: the chief financial officer's mailbox was opened overnight from a device nobody recognizes. At 10:05 the treasury team gets an email from her address. Because of the "situation," an urgent payment must be redirected to a new account today, and nobody should discuss it by phone. It carries the right signature block. A treasury analyst calls you instead, because she's been burned by this kind of email before, and asks whether you know anything. You step into the corridor with the phone against your ear and watch the lift numbers climb. Your palms are damp. You've never been more grateful for a boring habit.

**Tuesday, 10:30, the boardroom**

The crisis meeting convenes at 10:30, in the boardroom with the blinds half drawn. Water jugs stand untouched. A phone buzzes face-down on the table and nobody reaches for it. The general counsel says to call the police and the lead bank, then puts on the wall screen the clip that has been moving through two investor chats since nine. It isn't a flat video. It's built as a room, his office at life size, so the wall seems to open onto it. Your chief executive sits at what looks like his own desk, his own window behind him, turns as if to face whoever is watching, and a voice that sounds exactly like his fills the room: "the numbers will not hold through the quarter, and I would not count on the facility." The hair lifts on your arms. Your stomach drops before you've thought anything at all. Colleagues who have worked with him for ten years go still, and someone at the end of the table starts typing a note to pause the refinancing. "That sounds like me," he says. "I don't remember saying it." He says it twice. For a moment even he isn't sure.

**Three weeks later**

Three weeks later the refinancing closes, a day late, and the lead bank's confirmation comes through at four with a signed page attached. Your deputy asks whether you want her to verify it against the original. You tell her not to bother, that one more version won't settle anything, and you go with how the call felt. It's the first time in fifteen years you haven't checked a document before sending it on.

At the closing dinner the chief executive raises a glass and thanks you in front of the table. You smile, and what you hear is the other voice, thanking someone else. That night a voice note arrives from your director. At the first word your pulse jumps, and it keeps pounding through the whole message, which is only about Thursday's schedule. You ring him back on the number you wrote down years ago, and he repeats it, in the same voice, and you still can't unhear the other one. You let calls go to voicemail now, in case the voice isn't the one you think it is. You wake at three and replay the pauses in the clip, then your memory of the call you were on, to be sure she really didn't say it.

For fifteen years you've said "that is false" and been believed. You open a blank page for the holding statement. Your hands know the first line, and you leave the cursor blinking after it.

## The Name

That's **The Synthetic Storm**.

## Definition

The Synthetic Storm is the sequence where a high volume of generated and altered versions of a story drowns out the original, fabricated or edited media puts words in real mouths, records are altered, and an immersive or realistic experience produces a felt reaction before anyone can check it, using Gish Gallop / Firehose of Falsehood, Forged Screenshots / Edited Evidence, Cyber Manipulation Operations (CMOs) and Immersive Manipulation (VR/AR) to convert trust in what you see and hear into a loss of confidence in any evidence. It may benefit anyone who gains from a market move, a payment, a delay or a damaged reputation. It is hard to detect because each item can look plausible, because checking takes longer than sharing, and because the volume makes checking feel pointless. What can be lost is money, a transaction, standing with lenders and investors, and the ability to rely on a recording at all. Whether a given item is fabricated is a question for qualified technical and legal review, not for impression.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Gish Gallop / Firehose of Falsehood** — Many versions and claims at once: four versions of the call, a stranger's post, a chart, a "leaked" thread, and fourteen unidentifiable messages in one chat, each quoting a different item. This functions by making each rebuttal cost more than each claim, so that the original story is buried. Concern rises when the number of versions grows faster than anyone can verify and the versions do not agree with each other.

2. **Forged Screenshots / Edited Evidence** — A transcript and a page of board minutes: each looks right, with the right date, typeface and footer, and each holds a line that was never said and a vote that never happened. This functions by borrowing the authority of an official-looking record. Concern rises when a document has the right layout but no provenance, no metadata you can lawfully check and no matching record on file.

3. **Cyber Manipulation Operations (CMOs)** — Accounts and channels used, with action attached: the chief financial officer's mailbox opened overnight from an unknown device, then an email from her address demanding a payment redirect today and no phone calls. This functions by using the crisis to make an urgent instruction seem more credible. Concern rises when a request for money or access arrives during an information flood, asks for secrecy, and bypasses normal approval. No technical claim is made here about how any clip or message was made, which would need expert verification.

4. **Immersive Manipulation (VR/AR)** — A lifelike room on the wall screen: his office built at life size, the chief executive turning to face the watchers in his exact voice, saying the numbers will not hold, hair lifting, a stomach dropping, someone typing a note. This functions by pushing the person's body to decide before their checking habits start. Concern rises when a realistic presence makes a request that skips the usual verification, and the realism itself is treated as the proof.

## Why This Order

The **firehose** comes first because volume is the precondition for everything else. If there is one false claim, a company can answer it. If there are fourteen by lunchtime, it cannot, and the effort of answering becomes part of the story. The first job of the storm is to make a response feel futile. It has to come before the transcript and the minutes, because only a pile too big to answer leaves room for pages that look exactly like the real records.

> Think of one rumour about you at work. You would find the person and clear it up. Now picture ten different ones landing before lunch. You can't answer ten, and you start to wonder which one people believe. Flip it: first a coworker holds up a printout of a message with your name on it that you never wrote, and you find her and clear it up; only then do ten different rumors land before lunch.

**Forged documents** then supply the specifics that the noise lacks. A transcript is a persuasive form because it looks like the thing itself. An altered minute page is persuasive because the organization does not usually publish minutes, so few outsiders can compare. In both cases, the editing is small and the surrounding material is genuine, which is what gives the fake its weight. It has to come before the break-in to her mailbox, because the false transcript and minutes already have lenders asking what is real, which is when an urgent email from her address will be believed.

> A neighbor says you promised to lend them your car. It is your word against theirs. Then they hold up a text with your name at the top that you never wrote. Flip it: first a message from your own account tells your neighbor the car is hers tonight, and you did not send it; only then does she say you promised it and hold up the text.

The **digital operation** is what turns belief into action. Distress and confusion are an ideal setting for an urgent instruction, such as a payment redirect, that would be rejected on a quiet day. This is also where deniability sits: the sender is anonymous, the channel is not the company's, and a technical explanation may take weeks. It has to come before the clip on the wall screen, because by 10:30 the room is already shaken by the mailbox and the payment request, and has no calm left in which to check a voice.

> Picture a message from "your landlord" arriving on the one day your family is in chaos, telling you to pay somewhere new by tonight. On a calm day you would phone and check. Flip it: first a voice message that sounds exactly like your landlord makes your chest tighten before you can think; only then does the same voice tell you to pay somewhere new by tonight, and you phone to check.

**Immersive manipulation** is the point of conversion. A voice that sounds like the chief executive produces a bodily reaction faster than any checking can. Once colleagues have felt that jolt, even he doubts himself, and the loop is closed: real recordings are now also deniable. It has to come last, because it needs the transcript, the minutes and the mailbox break-in to have left everyone doubting what is real, so that a convincing voice lands with no defense.

> You know a recording of a loved one's voice saying words they never said is fake, and your chest still tightens before your head catches up. Flip it: first you hear a recording of a loved one's voice saying words they never said, and your chest tightens; only then do you learn their account was opened by someone else and the recording posted from it.

What would weaken the sequence is boring and works: a call-back rule on a known number for any payment change, a single holding page with the authoritative documents, and an agreed phrase or second channel for confirming that a person said what they are said to have said.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. It falls out of what a listed company in the middle of a financing is built to produce. A deadline makes every hour matter, and lenders and investors want answers in minutes. Company documents such as call transcripts and board minutes are rarely published, so few outsiders can compare a copy with the original. Treasury runs on routine instructions from known senders. And a single clip can be played to a room full of people at once.

The firehose of falsehood comes first because volume is the precondition for everything else. One false claim can be answered. Fourteen by lunchtime cannot, and the effort of answering becomes part of the story. In a deadline week, with people already tired, every denial is one more item to screenshot, and the people who most need an answer are the ones who fall behind.

Forged Screenshots and edited evidence come second because the noise lacks specifics, and a transcript or a minute page supplies them. They look like the thing itself, the surrounding material is genuine, and the editing is small. Because the organization does not usually publish its minutes, few outsiders can compare.

Cyber Manipulation Operations come third because confusion is the ideal setting for an urgent instruction. A payment redirect that would be rejected on a quiet day arrives from a real mailbox, with the right signature block, in the one week when everyone is told to avoid the phone. The sender is anonymous and the channel is not the company's, so a technical explanation can take weeks.

Immersive Manipulation comes last because it needs the others to have done their work. A voice that sounds like the chief executive produces a bodily reaction faster than any checking can. Once colleagues have felt that jolt, even he doubts himself, and real recordings become deniable too.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a volume that cannot be answered, documents that cannot be compared, an instruction that cannot be queried, and a voice that cannot be unheard. What it turns the person into is someone who answers every item and trusts none.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A deadline week, documents that outsiders cannot compare and routines built on trust are what this setting produces, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a finance director. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Idris Holson, finance director of a mid-sized logistics firm, was a week from signing a supply contract when her phone began to fill at dawn. There were five versions of an email about her firm's payment terms, a post from someone claiming to be a former employee, a chart, a "leaked" thread with her firm's logo. Fourteen messages in one chat, none quoting the same item. A buyer at the supplier sent her a transcript of a call between her and her own chief executive; it had him saying the firm could not honor the terms. He had not said it. A page from the board minutes arrived with a vote that had never taken place. That afternoon the accounts team received an email from her own mailbox, which IT then found had been opened overnight from an unknown device, instructing an urgent payment to a new account. A junior colleague phoned her instead of paying. At four a clip began to circulate of her chief executive, in what looked like his own office, speaking in a voice she would have sworn was his. Her chest tightened before she had a thought. The chief executive said he did not remember saying it.

Rafael Duquesne, the rival bidder, was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend he enjoyed a loud morning.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person responding may find themselves answering items one by one and falling behind, then doubting their own memory of a call they were on. Over time, they may become suspicious of every real document, and a real recording may begin to feel suspect to them as well.

Colleagues may start to second-guess each other: "Did you say that?" The executive whose voice appears to have been imitated may feel an uncomfortable sense of unreality. Those handling payments may feel pressure to act quickly to be useful, and then feel intense guilt if they have.

## Institutional Echo

Listed companies, lenders and investor groups operate on rapid circulation of documents, calls and group chats, and a refinancing or results period concentrates attention and urgency. Finance teams routinely receive instructions from executives by email, and a change to payment details is a known target for impersonation.

Minutes, transcripts and board papers are sensitive records, and where they are only visible to a small group, a forged excerpt is hard for outsiders to dispute. Where control of recordings, access logs and approved communication channels is weak, an altered record or a cloned message can be harder to rule out. No claim is made about the prevalence or reliability of any technology.

Intent would require additional evidence, and attribution requires professional investigation. A mistaken or garbled document in circulation can resemble a forgery for a few hours.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that seeing is believing, and that a voice you know is proof of the person. For most of history that was a good rule, because faking a face or a voice was expensive.

The value being exploited is trust in sensory evidence and in the people we recognize, which is the basis of every business call. Nothing here is an argument against calls, recordings or minutes.

The public story is a company in trouble. The private story, where this pattern operates, is a company that may not be in trouble at all and is being made to look it, and a world in which a real recording would be easy to dismiss. The loss is the evidence itself.

## Observable Indicators

1. Several versions of the same document or clip circulate, with differences that do not match each other.
2. A transcript or minute excerpt contains a line that does not appear in the company's own record.
3. An audio or video item cannot be traced to a known source or a known meeting.
4. Items arrive through group chats and strangers rather than the company's normal channels.
5. A payment or access request arrives during the flood, with urgency and a request for secrecy.
6. The request bypasses the usual approval or call-back.
7. Unfamiliar accounts amplify the same items within a short time.
8. A document carries correct branding but no verifiable provenance.
9. Colleagues disagree about whether a clip "sounds like" a named person.
10. The pace of items outstrips the capacity to check them.
11. Real documents are met with "that could be fake too."

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every version of every item received, with the date, time, channel and who sent it to you.
- The company's own authoritative records for the same meetings, dated and stored separately.
- A list of the differences between the circulating item and the authoritative record.
- Original files or links as received, not screenshots of screenshots; do not open or run unknown attachments on company systems.
- The payment-redirection request, the sender details and the call-back log.
- A timeline of when each item appeared and who first raised it.
- Who attended the real meetings or calls, and their same-day notes.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Not every odd document is a forgery, and not every audio clip that sounds off is generated. Genuine edits, translations, transcription errors and out-of-context excerpts are common.

The discriminator is **provenance plus the match to the authoritative record**. A real item can be traced to a meeting, a file and a person, and matches the record. A fabricated one often has the look of a record without the trail.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Honest transcription error.** Automated transcripts can mishear numbers and names.
- **Out-of-context quotation.** A real remark may be quoted unfairly without being forged.
- **Genuine urgent payment change.** A supplier may truly change bank details, which is why the call-back rule exists.
- **Market rumor.** Investors speculate loudly in a refinancing window.

Concern strengthens when: the document contains a line absent from the company's own record; the payment request asks for secrecy and skips approval; the same item appears through several unfamiliar accounts.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Record Test:**
Ask: "Does this match our own copy of the minutes, the call notes and the file?"
Concern rises if: a line exists only in the circulating version.

**SPOT IT — The Call-Back Test:**
Ask: "Did I confirm this payment change on a number I already held, not one in the message?"
Concern rises if: the instruction arrived with urgency and a request not to call.

**SPOT IT — The Channel Test:**
Ask: "Did this reach us through a normal channel, or through strangers and group chats?"
Concern rises if: the first appearance was anonymous and the volume followed quickly.

**SPOT IT — The Jolt Test:**
Ask: "Am I reacting with my body before I have checked anything?"
Concern rises if: the item feels convincing mainly because the voice or face feels familiar.

## Chain Breakers

- **Do not answer item by item.** Publish one authoritative page of facts with the genuine documents and update it.
- **Hold a call-back rule.** Any change to payment details is confirmed on a known number by a second person, with no exceptions during a crisis.
- **Compare with the record.** Ask the company secretary or counsel to compare each circulating item with the authoritative file and note the differences.
- **Preserve and do not forward.** Keep originals as received, and share them only with counsel, security and the police.
- **Agree a second channel.** Decide in advance how executives confirm that they said something.
- **Do not claim a technical conclusion.** Say "we are verifying" until qualified examiners have reviewed.
- **Inform the lenders and counsel early.** Tell them what you know and do not know, in writing.
- **Do not hit back with accusations.** Naming who you think did it, without evidence, creates its own exposure.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A document or clip that does not match your records; a request to redirect payment that skips approval; the number of versions outstrips your ability to check.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Material that could move a transaction, share price or covenant; a payment actually redirected; an altered board record; lenders or investors acting on the items. Relevant routes may include counsel, the audit committee, the lead banks, independent digital-forensics specialists, the company's insurer and, where market-moving statements are involved, the securities regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** A payment fraud in progress, threats or extortion tied to the material, or intimidation of staff or family. Contact the police and the bank's fraud team at once, tell counsel, and do not confront anyone or negotiate under threat.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "We are aware of documents circulating that do not match our records. We are verifying them and will publish the authoritative materials."
- "I'm not able to act on a payment change without a call-back on a number we already hold."
- "Please send me the original file, not a screenshot, and tell me where it first reached you."
- "Can the company secretary compare this excerpt line by line with the minute book?"
- "I can't confirm that clip is genuine or generated. We have asked qualified examiners."
- "Before we respond publicly, could counsel review the statement?"
- "Can we agree on a second channel to confirm anything that the chief executive is reported to have said?"
