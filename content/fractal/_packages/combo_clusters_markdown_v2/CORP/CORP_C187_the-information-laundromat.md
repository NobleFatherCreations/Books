# C187: The Information Laundromat — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that what seems widely believed must be true, and a sense that checking the evidence no longer matters when everyone around you already agrees.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic sentence when asked where you heard something, "it's just what everyone's saying," with no source in mind.
- A habit of trusting a claim more each time it appears, without noticing it may all come from one place.
- A weariness with the question of what is true, and a pull toward whatever feels right for your side.

**A point in a life.** Midcareer, advising senior leaders.

**Where it happens.** An industrial manufacturer's strategy offsite.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a head of competitive intelligence, forty-two, at an industrial firm that makes pumps and valves, and you are good at one thing: knowing where a claim came from. It is the whole job. Your team keeps a source log, and the first column is never the claim. It is the origin.

**Three months earlier, your desk**

A rival's name has started turning up in places you read. An analyst note you don't subscribe to says the rival is "taking share fast in the mid-range." You do what you always do. You go looking for the first column. It takes two evenings, with a bowl of cold noodles beside the keyboard. The note cites a regional survey. The survey is a short, undated report from an anonymous consultancy with no author, no sample size and a methods page of three sentences. You find no earlier source behind it. You save the three links into your team's shared folder, flagged as unverified, and write it up in a paragraph for your boss, the chief strategy officer, who thanks you and does not reply.

**The strategy offsite, a converted lodge**

Now it's the offsite. A converted lodge, a screen the width of a wall, fourteen executives with coffee. In the pre-read, the slide titled "Market view: the rival is winning the mid-range" has your team's template on it and a footer that reads Compiled by Competitive Intelligence. You didn't compile it. Someone in strategy did, from your shared folder, using the three links you flagged as unverified.

The slide has seven footnotes: the analyst note, a newsletter, a forum thread, a market commentary podcast, an internal sales memo, a quote from a "former industry executive," and the regional survey. You trace them: the newsletter cites the note, the thread cites the newsletter, the podcast cites the thread, the sales memo cites the podcast. Not one of them has been to the survey.

The forum thread is a row of procurement voices agreeing with each other, each sounding as if it had just noticed. The former executive's quote appears a week after the newsletter and uses the same phrase, "taking share fast." Two friendly contacts in your own network have each mentioned it to you this month, as if it were their own observation.

At the coffee break you stay at your seat and open your monitoring tool. Over ten days the phrase "taking share fast" has been posted forty-one times from accounts a few weeks old, almost exactly nine minutes apart. The tool lists the topic as trending. The automatic summary that went round with the pre-read puts it first. Your hands go cold on the keyboard.

Back in the room, the chief executive says, "This is what I keep hearing everywhere." Heads nod. Your sales director's forwarded message from last week begins, "Seems to be what everyone thinks now." Your own head nods a quarter inch before you stop it.

Someone says the rival's pricing is already being matched in two regions, and the room moves on to what it means for the capital plan. Someone asks how much, not whether. Budget is being reallocated away from the mid-range line you know to be healthy.

Your own internal sales data, which you pulled on Tuesday, shows no loss of share. It's in a spreadsheet on your laptop. You raise your hand. You say, carefully, that the footnotes may all lead back to one survey. The chief strategy officer smiles and says that's a fair challenge, and that the point of a view like this is that it doesn't depend on any one source. The chief executive says, kindly, that you are the person he trusts to tell him what the market thinks, and the market thinks this. Nobody is hostile. Nobody lies in the room. The slide is tidy and the footnotes are numbered.

**Four months later, your desk**

Four months later it's a Thursday, and a junior analyst asks where you got the claim that a supplier is about to fold. You hear yourself say it has come up in three places this week. You don't ask which three. The claim goes in column one now, because that's what people ask for, and you've stopped being the one who asks.

The mid-range line missed its quarter, because its budget moved. You said once that the footnotes might lead to one survey, and nobody has raised it since. On the train you read a post about a different rival. It feels right, it sounds like what your side already thinks, and it has been shared a great deal, and you press the arrow to pass it on before the station. You are tired of the question of what is true. You eat lunch at your desk so nobody asks you what the market thinks.

The sales numbers are still on your laptop, flat and healthy. They were right, and you haven't said so to anyone.

## The Name

That's **The Information Laundromat**.

## Definition

The Information Laundromat is the sequence where a weak or planted claim is fed through friendly channels until its origin is lost and it looks like common knowledge, using Context Poisoning, Information Laundering, Astroturfing / Sock Puppets, Computational Propaganda, False Consensus / Social Proof Pressure, Normalization / Overton Window Manipulation and Post-Truth Epistemic Manipulation to convert trust in what seems widely believed into a loss of the shared basis for telling true from false. It may benefit whoever wants a decision, budget or valuation to move, and it exploits the sensible habit of treating agreement as evidence. It is hard to detect because each step looks like ordinary commentary and each citation really exists. In a company, what can be lost is capital allocation, a credible intelligence function, and the willingness of colleagues to trust each other's sources. Whether any of this is designed or simply what happens when a loud claim meets a busy room needs additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Context Poisoning** — A short undated report from an anonymous consultancy with no author, no sample size and a three-sentence methods page, found after two evenings of tracing, with its three links saved in the team's shared folder as unverified. This functions by shaping the pool of "available" information before anyone starts to evaluate it. Concern rises when the first item in the pool has no named author and the pool is not otherwise balanced.

2. **Information Laundering** — A pre-read slide in the team's own template and footer, built from that folder, with seven footnotes that all trace back to one survey: the newsletter cites the note, the thread cites the newsletter, the podcast cites the thread, the sales memo cites the podcast. This functions by exchanging a claim's weak parentage for the apparent independence of several publishers. Concern rises when sources are numbered but cannot be traced to anything outside each other.

3. **Astroturfing / Sock Puppets** — A forum thread of procurement voices agreeing with each other, a "former industry executive" repeating the same phrase a week after the newsletter, and two friendly contacts mentioning it as if it were their own observation. This functions by supplying the look of unprompted agreement. Concern rises when phrasing matches across supposedly unconnected voices, and when the voices cannot be verified as real, separate people.

4. **Computational Propaganda** — The monitoring tool shows "taking share fast" posted forty-one times in ten days from weeks-old accounts, almost exactly nine minutes apart, listed as trending and placed first in the automatic summary sent with the pre-read. This functions by making a claim appear in more places, and more often, than any person could produce. Concern rises when volume is out of proportion to the underlying activity, or when a technical explanation is offered without checking.

5. **False Consensus / Social Proof Pressure** — Heads nod when the chief executive says "This is what I keep hearing everywhere," and the sales director's forwarded message from last week says the matter is already the settled view. This functions by making disagreement feel like a position held against the room. Concern rises when the word "consensus" is used instead of a count of who actually holds the view and why.

6. **Normalization / Overton Window Manipulation** — Someone says the rival's pricing is already being matched in two regions and the room moves straight to the capital plan, with budget shifting away from the healthy mid-range line and no one asking whether the claim is true. This functions by moving the boundary of what is reasonable to question. Concern rises when asking "what is the source?" is described as slowing things down.

7. **Post-Truth Epistemic Manipulation** — Internal sales data showing no loss of share and a traced single source lose to the chief strategy officer's point that the slide does not depend on any one source and the chief executive's "the market thinks this." This functions by making the claim immune to checking. Concern rises when contrary data is described as "just one view" while repetition counts as proof.

## Why This Order

**Context Poisoning** comes first because the pool has to be seeded before anything else can look natural. A claim that has no earlier version cannot be corrected by an earlier version. The planted material need not be false. It only needs to be the first thing there. It has to come before the chain of citations, because every later footnote needs an anonymous survey and a first analyst note to point back to.

> Someone leaves a note on the staff fridge saying the milk is going off. Nothing else is written there, so it is the first thing everybody believes. Flip it: one colleague tells you she read that the milk is going off, and another repeats it as fact; you check the fridge and find no note, and only later does someone stick one up.

**Information Laundering** is the conversion step. It takes a claim with one weak parent and gives it the appearance of many. The circular chain in the cold open is the fingerprint: seven footnotes, one origin. Nothing in the chain is a lie. Every citation is accurate, and that is exactly why it survives a casual check. It has to come before the forum thread of agreeing voices, because those voices only sound independent once the note, the newsletter and the podcast already seem to confirm one another.

> A friend says she read it in a magazine. The magazine got it from a blog, and the blog from her friend. Each link is true. None of them knows where it started. Flip it: first several people you hardly know tell you, in the very same words, that they read it in a magazine; you ask your friend, and only then does she say she read it too.

**Astroturfing** then supplies the unprompted voices that a real market would have, which is what makes people stop asking where the claim started. It has to come before the burst of posts, because the thread's voices make the phrase look spontaneous, and a repeated phrase is then taken for a real conversation.

> A restaurant's page fills with five-star reviews that all use the same phrase. You stop asking whether anyone you know has eaten there. Flip it: first the restaurant's name tops every page you open this week, and by Friday you are sure it must be good; only then do five-star reviews, all in one phrase, fill its page.

**Computational Propaganda** raises the volume and the frequency so that the claim does not merely exist but is encountered everywhere. This is the step that weakens independent evaluation the most, because ranking tools and summarizing tools may repeat it back as if repetition were evidence. Whether any automation is involved in a given case requires verification. It has to come before the chief executive's remark that he keeps hearing it, because the forty-one repeats and the trending label are what he will have met everywhere he looked.

> A song you have never heard is at the top of every playlist you open this week. By Friday you are humming it, and you assume it must be good. Flip it: first everyone you know says the song is brilliant, and when you say you have never heard it you are the odd one out; only then does it top every playlist you open.

**False Consensus** is the point of conversion. Until it, someone who doubts the claim is only a person with a question. After it, they are a person disagreeing with everyone. The cost of dissent now falls on the person raising it. It has to come before the move to the capital plan, because the room can only skip whether the claim is true once his remark has made doubting it feel odd.

> Everyone at the table says the film was brilliant. When you say you found it dull, you are suddenly the odd one out. Flip it: first the table's talk settles, week after week, on the film being "only a bit slow," so the question is how slow; then everyone says it was brilliant, and you say you found it dull.

**Normalization** hardens that into the baseline. It has to come before your raised hand about the footnotes, because only once the room is arguing about how much can your question about where it began be set aside.

> After a month of everyone saying the new office is "only a bit cramped," the argument is about how cramped, not whether the move was a good idea. Flip it: first you show people the measurements for the new office, and someone says, "Everyone knows it's fine"; only after a month of that do they call it "only a bit cramped."

**Post-Truth Epistemic Manipulation** closes the loop: once repetition outranks tracing, no new evidence can reopen the question. It has to come last, because your sales data and your raised hand can be answered with "the market thinks this" only after the room has stopped asking whether the claim is true.

> You show your cousin the receipt, and he says, "Everyone knows what happened." The receipt is no longer the point. Flip it: first you show your cousin the receipt and he says, "Everyone knows what happened"; only after a month of him calling the whole thing "just a small mix-up" does the argument turn to how small.

What would weaken the sequence is cheap and dull: a source log with the origin as column one, and a request for the count of independent sources rather than citations.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. It falls out of what a strategy offsite is built to produce. Pre-reads are assembled quickly, from whatever sits in a shared folder, under a template that carries one team's name. Executives hear the market from many directions and have little time to ask where any of it began. And tools that rank and summarize reward whatever is repeated.

Context Poisoning comes first because a claim with no earlier version cannot be corrected by an earlier version. A market is always full of loose notes and surveys, and whichever one is there first becomes the thing everything else points back to. It need not be false, only first.

Information Laundering comes second because a pre-read must cite something, and a citation looks like evidence. A newsletter that cites a note, and a thread that cites the newsletter, each tells the truth about what it read. Put in a row, they give a weak claim the look of many parents.

Astroturfing comes third because a real market has chatter, and a claim with no chatter around it looks lonely. Voices that agree with each other and sound as if they have just noticed are easy to supply, and they are what makes people stop asking where it started.

Computational Propaganda comes fourth because the pool is read by ranking and summarizing tools that count repetition, so a phrase posted often and on a schedule rises and earns a "trending" label. Whether any automation is involved in a given case needs checking, but the effect on the reader is the same.

False Consensus comes fifth because by now the claim is met everywhere. A leader who says, "I keep hearing this," is reporting honestly what reached him, and the person with a doubt is no longer someone with a question but someone disagreeing with everyone.

Normalization comes sixth because a budget meeting needs a premise to work with. Once the claim sits on a slide, the discussion turns to what follows from it, how much and where, and the question of whether it is true quietly leaves the room.

Post-Truth Epistemic Manipulation comes last because decisions move faster than checking. When the market is said to think something, a spreadsheet of your own sales looks like one person's contrary opinion, and no new evidence reopens the question.

Put it together and the order is the order a busy, well-meaning setting needs: a first note, a set of citations, a crowd, a volume, a belief that is everywhere, a premise to plan on, and a room that treats evidence as an argument. What it turns the person into is someone who stops asking for the origin.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A pre-read built from a shared folder, tools that reward repetition and executives short of time are what this setting produces, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Kiran Torton, head of regional sales at a distribution firm, found a line in an industry newsletter saying his region's pipeline was thin. It cited a short, anonymous survey he had never heard of. Two days later a second newsletter cited the first, then a podcast cited the newsletter, and then a message on the sales floor began, "Apparently." Over the week a trade forum filled with replies from accounts he didn't recognize, all in the same phrase. His dashboard showed the phrase posted dozens of times, minutes apart. At Monday's leadership meeting the managing director said it was what he kept hearing, and colleagues nodded; Kiran saw a peer he had mentored nod too. By Wednesday the budget talk was about how much to move out of his region, not whether the line was true. Kiran brought his own figures. The managing director thanked him and said everyone knew how the quarter was going.

Magnus Wenton, the regional head who wanted the budget, was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend he never had to say anything himself.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person who traces the claim may find themselves in a strange position: right about the origin, and unable to use it. Over time, they may begin to second-guess their own checking, because everyone else seems untroubled.

They may soften their language so as not to look obstructive, stop circulating their own data because it is "only internal," or come to feel that being careful is being disloyal.

## Institutional Echo

Strategy functions, investor relations teams and market-research groups routinely compile "what the market is saying," and a consensus slide with numbered footnotes is a normal artifact. The same artifact can carry circular sourcing without anyone intending it: a junior analyst cites what a senior analyst cited, and a shared folder preserves the link but not the doubt.

Where decks are built from earlier decks and an unattributed report passes from team to team, a weak claim can come to look strong with no one deciding that it should. In deal and fundraising settings, claims about traction, investor interest or a rival can likewise travel through friendly channels before reaching a decision-maker.

Intent would require additional evidence. The most useful questions are whether the earliest item in the chain was ever authored by a named, accountable person, and whether anyone benefits from the budget or valuation the claim moves.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that the market is a kind of collective intelligence, and that if enough people say a thing, it has been tested. Around it sits the belief that a senior person's job is to read the room and move with it, and that questioning provenance is pedantry.

The value being exploited is a good one: we really do learn from other people. Nothing here is an argument against listening to the market.

The public story is a consensus. The private story, where this pattern operates, is one claim wearing several coats, and the loss is that the group can no longer tell what it knows from what it has heard.

## Observable Indicators

1. A "consensus" slide or memo cites sources, but several trace back to the same original item.
2. The earliest source in a chain is anonymous, undated, or has no methods description.
3. Different sources use the same distinctive phrase within a short period.
4. Supporting voices, such as forum posts or quoted former insiders, cannot be verified as independent real people.
5. The claim appears in many places at once, in volume that does not match any change in your own data.
6. The word "consensus" or "everyone" replaces a count of independent sources.
7. Internal data that does not fit is called "just one view" or "only anecdotal."
8. A request to see the origin is described as slowing the decision.
9. A slide carries one team's label but was assembled by another from items flagged as unverified.
10. A budget or valuation change follows closely after the claim begins circulating.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The earliest version of the claim you can find, with the date you found it and where.
- A source log that lists the origin first, with each citation chain written out.
- Dated captures of the footnoted pages and posts before they change.
- Your own internal data on the same question, with the date pulled and the query used.
- The pre-read, the slide as presented and any later versions, with their authorship fields.
- Messages in which the word "consensus" or "everyone" replaces named sources.
- Any request you made for the origin and the answer, with date.
- Names of colleagues who also questioned the sourcing.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Real consensus exists, and a view held by many independent people is evidence. Analysts do read each other, and a newsletter that cites an analyst note is normal.

The discriminator is **independence at the root**. Count the roots, not the footnotes. A genuine consensus has several different origins: different data, different authors, different methods. A laundered one has one origin and many coats.

Other lookalikes:

- **Ordinary market chatter.** Rivals gain share, and early reports can be right.
- **Honest shorthand.** A strategist who writes "consensus" may simply mean "several sources I did not trace."
- **Genuine referral.** Friends and contacts passing on what they heard is normal and usually sincere.
- **Weak but real research.** A short, anonymous survey can be flawed without being planted.

Concern strengthens when: every chain leads to one origin; the origin is anonymous and untraceable; requests to trace it are treated as obstruction.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Root Test:**
Ask: "If I follow every footnote to the end, how many different origins do I reach?"
Concern rises if: the answer is one, however many footnotes there are.

**SPOT IT — The Author Test:**
Ask: "Who wrote the earliest item, and can they be named?"
Concern rises if: nobody can say, and the item is still cited as established.

**SPOT IT — The Echo Test:**
Ask: "Do these sources use the same words?"
Concern rises if: a distinctive phrase recurs across supposedly separate voices.

**SPOT IT — The Own-Data Test:**
Ask: "What does our own data say, and was it asked for?"
Concern rises if: internal evidence is dismissed as one view while repetition counts as proof.

## Chain Breakers

- **Build the source log with the origin as column one.** For every claim that moves money, write who first said it and when.
- **Count roots, not citations.** On any consensus slide, ask for the number of independent origins, in writing.
- **Trace before presenting.** If your name or team label is on a slide, ask to see the sources before it goes up, and ask for the label to be removed if you cannot stand behind it.
- **Put your own data beside it.** Bring the internal figure, with the date and method, as a neighbor to the claim, without calling anyone wrong.
- **Ask for a short pause, not a reversal.** "Can we mark this as unverified until the origin is traced?" is easier to accept than "this is false."
- **Send a written summary after the meeting.** "To confirm, we discussed that footnotes one to six trace to footnote seven."
- **Do not post guesses about who planted anything.** Keep to what you can document, and save dated copies of the footnoted pages.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A decision-relevant claim whose chain you can trace to one anonymous origin; your own data contradicts it; your request to review the sourcing is deflected twice.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A budget, valuation, investor communication or public statement is about to rely on the claim; fabricated "independent" voices appear likely; your label is on material you did not approve; or retaliation follows your question. Relevant routes may include the audit or compliance committee, an independent director, counsel, and, where a securities or investor communication is involved, qualified advice on disclosure duties.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation or reprisals against the person who questioned the sourcing, or evidence that fabricated material is being used to defraud investors or counterparties. Contact emergency services where there is danger and qualified counsel or law enforcement for suspected fraud, and do not confront anyone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we add a line showing how many independent origins sit behind this consensus?"
- "I traced footnotes one to six to the regional survey. Can we mark this as single-source until we confirm otherwise?"
- "Our own sales data for the same period shows no change. May I share it next to the slide?"
- "Please remove the Competitive Intelligence label until the sourcing is reviewed."
- "I'm not saying the claim is wrong. I'm saying I can't yet show where it started."
- "Can we hold the reallocation until the origin of the main source is confirmed?"
- "For the record, my summary of the discussion is that the sources are circular. Please correct me if the room recalls it differently."
