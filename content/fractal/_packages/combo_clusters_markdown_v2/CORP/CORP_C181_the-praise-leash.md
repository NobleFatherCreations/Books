# C181: The Praise Leash — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a need for praise that steers what you do, a reflex to look after another's feelings before your own, and a wariness about anything private you have shared.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of shaping your work around what earned a smile last time, rather than what you think is right.
- An automatic thought after someone confides in you, "what will they want from me now?"
- A panic when a warm person goes silent, and a rush to work out what you did.

**A point in a life.** The first years of a career.

**Where it happens.** A large company's executive suite.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're an executive speechwriter, thirty-five, and you write the words the chief executive says in public. The all-company letter. The investor remarks. The short, human paragraph at the end of an earnings call that makes people forget they are listening to a spreadsheet. You are good at it, and for the first time in a career of being the person in the back of the room, someone has noticed.

**September, the executive suite**

The CEO is Catherine. She reads your drafts in her office suite with a green pen, and she writes in the margins in a looping hand. "Yes." "This is exactly me." "Where did you learn to hear me like this?" You keep the pages in a folder in your desk drawer. Nobody has ever told you that you were exactly anyone before.

She likes the drafts that are warm and certain, the ones that turn each hard quarter into a story of resolve. When you write one that wonders aloud whether a target was missed, the margin says only "Hmm." The next draft is lighter. You adjust without meaning to. The next page comes back with a tick and a line in the margin calling you a gift. On the train home you start drafting in her voice, hearing where she would breathe.

By October you can tell her mood before your first coffee. You read her assistant's tone, the length of her emails, the speed of her replies. On a good day she thanks you in front of the whole team. On a flat day you write a paragraph meant to cheer her up, then stay late to rework the remarks she dreads. Nobody asks how your week has been. You tell yourself it is part of the job, to sound like her.

**April, a private dinner**

After a conference, she invites you to dinner, just the two of you, at a small restaurant with candles in jars. Over the second glass she tells you, quietly, about her own difficult year: a mother who was ill, a marriage ending, a stretch in which she did not sleep for most of a winter and nobody at the company knew. "I don't tell anyone this," she says. "I think you're the only person who sees what it costs." You are moved.

Before dessert your phone buzzes on the tablecloth. It is a long message from her, with the dates of that winter, headed in her own words "in confidence." You answer it there, across the table, with your father's illness and the leave you took in your twenties and never explained. She puts her hand on yours, briefly, and says she is so glad you told her. You feel lighter than you have in years. The candle between you gutters, and she asks for the bill.

**November, a calibration meeting**

In November you sit in the corner of the calibration meeting as an observer for the communications team, with a notebook you do not open. The room smells of coffee and printer toner. Someone mentions your name. Catherine, kindly, in front of five senior leaders, says she worries about you. She says she knows about your father and about that stretch in your twenties. She wonders aloud whether the pressure of this role is sustainable. Her tone is tender. Somebody writes something down. The talk about your promotion moves on to the next name. You sit very still, with your hands under the table, and nod when someone looks at you.

**January, the executive suite**

In January the annual letter draft has a paragraph on the company's difficult results. You write it honestly. The page comes back with nothing in the margin at all. Her office door, which has been open for a year, is closed. For three weeks she answers your emails with a single word, if at all. She stops copying you on the investor prep. Your stomach is tight by the time you reach your desk each morning. After the floor empties you redraft the paragraph in a lighter key and leave it on her chair.

**Four months later, your flat**

It is a Sunday in May, five in the morning, and you are awake before the light. You check the length of her last email before you check anything else. Four words, no signature. Her door has been open again for a month, and last week she thanked you in the corridor, by name, with a hand on your arm. You write the way you now speak: lighter, certain, nothing that wonders.

Your friend Jo has texted that her week has been terrible and that she has something to tell you. You find yourself weighing what she might do with whatever you say back. You answer that you are here. You say nothing about your own week, and nothing true about your father. When she has not replied by noon your heart picks up, and you open the thread and read your own last three messages again, checking every word.

The paragraph on the laptop took you all of Saturday. You wrote it three ways, then kept the one a green pen would tick. It is not the one you believe. The flat is quiet. You put the kettle on and do not drink it.

The text thread from April is still open on your phone. At the top it says "in confidence." You have not told anyone anything true in four months, and nobody has noticed.

## The Name

That's **The Praise Leash**.

## Definition

The Praise Leash is the sequence where praise rewards whatever serves the other person, you end up managing their feelings, a staged confidence draws out yours, what you shared is turned into leverage, and warmth is withdrawn when you resist, using Grooming Through Praise, Emotional Parasitism, Bait Disclosure, Vulnerability Weaponization and Withholding / Silent Treatment to convert the need for recognition into loss of privacy and self-trust. In this sector it concentrates in the close working relationship between a senior executive, partner or board chair and a trusted junior, such as a speechwriter, chief of staff or associate, where closeness to power is both the reward and the risk. It is hard to detect because genuine mentorship also involves praise and personal sharing, and because the leverage is exercised in sympathetic language in rooms where the person cannot respond.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Grooming Through Praise** — Looping margin notes of approval on the drafts that are warm and certain ("Yes," "This is exactly me") and only "Hmm" on the one that wonders whether a target was missed, after which the next draft comes back lighter. This functions by teaching, through reward, which version of you is wanted. Concern rises when praise tracks the other person's comfort rather than the quality of the work.

2. **Emotional Parasitism** — Catherine's moods read before the first coffee from her assistant's tone, the length of her emails and the speed of her replies, with sentences written to cheer her up on flat days. This functions by making the junior's attention a standing resource that is never named or compensated. Concern rises when you plan work around someone's emotional weather.

3. **Bait Disclosure** — At a private dinner Catherine tells of her difficult year and says "I don't tell anyone this," and you answer with a father's illness and a leave taken in your twenties; a text thread headed "in confidence" follows. This functions by making disclosure feel reciprocal and safe, while the exchange is not equal in power or risk. Concern rises when the confidence arrives suddenly, is described as unique to you, and is followed by a request, explicit or implied, to share your own.

4. **Vulnerability Weaponization** — At the November calibration Catherine says, tenderly, in front of five senior leaders, that she worries, that she knows about the father and that stretch in the twenties, and that the role may not be sustainable, which ends the talk of promotion. This functions by converting trust into leverage in settings where you are not present to respond. Concern rises when personal details appear in a review without your knowledge, framed as care.

5. **Withholding / Silent Treatment** — After an honest paragraph on the difficult results, the margin says nothing, the open door closes, emails get a single word if anything, and the speechwriter is no longer copied on the investor prep. This functions by making the loss of approval the cost of disagreement. Concern rises when the withdrawal follows a specific act of independence and ends when you return to the flattering version.

## Why This Order

**Praise** has to come first because it sets the currency. Before any confidence is exchanged, you must already care about this person's approval more than most. The praise is also deliberately specific, so that you learn exactly what earns it. By the time you understand the pattern, you have been shaped by it, and it feels like a talent. It has to come before the reading of her moods, because you only begin to study her emails and her assistant's tone after her green pen has become the approval you most want.

> A new teacher writes "excellent!" on every drawing with a horse in it, and only "hmm" on the rest. Soon you only draw horses. Flip it: if you were already watching the new teacher's face for a good mood before any drawing got marked, you would draw what you liked and ask how you were doing.

**Emotional Parasitism** follows because it needs the approval to have taken hold. You will not manage a stranger's moods. You will manage the mood of the person whose pen decides whether you are "exactly right." The role then expands, quietly, from writing the person's words to carrying the person's feelings, without any change in title or pay. It has to come before the dinner confession, because you only sit across from her ready to take in her hard year and answer it once you have spent months looking after her moods.

> You know your friend's mood from how she signs off a text. You cheer her up before she asks, and nobody ever asks how you are. Flip it: if your friend had shared a sad secret and waited for yours before you ever learned her moods, you would listen kindly and share only what you chose.

**Bait Disclosure** is the pivot. After weeks of closeness, a confidence is offered, and the reciprocal one comes easily because you have been groomed to give it. The disclosure that matters is not hers. It is yours, and it is requested in the language of trust. The exchange feels equal and is not: hers is selected, and yours is unguarded. The "in confidence" label is the document that locks it in, and it protects only one of you. It has to come before the remark at calibration, because the worry she voices there rests on your father and your leave in your twenties, which you gave her at dinner.

> A friend says, "I've never told anyone this," and shares something sad. You share something sad back. Only later do you wonder who decided which stories got told. Flip it: if your friend had brought up your private worries in front of others before sharing any sad story, you would ask her to keep your things private and stop telling her more.

**Vulnerability Weaponization** uses what the earlier steps collected. At calibration, the same warmth used to draw out your story is used to describe you as fragile, and the care in the sentence makes it nearly impossible to answer. You cannot object to concern. This is the conversion point of the combo. It has to come before the closed door, because the shut door lands hard only after the calibration meeting has left you exposed and in need of her goodwill for your promotion.

> At a family dinner an aunt says, in front of everyone, "I'm just worried about you, after what you told me about your marriage." You cannot be angry at worry. Flip it: if the aunt had gone silent for days after you disagreed, before any comment at dinner, you would ring her and ask what was wrong.

**Withholding** comes last, and it can come earlier too, because it is the enforcement that keeps the loop running. A closed door after an honest draft teaches the whole sequence in three weeks. It also provides deniability: nobody has threatened you. A person has simply become busy. It has to come last, because a closed door and one-word replies sting this much only once you have given her your father and your leave and heard her call you fragile in front of five leaders.

> A friend who always replies within minutes takes four days after you disagree with her. Nothing was said. You write the next message more carefully. Flip it: if your friend's four days of silence came first and only later she brought up your private worries in a group, you would tell her plainly that was not okay.

What weakens the sequence is a named set of working standards, feedback in writing, and disclosure kept proportionate to the working relationship.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A chief executive's speechwriter works one step from the top with one reader, whose approval is the only review the work gets. Attention at that level is scarce, so it is spent unevenly. The company also runs a yearly calibration, a short meeting in which a few leaders talk about many people and every sentence counts. And the language of that meeting is concern, because concern is the polite way to talk about a colleague who is not in the room.

Grooming Through Praise comes first because a lone reader marks drafts in the margin, and a margin is the only feedback there is. Warm lines go beside the pages that sound confident, and the pages that sound unsure get a word or nothing. Nobody plans a currency. It forms because there is one reader and her time is short.

Emotional Parasitism comes second because a speechwriter's job is to sound like the person, and sounding like her requires knowing her mood. The role grows from writing her words to carrying her days, with no change in title or pay.

Bait Disclosure comes third because closeness at the top is lonely, and the one person nearby is the obvious listener. A private dinner after a conference is where a leader with no peers finds someone to talk to, and the exchange of stories feels equal because both are given.

Vulnerability Weaponization comes fourth because calibration runs on short remarks. A leader who knows your history has the quickest sentence available, and concern is a sentence that nobody in the room can answer without seeming to argue with kindness.

Withholding / Silent Treatment comes last because the structure offers no formal way to say that something is wrong. A closed door and one-word replies fill the gap, and they cost the person doing it nothing, since nobody can point to a rule that was broken.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a reader who matters, a role that grows to cover her moods, a confidence at dinner, a sentence at the calibration table, and a silence in January. It turns a gifted writer into someone who reads the room for her first, writes lightly and carries a secret that is no longer hers.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A calibration calendar, a short promotion list and a closed door shaped it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Vera Darby was in his first year as an analyst at a consultancy when the firm placed him on a team led by a partner, Imogen Tait. In their first week she wrote "exactly the clarity I want" across his slides, but only on the ones that agreed with her view; the others came back with a single question mark. Within a month he was reading her moods from the length of her emails, booking her travel, and rewriting her pitch decks at night. No one asked how he was. At a team dinner in the spring Imogen said, in a low voice, that she had nearly left the firm the year before and had told nobody. She asked what had been hardest for him. He told her he had failed a professional exam twice and had hidden it from the team. She said she was glad he had said it. At the mid-year review, before two other partners, she said she was concerned about him, given the exams, and wondered whether this level of work was sustainable. His name came off the shortlist for the promotion round. The following week she did not reply to his emails for nine days. He rewrote a deck she had not asked for, in the shape she liked. Imogen told a fellow partner over lunch that first-year analysts were the easiest people in the building to keep close.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to write for one reader's reaction rather than for the audience or the facts. Over time, they may check messages for tone, rehearse conversations in advance, and feel anxious when approval disappears. They may regret what they shared and be unsure whether their regret is reasonable. They may begin to doubt their own judgement, because the same person who called them exactly right now describes them as struggling. Many feel both grateful and trapped.

## Institutional Echo

Senior roles bring close working relationships with junior staff, and informal confidences, flattery and mood management can be a normal part of them. Where evaluation relies on sponsor impressions, calibration sessions may rely on anecdote rather than record. Where communications and executive-office roles sit close to a leader, the person's performance may be judged by how a leader feels, as much as by outputs.

Personal information shared in confidence can reach an evaluation without any policy being broken. Whether and how health, family or leave information may be used in an employment decision is governed by rules that vary and need qualified review. Intent would require additional evidence; a well-meaning executive can do each of these things without design.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is the mentor who sees something in you, the trusted aide whose loyalty is rewarded with closeness to power. It is old and, in many cases, true. A second myth says that confidence is a sign of trust, and that someone who shares their own hard year is therefore safe to share yours with.

The language of the pattern is the language of care: "I worry about you," "I'm only telling you because I trust you," "you carry so much." The value being exploited is recognition, which people need and are right to want. The public story is a leader who supports her team through hard times. The private loss is a person who now knows what it costs to have been honest.

## Observable Indicators

1. Praise is specific and enthusiastic for drafts that flatter, and absent or minimal for others.
2. Feedback on content is replaced by comments on tone, mood or the leader's comfort.
3. The junior person's calendar and work is shaped around the senior person's mood.
4. A personal confidence from the senior person arrives suddenly and is described as shared with no one else.
5. A request, implied or stated, follows for a personal disclosure in return.
6. A private message thread is labelled confidential and continues out of hours.
7. Personal details shared by the junior person reappear in evaluation, calibration or promotion talks.
8. The language used is concern for the person, with no reference to work evidence.
9. Replies, access or invitations reduce after an unwelcome draft or opinion.
10. Warmth returns after the junior person reverts to the earlier style.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Drafts with margin comments, dated, showing which versions were praised and which were not.
- The private message thread, preserved with dates and the label "in confidence," saved lawfully and in full.
- Your own notes made close to the time on what you shared and when.
- Calibration, promotion and review documents you are entitled to see, and any record of personal details cited.
- A log of changes in reply times, access, copied recipients or invitations following specific drafts.
- Names of colleagues who observed the same reaction patterns or who received the same kind of confidence.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Good mentors praise, and good leaders sometimes share personal hardship, sincerely and without strategy. Real concern about a colleague's wellbeing exists, and a leader may mention it with care. A leader who goes quiet after a disappointing draft may simply be stressed or busy.

The discriminator is whether the confidence is used on you. In genuine mentorship, praise tracks the quality of work, disagreement is welcomed, disclosures stay private, and concern comes to you directly. In The Praise Leash, praise tracks comfort, disagreement costs warmth, and concern is voiced to others.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Sincere personal sharing.** A leader's hardship may be offered with no expectation.
- **Real concern.** Worry about a colleague's load may be genuine and handled well.
- **Normal busyness.** A slower reply may have no connection to your draft.

Concern strengthens when: personal disclosures appear in evaluations; warmth drops after specific acts of independence; praise is tied to flattering versions only; and concern is voiced to others but not to you.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Margin Test:**
Ask: "Is praise following the quality of the work, or the comfort of the reader?"
Concern rises if: the lighter, more flattering version is always the one praised.

**SPOT IT — The Weather Test:**
Ask: "Am I planning my work around someone's mood?"
Concern rises if: you are adjusting drafts or timing before any feedback.

**SPOT IT — The Trade Test:**
Ask: "Was my confidence requested after theirs, and was theirs picked for me?"
Concern rises if: the exchange felt equal but only one side's disclosure can be used.

**SPOT IT — The Calibration Test:**
Ask: "Has anything I told in private been cited in a review as a concern?"
Concern rises if: it has, framed as care, without my having been asked.

**SPOT IT — The Door Test:**
Ask: "Did the warmth stop after I wrote something unwelcome?"
Concern rises if: it returned only after I changed course.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for written feedback on content.** "Could you tell me in the margin what you'd like different, and why?"
- **Keep disclosures proportionate.** Share about work, not health, family or past leave, with someone who evaluates you.
- **Treat "in confidence" as a label, not a protection.** Assume anything shared could be repeated.
- **Keep your own dated notes** on what you shared, with whom, and what was said in return.
- **Ask for the standards.** Request the promotion criteria and calibration process in writing, and ask how personal information is handled.
- **Redirect concern to the record.** "Thank you for caring. I'd like to discuss my work and the evidence for the rating."
- **Build a second sponsor,** such as another senior leader, a peer mentor or a professional network, so no single person controls your recognition.
- **Do not retaliate or share the thread** with colleagues or press; take it to appropriate channels with advice.
- **Seek professional support** for your own wellbeing from a doctor or counsellor, not from your evaluator.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Praise following flattery only; a confidence requested in return; a quiet period after an unwelcome draft; a tone-based rather than evidence-based comment on your work.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Personal or health details cited in calibration, promotion or separation talks; a rating that changes after a disclosure; a closed door that follows each act of independence; requests to raise concerns being met with the leader's own distress. Relevant routes may include human resources or employee relations, a trusted senior leader outside the reporting line, a qualified employment attorney, a union or works council, and an employee assistance service.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to disclose personal information; blackmail or pressure tied to what you shared; unwanted physical contact or sexual pressure; retaliation after a complaint; statements of self-harm by you or anyone else. Contact emergency services, a crisis line, or qualified counsel as appropriate. Do not confront the person, and preserve the evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "I appreciate the encouragement. Could you tell me in the margin what you'd like to see changed in this paragraph?"
- "I'd like to keep our conversations focused on the work."
- "I'm not comfortable having personal matters discussed in my evaluation. Could we agree on how that information is handled?"
- "Could you share the criteria for this promotion round in writing?"
- "I'd like to hear any concern about me directly, and with examples from my work."
- "I need a few days to think about that draft. I'd like to send my reasoning with it."
- "Thank you for sharing that. I'll keep it private."
