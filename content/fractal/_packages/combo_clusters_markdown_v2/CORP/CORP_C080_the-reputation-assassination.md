# C080: The Reputation Assassination — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a fear that a rumour can arrive just before your most important moment and leave no trace, and a belief that once people go quiet around you, the story has already won.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of bracing before every big day, interview or decision, expecting something to undo it at the last minute.
- A pull to read every cool greeting as proof that people have heard something about you.
- An automatic thought, "I'll never know who started it," that keeps you from asking anyone directly.

**A point in a life.** The final weeks before a promotion.

**Where it happens.** A company choosing its next chief executive.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a senior vice president, you are forty-eight, and you have been told, in the careful language boards use, that you are "in the conversation." The chief executive retires in the spring. The nominating committee has a slate of three, and your name is on it with two others, one internal and one from outside. You have spent twenty-two years building the kind of name that does not need a campaign: delivered numbers, loyal teams, a reputation for being straightforward.

The slate memo is two pages. It lists the candidates alphabetically, which you take as a good sign, and it says that reference conversations will be conducted "on a confidential basis by members of the committee and its advisor" in the three weeks before the decision. You read it twice and put it in a drawer.

**Week one, your office**

The window opens on a Monday. On the Tuesday a peer, someone you like, stops at your door and asks you carefully whether you are "doing okay," and whether things are "all right with the numbers in the Northern division." They are. You say so. He nods too quickly. There are not even three weeks left, too few to trace where a question came from.

On Wednesday a director you barely know leans in the doorway on her way to a meeting and says she "heard there were some concerns about judgment." She cannot say about what. She smiles, and says she is sure it is nothing. Two days later an executive search consultant, who has known you for years, calls your desk phone and mentions that a reference call about you had "an interesting note." No details. He is friendly. He says, "You might want to get ahead of it." You ask what it is. He does not know. It is "more of a general sense." Three people, four days, and none of them can tell you who said it.

You put the phone down and look at the drawer where the memo sits. You do not open it. You count back to Monday and forward to the decision, and you try to find a day on which you could have made a difference.

**Week two, the chair's office**

You ask for a conversation with the chair, and he gives you twenty minutes on a Tuesday in his office, which smells of lemon and old paper. He is warm. He says the committee has "received a range of views," that these things happen at this stage, and that it would be inappropriate to discuss what individuals said. He thanks you for your patience. You ask whether you can see what was said. He tells you, with real regret, that the file belongs to the committee. You leave with a handshake and nothing in your hands.

**Week three, the leadership meeting**

By now the story has a shape. It is about a decision in the Northern division. It is about a former team member who left unhappily. It is about whether you are "as collaborative as people say." Each person who mentions it has a different version, and each of them heard it from someone else. Before the leadership meeting starts, the general counsel tells you, kindly, that it is "about integrity." You cannot take a document to that.

Nobody repeats it in front of you. You read it instead. In the meeting a director asks you a careful question about how you handle disagreement, and two people glance at each other when your division comes up. The peer who stopped at your door is suddenly very polite and holds the door for you. When you ask one of them straight out what is being said, the room goes still. Then he says, gently, "It's not one person. Everyone sees it." You hear your own voice sounding anxious, and you start to wonder what everyone sees.

**After the decision, your office**

The committee meets on Thursday. On Monday morning you read the announcement at your desk, with your coat still on. The external candidate is named. The following week, the whispers go quiet. The former team member you were told about calls and is happy to talk to you, and says he has never spoken about you to anyone. The search consultant says, awkwardly, that he must have misunderstood. People are very kind to you now.

**Eight months later, a hotel lobby**

Eight months later it's a Thursday in November, and you're in a hotel lobby in another city the night before a final interview at a smaller company, where nobody has ever heard of the Northern division. You have two printed copies of every reference letter in your bag. You have called each of the referees to tell them the date.

The recruiter's text lights your phone, and you look at it for a full minute before you open it. It says the start time has moved by half an hour. At the bar a man from the interview panel nods at you, coolly, you think, and turns back to his drink. You go over the nod: what he might have heard, and from whom. There is a name you could ask. You do not ask anyone anything. You tell yourself nobody would own up, and you sit on a lobby sofa with your phone face down on your knee.

You stopped accepting lunches. You walk into a room and read faces before you read the agenda, and you cannot tell any more whether a kind word is only kind. You have kept your job, your twenty-two years, and your name, and you are still waiting, every morning, for the next one.

## The Name

That's **The Reputation Assassination**.

## Definition

The Reputation Assassination is the sequence where a rumor is released at the moment it will do the most damage, spread so no source can be found, taken up by people with authority, allowed to harden into something that cannot be answered, confirmed by the group's own reactions, and then dropped once the decision is made, using Strategic Timing, Untraceable Seeding, Institutional Contamination, Narrative Mutation, Social Proof Amplification, and Strategic Dissolution to convert reliance on a reputation built over time into the loss of standing at the exact moment it matters most. It may benefit a rival or anyone who prefers a different outcome. It exploits the way confidential reference processes and small networks carry information without accountability. It is hard to detect because it leaves no author, and because the effect is visible only afterwards. In this sector it can cost a promotion, a board seat, a funding round or a client.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Strategic Timing** — A question lands at the start of a three-week window: on the Tuesday after the memo's reference period opens, a peer asks over lunch whether you are "doing okay" and whether the numbers in the Northern division are "all right." This functions by ensuring there is no time to investigate or answer before the decision. Concern rises when questions about your conduct appear only as a consequential decision approaches, though the underlying events are old.

2. **Untraceable Seeding** — Remarks turn up in separate rooms: a director at a reception "heard there were some concerns about judgment," and a search consultant mentions a reference call with "an interesting note," and none of them can say who said it. This functions by making the story seem independently corroborated while denying you a source to question. Concern rises when several people mention the same concern in the same weeks and none can say where it began.

3. **Institutional Contamination** — The story reaches the chair: he says the committee has "received a range of views," that it would be inappropriate to discuss what individuals said, and he thanks you for your patience. This functions by turning gossip into a recorded "view" that carries institutional weight. Concern rises when authority figures confirm that "views were received" but cannot or will not say what they were.

4. **Narrative Mutation** — The tale changes shape: from a decision in the Northern division, to a team member who left unhappily, to whether you are "as collaborative as people say," and by the third week it is "about integrity." This functions by moving the allegation beyond the reach of any factual rebuttal. Concern rises when each retelling is vaguer and more serious than the last, and the original detail disappears.

5. **Social Proof Amplification** — The room as the evidence: a careful question about disagreement, glances when your division comes up, a door held, and when you ask straight out, a gentle "It's not one person. Everyone sees it." This functions by making the target's reputation seem to have changed. Concern rises when you can see the reaction but never the claim.

6. **Strategic Dissolution** — After the decision the whispers stop: the former colleague says he never spoke about you to anyone, the consultant says he must have misunderstood, and people are very kind to you now. This functions by erasing the trail and making the target's concern look like paranoia. Concern rises when everyone who mentioned it now says they misunderstood, and the record of what was said to the committee remains out of reach.

## Why This Order

**Strategic Timing** comes first because everything else depends on the window. A rumor released a year before a decision can be investigated, rebutted and forgotten. Released three weeks before, it only has to be plausible. It has to come before the hints with no source, because a question raised three weeks before the decision leaves too little time to trace where it came from.

> Picture a rumour about you reaching your landlord a year before the lease is up. You have time to clear it. Now picture it landing the week before you sign. Flip it: if you kept hearing a vague rumour about yourself with no clear starting point, you would write down who said it and talk to your landlord directly.

**Untraceable Seeding** follows because the story must not have an owner. If it had a source, the target could go to that source. Seeding it across several channels at once also gives it the appearance of independent corroboration, which is what makes people take it seriously. It has to come before the chair's "range of views," because the chair can only say he has received views once several people are repeating something that none of them can source.

> You hear the same thing from three different people in one week, and each says someone else mentioned it. It feels like three sources, though you can't find the first. Flip it: if the person who decides about you had heard the story before anyone else, you would ask them, "Can I answer what you've been told?"

**Institutional Contamination** is the conversion step. Until an authority figure registers the story, it is gossip. Once the committee or its adviser has "received a view," it becomes a factor in the decision, and the confidentiality of the process then protects it from challenge. This is also the point where the pattern most resembles ordinary diligence, since committees genuinely do hear concerns and must weigh them. It has to come before the shift to "integrity," because that word only replaces the Northern division once the committee holds a file you cannot read.

> Say a neighbor tells the person who decides who gets the allotment plot that you leave a mess. They can't un-hear it, and they won't tell you who said it. Flip it: if the allotment decider first heard only "They're not the type for a plot," you would ask for one real example you can answer.

**Narrative Mutation** ensures the story cannot be answered. A specific claim about a decision can be rebutted with documents; a claim about integrity cannot. It has to come before the room going still, because a story that has become "about integrity" cannot be answered with a document, and asking about it only makes you look anxious.

> "You were late on Tuesday" can be answered with a train timetable. "You're just not reliable" cannot. Flip it: if the whole office went quiet when you walked in before anyone called you unreliable, you would ask a friendly colleague what was being said.

**Social Proof Amplification** then raises the cost of defending yourself, because the more you ask, the more you look anxious. It has to come before the whispers going quiet, because a room only seems to agree once the story is too vague to rebut, and the decision is announced before anyone has to answer for it.

> You walk into a room and the talking stops for a second. Nobody says a word, and you still know. Asking "what's going on?" only makes it worse. Flip it: if everyone swore they had said nothing about you before you even walked into the room, you would ask a trusted friend what was really going round.

**Strategic Dissolution** closes the loop and supplies the deniability: when the decision is made, the rumor has no further use and everyone can say they never intended anything. It has to come last, because it needs a decision already made: the consultant and the former team member can only say they meant nothing once the external candidate is named.

> After the party, everyone who was whispering says, "What? I never said anything." There is nothing left to point at. Flip it: if the whispering guests had already denied everything and then went quiet when you came in, you would ask a friend you trust what was said.

What would weaken the sequence: asking early, in writing, for the nature of any concern to be put to you; having a trusted sponsor on the committee side; keeping your own references prepared and informed; and noticing the pattern while the window is still open.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A leadership succession runs on a short window and a closed process. A committee has three weeks to gather views before a decision. References are taken in confidence, so that people will speak frankly, which means nobody being discussed can see them. And the candidates are few, senior and well known to the same small circle. None of this was built to hurt a candidate. It simply makes a late, unsourced remark both easy to pass along and impossible to check.

Strategic timing comes first because the window is fixed and public. Everything that happens inside it counts at full weight, and there is no time after it to correct anything.

Untraceable seeding comes second because the process asks several people to speak informally and in confidence. A remark that arrives from three directions looks like three independent views, though each may trace to the same passing comment.

Institutional contamination comes third because a committee that receives a view has to treat it as information. Once it has been received, the process that protects the sources also protects the content from challenge.

Narrative mutation comes fourth because a specific claim can be tested and a general one cannot. As the weeks pass and versions pile up, the story drifts from a decision in the Northern division to a word like integrity, which no document can answer.

Social proof amplification comes fifth because senior people read each other closely. A glance, a polite pause or a held door is a signal in a small group, and each one makes the next person more sure there is something to see.

Strategic dissolution comes last because the story only has a use until the decision. After that, nobody has a reason to keep it alive, and everyone involved can say honestly that they never intended anything.

Put it together and you get the order this setting needs: a short window, a story with no owner, a file that cannot be read, a claim that cannot be answered, a room that seems to agree, and a silence afterward. Nobody had to plan it. The structure rewards whatever is hard to trace, and it turns a person with a long-built name into one who reads every face before the agenda.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A fixed window, a confidential process and a small circle of senior people did the work, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a rival. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Desmond Achebe had been told he was one of two candidates for the regional director post, with the decision due at the end of the month. In the first week a colleague asked him in the corridor whether everything was all right with the quarterly numbers, and Desmond said it was. A second said she had heard there might be concerns about his judgment, and could not say what. A third said he might want to get ahead of something. None of them knew where it came from. When Desmond asked the chair of the panel, the chair said warmly that the panel had received a range of views and could not discuss them. By the third week the talk was no longer about numbers. A colleague told him, kindly, that it was about trust. In meetings people glanced at each other when his project came up, and a friend said, "It's not one person. Everyone sees it." The other candidate, Corinne Jankowski, was named on the Monday. The following week the same colleagues said they had never said anything, and were very kind. Corinne told a friend that it had been the quietest campaign she had ever run.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may sense that something has changed before anyone says it, and find it difficult to describe. Over time, the person may become guarded, rehearse answers to accusations that were never made, and watch faces for signs of what is known.

They may be tempted to defend themselves to everyone, which can look like the guilt the rumor implies. They may lose sleep, doubt their own memory of decisions, and feel an isolating shame about being discussed. Afterwards, they may find the damage cannot be traced, and that they are expected to move on.

## Institutional Echo

Confidential reference processes and committee deliberations can function as a channel for unverified information, because the same confidentiality that protects candidates also protects those who speak against them. Where reference calls are not documented, and concerns raised are not put to the candidate, an uncorroborated remark can influence a decision with no opportunity to respond.

Where an adviser or search consultant conveys a "general sense" without detail, the process may carry weight without evidence. Whether any particular process is fair, or any remark actionable, depends on jurisdiction and facts. The honest reading in many cases is that committees do hear real concerns and are right to test them, and that a candidate may simply be less favored. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the concern surfaced only near the decision, whether it changed form, and whether it vanished afterwards.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good name defends itself, and that if it were untrue it would not stick. Alongside it sits the belief that smoke means fire, and that serious people do not demand to know who said what.

The social value being exploited is trust in confidentiality: the idea that candid references protect everyone. The language that makes it sound normal includes "a range of views," "a general sense," and "it would be inappropriate." The public story is a careful succession process. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is a career-defining decision made on something the candidate was never allowed to see.

## Observable Indicators

1. Concerns about your character or judgment surface only in the weeks before a decision.
2. Several people mention the same concern in the same period, without a source.
3. A search consultant or committee member refers to an unspecified "note" or "sense."
4. Each retelling is vaguer, more general and more serious than the last.
5. The concern shifts from a specific event to your integrity or collaboration.
6. People become noticeably careful, polite or distant around you without explanation.
7. Requests to know what was said are met with reference to confidentiality.
8. The people who raised the concern do not recall it afterwards.
9. A former colleague named in the rumor says they never discussed you.
10. The matter disappears as soon as the decision is made.
11. No document records the concern or when it was received.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The slate memo, committee timetable and any written description of the reference process, with dates.
- A dated, factual note of each conversation where the concern was mentioned: who, when, where, and the words used, as close to verbatim as you can lawfully recall.
- Any written request you made to know the nature of the concern, and the reply.
- Messages from the search consultant, adviser or committee members about the process.
- Your own prepared references and the dates you briefed them.
- Names of anyone who says they did not speak about you, and anyone who heard the same story.
- Records of the date each version of the story reached you.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Succession processes legitimately gather candid views, and a committee may simply prefer another candidate. A concern may have a real basis, and a candidate may be asked about it in good faith. Vague feedback is often the result of confidentiality rules, not a campaign.

Specific lookalikes include:

- **Genuine diligence.** The concern is put to you, specific and testable, and you have a chance to respond.
- **Honest preference.** A different candidate is chosen on stated criteria that you can discuss.
- **Ordinary gossip.** Talk without a pattern, not tied to the timing of the decision.
- **Clumsy confidentiality.** Process rules prevent sharing detail, and are applied evenly.

Concern strengthens when: the same claim arrives through several channels in the weeks before the decision; the claim becomes vaguer and more serious with each retelling; people who mentioned it later deny or forget it; and no one will put the concern to you in writing.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Window Test:**
Ask: "Why is this being said now, and what has changed since last year?"
Concern rises if: nothing has changed except the date of a decision.

**SPOT IT — The Source Test:**
Ask: "Can anyone tell me who said it first?"
Concern rises if: every person heard it from someone else.

**SPOT IT — The Drift Test:**
Ask: "Has the claim become more general each time I hear it?"
Concern rises if: it began as a decision and became a character claim.

**SPOT IT — The Put-To-You Test:**
Ask: "Has anyone put the concern to me in a form I can answer?"
Concern rises if: the answer is always that it would be inappropriate.

**SPOT IT — The Afterglow Test:**
Ask: "Did the story disappear once the decision was made?"
Concern rises if: it did, and nobody remembers it.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write down every mention the same day**, with date, person, place and wording, and keep it apart from anything you send.
- **Ask in writing, calmly, for the nature of any concern to be put to you.** "If any concerns have been raised about me, I would welcome the chance to respond to them."
- **Do not chase the rumor to every person.** Tracking its source through many conversations can look like the anxiety the story predicts.
- **Brief your references and sponsors early**, with facts about your record, not about the rumor.
- **Check "what everyone thinks"** with two or three people you trust, one at a time.
- **Prepare a short, factual account** of the decisions or events most likely to be mischaracterized, with documents.
- **Ask who owns the process**: the chair, the adviser, or the committee. Raise the question with them rather than with the group.
- **Do not make public accusations** or blame named people without evidence. Doing so can create defamation exposure.
- **Seek independent advice** from your own counsel or a trusted adviser outside the company if the decision materially affects you.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** The same concern reaches you from several people in the weeks before a decision; the concern grows vaguer; you are told that views were received but not what they were.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A specific, damaging claim is attributed to you or about you but never put to you; the process refuses any chance to respond; the claim affects a decision on pay, role or position; someone credible offers to describe the same pattern. Relevant routes may include your own attorney, an independent director or the lead director, the company's audit or compliance committee, and an employment regulator where relevant.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, blackmail or extortion tied to what could be said; stalking or intimidation; fraud in progress; or self-harm statements by you or anyone involved. Prioritize safety and emergency support, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "If any concerns have been raised about me, I would welcome the chance to respond to them directly."
- "Could you tell me whether anything has been put on record, and whether I can see it or respond?"
- "I've heard several versions of a concern. I'd like to understand what, specifically, is being said."
- "I'm happy to provide documents on the Northern division decision if that would help."
- "I understand if you can't share names. Can you share the substance?"
- "I'd rather address a real concern than a general impression."
- "Could I confirm in writing what I've understood about how reference calls will be used?"
