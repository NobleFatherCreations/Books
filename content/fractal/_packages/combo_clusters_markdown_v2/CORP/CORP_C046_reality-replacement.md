# C046: Reality Replacement — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the official or most repeated version of events must be the true one, and a habit of doubting your own memory whenever it does not match.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of waiting for someone in charge to tell you what happened before you trust what you saw.
- A pull to stay silent when many voices at once insist on another story, because you cannot answer them all.
- An automatic thought, "maybe it didn't happen like that," arriving even about things you were there for.

**A point in a life.** The later years of a career.

**Where it happens.** The boardroom of a private company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're an independent director, fifty-eight, on the board of a privately held company, and you have just sat through the most difficult meeting you can remember. It lasted four hours. The long table smells of coffee gone cold, and the carpet tiles are the colour of wet slate. The board was asked to approve a services contract with a company connected to the chair's family. You asked three questions about the pricing and how it had been checked against other suppliers. Two other directors asked about the approval procedure. The chair said the matter had been reviewed. The chief financial officer said he was unable to confirm the comparison. The vote was close. You abstained, and you said clearly why: you had not been given enough to judge it. While the others gather their folders, you open a legal pad and, in your own hand, write six pages: the question, the answer, the abstention, your exact words. Nobody looks at you as you write. The chair's pen is already capped.

**Eleven days later, your kitchen**

The draft minutes arrive from the company secretary at ten past eight, with one line above them: "Please review and return any comments by Friday so we can adopt at the next meeting." You read them at the kitchen table with the radio off. The board "reviewed the proposed arrangement and noted the benchmarking prepared by management." Directors "expressed support." The vote is recorded as unanimous. One line says "one director raised some process queries which were addressed to her satisfaction." You did not say you were satisfied. You said the opposite. You fetch the legal pad from your bag and lay it beside the laptop. Your notes record the question about pricing, the answer, the abstention and your statement. None of it appears in the draft. You read the page twice, then close the laptop gently, as if it might wake someone.

**The following week, the director's room**

On Monday the general counsel stops in the doorway of the small director's room on the second floor. "Good, thorough discussion, that," he says, and taps the frame twice. On Tuesday the chief financial officer, whom you had pressed for an answer, leans in with his coffee. "We covered everything," he says. Both say it warmly, already turning to go. You find you have begun to nod before you have decided whether you agree.

That afternoon you ring one of the two directors who asked about procedure. He is warm and slightly distracted. "I thought it went fine," he says. "I think we all felt that, in the end." Within the hour the chair writes to the whole board: "A reminder that cordial relations depend on our all being constructive. I'm sure we're all aligned on the record." Your own recollection starts to feel like a position held by one person.

· · ·

You write to the company secretary to ask who prepared the draft and who saw it first. Her reply is polite and fast: "Minutes capture the sense of the meeting, not every contribution. The chair has reviewed the draft. It's standard." She adds that the management memo, a late document you have never seen, was "before the board." You take your meeting pack out of your bag and count the papers, squaring them on the desk. It is not among them.

**Wednesday evening, your kitchen**

You ring the same director again to say it plainly: you abstained. "Oh, did you? I'm sure the minutes reflect it." You say they do not. "I wouldn't worry. Minutes are a summary." You hear yourself say "of course," and put the phone down.

At nine an attachment arrives: a management memo of eighteen pages with tables, labelled "background to the board's deliberations." You scroll. Tables, appendices, notes to the tables. You cannot tell whether you are reading what was shown or what was assembled after, and there is no time before Friday to set one page against another. You open your legal pad and read your notes aloud to the empty kitchen. The words are what you wrote. They are your handwriting. And for a second you wonder whether the unanimous version is the one you misremember.

**Three months later, the boardroom**

Same long table, same slate carpet. The company secretary reads the previous minutes aloud, and when she reaches the vote you look at her face before you look at your own page. The chair asks whether everyone is content with the record. You hear yourself say, "If that is what's recorded." Nobody turns. Your hand rests flat on a folder you did not need to bring. The chair asks for any other business. You have none.

As the folders close, a director from the far end mentions the morning the fire alarm sent everyone into the car park last spring. You were there. You stood beside him. "Was that this year?" you ask, and wait for him to tell you.

Each night you still photograph the six pages and email them to yourself, and you no longer open the photographs. You sent your comments that Friday. They were two lines long, and you do not know what became of them. You have not changed a word of what you wrote. You have stopped being the person who was sure of it.

## The Name

That's **Reality Replacement**.

## Definition

Reality Replacement is the sequence where the record of an event is rewritten, repeated, endorsed by others and controlled, until an institutionally supported alternative account stands where the original was, using Revisionist History, Repetition as Truth, Social Proof, Information Control, Gaslighting, and Memory Flooding to convert reliance on institutional or authoritative accounts of events into loss of access to an accurate, shared version of what happened. It benefits whoever would be held to the original record, and it exploits the fact that, in an organisation, the official version is usually the one that survives. It is hard to detect because minutes are legitimately summaries, memory differs, and each person's account sounds reasonable. What can be lost is an accurate governance record, the standing of a dissenting voice, and in some roles, personal protection that depends on what the record shows. This script describes a hypothetical composite and implies no wrongdoing by any real board or person.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Revisionist History** — Minutes that differ from the meeting: the draft says directors "expressed support," records the vote as unanimous, and says one director's process queries were "addressed to her satisfaction," though you abstained and said the opposite. This functions by replacing what happened with a version that omits dissent. Concern rises when the written record differs on material points from what several attendees recall or noted.

2. **Repetition as Truth** — The same few words from several mouths: the general counsel says it was a "good, thorough discussion," the chief financial officer says "We covered everything," and you find yourself nodding. This functions by repeating the new account until it feels established. Concern rises when the same phrases appear in the words of different people.

3. **Social Proof** — A consensus offered: another director says "I thought it went fine. I think we all felt that, in the end," and the chair writes to the whole board "I'm sure we're all aligned on the record." This functions by showing that others accept the version, so doubting it feels isolating. Concern rises when agreement is cited as proof without examining the record.

4. **Information Control** — Control of the record: the company secretary says "The chair has reviewed the draft. It's standard," and says a management memo you never saw was "before the board." This functions by deciding who writes the record and what material counts as having been seen. Concern rises when the person drafting is not neutral and supporting documents appear after the fact.

5. **Gaslighting** — Doubt placed on your account: asked again about your abstention, the director says "I'm sure the minutes reflect it" and "Minutes are a summary," and you say "of course." This functions by inviting the person to doubt their own recollection or notes. Concern rises when a person who kept contemporaneous notes is encouraged to defer to the official account.

6. **Memory Flooding** — A flood of pages: an eighteen-page memo with tables, labelled "background to the board's deliberations," arrives with comments due Friday, and you cannot tell what was shown from what was assembled after. This functions by overwhelming attention with detail so that the person struggles to isolate what was actually before them. Concern rises when volume increases at the moment a dispute arises.

## Why This Order

**Revisionist History** has to come first because the pattern needs a document. Once a draft states what happened, it becomes the thing others will react to. The first version, if unchallenged, is the starting point of every later conversation. It has to come before the repeated reassurances, because the general counsel and the finance chief can only echo a record that has already been written down and sent to you.

> A friend "summarises" the group chat plan afterwards, and the place you never chose becomes the place "we all agreed on." Now the summary is what everyone replies to. Flip it: if your friend kept telling the group "we all agreed on that place" before any summary had been posted, you would scroll the chat, find no plan, and ask when it was agreed.

**Repetition as Truth** gives the version momentum. A phrase said by the general counsel, the finance chief and a fellow director begins to feel independent, though they may all be repeating the same source. It has to come before the chorus of agreement, because the director on the phone can only say "we all felt that" after hearing the same warm phrases from the counsel and the finance chief.

> Three people in your building tell you the lift "has always been slow." By the third time, you can't recall it ever being quick. Flip it: if the three people in your building each said "everyone agrees the lift is slow" before anyone had said it was slow even once, you would ride it and judge it yourself.

**Social Proof** is built on that illusion: a lone dissenter is up against what appears to be a consensus of recollection. It has to come before the narrowing of who sees which papers, because the secretary's "it's standard" only sounds ordinary once the board seems settled on a single account.

> Your housemates all say the pasta was fine, though you remember it was raw in the middle. You decide you must have fussier taste. Flip it: if your housemates scraped the pot clean before you could show them the raw middle, and only then all said it was fine, you would trust your own mouthful and ask to see the pot.

**Information Control** is the structural step. By choosing who drafts, who reviews first and what counts as having been before the board, the process makes the version hard to contest through normal channels. It has to come before the step that makes you doubt your own account, because "I'm sure the minutes reflect it" lands only once you cannot check a single page yourself.

> The one person who keeps the shared photo album decides which pictures go in and who gets the link. You can only argue about the pictures you are allowed to see. Flip it: if the album's keeper kept asking, kindly, "Are you sure that's the picture you took?" before choosing who gets the link, you would hold up the picture you remember and ask for the link.

**Gaslighting** then targets the dissenter's confidence. It has to come before the eighteen-page attachment, because the tables only bury the question once you have started to doubt the six pages of notes you would check them against.

> "Are you sure that's what I said?" It is asked kindly, again and again, until you go looking for the message to check your own recall. Flip it: if the person sent you a long run of old messages all at once, before asking even once "Are you sure that's what I said?", you would look for the one message and read it aloud.

**Memory Flooding** buries the question in volume so that the person cannot easily isolate what was seen. Intent would require additional evidence; each step can be explained innocently, which is how plausible deniability is built into the structure. It has to come last, because the attachment only bears down once "I wouldn't worry" has left you unsure of your own six pages, and Friday is a day away.

> You ask one question about a bill and are handed a forty-page folder with a smile. There is no way to find the answer before the deadline. Flip it: if you were handed the forty-page folder first, with a smile, and only then asked "Are you sure that was the charge you queried?", you would ask for the one page that answers it.

What weakens the sequence is a contemporaneous note, a practice of circulating a short list of decisions and votes straight after the meeting, minutes adopted only after a full read-through by every director, and independent legal advice about how a dissent is recorded. The key conversion point is the moment the dissenter doubts their own notes.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A company board is built around paper and a calendar. One office drafts the minutes, one office assembles the pack, and directors meet only every few weeks, so whatever is written down in between becomes the record by default. Comments are invited inside a short window, and the next meeting adopts whatever survived it. Nobody has to design a sequence for what follows: these facts produce it.

Revisionist History comes first because a draft has to exist before anything can be said about it. The secretary's office writes the first version quickly, and a quick summary leans toward what is easy to record: support, procedure followed, vote settled. Nothing here needs a villain. A dissent is awkward to phrase, and a unanimous vote is one clean line.

Repetition as Truth comes second because people who attended a long meeting talk about it in corridors, and the easiest thing to say about four hours is a short phrase. Phrases get passed along the way phrases do. By the third hearing they sound like separate observations.

Social Proof comes third because directors compare notes by phone, and each has a reason to say it went fine: the working relationship with the chair, a busy week, the wish to stay cordial. One careful account set beside three friendly ones looks like the odd one out.

Information Control comes fourth because the pack, the drafts and the late papers are all held in one office, and access follows whoever is asked to hold them. Which papers count as "before the board" is a filing decision made by whoever files. A question about who drafted it is answered with what is standard.

The step that makes you doubt your own account comes fifth because the record now looks settled and the people around you are calm. A director who says "I'm sure the minutes reflect it" is not inventing anything; he is describing how minutes usually go. That is enough to send you back to check your own recall.

Memory Flooding comes sixth because eighteen pages of tables and appendices are what a management team produces when asked for background, and adoption is set for Friday. Volume and a deadline together leave no time to set one page beside another.

Put it together, and the order is the order this setting needs: a first draft, a settled phrase, agreement among colleagues, a closed set of papers, a calm correction and a heap of material. It turns a director with six pages of notes into someone who waits to be told what happened.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A board calendar, a company secretary's office and a Friday deadline shaped the record, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newer colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Nia Castellano had been a project lead for four months when the programme director, Alistair Crane, sat in on her review of a supplier's proposal. She said the price sat well above the three quotes she had gathered and that she could not sign it off. That evening Alistair's summary went to the whole team: Nia "agreed the proposal was sound and raised only minor queries." By the next afternoon two colleagues she liked had used the word "sound" to her, one in the lift and one by message. A third told her the whole room had been behind it. Her folder of quotes was moved, and she found that the new link had gone to everyone but her; when she asked, she was told it was standard. Alistair stopped her in the corridor and said, kindly, "Are you sure that's what you said? I remember it differently." She went home and searched for her own message to check. On Thursday a workbook of sixty tabs reached her inbox, labelled "supporting analysis," with sign-off due on Friday.

Alistair was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that by Friday Nia would not trust her own notes.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may reread their notes obsessively, ask colleagues what they recall, and begin to wonder whether they are the outlier. They may feel isolated at the next meeting and reluctant to repeat their objection.

They may soften their comments in order to be accepted. They may feel professional anxiety about their own exposure if the record is wrong. And they may find it hard to say to anyone outside the board what has happened, because it sounds like a dispute about wording.

## Institutional Echo

Boards of private companies commonly rely on a company secretary or counsel to draft minutes that the chair reviews before circulation, and minutes are conventionally summaries rather than transcripts. Where drafting, review and adoption sit with the same group, where supporting papers can be attached after a meeting, and where individual dissent is recorded only on request, a record can diverge from events.

Minutes can also be inaccurate for innocent reasons, and a secretary may sincerely summarise the sense of a meeting. How dissent and abstentions must be recorded, what directors' duties are, and what legal weight minutes carry vary by jurisdiction and need qualified review. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether similar divergences occurred in other meetings and whether notes from several directors agree.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that the official record is the neutral truth, that good boards speak with one voice, and that a director who insists on the details is difficult. It contains real wisdom about unity, discretion and avoiding needless conflict.

The value exploited is trust in institutions. The public story is a board that governs calmly. The private loss, where the pattern is present, is that the one document designed to protect accountability becomes the instrument that removes it.

## Observable Indicators

1. Draft minutes omit or alter a recorded abstention, dissent or material question.
2. A vote is described as unanimous when a director recalls abstaining or objecting.
3. A statement is attributed to a director that they did not make.
4. The draft is reviewed by the chair or a conflicted party before it reaches other directors.
5. Papers are described as having been before the board that directors do not recall receiving.
6. Several people use the same phrases about the meeting afterwards.
7. A director with notes is told that minutes are only a summary.
8. Large volumes of material arrive after the dispute begins.
9. Requests to amend are met with comments about collegiality.
10. The deadline for comments is short.
11. A director who disputes the record is described as obstructive.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Your contemporaneous notes of the meeting in original form, with the date and time they were made.
- The board pack as received, with the date and time you received each paper.
- Every version of the draft minutes, with the covering message and any marked changes.
- Your comments on the draft and the replies.
- Any agenda, notice or resolution on the matter, including the related-party disclosure.
- Messages with other directors, the secretary and counsel about the record.
- The date and manner in which any late papers reached you.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Minutes are summaries, and a secretary may genuinely compress a long meeting. Directors can remember differently, particularly after four hours. Late papers may be added as honest background, and a chair may review drafts for accuracy. The discriminator is whether the divergence concerns a material point, whether it reflects one direction, and whether corrections are readily accepted.

Concern strengthens when: a recorded dissent or abstention is missing; late papers are described as having been before the board; and comments to amend are met with pressure on collegiality.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Dissent Test:**
Ask: "Is my abstention or objection in the draft?"
Concern rises if: it is gone or softened.

**SPOT IT — The Source Test:**
Ask: "Are people repeating one source, or recalling independently?"
Concern rises if: the same phrases appear.

**SPOT IT — The Papers Test:**
Ask: "Did I receive this paper before the meeting?"
Concern rises if: the timestamps say otherwise.

**SPOT IT — The Author Test:**
Ask: "Who drafted and reviewed this before it reached me?"
Concern rises if: someone with an interest in the outcome.

**SPOT IT — The Correction Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I ask for an amendment?"
Concern rises if: my tone becomes the topic.

## Chain Breakers

- **Make contemporaneous notes** and keep them in original form.
- **Write to the secretary promptly,** factually: "The draft does not record my abstention and the reasons I stated. Please amend."
- **Ask that your dissent be recorded** in the minutes by name.
- **Ask for a full read-through and adoption vote,** not adoption by default.
- **Request the board pack metadata:** dates and times papers were sent.
- **Ask other directors, individually,** what they recall and what they noted, in writing, without leading them.
- **Take independent legal advice** on how to protect your position and record dissent.
- **Do not alter the draft yourself, access board systems you are not authorised to use, or circulate the matter outside proper channels.**

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Draft minutes omit a recorded abstention or question; papers are described as seen that you did not receive; peers repeat identical phrases.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A request to amend is refused or met with pressure; the matter involves a related-party arrangement or other material decision; minutes are adopted despite your recorded objection. Consider independent legal advice, the senior independent or lead director, the audit or governance committee, the company's auditor, and, where relevant, a regulator or whistleblower channel.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, blackmail, fraud in progress, retaliation of a severe kind, or statements of self-harm. Prioritise safety and counsel, contact law enforcement or emergency services where danger is present, do not confront, and do not delete or alter records.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "The draft does not record my abstention or the reasons I gave. I would like both recorded."
- "Could you circulate the date and time each paper was sent to directors?"
- "I would like the minutes to be adopted after every director has had time to compare notes."
- "My notes show a different sequence. Could we reconcile them before adoption?"
- "I'd like to have my own counsel review how dissent should be recorded."
- "Could you confirm the benchmarking was before the board at the meeting?"
- "I'm not alleging anything. I'm asking for an accurate record."
- "Please note I am not able to approve these minutes as drafted."
