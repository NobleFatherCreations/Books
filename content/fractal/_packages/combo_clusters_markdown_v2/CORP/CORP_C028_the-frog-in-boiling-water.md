# C028: The Frog in Boiling Water — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a sense that whatever is happening now must be fine because it is only a little worse than yesterday, and a habit of measuring harm by the last step instead of the start.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of saying yes to one more small change at work or at home without noticing how far things have moved.
- A pull to stay in places where the air feels tense but nothing you could point to has happened.
- An automatic sentence, "it's not that bad, really," spoken about things you would once have refused outright.

**A point in a life.** The later years of a working life.

**Where it happens.** A small family manufacturer's finance function.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're fifty, a fractional chief financial officer, and you are the whole finance function of a small family manufacturer. The owner is in his sixties and runs the plant like a household. He knows every machine and every employee's children. He pays on time. He is also, you realise when you do the sum one evening, most of your income. The original statement of work is a clean document: eight hours a week, monthly close, cash forecast, a board pack for a family board that meets quarterly, a fixed monthly fee. You wrote it carefully, because earlier clients taught you to.

**The second month, your home office**

The first extra arrives on a Tuesday, by email, while you are still on your first coffee. "Could you also look at the supplier terms? Just a quick pass." It is two hours. You say yes, because it is a quick pass and he is a good client, and because saying yes to a good client is part of being good at this.

In the fifth month, at the same desk, with your headset on: "Could you sit in on the bank call? Just listen." You listen, and then the bank asks a question, and you answer it. Afterwards you think about amending the statement of work. You reply, "Happy to," and close the file.

**The eighth month, the plant's back office**

The back office smells of machine oil and instant coffee, and the fan in the old computer whirs. The weekly sync is meant to be forty-five minutes. It runs two hours. The bookkeeper is out for a fortnight, so you cover the payables, and when she comes back the payables are still on your list. Your Wednesdays no longer exist.

You mention, gently, that the fee was set for eight hours. He looks genuinely puzzled. "But you've been doing this for months," he says. "I thought it was part of what we do." He is not being clever. You can see that. You almost apologise.

In the eleventh month the sync ends with five new asks, each said lightly. "Nothing big." "Just while we're on the subject." "You know how it is." You feel your objection get smaller than the ask, and then you feel foolish for having had one. Nobody wants to be the person who counts minutes. You write all five down.

· · ·

By the third year you are a stand-in controller, a payroll approver and the person the bank calls when he is unreachable. At this same table you have trained his staff on the software the owner prefers, which you bought and set up yourself. You have told his daughter, who has recently joined the business, that you will help with her first year. You have turned down two other engagements because you could not take on more. Each of these was a yes that made sense when you said it.

**The fourth year, beside the press line**

You raise the fee in spring. The answer is kind: "Of course, we should look at that. After the audit." The audit finishes. The conversation does not happen.

In September you raise it again, on the plant floor, standing by the press line where he likes to talk. The machines thump under your feet. He looks at you kindly. "Four years," he says. "The staff, the systems, the family board. After all of that, we're not going to start counting hours now, are we?" You think of the four years, the software, his daughter, and of what it would mean to step away from all of it. You hear yourself say, "No."

Nobody on this floor raises a voice. Nobody has to. The meetings run long. On Fridays at six he meets you by the loading door with a folder, and sighs when you say Monday. You drive home with the folder on the passenger seat, and your shoulders are tight when you sit down to dinner.

**A year later, your home office**

A year later it is a Wednesday evening, and you open the original statement of work for the first time in a long while. The scope paragraph is four lines. Beside it you try to list what you actually do. You run out of room on the page.

A prospective client rang last month: a tidy business, a clear brief, a director who said in the first call exactly what they would never ask of you. It should have been a relief. Instead the calm of it made you uneasy, as if something were being kept from you, and you let their follow-up email sit until they stopped writing.

At home your brother-in-law has been sleeping in your spare room for eleven weeks. He came for a weekend. Then it was until his lease started, then until the lease was sorted. You have not asked when. Your partner asks if you mind, and you hear yourself say, "Honestly, it's fine. It's barely more than last month."

You skipped a friend's dinner last month to finish a forecast nobody needed by Thursday. When she rings to ask how work is, you say, "Busy, but fine," and you mean it.

You look at the four lines on the screen. You agreed to every single step. You never once looked back at where you started, and five years of your working life now belong to a job you never agreed to take.

## The Name

That's **The Frog in Boiling Water**.

## Definition

The Frog in Boiling Water is the sequence where an arrangement worsens by increments too small to trigger alarm, using Boundary Testing, Normalization, Minimization, Commitment Traps, Sunk Cost Exploitation, and Ambient Abuse to convert adaptability and tolerance for gradual change into the loss of a baseline sense of what is normal or acceptable. It is hard to detect because every step is reasonable, every request is friendly, and no single day is the day it went wrong. Agile scope evolution is real and often welcome. The concern is a drift in which the baseline moves, fee and scope separate, and the person who is changing the arrangement is not the person who is paying for it.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Boundary Testing** — Small requests that test the line: "Could you also look at the supplier terms? Just a quick pass," then "Just listen" on a bank call, each accepted with "Happy to" and no change to the statement of work. This functions by finding how far a small ask will go before it meets resistance. Concern rises when each ask is only slightly larger than the last.

2. **Normalization** — Extra work becoming routine: the bookkeeper cover, the two-hour sync, Wednesdays gone, and the owner saying, "I thought it was part of what we do." This functions by turning exceptions into expectations. Concern rises when you can no longer separate the original scope from the additions.

3. **Minimization** — Light dismissals of each ask: five new requests at the end of the sync, said as "Nothing big," "Just while we're on the subject," and "You know how it is." This functions by presenting each ask as trivial so that objecting seems petty. Concern rises when you hesitate to raise a concern because it would sound small.

4. **Commitment Traps** — Growing ties: you buy the owner's preferred software, train his staff, promise his daughter help with her transition, and turn down two other engagements. This functions by making withdrawal costly. Concern rises when exit would cost you both income and reputation.

5. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — Years used as a reason to stay: the fee talk is put off until after the audit, then answered with "Four years... we're not going to start counting hours now, are we?" This functions by tying past investment to continued acceptance. Concern rises when the case for not raising a fee is what you have already put in.

6. **Ambient Abuse** — A steady background pressure: long meetings, Friday six o'clock asks, a sigh when you say Monday, and an unspoken expectation that you will be reachable. This functions by wearing down resistance without any dramatic event. Concern rises when fatigue, not agreement, is the reason you say yes.

## Why This Order

**Boundary Testing** comes first because the structure needs to learn where the line is. A small request shows whether it will be refused, and a "yes" sets the new baseline. It has to come before the sync that runs two hours, because the payables only feel like part of the job once the quick pass and the bank call have each been met with yes.

> Picture a flatmate who asks to borrow one egg, then the pan, then the car "just to the shops." Each yes tells them where your line is not. Flip it: if the flatmate simply started using your car every day as if it had always been shared, before ever asking for one egg, you would notice at once and say it was yours.

**Normalization** follows because a baseline that is not repeated does not stick. Doing it three times makes it the job. It has to come before the five light asks, because "nothing big" only works once "part of what we do" has made the extra hours the baseline you measure from.

> Think of the one time you gave a colleague a lift, and how by the third week it was simply your route. Flip it: if your colleague said "it's only five minutes out of your way" before you had ever given them one lift, you would weigh the five minutes fresh and could easily say no.

**Minimization** protects the process from scrutiny: if each ask is trivial, objecting is disproportionate, and the person who objects looks rigid. It has to come before the software and the promise to his daughter, because each of those only felt small enough to agree to after every ask had been called nothing big.

> Imagine saying "it's only five minutes" about a favour. Now imagine being the one who says no to five minutes. Flip it: if the favour first came with things to buy and people to promise, before anyone called it "only five minutes", you would see its real size and could decide calmly.

**Commitment Traps** grow around the person as the work grows: new software, new relationships, new dependence. It has to come before "Four years, the staff, the systems", because that line needs the software you bought, the staff you trained and his daughter to point at.

> You join a friend's side project, buy the kit, tell their mum you'll be there. Every one of those makes leaving harder than starting was. Flip it: if your friend said "you've come this far" before you had bought any kit or told their mum anything, you would laugh, because you had not come anywhere yet, and could leave easily.

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** then converts all of that into a reason not to disturb the arrangement. It has to come before the Friday folders and sighs, because a sigh only weighs enough to keep you quiet once you have agreed not to start counting hours.

> It is like staying in a film you hate because you have already watched an hour. Someone says, "You've come this far," and the last hour feels smaller to give up than to sit through. Flip it: if the room felt tense and tiring from the first minute, before anyone said "you've come this far", you would notice the tension early and could get up and go.

**Ambient Abuse** is the fatigue that results, which makes saying yes easier than saying no. It has to come last, because the Friday folder only goes unremarked once you have said "No" by the press line and given up counting hours.

> After a long week of small pressures, even a simple "no" can feel like more work than another "yes." Flip it: if the long week of small pressures came first and only then someone said "you've come this far", you would see the tiredness talking, not the effort, and could rest before deciding.

The conversion point is when the fractional CFO stops comparing the work to the statement of work. Whether this reflects deliberate escalation or an owner who simply delegates freely is not obvious; intent would require additional evidence. What weakens it: amending the statement of work with every addition, noting hours monthly, and pricing the work as it grows.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A fractional finance role in a small family business sits on a few structural facts. The scope is written once, at the start, and nothing in the arrangement prompts anyone to look at it again. The business has no other finance staff, so every gap, from a bookkeeper's absence to a bank's question, flows to whoever is nearest and most capable. And one client can quietly become most of the adviser's income, which makes every request from that client feel more like weather than like a negotiation.

Boundary Testing comes first because small businesses run on small favours. An owner who runs the plant like a household asks for help the way family members do, a little at a time. Nobody is checking a line; the first quick pass simply shows that the scope will stretch.

Normalization comes second because repetition is how a small firm writes its job descriptions. There is no handbook. Whatever someone did three times becomes what they do, and an owner can honestly believe it was always part of the arrangement.

Minimization comes third because each new task really is small when it arrives. In a setting where nobody tracks hours, every ask is weighed alone, and objecting to something small looks out of proportion.

Commitment Traps come fourth because capable people fill gaps with lasting things. Software is bought, staff are trained, a daughter is promised help. Each makes the business run better, and each ties the adviser more tightly to it.

Sunk Cost Exploitation comes fifth because long relationships in family businesses are valued for their length. When a fee is finally raised, the history is the first thing to hand, and four years of shared work can be offered as a reason not to start counting now.

Ambient Abuse comes last because, by this point, the load itself produces the strain. Long meetings, Friday folders and quiet sighs need no harsh word. The tiredness alone makes agreeing easier than refusing.

Put it together and the order is the order an under-resourced business with a written scope nobody rereads tends to produce. Nobody has to plan it. It turns a careful professional who once wrote clean contracts into someone carrying a whole department for a fee set for a day a week.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A scope written once and never reread, a business with nobody else to absorb the gaps and an adviser dependent on one client did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a part-time office manager. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Gemma Hart took a two-day-a-week office manager role at a small consultancy run by Neil Torwick. In her first month Neil asked whether she could also order the team lunches, just this once. By the spring, booking everyone's travel had become "your bit, as always." Each new thing arrived as "a tiny one" or "two seconds of your time," and Gemma began to feel petty for noticing. She set up a new booking system on her own account and promised two new hires she would see them through their first month. When she asked, in the third year, for her days to be increased to match the work, Neil smiled and said that after everything she had built there, they were hardly going to start quibbling about days. After that, messages came on Sunday evenings, and a heavy sigh followed whenever she said she would deal with something on Monday. Nothing was ever said that Gemma could repeat to anyone.

Long after she left, a former colleague told her Neil had once joked that Gemma was the best full-time hire he had never paid for.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The consultant may lose track of what the original job was. Over time, they may stop turning down small requests and start apologising for raising fees. They may decline other work to protect time, feel tired without a clear cause, and doubt whether they are being unreasonable. They may tell friends it is not that bad, long after it has stopped being true.

## Institutional Echo

Consulting engagements often drift beyond their original scope, and scope creep is a recognised feature of professional services. Rolling addenda agreed by email can be a flexible and fair way to work, or can accumulate unpriced obligations. Anchor-client concentration is common among independent professionals and raises risk for both sides. Whether email addenda amend a statement of work depends on its terms and applicable law. Intent would require additional evidence: whether requests are routinely unpriced, whether the client acknowledges the additions, and whether a fee conversation is repeatedly deferred.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good consultant is flexible and that a loyal partner does not nickel-and-dime. Professional service rewards responsiveness. The language that makes it sound normal is partnership, "you know how it is," "we're family here." The public story is a trusted advisor embedded in a family business. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is an expanding job at an unchanged price and a baseline that moved without anyone deciding it should.

## Observable Indicators

1. Requests arrive as small additions to the existing scope.
2. Additions are agreed by email and the statement of work is not updated.
3. Weekly meetings run longer than planned and end with new asks.
4. Asks are described as "quick" or "nothing big."
5. Fee conversations are deferred to an event that then passes.
6. One client represents most of the consultant's income.
7. The client expresses surprise when the original scope is mentioned.
8. The consultant has turned down other work because of the client's demands.
9. The consultant has invested in tools, training or relationships specific to the client.
10. The original scope can no longer be listed from memory.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The original statement of work and every addendum, including email agreements, with dates.
- A monthly log of hours spent, by task, set against the original scope.
- Messages containing requests that expanded the scope.
- Records of deferred fee conversations and what they were deferred to.
- Invoices against hours worked.
- Records of other work turned down and why.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Scope evolves in healthy engagements, and a client who gives more work may be a good partner. The discriminator is **whether additions are priced and acknowledged**. A genuine evolution can be written down, adjusted in fee, and agreed by both. This pattern leaves the additions unpriced and the fee conversation always one event away.

Benign lookalikes:

- **Agile scope.** Change orders agreed in writing are normal.
- **Genuine emergencies.** A bookkeeper's absence can create real short-term needs.
- **Relationship generosity.** A consultant may choose to give extra time.
- **Client unawareness.** An owner may not realise how much has accumulated.

Concern strengthens when: additions stay unpriced after repeated requests; the client treats the original scope as forgotten; a fee conversation is repeatedly deferred.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Scope Test:**
Ask: "Can I list what I do now against the statement of work?"
Concern rises if: the list is much longer and the fee has not changed.

**SPOT IT — The Week Test:**
Ask: "How has my week changed since the first month?"
Concern rises if: days have disappeared into client work.

**SPOT IT — The Fee Test:**
Ask: "When did I last raise the fee, and what happened?"
Concern rises if: the conversation was always deferred.

**SPOT IT — The Concentration Test:**
Ask: "What share of my income depends on this client?"
Concern rises if: it is so large that I cannot say no.

## Chain Breakers

- **Update the statement of work whenever scope grows,** even by email: "Adding this at X hours per month."
- **Log hours monthly** and send a one-line summary.
- **Price additions at the moment of the request:** "Happy to. That adds about four hours a week; shall I adjust the fee?"
- **Build a second client** before you need one.
- **Set a fee review date** in the agreement.
- **Take advice on the termination and notice terms**.
- **Talk to a peer outside the client's world** about what is normal.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Scope has grown by more than a third without a written change; a fee review has been deferred twice; additions are called part of the job.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The client disputes the original scope or refuses payment for added work, or retaliates when you set a boundary. Relevant routes may include a commercial attorney, an independent accountant, mediation if the agreement provides for it, and a small-business protection authority.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, or a mention of self-harm by you or anyone involved. Contact local emergency or crisis services and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Happy to. That would add about four hours a week. Shall we adjust the fee?"
- "Could we update the statement of work to include this?"
- "I'd like to review scope and fees at our next sync. Can we put it on the agenda?"
- "To confirm what we agreed today: I'll cover payables for two weeks, billed at the hourly rate."
- "I want to keep supporting you, and I need the fee to match the work."
- "Can we set a quarterly review of scope?"
