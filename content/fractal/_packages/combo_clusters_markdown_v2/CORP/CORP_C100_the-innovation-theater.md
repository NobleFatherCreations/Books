# C100: The Innovation Theater — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that a shiny new programme or tool means the real problem is being solved, and a habit of trusting the dashboard over what you see with your own eyes.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of greeting each new launch with hope, then feeling tired when the old problem is still there.
- A pull to trust usage numbers and upbeat words over your own careful watching of the people in front of you.
- An automatic thought, "maybe the system knows better than I do," when the screen disagrees with what you saw.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, two decades into the work.

**Where it happens.** An industrial manufacturer's digital transformation office.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a digital transformation lead at an industrial manufacturer, forty-four, with twenty years behind you in plants and planning offices. You were hired to fix a real problem. Unplanned downtime on the older lines was costing the company shifts every month, and the maintenance supervisors were working from paper logs and memory. You knew it, they knew it, and in your first week you wrote a four-page note that said so.

**Month three, the steering committee room**

That note disappears into a larger deck. By the time it reaches the steering committee, the problem has a new name: "lack of a unified digital operating layer." Your four pages have become a footnote under it. You sit at the long table, in a room that smells of toner and burnt coffee, and hear your own problem described in someone else's words, and you cannot find the sentence to take it back.

It has also acquired a solution. A consulting firm that ran the workshop turns out to have a preferred vendor, and the vendor's platform turns out to be, by happy coincidence, the answer. The procurement is fast. The business case is thirty slides. Nobody on the committee has seen a comparison with alternatives, and when you ask, a senior consultant says that the options analysis informed the recommendation. Everyone nods, and you find you are nodding too.

**Month seven, a workshop room**

The platform arrives with a vocabulary. It is an "intelligent plant orchestration layer." The sensors are "edge-native cognitive nodes." The dashboards have "predictive insights," though when you read the documentation, the prediction is a threshold alert of the kind your maintenance team already built in spreadsheets. You say so at the vendor's training session, mildly. The vendor's account director smiles and calls that an exciting way of seeing the foundation. Your face warms, and you do not say it again.

Then come the metrics. Months in, the rollout is judged on active users, dashboards created, and the share of lines connected. Downtime, the original problem, appears on slide eleven in a small font with a footnote on data quality. You look around the room. Nobody else is looking at slide eleven.

**Month eleven, the plant floor**

You walk down to line four in your steel-toed boots, past the old paper logs now boxed up by the board. The supervisors, who know the lines better than any screen, are asked to log faults in the platform instead of on the floor board. Their experience is being replaced by tickets. Several are told, kindly, that the platform's alerts take precedence over a supervisor's call to stop a line. One of them tells you that the alert fired after the bearing had already seized. He says it quietly, looking at his boots.

**Month fourteen, the steering committee room**

Today you present to the steering committee. The vendor has prepared a deck: "Adoption Dashboard: Impact Realised." Active users have quadrupled. Dashboards created: three hundred and twelve. A green arrow points to the phrase "transformation momentum."

You ask a question, as precisely as you can. "Has unplanned downtime on the legacy lines changed since go-live?" The sponsor says that's an important question and that the working group will certainly look at it. The vendor lead says downtime is influenced by many factors. The consultant says the platform is "an enabler, not a guarantee."

You are thanked for your rigor. You are asked to chair the Value Realisation Task Force. The room moves on to the next item, a celebration of an innovation award the programme has won. The slide has a photograph of the plant manager holding a trophy beside a screen showing a dashboard.

You look at the green arrow. You think of the bearing.

**A year later, a different plant**

A year later you are on another factory floor, on the morning a new scheduling system goes live. A banner says "Smarter Shifts." You feel it rise in your chest, the old hope, and you are surprised by how much you want it to be true. Near line four a bearing is whining, thin and high, a sound you would know in your sleep. You look up at the screen above the line. It shows a green tile and a number: healthy.

You stand there with your head tilted, listening, then at the screen, then listening, until a technician asks if you need anything. You say no. You write the screen's number on your clipboard, not the sound. By ten the tile is still green. By eleven the line is down.

Your emails these days are three lines long. You have stopped writing four-page notes. Nobody blames you. You heard it first, and you wrote down what the screen told you.

## The Name

That's **The Innovation Theater**.

## Definition

The Innovation Theater is the sequence where an organisation performs transformation while structurally preventing it, using Problem Identification (Selective), Vendor Solution Adoption, Language Inflation, Metric Substitution, Teacher Displacement, and Critique Absorption to convert trust that visible change reflects substantive change into the loss of accurate information about whether a real problem is being solved. It may benefit vendors, consultants, and sponsors who gain from a launch, an award, or a renewal, and it can also arise without any plan, through procurement habits and the pressure to show progress. It exploits the genuine desire to modernize. It is hard to detect because activity, spending, and enthusiasm are all real. What may be lost is money, operational capacity, the experience of practitioners, and the opportunity to fix the original problem. Intent would require additional evidence; poor transformation programmes are common and often well-meant.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Problem Identification (Selective)** — Your note on downtime and paper logs reaches the steering committee renamed as "lack of a unified digital operating layer," with your four pages reduced to a footnote. This functions by choosing the version of the problem that fits an existing product. Concern rises when the problem statement changes between the practitioners' note and the committee deck, and the change points toward a purchasable solution.

2. **Vendor Solution Adoption** — A fast purchase from the consultant's preferred supplier: thirty slides of business case, no comparison with alternatives, and "the options analysis informed the recommendation." This functions by moving the decision from evidence to procurement momentum. Concern rises when the consultant who framed the problem also recommends the vendor, and alternatives are described as "considered."

3. **Language Inflation** — Grand words for ordinary things: an "intelligent plant orchestration layer," "edge-native cognitive nodes," and "predictive insights" that are threshold alerts like those your team built in spreadsheets. This functions by letting marketing language disguise product limitations. Concern rises when technical questions are answered with vision statements.

4. **Metric Substitution** — Counting activity: active users, logins, dashboards created and the percentage of lines "connected," while downtime sits on slide eleven in a small font with a footnote on data quality. This functions by replacing evidence of outcome with evidence of activity. Concern rises when the original measure, such as downtime, is placed last or footnoted.

5. **Teacher Displacement** — Screens over the floor: supervisors log faults as tickets and are told the platform's alerts take precedence over their call to stop a line, though one alert fired after the bearing had seized. This functions by shifting authority from the people who observe the work to the system that records it. Concern rises when practitioners are asked to follow alerts over their own observations, and the consequences of a late alert fall on them.

6. **Critique Absorption** — Your question is thanked and filed: the sponsor says the working group will "look at" downtime, and you are thanked for your rigour and asked to chair the "Value Realisation Task Force." This functions by converting criticism into a committee role that does not change decisions. Concern rises when the critic is promoted into a structure with no authority over scope or spending.

## Why This Order

**Selective problem identification** has to come first, because every later step depends on a problem that is shaped to fit a solution. If the original note, with its focus on paper logs and downtime, had remained the brief, a procurement for a general platform would have been difficult to justify. It has to come before the preferred vendor's platform, because a platform only looks like the fix once the problem has been renamed as a missing digital layer instead of paper logs and downtime.

> You tell the garage the car will not start because the battery is flat. They tell you it is "a powertrain issue" and quote for a new engine. Flip it: the mechanic at the garage offers you a new engine, the only one in stock, at three times your budget before saying what is wrong, and you ask him what it would fix.

**Vendor Solution Adoption** then follows with a logic of its own: once the problem has been framed as a missing platform, only a platform can fix it. Procurement is quick and the business case is long, and length is mistaken for rigour. Anyone who now asks whether the platform is the right answer is questioning a decision, not a proposal. It has to come before the grand vocabulary, because the big names only protect the purchase once the committee has already agreed that this platform is the answer.

> A shop assistant says, "Let me show you our best one." It is the only one on the shelf, and it costs three times what you planned. Flip it: the shop assistant calls the only item on the shelf "our most exciting range" before you have asked about it, and you ask what it does, in plain words.

**Language Inflation** protects the purchase. If the product is described in terms that exceed what it does, no one is lying exactly, and every shortfall can be explained as the user's failure to see the potential. It also makes questions feel unsophisticated, which weakens independent evaluation among people who do not want to look behind the curve. It has to come before the adoption counts that replace downtime, because a shortfall is easier to wave away once the platform has been described in words bigger than what it does.

> The holiday brochure calls the building site next door "an exciting regeneration quarter." Pointing at the cranes makes you the one who does not get it. Flip it: the brochure proudly reports how many guests booked, and only then calls the building site next door "an exciting regeneration quarter"; you ask how many of them returned a second year.

**Metric Substitution** is the pivot. Once adoption counts replace outcome counts, success can be declared at any point. The vendor and sponsor now share an interest in the activity numbers, and plausible deniability is complete, since the dashboard is accurate about what it measures. It has to come before supervisors logging faults as tickets, because tickets only seem to matter once the rollout is scored by activity and not by whether lines stop.

> A gym proudly reports how many people joined this year. It does not say how many ever came back. Flip it: the gym's new screen tells the instructor to put a member on a machine he knows is broken, and he does, though he has coached here for thirty years; you ask what the screen is counting.

**Teacher Displacement** is where the cost lands on people, because judgement that corrected the old system is subordinated to the new one. It has to come before the task force, because a critic is only worth inviting in once the supervisors' own calls have stopped counting and the question has nowhere else to go.

> A new satnav tells the taxi driver to take a road he knows is closed. He takes it. He has driven these streets for thirty years. Flip it: the taxi driver tells the company its satnav sends him down closed roads and is invited onto a panel, thanked every month, and he asks at the next meeting what has changed in the satnav.

And **Critique Absorption** closes the loop: an informed critic is thanked, appointed, and positioned to be responsible for realised value without the power to decide. It has to come last, because handing you the task force only contains your question once the dashboard, the tickets and the alerts are already treated as the answer.

> You complain about the service and are invited onto the "customer voice panel." You are thanked at every meeting, and the service stays the same. Flip it: you complain about the service and are invited onto the "customer voice panel," thanked at every meeting, and only then does the panel receive a fixed script it must follow word for word; you ask who wrote it.

The key conversion point is the green arrow slide, where activity is presented as impact and a question about downtime is answered with "it's an enabler." What would weaken the sequence: a baseline for the original problem recorded before purchase; an options comparison with named alternatives; and a rule that adoption metrics are never reported without the outcome metric beside them.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A large manufacturer has a real problem that is dull to describe, and a committee that decides things by deck. Procurement runs on business cases, and a long business case reads as a careful one. Consulting firms that run workshops often have a vendor they know well. A platform can produce activity counts on its own, without anyone visiting a line. And an innovation award is good news that travels. Those facts alone produce the sequence you lived through.

Problem Identification (Selective) comes first because a steering committee cannot buy a solution to a note about paper logs. It can buy a solution to a missing digital layer. Renaming the problem is simply the first slide of the deck, and the four pages that started it end up as a footnote.

Vendor Solution Adoption comes second because once the problem is a missing platform, the workshop's own vendor is the fastest route. Procurement is quick, the business case is long, and nobody on the committee has time to run a comparison.

Language Inflation comes third because a platform has to be described to a plant in words that suit a board. Ordinary threshold alerts get grand names, and a question about the foundation sounds unsophisticated in a room where nobody wants to look behind the curve.

Metric Substitution comes fourth because the vendor's platform can report logins and dashboards with no effort, while downtime takes weeks to show up. Activity is counted because it is available, and success can be declared at any point.

Teacher Displacement comes fifth because a platform that sends alerts needs people to obey them. The supervisor's call to stop a line was the old system's safety valve, and it now sits underneath a ticket.

Critique Absorption comes last because the one person who asks about downtime must be somewhere sensible. A task force with a good title and no budget thanks the critic every month while the dashboard goes on.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a new name for the problem, a purchase, a new language, a new count, a new chain of command, and a place for doubt. What it turns you into is someone who reads the screen louder than the line.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A steering committee that decides by deck, a procurement cycle and a platform that counts its own use did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to an analyst. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ravi Dolan was an operations analyst at a distribution company, and he wrote a short memo about late deliveries from the loading docks. By the time it reached the steering group, a vice president, Marguerite Lindahl, had retitled the problem as "fragmented fulfillment orchestration," and his memo was listed in an appendix. A consulting firm presented a scheduling platform from a vendor she had hosted at a conference, with a long business case and no comparison. When Ravi asked about alternatives, he was told the options analysis informed the recommendation. At rollout the platform was an "intelligent dock orchestration layer," which, Ravi saw, was a rules table. Marguerite called his observation an exciting way of looking at it. The rollout was reported by logins and dashboards created, and late deliveries sat on slide nine in small type. Dock leads were told to log delays in the platform instead of on the whiteboard, and its alerts overruled their calls. At the next review Ravi asked about late deliveries. He was thanked for his rigor and appointed to chair an "Impact Task Force."

Marguerite was never confused about what she was doing. She told a friend that the easiest critic to manage is the one you give a title.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may find themselves defending a project they privately doubt. Over time, they may begin to speak in the vocabulary, since the alternative is sounding unsophisticated. Practitioners may stop reporting what they see, because reports now go into tickets that nobody reads.

The transformation lead may feel pressed between sponsors who need good news and floor teams who need the problem solved. They may accept the task force role because refusing seems ungracious, and then notice that their name is on a result they did not choose.

## Institutional Echo

Large enterprises can slide into this pattern through procurement cycles, annual budget rhythms, and the pressure to show progress to a board. Consultants who frame a problem and then recommend an implementation partner face a conflict of interest worth examining, even if it is disclosed. Vendor-supplied dashboards often report platform usage, because that is what the platform can measure.

Innovation awards, steering-committee reviews, and renewal negotiations can reward visible adoption. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether alternatives were evaluated, whether a baseline was set, and who benefited from the framing.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that technology is progress and that hesitation is resistance. Beneath it sits the older belief that a modern company must be seen to be modern, and that failing to adopt the newest thing is a form of decline. The language is "digital," "intelligent," "future-ready."

The value being exploited is real: plants do need better data, and many transformations succeed. The public story is a company that embraced the future. The private cost, when this pattern operates, is a line that still goes down, and a team that has stopped saying so.

## Observable Indicators

1. The problem statement in the final deck differs from the original practitioner note.
2. A consultant who framed the problem also recommends a vendor.
3. No documented comparison with alternatives exists, or it is described but not shown.
4. Product descriptions use terms that documentation does not support.
5. Success metrics count logins, users, dashboards, or connected assets.
6. The original outcome measure is absent, delayed, or footnoted.
7. Practitioners are told to follow system alerts over their own judgement.
8. Questions about outcomes are answered with statements of intent.
9. Critics are given roles without decision rights.
10. Awards or announcements are cited as evidence of results.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Your original problem note, and every later version of the problem statement.
- The options analysis or procurement file, or the record of your request for it.
- Vendor documentation and marketing claims compared with the product's actual functions, with dates.
- The baseline for the original problem, such as downtime logs, before go-live.
- Each version of the adoption dashboard and the definitions behind each metric.
- Notes of incidents where an alert and a practitioner's judgement differed.
- The task force charter, if offered, including its authority and reporting line.

Plus the Universal Evidence list in the Sector Standards.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Transformation programmes take time. Adoption is a legitimate early metric. Some platforms really do deliver, and the language of a vendor does not by itself prove the product is weak.

Benign lookalikes specific to this pattern:

- **Adoption as a leading indicator.** Usage may need to rise before outcomes can.
- **Consultants with a preferred partner.** A disclosed relationship, with comparison, can be fair.
- **Marketing vocabulary.** Vendors use inflated language for products that still work.
- **Phased outcomes.** Downtime benefits may legitimately arrive after training.

Concern strengthens when: a baseline for the original problem was never set; outcome questions are consistently deferred; and practitioners' warnings about missed alerts are not logged.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Baseline Test:**
Ask: "What was the original problem, and what was its measure before launch?"
Concern rises if: no baseline exists or the measure is missing from the deck.

**SPOT IT — The Alternatives Test:**
Ask: "Which other options were compared, and where is the comparison?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "it informed the recommendation."

**SPOT IT — The Activity Test:**
Ask: "Does this number show people using the tool, or the problem getting better?"
Concern rises if: the figures count usage only.

**SPOT IT — The Floor Test:**
Ask: "What do the people who run the line say?"
Concern rises if: their warnings are absent from the report.

## Chain Breakers

- **Restore the baseline.** Reconstruct and date the original downtime figures from logs you are authorised to use.
- **Ask for the problem statement in writing.** "Could we confirm the problem this platform is meant to solve, and the measure that shows it?"
- **Request the options comparison.** "May I see the alternatives evaluated and the criteria?"
- **Pair every adoption metric with an outcome metric.** Propose a one-line rule for all steering reports.
- **Log practitioner overrides.** A simple record of when an alert and a supervisor's call differed, with outcomes.
- **Set the task force terms before accepting.** Ask for authority, a sponsor, a budget, and a date for a stop-or-continue decision.
- **Ask for an independent review.** Internal audit, an independent director, or an outside engineer not connected to the vendor.
- **Do not overclaim.** State what the data shows and what it does not, and avoid accusing the vendor or consultant of bad faith.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** The baseline is missing; outcome measures are repeatedly deferred; or practitioners are told to follow alerts over their judgement.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Material spend continues despite no outcome improvement; a related-party or undisclosed relationship between the consultant and the vendor is found; or records are being changed to show better figures. Routes include internal audit, an audit committee, independent directors, a compliance function, and counsel where contracts or disclosures are in question.

**HIGH → URGENT:** A safety-critical failure follows ignored practitioner warnings, such as an injury risk on a line; falsified safety or maintenance records; or retaliation against a person who raised a safety concern. Prioritise safety: stop the line through the proper authority, contact local emergency services, and the relevant workplace safety authority. Do not confront anyone. Do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we agree on the problem we're solving and the measure that shows it, before the next review?"
- "May I see the alternatives that were evaluated and how they were scored?"
- "Adoption numbers are useful. Could we show unplanned downtime beside them in every update?"
- "What would we expect downtime to be at six months, and what would tell us we're off track?"
- "I'd like the supervisors' feedback on missed alerts recorded in the report."
- "Before I chair the task force, could we write down its authority and its decision date?"
- "Could someone independent of the vendor and the consultant review the results?"
