# C178: The Big Ask — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that agreeing to something small means you must agree to the next, and a reflex to judge a fair deal by the first number you heard.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A relief when a large demand shrinks, and a sense that the smaller one must be a fair middle ground.
- An automatic sentence when you hesitate, "but I already said yes to the rest," that carries you further.
- A feeling of guilt when you finally refuse, and a sense you owe something for every step before.

**A point in a life.** The middle years of a career.

**Where it happens.** A software firm's partnership negotiation.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're the head of partnerships at a mid-sized software company, thirty-six, and you have wanted this deal for two years. A larger platform company has a distribution channel your firm could never build alone. If your product appeared in their marketplace, your sales cycle would shorten by months. You have said this at three board meetings.

**March, your car**

The negotiation begins with a series of calls. Their lead is Anders, the sort of negotiator people describe as reasonable. He listens, restates your position better than you stated it, and says he wants a partnership, not a vendor relationship. Then the first draft term sheet arrives, and you open it on your phone in the car park, with the engine off and rain ticking on the roof. It makes you laugh out loud, alone. Worldwide exclusivity, five years, no right to list on any competing marketplace. A revenue share of forty in every hundred pounds to them on all your sales, not just those through their channel. You write back politely that this is far outside anything you can accept. Anders replies within the hour, gracious. He says this is the starting draft, that his own leadership asked him to open high, and that he will work on it.

**April and May, your office**

Three weeks later the revised paper arrives. Exclusivity is now two years and limited to your core product. The revenue share is twenty-two in every hundred pounds, on marketplace sales only. Anders writes: "I fought hard for this. I think you'll agree it's a significant move." And you do agree. Compared to the first draft it is a different universe. Your shoulders come down. You read the new numbers for an afternoon at your desk, with the blinds half-down, and find nothing wrong with them. You do not think about what a fair number would have looked like before you saw the first one.

Then come the small yeses. Could your team provide a technical contact for the integration, ahead of signature, so work can begin? Yes. Could you share your anonymised pipeline data so their team can size the opportunity? Yes, it's reasonable. Could you hold the date of the joint announcement, since their marketing team needs to plan? You hold it. Could you take the draft memorandum to your board for preliminary comment? You do, and your chair says congratulations. Each request is small and each answer is yes. Over those weeks, two of your engineers spend six weeks on integration work for a partner with whom you have no signed agreement.

**The Thursday in June, their glass room**

The final meeting is at their offices, in a glass room on the fourteenth floor. The partnership memorandum is on the table. The twenty-two is in it. So is a new clause, a co-marketing exclusivity that bars you from promoting any competing channel for three years. Anders says the clause is only open until five on Friday because a rival is waiting. Regretfully, if the memorandum is not signed by then, the opportunity moves to the next company. Your mouth goes dry.

You ask for a week for counsel to review the exclusivity. He says that he understands, truly, but the clause expires Friday. He mentions, lightly, the integration work already completed, and the board's expectations, and how disappointing it would be to lose it now. Heat climbs your neck.

You look at the engineers' calendar invites. At the draft with your chair's handwriting on it. At the number, twenty-two, which you have been calling reasonable for weeks. You try to remember if anyone ever asked you what a fair number was, and cannot.

**The Friday, the car park**

You have not slept past four. You are sitting in the car park with the engine off, in the same space where you laughed in March. The engineers' calendar invites are open on your phone. Your chair texts: "Great news. We should celebrate once it's signed." You type back "twenty-two is reasonable," and it looks like someone else's sentence. The clock on the dashboard says 08:41. You do not start the car.

**A year later, your kitchen**

It is a Sunday in October, and a builder's quote lies on the table: forty thousand pounds for the extension. You say you cannot, and he says he will see what he can do, and by Tuesday it is thirty-one. Your whole body lets go. You thank him twice and shake his hand in the hall before the number has settled, and only afterwards, while you are washing up, do you realise you have never asked another builder.

Your sister rings to ask whether she can borrow your car for a week, after the weekend with the dog and the loan of your drill, and you hear yourself say, "Well, I have been helping already," before you have thought. When you finally say no to something small, a neighbour's request about the shared fence, you spend the evening composing an apology nobody has asked for, and you cannot work out what you owe, or to whom.

On Fridays at five you still look at the clock, and you do not know what you are waiting for to expire.

## The Name

That's **The Big Ask**.

## Definition

The Big Ask is the sequence where an outsized request makes the real one look reasonable, the first number frames everything after it, each small yes is used to justify the next, and refusal is finally met with a deadline and a loss, using Door-in-the-Face, Anchoring, Commitment Traps / Foot-in-the-Door, Ultimatums and Obligation Enforcement to convert the wish to be fair and reasonable into agreement to terms the person never independently judged. In this sector it concentrates in partnership and distribution deals, advisory fee and equity negotiations, side letters and consulting scope, where the first number is routinely high and the reasonable response is to meet it halfway. It is hard to detect because opening high is ordinary negotiation, concessions are real, and the person's own cooperation is used as the evidence that the next step is fair.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Door-in-the-Face** — An opening draft of worldwide exclusivity for five years, no listing on any competing marketplace and a forty percent revenue share on all your sales, which you refuse with a laugh, and he calls it the starting draft his leadership asked him to open high on. This functions by making the real ask feel like a concession from the other side. Concern rises when the first demand is not seriously expected to be met and the later one is presented as a favour.

2. **Anchoring** — A revised draft arrives with two years' exclusivity on the core product and twenty-two percent on marketplace sales, and Anders writes "I fought hard for this," which you see as a different universe from forty. This functions by shifting the discussion from what is fair to how far the number has fallen. Concern rises when no independent benchmark is offered and the other side's first figure becomes the reference point.

3. **Commitment Traps / Foot-in-the-Door** — Small requests before signature, each answered yes: a technical contact, anonymised pipeline data, a held announcement date and a draft shown to your board, and by June two engineers have spent six weeks on integration. This functions by building investment and a self-image of cooperation, so that refusing the larger request feels inconsistent. Concern rises when meaningful work, data or reputational commitment is requested before terms are signed.

4. **Ultimatums** — At the Thursday meeting a new co-marketing exclusivity clause sits in the memorandum, open only until five on Friday because a rival is waiting, with the opportunity moving to the next company if it is not signed. This functions by compressing review to the point where independent advice cannot reasonably be obtained. Concern rises when the deadline sits on the clause that matters most and its reason cannot be tested.

5. **Obligation Enforcement** — When you ask for a week for counsel, he says he understands but the clause expires Friday, and mentions the integration work already completed, the board's expectations and how disappointing it would be to lose it now. This functions by treating your past cooperation as a debt that the next term must repay. Concern rises when sunk work is cited in place of reasons for the term.

## Why This Order

The oversized demand has to come first because everything else is measured against it. If the first proposal had been twenty-two percent, you would have scrutinised it. Because it arrived second, after forty, it arrived as relief. This is the real mechanism of the **Door-in-the-Face**: it is not that the first demand is accepted, but that it changes what the second demand is compared to. It has to come before the second paper, because the revised terms only feel like a gift once the first draft with worldwide exclusivity has made them look small.

> Your teenager asks to stay out until three. You say no. Then "midnight?" sounds like a lovely compromise, although an hour earlier you would have said no to midnight. Flip it: if your teenager had first said "my friends all stay out till dawn" before asking anything, you would set your own time without measuring it against theirs.

**Anchoring** follows immediately and does the quieter work. Once a number has been named, later discussion is about distance from it. You stop asking what a fair share is, and ask whether you can live with the movement. The fairness question is quietly replaced by a negotiation question, which the other side is better equipped to answer. It has to come before the small yeses, because you only agree to a technical contact and your pipeline data once the revised twenty-two feels like a fair middle.

> A shop shows a jacket as "was £200, now £120." You judge £120 against £200, not against what a jacket is worth to you. Flip it: if the shop had you try on, then hold, then reserve the jacket before any price tag appeared, you would ask the full price and decide fresh.

**Commitment Traps** are what convert a good-faith conversation into dependency. Small yeses are essential because no single one is a decision. Together they produce engineers, shared data and a board that has congratulated you. By the time the larger term arrives, refusing would be inconsistent with everything you have already done, and being consistent is something reasonable people value. It has to come before the Friday deadline, because a deadline only lands on someone whose engineers, data and board are already involved in the deal.

> A neighbour borrows your drill, then your ladder, then your car for a week, then your spare room. Each yes makes the next "no" feel unfair. Flip it: if the neighbour said "decide right now or the offer's gone" before borrowing anything at all, you would take your time and say no if you liked.

**Ultimatums** arrive only when the investment is sufficient. A Friday deadline at the start would have been dismissed. Placed at the end, it lands on a person who has already paid. The conversion point is the moment the person asks for time and is told, kindly, that time is the one thing not available. It has to come before his mention of the integration work and the board's expectations, because that mention only weighs on you once the deadline has taken away the time to ask counsel.

> You have queued all morning for a table, and the host says, "We hold it for five minutes, then it goes to the next group." You do not leave to ask your friend. Flip it: if your friend reminded you of every favour you owed before the host set any time limit, you would still step out of the queue to talk it over.

**Obligation Enforcement** closes the loop and supplies the deniability. Nobody has threatened anything. The other side only mentions the work already done. The sunk investment is doing the enforcing, and the person's own decency is the lock. It has to come last, because the engineers' six weeks only press on you once a Friday deadline has taken away the time to ask counsel.

> A friend you helped move house last month says, "I really need you on Saturday. I'd do it for you." You cancel your plans, and nobody has raised a voice. Flip it: if your friend's "I'd do it for you" came first and only later a tight deadline was put on your answer, you would ask for time and check your own plans.

What weakens the sequence is an independent benchmark of what is fair before any number is seen, no work or data shared before signature, and a clause that cannot be signed without a week of counsel.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A negotiation between a large platform and a smaller firm is built from a few structural facts. The larger side has a standard way of working: it opens a draft, and the draft moves toward the middle. Its calendar is full, its marketing team plans around launch dates, and an integration needs people on both sides before a contract is signed. And the smaller firm answers to a board that wants the deal.

Door-in-the-Face comes first because a first draft is where a large company places its widest position. Everything that follows is then measured against it, and a second paper that looks like a concession arrives as relief.

Anchoring comes second because, once a number has been named, later talks are about distance from it. The question of what a fair share would be is replaced by a question about whether the new figure can be lived with, and the larger side is better equipped for that one.

Commitment Traps / Foot-in-the-Door comes third because an integration needs early work. A technical contact, some data, a held date and a word to the board are each reasonable by themselves, and together they produce engineers on the payroll and a chair who has said congratulations.

Ultimatums comes fourth because the late stage is when a deadline costs the other side least and the person most. A Friday clause at the start would have been set aside. After six weeks of work, it lands on someone who has already paid.

Obligation Enforcement comes last because nothing needs to be threatened. A mention of the work completed and the board's expectations is enough, and the person's own decency does the rest.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: open wide, narrow gently, collect small yeses, set a clock, and mention what has been spent. It turns a head of partnerships who wanted the deal into someone who can no longer say what a fair figure would have been.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. Negotiating practice and launch calendars did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a smaller supplier. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Fenella Brenby ran procurement at a small manufacturer when Rupert Calby, a buyer for a large retailer, sent her his first terms: exclusive supply for five years, at a price so low that she laughed. She replied politely that she could not agree. He wrote back, warmly, that his own bosses had pushed him to open there, and three weeks later he sent a second paper: two years, a smaller discount, a single product line. "I fought hard for this," he said. She agreed it was a very different offer.

Then came small requests. Could her team run sample batches before signing? Yes. Could she share her production schedule so his people could plan? Yes. Could she hold a date for the launch? She held it. Could she mention the deal to her board? She did, and was congratulated. By June, her factory had run three weeks of trial output for a customer who had signed nothing.

At the last meeting Rupert slid a new clause across the table and said it was open until five on Friday, because another supplier was waiting. She asked for a week to take advice. He said he understood, and then mentioned the trial output and her board's hopes.

Rupert was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that suppliers who had already started work never walked away.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to describe each concession as reasonable and to forget what they agreed to at the start. Over time, they may find it hard to say what they would have wanted, because the other side's numbers have become their own reference. They may feel proud of having negotiated hard, while having conceded the structural points. Afterwards, they may blame themselves for being naive, though nothing they did was unusual, and may hesitate to push back in later deals for fear of appearing difficult.

## Institutional Echo

Partnership and distribution negotiations in this sector often start with a draft from the larger party, whose terms set the frame. Exploratory work before signature, such as integration spikes, data sharing and joint planning, is common and can create real dependence on a deal that has not closed. Deadlines tied to a counterpart's internal approvals, quarter ends or rival suitors may be genuine, and sometimes are.

Memoranda of this kind often combine commercial terms with exclusivity and co-marketing clauses whose cost shows only in later years. Enforceability of exclusivity, non-binding memoranda and revenue-share structures varies, and any such question needs qualified review. Whether a particular negotiator acted with design would require additional evidence; the same sequence can come from habit and training.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good negotiator splits the difference, and that a reasonable person meets a reasonable person halfway. Entire textbooks teach that the first offer is only an opening. Reasonableness is treated as a virtue, and refusing a concession is described as rigid.

The value being exploited is fairness itself. The wish not to be the difficult one is precisely what makes a number feel like a gift. The public story is a hard-fought, balanced partnership. The private loss is a sense of what you agreed to, and why.

## Observable Indicators

1. The first proposal is far outside any figure the other side could expect to be accepted.
2. The second proposal is introduced as a significant concession, with an account of how hard it was to win.
3. No independent benchmark or comparable is offered for any figure.
4. Requests for technical contacts, data, dates or board previews arrive before signature.
5. Each request is small and phrased as a reasonable step, and each acceptance is acknowledged warmly.
6. A new clause appears at the final meeting that was not in earlier drafts.
7. The deadline attaches to the new or most valuable clause.
8. Requests for time for counsel are met with sympathy and no change to the date.
9. Work already done is mentioned when you hesitate.
10. The counterpart refers to what leadership has authorised, without a way to check.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every draft term sheet and memorandum, with dates, in the order received.
- A written log of each pre-signature request made and what you provided.
- Records of internal time and cost spent on pre-signature work, including calendar invitations and timesheets.
- The first proposal and any message describing the later proposal as a concession.
- Messages that give the reason for the deadline, and who set it.
- Your own notes on what you considered fair before seeing any number, written early.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Opening high is ordinary negotiation, and a counterpart may genuinely move a long way. Deadlines can come from a real approval calendar or a rival bid. Requests for early technical work are also common and may be in both sides' interest.

The discriminator is whether the first figure was ever defensible and whether concessions ever cost the other side anything. In a genuine negotiation the opening position can be explained, the movement can be tied to reasons, and the deadline moves when counsel needs time. In The Big Ask, the opening cannot be explained, the movement coincides with a new clause, and the deadline is fixed.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Real constraints.** A larger partner may be bound by internal approval timelines.
- **Mutual early investment.** Both sides may genuinely contribute work before signing.
- **Sincere friendliness.** A warm, constructive negotiator is not necessarily applying a technique.

Concern strengthens when: the opening position has no rationale; the final clause is new; the deadline cannot be moved for counsel; and sunk work is invoked in place of reasons.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Benchmark Test:**
Ask: "What would I have called fair before I saw their first number?"
Concern rises if: you cannot say, because their number is now your reference.

**SPOT IT — The New Clause Test:**
Ask: "Was this clause in any earlier draft?"
Concern rises if: it first appears at the final meeting, attached to the deadline.

**SPOT IT — The Sunk Work Test:**
Ask: "What have we done for them before signature, and is it being cited as a reason?"
Concern rises if: past cooperation is offered in place of an explanation for a term.

**SPOT IT — The Week Test:**
Ask: "Can the deadline move by a week for counsel?"
Concern rises if: the answer is sympathy and no change.

## Chain Breakers

- **Set your benchmark before the first number.** Write down what a fair split looks like using outside comparables and advice.
- **Ask for the rationale behind each figure,** not only its movement. "What is this twenty-two percent based on?"
- **Limit pre-signature work.** Offer a time-boxed, scoped discovery period, in writing, with no obligation attached.
- **Keep a written log of every request and what was provided,** and say aloud when a request is being counted.
- **Separate the clauses.** Ask to sign the commercial terms now and take exclusivity separately, with time for counsel.
- **Ask for the deadline's source in writing,** and ask what happens if counsel needs a week.
- **Get independent advice on any exclusivity,** especially one that restricts future alternatives.
- **Be prepared to walk away,** and agree beforehand with your board or chair what your walk-away point is.
- **Do not threaten or bluff.** State your boundary calmly and keep records.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A first proposal with no rationale; a new clause at the final meeting; pre-signature work requested without terms; a deadline with no stated source.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A deadline attached to an exclusivity clause that restricts alternatives for years; substantial internal work already sunk; counsel's request for time refused; terms that contradict earlier written understandings. Relevant routes may include a qualified commercial attorney, an independent board member or audit committee, a mediator where the contract points to one, and, where the counterpart's conduct raises competition concerns, a competition or consumer authority.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats against your company, staff or reputation if you refuse; any form of coercion or blackmail; pressure involving personal circumstances; statements of self-harm by anyone involved. Contact local emergency services where there is danger, and counsel and law enforcement as appropriate. Do not negotiate under threat.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before we discuss figures, I'd like to understand what the twenty-two percent is based on."
- "I'm happy to proceed with the commercial terms. The exclusivity needs a week for our counsel."
- "Could you tell me where this clause first appeared and why?"
- "We've done integration work in good faith, and I don't want that to be the reason we agree to anything."
- "I'd like to set up a time-limited discovery phase with defined scope, in writing."
- "Who set Friday, and is there a reason it can't move for legal review?"
- "If the window really closes, we'll accept that."
