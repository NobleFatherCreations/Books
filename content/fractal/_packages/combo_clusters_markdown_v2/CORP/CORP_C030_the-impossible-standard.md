# C030: The Impossible Standard — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that whatever you do will never be enough, and a reflex to read every missed target as proof that something is wrong with you, not with the target.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of apologising far too much after small mistakes, and trying to make up for them with extra hours.
- A pull to keep working once a goal is met, because you expect the bar to rise as soon as you reach it.
- An automatic thought, "I'm just not good enough," whenever two instructions pull you in opposite directions.

**A point in a life.** Midlife, ten years into a sales career.

**Where it happens.** A regional sales office of a large company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a forty-two-year-old regional sales director, and you have beaten your number in six of the last eight years. You are not easily rattled. You came to this company because the commission plan was generous and the territory was yours to build. What you want this year is plain: your number, your bonus, and the three new accounts you have already half-won.

**January, the vice president's office**

She slides the annual scorecard across her desk. One page, five lines. Line one: grow regional revenue by a fifth. Line two: hold gross margin at the company target. Line three: cut discounting. Line four: win three new strategic accounts. Line five: lift customer retention. At the bottom, in small type, the quarterly bonus pays only if "all primary objectives are met or substantially met."

You read it twice. The office smells of her peppermint tea, and the radiator ticks. You can do growth. You can do margin. You cannot do both at these prices, with a discount ceiling this low, against rivals who are cutting. You say so, evenly, with your pencil resting on lines one and two.

She smiles. "It's a stretch. We believe in stretch. I'm sure you'll find a way."

You nod. You like her. In your first week she remembered your daughter's exam dates. You fold the page into your jacket and thank her for her confidence.

**July, the head office review room**

A long table, a screen, a jug of water nobody touches. In the spring you were a fifth up on last year and margin was two points under. The chief commercial officer said, "Great growth." Then: "But margin." The room nodded. This quarter you held margin and growth slipped. The same voice said, "Good discipline." Then: "But growth."

Now a new slide. The chief commercial officer says, "Just a clarification: a new account counts toward the three only above a certain deal size." The size is a number nobody mentioned in January. A moment later retention changes too, and is now measured without customers who downgraded. Each change is delivered kindly, in the same font.

You update your spreadsheet on your knee. The account you thought you had is not quite the account that counts.

**September, your kitchen**

The light over the stove is the only one on, and it is past midnight. The coffee by your pad has gone cold, and the scorecard lies flat under the lamp, soft at the creases. You count the accounts in your territory that clear the new size and get two, where the scorecard asks for three. You try a lower discount, then a higher one, then the accounts moved between quarters. Thirty-one versions in all, each pencilled in its own column. In none of them are all five lines green.

You tell yourself you have missed something. You start again from line one.

**December, the vice president's office**

On her desk, a note: "Strong effort. Not all primary objectives met." The bonus is half what you expected. She leans in. "I keep coming back to it. Something in you has slipped this year, and I think you know it." Your throat tightens. You look down at your hands.

Then she turns to next year's plan. It asks for growth and margin both, and more of each. "From January you'll send a written recovery note to the whole leadership team every Friday," she says, "and open each review by saying what you did wrong." Over two points of margin. You nod through all of it.

In the spring, when you were a fifth up, the chief commercial officer stopped at your desk and called you the future of the region, and her door stood open all day. Since the summer her replies have shrunk to a line, and nobody has asked after your daughter's exams. Today she types while you talk. "When the numbers are good, we're all so proud of you," she says, still looking at her screen. "Let's get back to that."

You thank her.

**Two years later**

You're at a different company now, in a bright office that smells of new carpet. It's the second week of October and you've already passed your number. Your new director leans on your doorframe and says, "Take the rest of the quarter. You've earned it."

You thank him. An hour later you're back at your desk, rebuilding the forecast, certain the line will move the minute you stop watching it.

That afternoon two emails land. One says hold spend flat. The other says hire two people. You don't ask which he means. You read them both and decide, quietly, that it's you who has misread something, and you write back, "So sorry, I should have caught this sooner." Nobody had complained about anything.

At home you no longer say the figure aloud. Your daughter is at university now, and when she asks about work you say, "Busy," and she nods and asks nothing more.

You hit the number in October. You still feel behind.

## The Name

That's **The Impossible Standard**.

## Definition

The Impossible Standard is the sequence where contradictory demands coexist, success is redefined after it is achieved, and inevitable failure is framed as personal deficiency, using Double Binds, Moving Goalposts, Impossible Metrics, Shame Installation, Overcorrection Demands, and Conditional Love to convert a motivation to meet legitimate expectations into the loss of any sense that effort can ever be sufficient. It is hard to detect because each target is plausible on its own, and the contradiction appears only when they are computed together. Stretch targets and trade-offs between growth and margin are normal in sales organisations. The concern is a set of standards that cannot all be met and is nevertheless treated as a test of character.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Double Binds** — Contradictory demands: a scorecard asking for twenty-two percent growth and margin at target, a single bonus condition, and a review room that answers "Great growth... But margin," then "Good discipline... But growth." This functions by making any choice a failure on one line. Concern rises when you cannot find a path that satisfies all lines at once.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — Changing the rules: in July a strategic account counts "only if above a certain deal size," and in September retention is redefined, each delivered kindly as clarification. This functions by redefining success after it is achieved. Concern rises when the rules change after you have met the earlier version.

3. **Impossible Metrics** — A page that cannot be met: you carry the folded scorecard and compute the five lines at night, at every discount, and find no path where all five are green. This functions by guaranteeing partial failure. Concern rises when no one can show a case where the five lines were all met.

4. **Shame Installation** — A shift from the miss to the person: "Strong effort. Not all primary objectives met," a halved bonus, and "I believe you can be that person again." This functions by turning a structural gap into a personal deficit. Concern rises when you begin to apologise before speaking.

5. **Overcorrection Demands** — A response out of scale: "Focus on what you can control," more growth and more margin, a Friday recovery note to the leadership team, and an opening talk on what you will change. This functions by asking for greater effort against an unachievable standard. Concern rises when the remedy for missing a contradictory target is to try harder.

6. **Conditional Love** — Regard that follows the numbers: the chief commercial officer calls you the future and the VP's door stays open when growth lands, then replies shrink to a line when margin slips. This functions by making belonging depend on outcomes that cannot be reliably produced. Concern rises when appreciation tracks the latest quarter.

## Why This Order

**Double Binds** come first because the structure must be built into the plan. Growth and margin, set side by side with a single bonus condition, guarantee a miss on one of them. It has to come before the shifting definitions, because the July clarifications only land on a scorecard whose five lines already pull against each other and were presented to you as a stretch.

> Picture being told to "be more careful" and "work faster" in the same breath. You lose whichever one you pick. Flip it: you start working faster, and halfway through the job you are told the task is now to be more careful. You would stop and ask which instruction counts, while only one is on the table.

**Moving Goalposts** keeps the result from ever being settled: any success can be reclassified in the next review, so there is no stable finish line. It has to come before the unreachable count, because the deal-size line is what leaves you with two qualifying accounts in your territory when the scorecard asks for three.

> You run a race and are told, after crossing the line, that the course was "really" a bit longer. Flip it: before the race starts, you are told the course cannot be run in the time allowed. You would stay at the starting line and ask for the course to be measured.

**Impossible Metrics** make the failure statistical, not occasional. It has to come before the remark about you, because a comment that something in you has slipped only sticks after thirty-one pencilled versions of the plan have all failed.

> Imagine a quiz marked out of a hundred where only ninety questions have answers. However well you do, you never reach the top. Flip it: before the quiz is handed out, someone tells you that you are simply not clever. Then you find only ninety questions have answers, and you would ask who wrote it.

**Shame Installation** then converts the gap into character: if the goal can be reached, and you have not reached it, the fault must be yours. It has to come before the Friday notes, because a written account of what you did wrong only reads as fair to you once you already half-believe the fault is yours.

> After missing a bus that was never on time, you start to think, "I'm just bad at mornings." Flip it: before you think anything about yourself, you are told to leave an hour early every day and write a note about each late bus. You would ask why, when the bus is never on time.

**Overcorrection Demands** asks for more effort against the same contradiction, which deepens the shame. It has to come before her cooling replies, because her warmth only reads as a verdict on you once the Friday notes and the confession at the start of each review have made you the one on trial.

> You spill a little coffee and are told to write out an apology to the whole office and scrub the floor, every Friday. Flip it: after you spill a little coffee, the whole office goes quiet and only warms up on days you spill nothing. You would ask someone plainly what the quiet is about.

**Conditional Love** ties regard to results, so the person's sense of worth begins to move with the numbers. It has to come last, because only once the Friday notes are due can her open door and her short replies rise and fall with your figures, and be seen to.

> Picture a family that cheers when you bring home a good grade and goes quiet when you don't. Your mood starts to follow the report card. Flip it: the family cheers your good grade and goes quiet over a weak one, long before anyone asks for anything more. You would notice your mood following the report card and say so at the table.

The conversion point is the moment the director believes the failure is theirs. Whether the scorecard reflects deliberate design or poorly reconciled targets from separate leaders is not obvious; intent would require additional evidence. What weakens the sequence: computing the lines together and putting the arithmetic in writing, asking which line takes priority, and seeking an outside benchmark for what is reasonable.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A large company sets its targets in separate rooms: finance owns margin, sales owns growth, a strategy team owns new accounts, a service team owns retention. A single bonus rule then ties pay to all of them at once. Reviews arrive every quarter, and the definitions behind each line live in documents that different people edit at different times. Put those facts together and the story above follows.

Double Binds comes first because the five lines are written by five owners who do not sit together. Each target is reasonable alone. Set side by side beneath one bonus condition, two of them pull against each other, and nobody is placed to notice.

Moving Goalposts comes second because definitions are cheaper to change than targets. A signed-off target is hard to reopen, but the words that say what counts as a strategic account can be tightened in a clarification. A committee that meets every quarter revises the page whenever the numbers look awkward.

Impossible Metrics comes third because plans are built from ambition downward, not from the pipeline upward. A figure that sounds right in a planning meeting gets written into the scorecard whether or not the territory holds enough accounts to reach it.

Shame Installation comes fourth because the review form has a box for the person and none for the plan. Once a target is missed, the system needs a cause, and the only cause with a field on the form is the individual. "Development area" is easier to write than "the targets conflict."

Overcorrection Demands comes fifth because a miss has to be answered with something visible. A recovery note every Friday is a cheap way for a leadership team to show it is acting, and the cost lands on whoever writes it.

Conditional Love comes last because attention in a company follows results. A senior person's time is scarce, and it flows to whoever is making the number this quarter. Nobody decides to withdraw it. The calendar does.

Put it together and the order is the order a scorecard of this kind needs: a contradiction, a shifting finish line, a gap that cannot be closed, a flaw to explain the gap, a ritual to repair it, and regard that rises and falls with the figures. It turns a capable, proven performer into someone who reads every missed number as a verdict on the person.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. Five targets set by separate leaders, a quarterly review and a single bonus rule did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newly promoted account manager. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Anika Selby had just been promoted to account manager when Colm Torby, the head of accounts, took her under his wing. In their first meeting he handed her a plan that asked her to win new business and cut the discount budget by a third, both for one bonus. She said it looked hard. "A stretch," he said, smiling. By the second meeting the meaning of a won account had changed: it now meant signed, not agreed, and only above a size she had not been told. When she counted her pipeline against the new size, she found two accounts where the plan wanted four. She mentioned it. Colm looked at her kindly and said the people who struggle with a plan are the ones who haven't made it their own. "I thought you were someone who'd get this," he said. The next day an email asked her to send him a written account of what she had got wrong every Friday, and to open each team call by reading it out. Through the autumn, invitations to his lunches came when her figures were up and stopped when they dipped. His door, which had stood open, stayed shut.

Colm told a friend that Anika had stopped asking questions, and that he preferred her like that.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The director may begin to compute scorecard variations at night and carry the page everywhere. Over time, they may start apologising before they speak and read criticism into neutral comments. They may stop believing that effort changes outcomes, feel a growing shame when a quarter ends, and find it hard to separate their worth from the latest number.

## Institutional Echo

Sales scorecards with several weighted objectives, including growth and margin, are common, and trade-offs between them are normal management tensions. Plans that pay only when all primary objectives are met can make the combined target very hard to hit. Mid-year changes to commission terms can be allowed or restricted by the plan and by employment law. Typical attainment rates for such plans vary and should not be assumed. Intent would require additional evidence: whether anyone has met all lines, whether definitions change after results, and whether priorities are ever stated.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that high performers thrive on impossible goals and that stretch builds greatness. Ambition is rightly admired. The language that makes it sound noble is stretch, ownership, and "control what you can control." The public story is a company with ambitious standards. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a capable person who comes to think that effort is never enough.

## Observable Indicators

1. A scorecard contains objectives that pull in opposite directions.
2. Bonus or commission is conditional on meeting all primary objectives.
3. Definitions of an objective change after the year starts.
4. Praise for one line is followed immediately by "but" about another.
5. No one can show an example of all lines being met.
6. Requests for priority between lines are answered with "find a way."
7. Failures are described in terms of personal qualities.
8. The remedy is to work harder at the contradictory goals.
9. Regard from leaders changes with the latest quarter.
10. You apologise before speaking in reviews.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The scorecard and the commission plan, every version, with dates.
- Any written changes to definitions, thresholds or weights.
- Your own arithmetic showing the lines cannot all be met, with assumptions stated.
- Review notes, emails and slides where "but" follows praise.
- Answers, or lack of answers, to requests for priority between lines.
- Bonus statements against the plan.
- Names of peers who have asked the same questions.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Stretch targets, growth-versus-margin tension and strict bonus conditions are normal. A leader may also genuinely trust that you can find a way. The discriminator is **whether anyone can show the standard being met, and whether the rules stay put**. A genuine stretch has a stated priority and a known path.

Benign lookalikes:

- **Genuine stretch.** Hard targets can be achievable.
- **Legitimate trade-offs.** Leaders may have reasons for hard choices.
- **Market change.** Mid-year adjustments may reflect real shifts.
- **Honest encouragement.** A leader may truly believe in you.

Concern strengthens when: nobody has met all the lines; definitions change after results; failures are attributed to personal qualities.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Arithmetic Test:**
Ask: "Can all five lines be green at once, given my market?"
Concern rises if: I cannot find a path, and nobody will show me one.

**SPOT IT — The Redefinition Test:**
Ask: "Did any definition change after I met it?"
Concern rises if: yes, and the change was called clarification.

**SPOT IT — The Priority Test:**
Ask: "When two lines conflict, which one wins?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "find a way."

**SPOT IT — The Apology Test:**
Ask: "Am I apologising before I speak?"
Concern rises if: yes, and I did not used to.

## Chain Breakers

- **Compute the lines together** and write the result down with assumptions.
- **Ask in writing which line takes priority** when two conflict.
- **Ask for the commission plan terms** on changes to definitions during the year.
- **Ask whether anyone has met all lines,** and what it took.
- **Keep your record of decisions** and the targets as they stood at each date.
- **Benchmark outside:** talk to peers in other companies about what is reasonable.
- **Separate your worth from the number:** write down what you did well this year, independent of the scorecard.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Two objectives conflict and no priority is given; a definition changes mid-year; your effort is described as the gap.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Pay, bonus or role is cut because of a standard that cannot be met, or a performance plan cites the contradictory lines. Relevant routes may include HR or compensation committee, an employment attorney, a union or employee representative where one exists, and an employment or labour regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, severe retaliation, or a mention of self-harm by you or anyone involved. Contact local emergency or crisis services and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "When growth and margin conflict, which takes priority?"
- "Could you show me an example of all five lines being met in a comparable region?"
- "Could we confirm the definition of strategic account in writing?"
- "I'd like to understand how the bonus is calculated if two objectives conflict."
- "I'm committed to this. I'd like the standard to be one I can meet."
- "Could we review the scorecard together at mid-year?"
