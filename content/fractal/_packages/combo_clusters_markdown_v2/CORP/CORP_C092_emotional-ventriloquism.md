# C092: Emotional Ventriloquism — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that when several people say the same thing it must be true, and a habit of trusting a go-between's account instead of speaking with the other person directly.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of changing your mind after hearing the same point from three different people, without checking where it started.
- A pull to let a calm, neutral-seeming messenger carry your words instead of saying them yourself.
- An automatic thought, "if they all think so, I must be wrong," whenever several people repeat one message.

**A point in a life.** Midlife, as a family business changes hands.

**Where it happens.** A family-owned distribution company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a forty-five-year-old co-owner of the family distribution company your father built, and you have just asked, in writing, for a valuation before anyone discusses who runs it next. Your brother runs operations. You run finance and the customer accounts. For eleven years that arrangement was simply how it worked. Then your father's health made succession real, and you sent one email: an independent valuation and a decision process first, titles second. Your brother did not reply.

**Nine days later, your office**

The calls begin. The first is your cousin, who manages a warehouse. He is gentle. He says he is not taking sides, he just hates to hear that you are "going legalistic." You did not say that. He says, vaguely, that it is going around. The second is the longest-serving logistics manager, who taught you the routes when you were twenty-three. She says the drivers are nervous, that people are asking whether the company will be broken up, that she is sure you have not lost faith in your brother's operational judgment. She says it as a question, and then she waits. The third is your aunt, who does not talk about business and talks about it now. Your father, she says, felt so bypassed that you went to the lawyers before you came to him. You have not been to a lawyer. You say so. She says, kindly, that she is only repeating what she was told. Every caller is warm, worried and apparently independent, and not one says your brother asked them to call.

Then comes the fourth call. A family friend, sixty-eight, who has known you both since you were children and who sits on no board and holds no shares, offers to help. He has hosted every christening and every funeral for thirty years. He suggests dinner at his house, the two of you, informal, to "listen and see if I can be useful." He tells you he has spoken to your brother "just to hear him out," and that you will have your turn.

**Friday, the family friend's house**

You go. The table is laid with the good plates, and the house smells of roast lamb and lemon polish. He is a good listener, and for two hours you tell him everything: the valuation, the fear that your father's decline is being used to rush the decision, the way you were left out of the last two supplier renegotiations. He writes nothing down. At the door he squeezes your arm. "Leave your brother to me. Better the two of you don't talk until I have." You nod. It sounds like peace.

**Four days later, your kitchen**

An email arrives with a short subject line: "What I'm hearing." It is a one-page memo, courteous in tone, headed "Informal, not for circulation." It lists what he says he has gathered across the family. That you are moving too fast. That you have lost confidence in your brother's operational judgment. That your father feels bypassed. That staff are anxious about the valuation. That a "reasonable first step" would be to pause the valuation request and agree a joint statement to the managers, which he has helpfully drafted. You read it twice. The words are ones you have heard before: "legalistic," "bypassed," "operational judgment." They came to you in the first three calls, one at a time, from three mouths. Your cousin mentions the memo on the next call. Your aunt calls it "the very fair summary."

When you finally reach your brother, he is wounded. He has not asked anyone to say anything, he says. He has not seen the memo. He just wants what is best for Dad, and he is sorry you feel this way. You put the phone down with nothing to hold.

You are now explaining yourself to a gentle man of sixty-eight, to the whole family, and to a memo. You stop going to Sunday lunch, because every chair there has an opinion about you. You answer your cousin's texts with a thumbs-up. At night you go over your one email line by line and cannot find the legalistic part.

**Saturday, your father's house**

On Saturday you drive to your father's house on your own. You sit beside him with your five lines printed out, and read them to him in your own voice. He listens with his hands on the blanket, and when you finish he says only, "I wish someone had told me that it was five lines."

**Two years later**

It is a Tuesday in March, and you are in a coffee shop near the distribution center, across from your new operations lead, who has just told you the loading schedule you drew up is wrong. She says it, and then she says the night supervisor said it, and the dock foreman said it, and a driver mentioned it, too. Four people. You had spent a month on that schedule. You hear yourself say, "Okay. I guess it's wrong." You do not ask to see a single complaint. You do not ask which night.

You open your laptop and redraw the schedule on the spot, in the shape she describes. It takes forty minutes. When you are finished, you notice she has not touched her coffee, and that you cannot remember whether she ever said what was wrong with the old one.

Your brother's number is in your phone. You have not dialed it in two years. Every message to him goes through your assistant, who is soft-spoken and kind and rewrites your sentences so they sound like no one in particular. You tell yourself it is more professional. Last month your brother sent a birthday card to the office. Your assistant put it in a folder and told you about it a week later, gently, in the corridor.

At six you sit in the car with the card on your knee. Your father is in a care home forty minutes away. You have visited twice. You read your brother's handwriting once, and then once more, and you cannot tell what you are supposed to feel, because you are waiting to be told.

## The Name

That's **Emotional Ventriloquism**.

## Definition

Emotional Ventriloquism is the sequence where one party's message reaches the target through several apparently independent, apparently neutral voices, using Flying Monkeys, Proxy Recruitment, Triangulation, Priming, and Plausible Deniability to convert trust in a mediator or messenger into an unfiltered relationship with the other party that the target no longer has. In corporate and family-business settings it concentrates in succession disputes, partner exits and boardroom splits, where trusted insiders and mutual friends already carry information in both directions. It is hard to detect because each messenger is sincere, each message is gentle, and the person behind it never appears. What can be lost is a direct line to the other party, standing in a negotiation, and the target's accurate picture of what others actually think.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Flying Monkeys** — Relatives and staff carry the message: a cousin hates that you are "going legalistic," the logistics manager says the drivers are nervous, and your aunt says your father was hurt, each repeating what they were told. This functions by letting a request reach you wearing the face of someone you love, so that refusing feels like rejecting them rather than evaluating a position. Concern rises when several people carry the same phrasing, and when none of them can say where they first heard it.

2. **Proxy Recruitment** — A trusted friend is invited to help: the family friend of sixty-eight offers a dinner to "listen and see if I can be useful," having already heard your brother out first "just to hear him out." This functions by selecting a messenger whose neutrality is already assumed, so his information looks like an independent finding. Concern rises when the intermediary met one party before the other, or was approached by one party, and does not say so.

3. **Triangulation** — A go-between stands between you: at the door he says "Leave your brother to me. Better the two of you don't talk until I have," and every message now passes through him. This functions by replacing the direct relationship with a managed one, in which the intermediary decides what is repeated, softened or omitted. Concern rises when direct contact between the two principals is described as unhelpful, premature, or likely to make things worse.

4. **Priming** — The same words in every mouth: the memo's "bypassed," "legalistic" and "operational judgment" are phrases you already heard in the first three calls, one to your loyalty, one about the staff's fear, one about your father's hurt. This functions by making a single narrative feel like many separate observations that happen to agree. Concern rises when independent-sounding sources use identical wording, or tell you what you said before you have said it.

5. **Plausible Deniability** — The one behind it never appears: your brother says he asked nobody to say anything and has not seen the memo, and every statement is someone else's interpretation. This functions by distributing authorship, so that a challenge to any one claim leads to another person who only repeated what they were told. Concern rises when you cannot reach the origin of a claim, and when a request to ask the principal directly is met with discomfort rather than agreement.

## Why This Order

**Flying Monkeys** come first because the sequence typically follows a signal of independent action, here a request for a valuation. The reaction is not an argument. It is a change in the weather. When people call with concern before anyone has made a case, the target is already explaining herself rather than making a request. It has to come before the family friend's offer to help, because a friend only looks useful once warm callers have told you the family is worried.

> A friend of a friend says, "Everyone is a bit worried about you." You never asked, and nobody says what about. Flip it: if the friend of a friend first said, "I've been asked to have a chat with you," you would ask who asked and what they had been told, before saying anything about yourself.

**Proxy Recruitment** turns scattered concern into structure. The relatives and managers need somewhere to send their worry, and the informal mediator is that place. His acceptance gives the principal a single hub. It is also the step with the least visible design: a trusted person is simply asked for help. It has to come before the request that you and your brother not talk, because that request is only believable from a friend already trusted with your story over dinner.

> A kind neighbor is asked to "just have a chat" with you before the street meeting, and hears the other side's story first. Flip it: if the kind neighbor first said, "Let me carry messages between you and the other side before the street meeting," you would say you would rather speak to them directly, and still welcome a chat.

**Triangulation** cuts direct contact. Once a respected third party is "handling it," speaking to the principal looks like going around him. The target now hears the other side only as the mediator's summary, and the other side hears hers only as his version. Neither can check either. It has to come before the memo, because the one-page summary of what he has gathered is only believed once he has become the person both of you go through.

> Two siblings who could simply ring each other both go through their mum instead. Each message comes out a little different at the other end. Flip it: if mum first told you your sibling had been "difficult about the money," before any message passed between you, you would ring your sibling yourself and ask what they actually said.

**Priming** is what makes the memo possible. It happened earlier, but it only becomes visible when the pages carry the principal's own vocabulary. One narrative has been loaded into several people, each tuned to a different lever, and their agreement is presented as consensus, the strongest pressure available in a family firm. It has to come before your brother's denial, because the denial only sounds honest once the memo shows the family already using the phrases the callers used.

> Three different friends use the same odd phrase about you in the same week. It is hard to believe they each thought of it alone. Flip it: if the first friend to use the odd phrase about you said, "I only passed it on," you would ask who they heard it from and ask the other two as well.

**Plausible Deniability** closes the loop and is what makes the sequence so difficult to challenge. If you protest, the principal can sincerely say he asked nobody to say anything. Everyone acted from love, and the strings pulled themselves. It has to come last, because your brother's claim that he asked no one can only be said truthfully after the callers, the dinner and the memo are all in place.

> Everyone says, "I only passed it on." The person who started it says, "I never asked anybody to say anything." Both are telling the truth. Flip it: if the person who started it first said, "I never asked anybody to say anything," and only then three friends used the same odd phrase, you would ask each friend where they first heard it.

What would weaken it is direct, written contact between the principals, a mediator who discloses whom he spoke to, and tracing any claim to its first source. The key conversion point is the memo: the moment a summary becomes the document the family treats as the record.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A family company produces this order without a plan. Three facts about the setting do the work. Ownership, management and love all sit in the same few people, so a request about one lands on all three. There is no board to hear a dispute, so it travels by phone and by dinner table. And an informal elder whom everyone trusts is the natural place for worry to go.

Flying Monkeys come first because the first sign of independent action, a request for a valuation, makes a family anxious, and anxious relatives call. Each call is sincere. Together they arrive before anyone has made an argument, so the person who asked is already answering questions nobody posed.

Proxy Recruitment comes second because worry needs somewhere to go. Relatives and managers want to send theirs to someone safe and senior, and a long-standing family friend who sits on no board is that someone. His acceptance is simply a trusted person agreeing to help.

Triangulation comes third because once a respected friend is handling it, speaking to the other side directly starts to look like going around him. Each sibling hears the other only as his summary, and neither can check it.

Priming comes fourth because by the time the memo exists the vocabulary is already in circulation. The phrases heard in the first calls come back as the friend's own words, and repetition from several people looks like consensus, the strongest pressure available in a family firm.

Plausible Deniability comes last because nobody in the chain did anything but pass on care. The brother did not ask, the cousin only repeated, the friend only listened. Each of those sentences can be true at once, and the question of where it started has no one to answer it.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a family that cannot hold a hard conversation directly sends it around in circles until it comes back as a verdict. It leaves the person in the middle answering the whole family at once, rather than the one person they meant to ask.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A family with no board, an elder everyone trusts, and a worry that wanted somewhere to go did most of it, and the people inside were acting out of love. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Anika Sorensen, head of a regional sales team, asked in writing for a review of how commissions were divided. Within days three colleagues she knew from other offices each said, in a corridor or on a call, that people were "a little worried" about her and that she was "going a bit legalistic." A senior colleague, Callum Varson, offered to help; he said he had no stake in it and would just listen. She spent an hour telling him everything over coffee. At the door he said, "Leave the director to me. Better you don't talk to him until I have." Four days later she received a one-page note from him, "Informal, not for circulation," summarizing what he had "heard around the office." The phrases from the calls were in it, word for word. When she asked the director about the note, he said he had not seen it, had not asked anyone to say anything, and only wanted what was best for the team. Callum was never unsure what he was doing. He told a friend that Anika would stop asking once she heard what everyone thought.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The target may begin to doubt her own recollection, because so many people repeat a version she does not recognize. Over time she may stop saying what she wants, to avoid being quoted back, and may begin defending positions she never took. She may be unable to tell which relatives speak from their own concern and which repeat a script, and may withdraw from all of them.

She may agree to a pause she did not want because it seems the only way to stop the calls, and may feel isolated inside her own family while surrounded by people who say they care.

## Institutional Echo

Family and closely held businesses often have no formal channel for disputes between owners, which leaves informal mediators and trusted friends to carry messages in both directions. A memo headed "informal" can acquire the weight of a record without its safeguards: no mandate, no list of who was interviewed, no right of reply.

The same shape appears in the boardroom. A board observer or chief of staff who gathers views before a contested vote can, without bad intent, become the point through which one side's framing is distributed.

The honest reading in most disputes is that people do talk, that mediators do start with one party, and that worry is contagious. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the same phrases appear across unconnected people, and whether the mediator's account of whom he spoke to matches what others report.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that family does not need paper: a trusted friend of the family will sort it out, and a person who asks for a formal process has already failed at loyalty. Around it sits a second belief, that a person who needs outside advice is escalating.

The value being exploited is genuine: the friend at every funeral, the colleague who wants peace. These roles work, which is why they make a good channel.

The language that makes the pattern sound normal is the language of care. "I just want what's best for everyone." "I'm not taking sides." "Let's keep this in the family."

The public story is a gentle conversation among people who love one another. The private loss is that nobody in it heard the target's actual words.

## Observable Indicators

1. Several people independently use the same unusual phrase to describe your position, and cannot say where they heard it.
2. You hear what you supposedly said, or felt, from someone you did not say it to.
3. An intermediary describes himself as neutral and has spoken at length with the other party before speaking with you.
4. A written summary circulates before you have seen it.
5. Requests to speak directly to the other principal are redirected toward the intermediary.
6. The same concern arrives in different emotional registers: loyalty from one, fear from another, hurt from a third.
7. A "reasonable first step" proposed by the intermediary happens to match one side's earlier stated preference.
8. The principal is never present at, or credited with, any of the conversations.
9. People who were warm to you before the dispute become noticeably careful, as if they had been briefed.
10. Sources say they are "only repeating what I heard" and cannot name where.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Your own dated, factual notes of each call: who called, what was said about you, and what they said they heard and from whom.
- The mediator's memo or summary in every version, with the date and time you received it and the list of who else received it.
- Your original request (for example, the valuation email) and any reply or silence, with dates.
- Any draft joint statement and the date it first appeared.
- A phrase log: wording that recurs across callers, with each speaker and date.
- Records of whom the mediator says he spoke to, and in what order, where he has stated it.
- Names of people who describe being pre-briefed.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Family and small-company disputes are noisy by nature, and word genuinely travels. The discriminator is **source and repetition considered together**. Ordinary gossip is untidy, contradictory and open about where it comes from. The pattern produces narrower, tidier, more consistent messages, which trace back to one origin and arrive without a visible author.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Genuine concern.** Relatives and managers may be honestly anxious about their jobs and the company, and may have formed the same worry independently.
- **A good-faith mediator.** Hearing the other side first can be a scheduling accident, and a mediator may naturally summarize what he heard.
- **Honest bad news.** A hard message delivered through a friend may be the friend's own and accurate assessment.
- **Real confidentiality.** A mediator may be unable to say who he spoke to without breaching a promise.

Concern strengthens when: the mediator will not say whom he has spoken to or in what order; a request to talk directly to the other principal is discouraged; and the memo's points trace to one side's earlier, written list.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Origin Test:**
Ask: "Where exactly did you first hear that, and from whom?"
Concern rises if: several people give the same answer, or none can name anyone.

**SPOT IT — The Phrase Test:**
Ask: "Have I heard this wording from someone else this week?"
Concern rises if: the same phrase has come from people who do not speak to each other.

**SPOT IT — The Order Test:**
Ask: "Who did this mediator speak to first, and what did he share with them?"
Concern rises if: he met the other party first, or declines to say.

**SPOT IT — The Direct Line Test:**
Ask: "Can I put this question to the other principal myself, in writing?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that it would be unhelpful, and the reason given is the relationship.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write directly to the principal, in writing, calmly.** A short, factual message such as "I would like to agree a valuation process; can we set a time to talk about it?" restores a line that the intermediary has taken over. Keep it private and neutral in tone.
- **Ask the intermediary the order question.** A plain answer is reassuring; a reluctant one is informative.
- **Request a summary you can correct.** Ask that any memo be shared with you at the same time as everyone else, with room to add your own account.
- **Trace one claim to its source.** Choose one phrase, ask each caller where they first heard it, and write down the answers.
- **Slow the timeline.** Decline to agree a joint statement, a pause or a position on a call. "I will respond in writing by Friday."
- **Get independent advice before signing anything the mediator drafts.** A lawyer or accountant with no ties to either side can read what the draft would commit you to.
- **Do not pass on secondhand claims** about the other party. Repeating them makes you another proxy hop.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A phrase attributed to you that you did not say; a mediator who met one party first and will not say so; a memo circulating before you have seen it; a joint statement drafted for you to sign.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A pause in a valuation, sale process or decision being agreed on the strength of the memo; staff, customers or lenders being told one account; managers being asked to take sides. Relevant routes may include an independent attorney with family-business or corporate experience, an independent director or the company's own advisor, a neutral mediator both sides select in writing, a forensic accountant for the valuation, and professional bodies where a licensed adviser is involved.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Any threat to you, your family or your staff, any message that references your home or movements, pressure that includes self-harm statements by anyone, or a vulnerable elder being pressed to sign. In these cases contact local emergency services or crisis support and qualified counsel. Do not negotiate under threat and do not confront the sender.

## Safer Response Language

- "Thank you for raising this. Where did you first hear it, and from whom?"
- "I would like to talk to him directly. Can you help set up a time, or would you rather I write?"
- "Before this goes any wider, could I see the summary and add my own account?"
- "Whom have you spoken to so far, and in what order?"
- "I'm happy to take advice on a process. I would like it to be one we both choose, in writing."
- "I'm not going to respond to this on a call. I will reply in writing by Friday."
- "I may be misreading this. Can you tell me which points are your own view, and which you are passing on?"
