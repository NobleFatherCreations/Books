# C169: The Phantom Equity — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the reward for your work is always just around the next corner, and a fear that leaving now would throw away everything you have already given.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of accepting less pay now for a promise later, and not asking to see it written down.
- An automatic sentence when a date slips again, "it's coming, it's just complicated," said in someone else's words.
- A sinking feeling when anything finally arrives smaller than described, and a wariness of the word "partner."

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, taking a leap.

**Where it happens.** A small startup's product team.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a head of product who took a salary a third lighter than your last one, and you did it with your eyes open. You want a stake in something you build, and a day, years from now, when you can say it was worth it.

**The first week, the office corner**

The founder walks you through it over coffee in the corner of the startup's office, two chairs and a whiteboard of arrows. Cash is tight, but ownership is not. "I don't want you as an employee," he says. "I want you as a builder with real skin in this." He taps the table when he says "this." The offer letter says the same thing in more careful words. Base salary, a figure below what recruiters had quoted you. And one sentence you read twice: "A share of the company in an amount to be agreed will be papered following the cleanup of the cap table." It feels like being treated as a partner rather than a vendor. You sign on a Tuesday and ship your first roadmap in a month.

**Months two to five, the small meeting room**

Month two. You sit in the small meeting room on the second floor with the founder and a lawyer on speaker, and the whiteboard has not been wiped since January. The lawyers are in the middle of the seed-round cleanup. The grant letter, the board approval, the vesting schedule: all of it, the founder says, comes right after. You wait, and you ship.

Month three, same room. The pool mechanics have changed, so your grant will come from a refreshed pool, with better terms anyway. Month five: they are converting the structure of the shares, and the post-money numbers have to be redone first. You ask whether a pool refresh really stops a grant letter from being signed. The lawyer says it is complicated, and she is right: you do not know enough about cap tables to push back. Each explanation is plausible, and each delay arrives wrapped in an upgrade.

**Months eight to sixteen, the small meeting room**

You are not naive. You ask. In the month-eight review you get a slide with a cap table, your role listed as a placeholder row with a figure in grey. "Fully contemplated in the model," the finance lead says. You photograph nothing; it would feel like distrust. First the grant was waiting on the cleanup, then on the seed extension, now on the next round, which will close in the fourth quarter. Month eleven: first quarter. Month fourteen: within thirty days of closing. Each new date lets you breathe out.

Month sixteen, the round closes, and people hug around the long table with paper cups of cheap sparkling wine. You count thirty days on your phone and wait. Day thirty-one you ask. A new lawyer is getting up to speed. Day sixty: the true-up is more complex than anticipated, and the pool has been substantially allocated to new hires. Your position will be "contemplated in the next refresh, likely at the following round."

**Day ninety, the finance lead's desk**

A draft option grant finally arrives, attached to an email on a Friday. The number is roughly half what the grey row implied. The strike price is from a later valuation, and the vesting clock starts on the grant date rather than your start date. You walk it over to the finance lead's desk. "That's just how the round worked out," he says, kindly, and you believe he is sorry. There is no earlier number to hold it against. The grey row was never something anyone signed.

You do the arithmetic that you have been avoiding: sixteen months of work at a reduced salary, against a stake that has been one document away from existing the whole time. You say you have been wondering about your options, and he says, pleasantly, "Nothing's yours until it vests, of course. Anyone who left before then would leave with nothing." If you go now, you leave with nothing to show that a promise was ever made except an offer letter that says "to be agreed." If you stay, there is always the next refresh, the next round, the next thirty days.

**A year later, your kitchen**

It is a Sunday in October, and your new offer lies on the table in front of you: a fair salary, and a bonus that "we'll look at in the spring." Your brother reads it over your shoulder. "Is the spring part written anywhere?" he asks. You say, "Soon. The paperwork's in motion," and then you hear whose voice it was. Your hand goes to the pen anyway.

Last week a friend from your first company got a bonus that was smaller than her email had said. She laughed about it. Your stomach dropped on her behalf, and you stood up to wash the cups. At the new place somebody said "we're all partners here" in the first meeting, and you looked down and studied the grain of the table.

You no longer take coffee with the people from your old company, because they ask how it ended and you change the subject. The old offer letter is in a drawer, soft at the folds, and on bad days you take it out and read the one sentence. You gave sixteen months and a third of your pay to a line that said "to be agreed," and you still cannot say what you were given.

## The Name

That's **The Phantom Equity**.

## Definition

The Phantom Equity is the sequence where a promise of deferred ownership is used to secure below-market cash work and then kept perpetually just out of reach, using Alignment Framing, Paper Deferral, Complexity Shield, Milestone Migration, Dilution Disguise, and Exit Forfeiture to convert belief in a promised stake and reluctance to forfeit work already given into earned compensation lost and an unpapered stake that never existed. It can benefit the company, which keeps cash and retains the person. It exploits the real difficulty of equity mechanics and the real wish to be treated as a partner. It is hard to detect because every delay has a plausible explanation and equity processes genuinely take time. Intent would require additional evidence, since changed circumstances, disorganization, and legal queues can produce the same experience.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Alignment Framing** — A pay cut sold as partnership: over coffee the founder says "I don't want you as an employee. I want you as a builder with real skin in this," and the offer letter repeats it beside a base salary below what recruiters quoted. This functions by making the equity the centre of the exchange, so that the pay cut feels like shared commitment. Concern rises when the equity amount is left "to be agreed" while the pay cut is fixed from day one.

2. **Paper Deferral** — The grant letter, the board approval and the vesting schedule are all said to come right after the seed-round cleanup, so you keep working and wait. This functions by keeping the promise alive in conversation while the documents stay out of existence. Concern rises when sixty to ninety days of work pass without a signed grant or a dated board approval.

3. **Complexity Shield** — Reasons no one can check: a refreshed pool, a conversion of the equity structure and post-money numbers to redo first, each offered as why no grant letter exists yet and each too technical to question. This functions by making delay sound technical, so the holder lacks the expertise to test it. Concern rises when explanations grow more sophisticated while no document changes.

4. **Milestone Migration** — A date that keeps moving: waiting on the cleanup, then the seed extension, then the Series A (Q4, then Q1, then thirty days after closing), then "likely at the Series B," with a grey placeholder row on the month-eight slide. This functions by pairing each delay with a new date, so there is never a clear moment when the promise is broken. Concern rises when the cited milestone does not actually prevent papering the grant.

5. **Dilution Disguise** — A draft arrives on a Friday at roughly half the number the grey row implied, with a strike price from a later valuation and vesting counted from the grant date; "That's just how the round worked out." This functions by presenting the reduction as round mechanics, with no earlier written term to measure it against. Concern rises when the draft differs from what was described and the difference is framed as unavoidable.

6. **Exit Forfeiture** — Leaving means walking away empty-handed: "Nothing's yours until it vests, of course," while the offer letter still reads "to be agreed" and the founder still says "partner." This functions by making the cost of departure the entire unpapered balance. Concern rises when mentions of leaving trigger reminders of what would be lost or hints that "partners don't walk."

## Why This Order

**Alignment Framing** has to come first, because a pay cut is only acceptable if something is coming. Without the equity story, the structure is simply underpayment. It has to come before the promise to paper your share after the cleanup, because you only accept a lighter salary on a promise from someone who has called you a builder with real skin.

> A neighbour asks you to mind her shop for a month at half your usual rate, saying, "We're in this together, you'll be part of it." The "together" is what makes you say yes. The half rate alone you would have turned down. Flip it: if the neighbour offered you a payment, to be agreed later, for minding her shop at half your usual rate, you would ask her to write down the amount and date before you said yes.

**Paper Deferral** can begin only after the person has accepted the lower salary and started work. Before that, they have leverage; afterward, they have momentum. It has to come before the lawyer's remark that it is complicated, because a lawyer only has to explain a delay after the founder has said the grant letter comes right after.

> A builder says the invoice will follow once the job is done, and the job is always "nearly done." You have already moved the furniture out. Flip it: if the builder first told you the invoice depended on a technical step you could not check, and only then said it would follow, you would ask him to write the step down with a date.

Every delay needs an explanation, and the explanation has to be credible, which is where **Complexity Shield** comes in. Genuine technical complexity does the work: no honest person can say whether a pool refresh really prevents a grant letter without cap table expertise. It has to come before the new dates, because each new date only calms you once you cannot judge whether a pool refresh really stops a grant letter.

> Your plumber says the part has to be "recalibrated at the depot." You have no idea whether that is true, and you cannot check. Flip it: if your plumber kept changing the day he would return, you would ask him which day he really promised, write it down, and only then ask about the part at the depot.

**Milestone Migration** converts a delay into a promise. A delay alone causes worry; a delay with a new date restores confidence. Moving the date from event to event also means no single day can be named as the day the promise failed. It has to come before the draft grant that is half the grey row, because a smaller number can only be handed over after the delays have left you with no earlier figure to hold it against.

> A friend who owes you money says, "After payday." Payday passes: "After the bonus." Each new date feels like progress, and you never get a day to say the promise was broken. Flip it: if the friend handed you half of what they owed and said that was how it came out, you would ask for the rest and a date, in writing.

**Dilution Disguise**, if it arrives at all, comes at the point when the person has the least ability to object: the work is done, the round is closed, and no earlier written number exists to compare against. It has to come before the finance lead's remark that nothing is yours until it vests, because that remark only works once the grant is smaller and sixteen months have already been spent.

> The sofa you waited weeks for arrives smaller, in a different fabric, "as it came out in the end." You already gave the old one away, and you never wrote down what was promised. Flip it: if the shop told you that cancelling the order would cost you the whole deposit, you would ask for the size and fabric of the sofa in writing before you paid anything more.

**Exit Forfeiture** is the structural lock, and the conversion point is the moment the person does the arithmetic and realises leaving costs everything while staying costs only more of the same. Plausible deniability is built in: at no stage did anyone refuse the equity. It has to come last, because "leave with nothing" only holds you once you are looking at a draft that is smaller than the grey row and sixteen months are spent.

> Think of a loyalty card with nine stamps out of ten. Switching cafes now throws away all nine, so you keep going back, even when the coffee has got worse. Flip it: if the cafe said that switching away would waste all nine stamps, and only then told you the tenth coffee would count for half a stamp, you would stop collecting stamps and ask for the rules in writing.

What weakens the sequence: written equity terms before the first day, a deadline for signed documentation, a request to see the draft grant and the board approval, and independent advice from someone with no interest in the company.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A small startup is built from a few structural facts. It cannot pay market salaries, so it has to offer something else. Ownership of the company is a legal object that needs a board vote, a lawyer and an up-to-date record of who owns what. And the company's cash and its paperwork both follow its funding rounds, not the calendar.

Alignment Framing comes first because a pay cut needs a reason that a salary figure cannot supply. Talking about "skin in this" turns an underpayment into a shared bet, and it costs the company nothing to say.

Paper Deferral comes second because the paperwork really does wait for the cleanup. A grant needs a board vote and a clean record of who owns what, so the honest answer to "when" is "after," and the offer letter can say so in careful words.

Complexity Shield comes third because every delay needs an explanation, and the true explanations are technical. Pool mechanics, refreshes and conversions are real, and few people can judge whether one of them really stops a grant letter from being signed.

Milestone Migration comes fourth because a delay with a new date is cheaper than a delay without one. The next event on the funding calendar is always the natural date, and each date moved forward is a reason to stay for another few months.

Dilution Disguise comes fifth because by the time the grant arrives the company has changed. New hires have taken part of the pool, a later valuation has set the price, and nothing earlier was ever signed that the new number could be held against.

Exit Forfeiture comes last because vesting is built into the structure. A share that vests over time is worth nothing to someone who leaves early, so the finance lead can say it plainly, and the sixteen months already spent do the rest.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: frame it as a shared bet, push the paper to later, explain the delay, keep moving the date, hand over something smaller, and let the vesting clock keep the person in place. It turns a builder who took less for a day years from now into someone who counts what leaving would cost.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A funding calendar and a vesting schedule did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Tobias Varford was a designer who joined a small studio on a salary a quarter below his old one. Its founder, Philippa Ngata, told him over lunch that she did not want an employee, she wanted someone with real skin in the studio, and that his share of it would be papered after the restructure. In the second month the accountant said the restructure was more complicated than it looked. Philippa shrugged apologetically and said the paperwork would come once the next big pitch landed. After the pitch it was once the new quarter began. After the quarter it was once the annual accounts were filed.

In the spring a document finally arrived. It was for a share about half the size she had described at lunch, vesting from that day, not from his first day. "That's how it came out in the end," she said, and he believed she was sorry. When he asked, a month later, what would happen if he left before it vested, she said, kindly, that nothing is anyone's until it vests, and that people who leave early don't take much with them.

Philippa was never confused about what she was doing. She told a friend that people will stay a long time for something that is almost there.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin checking the calendar for the next milestone rather than asking about the last one. Over time, they may find themselves defending the delays to others, because admitting the problem means admitting sixteen months of trust. They may stay longer than they intended, partly out of hope and partly out of sunk cost.

They may avoid asking for written terms to protect the relationship, and then resent not having asked. They may find it hard to explain to family what they actually own, because the answer is a sentence rather than a document.

## Institutional Echo

Early-stage companies routinely trade cash for equity, and equity paperwork often lags the start of work. Offer letters that describe equity as "to be papered" or "subject to board approval" can leave the amount, price, and vesting start undefined. Cap table cleanups, option pool refreshes, and valuation updates are genuine processes that can change what is available and at what price. Where nobody is named as responsible, slow papering can also be deferred indefinitely.

Whether a verbal or letter-based equity promise is enforceable, how grants are priced, and how vesting and tax treatment work are jurisdiction-specific. The honest reading in most cases is a stretched founder and a busy lawyer. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether others were papered promptly, whether the cited milestones actually blocked the grant, and whether the draft diverged from earlier written figures.

## Civilizational Echo

The concealing myth is that equity is the reward for patience: that real builders wait, that asking for the paper is a lack of faith, and that sophisticated people do not need lawyers on every step. The cultural value being exploited is the pioneer's willingness to bet on a shared future.

The public story is the founder who gave early believers a piece of something that became large. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a stake that existed in conversation until the moment it mattered. Nothing here argues against taking equity. It argues for turning it into paper before the work is done.

## Observable Indicators

1. The offer letter describes equity as "to be agreed," "to be papered," or "subject to approval," with no amount, price, or vesting start.
2. Cash is reduced from the first day while equity terms remain undefined.
3. Documentation is repeatedly described as coming after a future event.
4. The reason for delay changes, and each new reason sounds more technical than the last.
5. The cited trigger date moves from one financing event to the next.
6. Verbal assurances arrive on calls but not in follow-up email.
7. Cap table slides show your row as a placeholder, or omit it.
8. Requests for a draft grant, a board approval, or a cap table version are redirected.
9. Others in comparable roles are papered promptly.
10. A draft grant arrives smaller, later-priced, or with a later vesting start than described, with the difference called unavoidable.
11. Questions about timing are answered with reminders of partnership or what leaving would cost.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The offer letter and every draft, with the exact equity sentence and any email in which the amount or percentage was mentioned.
- Dated cap table versions, slides, or models that show your row, preserved as you lawfully received them.
- A running list of each delay, the reason given, the milestone cited, and who gave it.
- Draft option grants or board consents, with dates and any difference from earlier figures.
- Your written requests for documentation and the replies, or lack of them.
- Evidence of how others in comparable roles were papered, where you lawfully know it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Equity genuinely takes time: seed cleanups, pool refreshes, and board calendars are real, and a founder who is overstretched may delay out of disorganization alone. A company that gives you a signed grant letter within a stated window, shows you the draft and the cap table row, and welcomes your counsel's questions looks very different from the pattern. A grant that arrives somewhat different from earlier talk may also reflect honest changes to the round, particularly when the change is explained in writing and applied to everyone.

The discriminators are paper and movement: whether a document with your name has appeared, and whether the trigger date keeps moving without producing one.

Concern strengthens when: the equity sentence stays undefined past a stated documentation window; the cited milestone does not actually prevent papering; and others have been papered while you were told it was not yet possible.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Paper Test:**
Ask: "After sixty to ninety days of work, do I hold a signed grant, a board approval, and a vesting schedule with my name?"
Concern rises if: the answer is no, and the explanation is still "coming."

**SPOT IT — The Explanation Test:**
Ask: "Are the reasons for delay getting more technical while the paper stays the same?"
Concern rises if: each new reason is more complicated and the outcome has not moved.

**SPOT IT — The Milestone Test:**
Ask: "Does the event they are citing actually prevent papering my grant?"
Concern rises if: nobody can explain why, or counsel not connected to the company says it need not.

**SPOT IT — The Comparison Test:**
Ask: "Has anyone in a similar position been papered, and when?"
Concern rises if: yes, and mine was described as impossible.

**SPOT IT — The Walk-Away Test:**
Ask: "If I resigned today, what would I be able to prove I own?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a letter that says "to be agreed."

## Chain Breakers

- **Get the equity terms in writing before day one.** Amount or percentage, class, strike price or valuation basis, vesting start and schedule, and what happens on departure. Have your own counsel read the letter.
- **Set a documentation date in the letter.** "Signed grant documentation within sixty days; if not delivered, the agreed cash equivalent becomes payable." Whether such a term is enforceable depends on jurisdiction and drafting.
- **Treat unpapered equity as unpaid compensation.** Ask in writing what the company proposes to do about the gap, and keep a record of what you have given.
- **Ask for the paper itself.** The draft grant, the board approval, and the cap table row. Keep requests short, written, and dated.
- **Write a same-day summary after each equity conversation.** "To confirm what we discussed today: my grant will be X, at Y, papered by Z." Silence in reply is itself informative.
- **Get independent advice on any draft grant.** Counsel or a qualified adviser should check the price, the vesting start, and any forfeiture on departure before you sign.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Documentation is still missing past the window you were told; the reason has changed more than once; a request for the draft grant or cap table row is answered only verbally.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A draft grant arrives materially smaller or later-priced than described; others are papered and you are not; mention of leaving brings talk of references or reputation; or the amount at stake is material. Consider an attorney with equity and employment experience, independent board members or an audit committee, a forensic accountant for cap table entries, and where relevant an employment or securities regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, or retaliation connected to your questions; pressure to sign a release before seeing figures; fraud in progress; or anyone, including you, speaking of self-harm. Contact emergency services and qualified counsel or law enforcement. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under pressure, and do not delete evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "To confirm what we discussed today: my grant is X, at Y, vesting from Z, and papered by this date."
- "Can you send me the draft grant and the board approval, or tell me who owns producing them?"
- "Which part of the cap table work specifically prevents papering my grant?"
- "I'd like to see my row on the current cap table version."
- "The draft differs from what we discussed in March. Can you walk me through each change in writing?"
- "I'm asking my counsel to read this before I sign."
- "Until the grant is documented, I'd like to discuss how my current work is compensated."
