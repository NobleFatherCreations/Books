# C047: The Confusion Protocol — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a reflex to drop your question when the answer turns long and tangled, and a belief that if you cannot follow the explanation, the fault must be yours.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of nodding along in meetings or talks you do not understand, rather than asking for a plain answer.
- A pull to accept whatever ends a confusing conversation fastest, even when it is not what you came for.
- An automatic sentence, "never mind, it doesn't matter," said when you notice the topic has quietly moved away again.

**A point in a life.** The early years of running a business.

**Where it happens.** A small advisory firm and its founder client.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a founder, forty-one, and you have decided. Not "leaning toward." Decided. Eleven months into an advisory agreement, you have a stack of invoices, a folder of decks you could not summarize in a sentence, and a calendar that shows fewer deliverables than retainer payments. The desk lamp hums in your home office, and your coffee has grown a skin. You have read the termination clause three times: thirty days' written notice, with fees accrued to the date of termination. It is the cleanest paragraph in the agreement. You have drafted the notice and saved it as a PDF, unsent, because you want to say it to his face first. That seems decent. You have a speech, practiced aloud at the desk until it takes thirty seconds: I'm ending the agreement under the termination clause. Please send me a final statement of what's owed to date. I'd like to keep this simple.

**Friday morning, the video call**

The call is on the calendar for twenty minutes. You open with the speech in the first two minutes, almost word for word, and your voice holds. He nods slowly. "I really appreciate you telling me directly." Then: "Can I tell you where I was when I first read the structure of your business?" A hotel lobby, a long flight, a moment when the shape of your problem became clear to him. He talks about the marble in the lobby, the name of the airline, the stranger in the next seat who said something wise. It goes on, fluent and warm. Two minutes. Four. You watch the small square where your own face sits, polite, nodding at a story you cannot use. You wait for it to land somewhere.

When he pauses you say, "Thank you. I just need to know how we close out the agreement." "You want it fast," he says. "But last quarter you told me you wanted to be more deliberate. I'm trying to understand which founder I'm talking to." He smiles, not unkindly. "Help me see the contradiction." You open your mouth and realize you are about to defend yourself. You did say deliberate, last quarter, about hiring. You close your mouth.

At minute eleven you ask it plainly: "Can I send you the notice today?" "I hear you," he says. "I think we're closer than you think. Let me come back to you with some thoughts." "Thoughts on what?" "On the best way forward." He has not said yes. He has not said no. His voice is as friendly as before. You look for the sentence you would object to, and cannot find one.

He does not hang up. He shares his screen: three columns, a values grid he uses with other clients. He asks what you notice. He asks whether you feel heard. There are terms, a framework with a name, and a second framework inside it. You look down at your notes. Your three sentences are there, and nothing else, and you cannot remember which one you were on.

You say you would feel understood if the question were answered. He says that is a fair point and he wants to sit with it. Then: "Before any notice, I'd want us to agree a process." You say you can agree one now. "Then first we'd need to agree what 'closing' really means." He describes endings as transitions rather than terminations. He says "inflection point."

At minute thirty you glance at the clock in the corner of the screen. Your twenty minutes ended ten minutes ago. You say it one last time, quieter. "Can I send you the notice today?" "Let's not make this heavier than it needs to be," he says, and in the same breath he is telling you about an unrelated deal he worked on in another country and what it taught him about patience. You do not ask again.

The call ends. You sit looking at the black rectangle where his face was, and you cannot say whether he agreed, refused, or negotiated. You cannot say what you were told. You are tired in a way that does not match a twenty-minute call. That night you do not eat, and in the morning you check the clause again, three times, in case you misread it.

**Four months later, a bank's meeting room**

A loan officer is walking you through a fee schedule printed on a single sheet. Her answer to your first question runs through two long paragraphs of terms. You begin to ask a second question and the words leave you. "Sounds fine," you say. You nod at each line after that, and she nods back, pleased. When she asks whether there is anything else, you hear yourself say, "Oh, it's nothing." The pen is already in your hand. You have not learned what happens if you repay early, and you sign.

The PDF is still in your drafts folder, with the clause highlighted in yellow. The retainer was paid on the first of the month again, and the month before that.

## The Name

That's **The Confusion Protocol**.

## Definition

The Confusion Protocol is the sequence where a clear, simple question is buried under so much adjacent talk that the person stops asking it and accepts whatever ends the confusion, using Word Salad, Circular Logic, Strategic Ambiguity, Cognitive Overload, Moving Goalposts, and Topic Switching to convert a person's limited capacity for sustained focus into the loss of the original question itself. It may benefit whoever would rather the question never be answered, because an unanswered question leaves the existing arrangement in place. It exploits the fact that in advisory, fund and contract settings, complex vocabulary, layered structures and warm rapport make it socially awkward to say "you have not answered me." It is hard to detect because every individual statement can be sincere, interesting and kind, and because nothing is refused. The person may lose the exit they intended, the timeline they planned around, and sometimes the confidence that their own question was reasonable. In this sector it concentrates in termination calls, fee disputes, waterfall explanations, equity papering conversations and side letter reviews.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Word Salad** — A long, fluent story that goes nowhere: after your speech he thanks you for telling him directly, then describes a hotel lobby, a long flight and the moment the shape of your problem became clear to him. This functions by filling the available time and attention with material that feels too meaningful to interrupt. Concern rises when the passages are emotionally loaded or jargon-heavy, and you cannot say afterwards how any of them answered what you asked.

2. **Circular Logic** — A loop handed back to you: "You want it fast, but last quarter you told me you wanted to be more deliberate. I'm trying to understand which founder I'm talking to. Help me see the contradiction." This functions by making the person audit their own consistency instead of pressing their question. Concern rises when you find yourself defending an earlier remark rather than repeating the request, and when the claimed contradiction does not hold up on a second look.

3. **Strategic Ambiguity** — Agreeable answers that commit to nothing: asked whether you can send the notice today, he says "I hear you," "I think we're closer than you think" and "Let me come back to you with some thoughts," and names only "the best way forward." This functions by giving the feeling of progress without any yes or no on the record. Concern rises when a direct question on a binding point, such as notice, fees owed or the status of equity, receives no direct answer across the whole conversation.

4. **Cognitive Overload** — Too much to hold: he shares a three-column values grid, asks what you notice and whether you feel heard, puts a second framework inside the first, and your three sentences sit unread on your notes page. This functions by degrading your ability to notice that the thread was lost. Concern rises when you leave unable to summarize what was said, even though you were present and attentive throughout.

5. **Moving Goalposts** — A bar that moves once you reach it: first feeling understood, then "Before any notice, I'd want us to agree a process," and when you offer to agree one now, "Then first we'd need to agree what 'closing' really means." This functions by making resolution a target that keeps relocating, so each restatement costs more energy. Concern rises when the stated condition for answering changes after you have met the previous one.

6. **Topic Switching** — A swerve at the moment of pressure: when you ask a last time to send the notice, he says "Let's not make this heavier than it needs to be" and starts on an unrelated deal in another country and what it taught him about patience. This functions by forcing you to re-orient each time instead of building on what you have established. Concern rises when the pivots arrive just as you are about to get a commitment, and when you introduced none of the new topics.

## Why This Order

**Word Salad** has to open the sequence because it creates the fog the rest depends on. In the first minutes, your question is sharp and your agenda is intact. A reply that argues against your decision would be recognizable as resistance and could be answered. Rich, warm, only loosely related material cannot be answered; it can only be endured, and while you endure it the clock runs and your question fades. It has to come before the claimed contradiction, because "which founder I'm talking to" only sounds like a fair reflection after minutes of lobby and airplane stories have worn your question thin.

> You ask a shop assistant whether the sofa will fit through your door. They talk warmly for five minutes about fabrics, delivery and the sofa they have at home. You leave without an answer. Flip it: if the assistant answered your question about the door with "Do you want soft or sturdy? Which is it?" you would say you only asked about the door and ask again for the measurement.

**Circular Logic** comes second because it needs a person who is already slightly off balance. Offered cold, a claimed contradiction looks like a debating trick. Offered after ten minutes of absorbing someone's story, it lands as a genuine reflection. Its job is to turn your attention from your question to your own consistency, and that turn is the first point where you stop asserting and start explaining. It has to come before the "closer than you think" reply, because that answer goes unchallenged only once you are busy defending what you said about being deliberate and have set your own question down.

> "You said you wanted a quiet evening, but you also wanted to see friends. Which is it?" Soon you are defending your own wishes instead of asking what time they are coming. Flip it: if you asked what time your friends were coming and heard only "soon-ish, we'll see" before anyone said "which is it?", you would ask for a time and write down whatever they give.

**Strategic Ambiguity** is the tactic that makes the sequence deniable, and it works because circular reasoning has already made you less sure what you asked. If he had said "no," you would have a refusal to respond to. If he had said "yes," you would have the exit. Because he says neither, there is no event to point to. Everything stays agreeable, and agreeable talk is hard to object to. It has to come before the screen share, because a grid and its framework only pile onto a call where nobody has said yes or no, so there is nothing to hold the columns against.

> You ask your manager for a date for the raise and hear, "I hear you, we're close." It is pleasant, it is neither yes nor no, and you leave unsure what you were told. Flip it: if your manager first handed you a page of pay bands, charts and review dates in one minute, and only then said "I hear you, we're close," you would ask which line is your raise.

**Cognitive Overload** is what the first three produce in combination, and it is also what makes the next steps invisible. Once you are carrying a story, a grid, a claimed contradiction and a stack of reassurances, you no longer have spare capacity to track what has been left out. It has to come before the demand to agree a process first, because you can only be asked to define "closing" once the grid and its inner framework have used up the attention you would check it with.

> A salesperson hands you three leaflets, a comparison table and a discount code in one minute. You nod, because there is no room to ask a question while keeping up. Flip it: if the salesperson first told you the discount applied only to the bigger plan, then only to a longer one, before handing over a single leaflet, you would ask which plan you were being quoted.

**Moving Goalposts** keeps you in motion. An overwhelmed person who is stationary may give up; an overwhelmed person who keeps chasing a relocating point spends the last of their energy. It has to come before the story about the deal abroad, because that story only ends the call once you have been sent from the notice to a process to a definition, with no answer left to wait for.

> You finally clean the whole flat, and are told the real problem is the garden. When the garden is done, it is the shed. Flip it: if the person who came to look at your flat began telling you about their new job before saying what needed doing, you would put the cloth down and ask what they came to say.

**Topic Switching** then closes the loop, because by now the pivots cannot be tracked. It also supplies plausible deniability: each topic was raised in good faith, and no single one was a refusal. It has to come last, because the deal abroad only works once forty minutes of chasing a definition have left you too tired to ask a fourth time.

> You start to say you are upset about the missed birthday, and your friend says, "Did I tell you about my new job?" By the time they finish, the moment to say it has gone. Flip it: if your friend answered your first words about the missed birthday with "Did I tell you about my new job?" and then said "I'll listen once you've been nicer about it," you would say it plainly again.

The key conversion point is the moment you stop restating the question. It is usually quiet and often feels like politeness. What would weaken the sequence: a written question in front of you, a time limit stated at the start, a request for a yes or no on one point, and a written summary sent afterward that makes the missing answer visible.

## Why the System Produces This Order

An advisory engagement is built from a retainer, a calendar and a contract. Fees accrue monthly whether or not a deliverable ships, the relationship is the product, and a termination clause turns on one written notice. Calls are short, often twenty minutes, and the person who runs them also runs the account. Nobody has to design a sequence for what follows: a call about ending the engagement meets these facts and produces it.

Word Salad comes first because the advisor's working language is frameworks, stories and rapport, and when a direct question arrives, that is what he has to hand. A long, warm answer is the default reply of someone paid to talk. It uses up the first minutes, which are the minutes your question is sharpest.

Circular Logic comes second because a story needs somewhere to go, and the nearest material is what you have said in earlier calls. A past remark about being deliberate is a piece of your own wording already on file, so it comes up as a contradiction to be examined.

Strategic Ambiguity comes third because the advisor cannot agree to the notice without losing the account, and cannot refuse it without a dispute. A warm sentence that is neither yes nor no costs nothing and leaves the retainer running.

Cognitive Overload comes fourth because every advisor keeps a library of grids and frameworks, and the screen share is how a call turns from your question to his materials. By then you are holding a story, a contradiction and a reassurance, and the columns fill what attention remains.

Moving Goalposts comes fifth because the contract and the notice are the only fixed points, and the everyday tools for delaying them are process words: first a process, then a definition. Each is reasonable on its own, and each lies between you and the sentence you came to say.

Topic Switching comes last because the clock is the only thing that finishes a call. When the twenty minutes have run out, a new topic fills the time that is left, and the question you asked has no place in it.

Put it together, and the order is the order this setting needs: a fog, a doubt about yourself, a pleasant non-answer, a pile of material, a shifting threshold and a changed subject. It turns a founder with a drafted notice into someone who leaves a call unsure what was said, and who does not mention it.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A retainer, a calendar and a habit of warm talk shaped the call, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a client. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Joaquin Ulmton ran a small catering company and had signed with a consultancy for a year. When he rang the account director, Corinne Maddox, to ask about the fee for a second year, she began with a story about the first restaurant she ever advised. It lasted most of the call. When he asked again, she said, "You told me you wanted growth, and now you want savings. Which Joaquin is this?" He spent ten minutes explaining that he wanted both. Asked for a number, she said, "We're very close, I'd say." He wrote down "close" and could not say what it meant. The next day she sent a sixty-slide deck and a link to three dashboards, with a note asking for his thoughts on the whole. When he wrote back that he only wanted the fee, she replied that a pilot would be needed first, then a baseline, then a review period. On their last call she asked how his daughter's recital had gone, and he told her at length, and the fee did not come up again.

Corinne was never confused about what she was doing. She told a colleague afterward that Joaquin had stopped asking, which was all she needed.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may leave a conversation they entered with a decision and be unable to say whether they won, lost, or simply stopped. Over time, they may begin to doubt that the question was clear to begin with, or wonder whether they were unreasonable to push. They may replay the call looking for the moment a yes or no was given and fail to find it.

They may feel unusually drained after a short conversation and attribute it to their own lack of stamina. They may postpone sending the notice because the call left things "in progress," even though nothing in writing says so. They may begin to dread follow-up calls and quietly stop initiating them. And they may carry a lingering embarrassment about having been a founder who could not hold their ground in twenty minutes, when the more accurate description is a person who asked a simple question that was not answered.

## Institutional Echo

Advisory and fund structures can produce the same effect without any single person choosing it. Where a fee calculation depends on several documents, an explanation of a waterfall or an equity entitlement can run through definitions, cross-references and exceptions long enough that the original question becomes hard to restate. When the same question about fees or papering is routed through an advisor, an administrator, outside counsel and "the process," each answer can be locally accurate while the whole fails to resolve anything.

Meetings convened to decide something can end with a reflection on "the journey," a framework and a promise of follow-up, leaving the decision-makers unsure whether a decision was owed. Standing agenda items such as "alignment" or "recalibration" can substitute for the question of whether to continue.

The honest reading in many cases is that complex structures are hard to explain, that advisors who care about a relationship sometimes talk when they are anxious, and that some people avoid direct answers out of conflict aversion rather than design. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the ambiguity recurs specifically when the question concerns exit, money owed or equity status, and whether the same people answer other questions directly.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that sophisticated people resolve things through conversation rather than paper, and that wanting a plain answer is a sign of rigidity. Close to it sits the belief that a person who insists on clarity does not understand nuance, and that a real partner will always take the time to explore the deeper picture.

In business culture this is reinforced by language that sounds respectful: "let's not rush," "let's make sure we understand each other," "let's find the right framing." Each phrase is often sincere. Together they establish a norm in which asking for a yes or no feels slightly crude.

The value being exploited is patience, the willingness to listen to another person's account before deciding. It is a good value, and nothing here is an argument against listening. The public story is a thoughtful conversation between two people who care about getting it right. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is that one of them asked a plain question at the beginning and never received a plain answer.

## Observable Indicators

1. A direct question about notice, fees owed or exit mechanics receives no yes or no across a conversation of ten minutes or more.
2. Personal stories, frameworks or case studies are offered in place of an answer to a stated question.
3. A claimed contradiction in your position is raised, and on review it does not hold.
4. The reason for not answering changes during the conversation: pace, understanding, process, definitions.
5. New topics are introduced just as a commitment is about to be stated.
6. Reassurances such as "we're close" or "let me come back with thoughts" are given with no timeline or content.
7. A request to confirm in writing is met with a verbal promise, deflected, or not acknowledged.
8. Meeting length expands well beyond the scheduled time without the question being addressed.
9. You cannot summarize afterward what was agreed, and neither can anyone else who was present.
10. The call ends without a follow-up date, a named owner or a document.
11. Later communication refers to the call as having settled something that was never stated aloud.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- **The termination clause and the notice section as they read in the signed agreement,** including the required method and address for notice.
- **Your drafted termination notice with its date,** and a record of when, how and to whom it was sent.
- **Calendar entries for the call,** showing scheduled and actual duration.
- **Your written question or agenda, prepared before the call.**
- **A same-day written summary of the call,** sent to the other party, stating your position, the question you asked, and what response you received; plus any reply or non-reply.
- **Any follow-up messages that describe the call,** especially those that characterize it as having decided something.
- **Fee statements and invoices before and after the call,** and any later demand that refers to it.
- **Names of anyone else who attended or heard about the call.**

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Real conversations about ending a professional relationship are often messy, and many advisors respond to a termination with emotion, defensiveness or an urge to save the relationship. Some people also genuinely talk more when anxious, and some founders themselves arrive with a vague position that makes a clear answer impossible.

Benign lookalikes specific to this combo:

- **Genuine anxiety.** An advisor who is shaken by a termination may ramble with no strategy behind it.
- **Honest attempts to retain.** A single clear retention pitch, followed by a direct answer to your question, is ordinary commercial behavior.
- **Legitimate contract complexity.** If notice mechanics, wind-down fees or survival clauses are genuinely intricate, an explanation may be long for good reason.
- **Your own question may be unclear.** If you asked for "a conversation about options," a wide answer is not evasion.
- **Cultural style.** In some professional cultures, relationship talk precedes business by design.

The discriminator is whether the direct question is eventually answered when it is repeated plainly and a time limit is set. Genuine anxiety usually yields a plain answer under those conditions; a sustained pattern usually does not.

Concern strengthens when: the question goes unanswered even after you ask for a yes or no and state a time limit; the call is followed by a message that treats something as agreed that was not; the same avoidance recurs on other questions about money or exit.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Answer Audit Test:**
Ask: "Across this conversation, how many times was my specific question answered with a yes or a no?"
Concern rises if: the count is zero and the conversation ran well past ten minutes.

**SPOT IT — The Topic Count Test:**
Ask: "How many subjects did we cover that I did not raise?"
Concern rises if: it is more than three, and each arrived as I approached a commitment.

**SPOT IT — The Fade Test:**
Ask: "Can I point to the moment I was persuaded to change my position?"
Concern rises if: I cannot, because the position faded rather than being answered.

**SPOT IT — The Summary Test:**
Ask: "If I write down what was agreed, will the other side confirm it?"
Concern rises if: the summary is ignored, disputed in vague terms, or met with a new conversation instead of a confirmation.

**SPOT IT — The Contradiction Test:**
Ask: "Am I really contradicting myself, or has a contradiction been suggested to me?"
Concern rises if: the supposed inconsistency vanishes when I write both statements side by side.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write the question down and keep it visible.** One sentence: "I am ending the agreement under the termination clause; please confirm the notice process and the amount owed to date."
- **Consider whether the call is needed at all.** A short written notice sent by the method the agreement specifies may do the job without a conversation. Have a qualified adviser confirm what the clause requires.
- **State a time limit at the start.** "I have fifteen minutes and one question that needs a yes or no."
- **Ask for the answer to one point, then stop talking.** After a direct question, silence is not rude; it is room for an answer.
- **Do not argue the claimed contradiction.** Say once, "That is a separate question; I would like the first one answered."
- **Send a written summary the same day.** "Per our call today, I stated that I am ending the agreement under the termination clause. I asked for the notice process and a final statement. I did not receive a confirmation. Please confirm or correct by [date]."
- **Do not treat a verbal "okay" or a promise of "thoughts" as agreement.** Treat the paper as the position until the paper changes.
- **Bring a second person or a counsel for the next conversation,** on the call or reviewing the notice beforehand.
- **Do not secretly record the call.** Ask whether recording is permitted, or rely on your written summary.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A direct question about notice, fees owed or exit has gone unanswered on the call and after a written summary; the other party describes the call as having decided something that was not stated; the same avoidance appears on a second topic.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A demand for fees arrives that does not match the notice you drafted or sent; a notice dispute, delayed settlement figure or auto-renewal claim appears after your notice; the other party disputes that notice was given. This may warrant an attorney with relevant contract experience, and in some arrangements mediation or an independent accountant to review amounts claimed.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, statements about harming your reputation to investors or clients as leverage, or any mention of self-harm by anyone involved. Do not negotiate under threat. Contact emergency or crisis services as appropriate, preserve what you have and tell someone you trust right away.

## Safer Response Language

- "I would like a yes or no on one point: can we end the agreement under the termination clause?"
- "I have fifteen minutes today. I would like to use them on the notice process."
- "I understand your perspective. I would like my question answered before we move to the next topic."
- "To confirm what I heard, I do not yet have a response on the notice. I will put that in an email."
- "I will send a short summary after this call. Please confirm or correct it by Friday."
- "I do not think that is a contradiction, and I would rather not debate it. Can you answer the first question?"
- "Who on your side is responsible for confirming the final statement, and by what date?"
- "I would like my counsel to review the notice before we discuss anything further."
