# CAT02: Exit Prevention & Entrapment Composites — Corporate & Consulting

## Category Introduction

If the first category is about how people get in, this one is about what it costs to leave. It usually begins once some dependency already exists: a founder whose strategy, introductions and investor relationships all run through one advisor, an executive whose unvested equity is the largest asset they hold, a consultant whose entire revenue depends on one anchor client, a family office principal who has handed a trusted advisor more and more of the daily decisions. Nobody locks a door. The door simply gets heavier, a little at a time, and the cost of leaving is priced in money, standing, loyalty and reputation all at once.

In this sector the weight has recognizable forms. Long notice periods and auto-renewal clauses. Exclusivity that quietly blocks alternatives. Retainers that continue regardless of deliverables. Vesting schedules, deferred bonuses and compensation calibrated to be just generous enough that walking away feels foolish. And then the softer weights: the mutual friend whose trust you would burn, the introduction that now carries your name, the small industry where everyone talks, the phrase that comes up whenever you mention leaving, "after everything we built together." Guilt and sunk cost do the work that no clause could.

The disguises are respectable. It is called retention, alignment, commitment, long-term thinking, golden handcuffs offered as a reward, or loyalty framed as a virtue and honor as a reason to stay. Some of this is entirely legitimate. Notice periods, retention packages and exclusivity tied to real consideration are common features of professional life, and a leader who is sad to see you go may simply be sad. What deserves attention is the pattern: leaving mentioned once, and the temperature changing; figures that arrive late; talk of references; the sense that every option outside has become strangely unthinkable.

The protective work happens early and quietly. Understand the exit costs before you commit, and put a real number on them. Keep your own financial footing, your own counsel, and some relationships and reference points outside the arrangement. Review, from time to time, what leaving would actually cost today, rather than what it felt like it would cost last year. If you are already inside, slow down and get independent advice before renewing, signing a release, or agreeing to new terms. Where the situation involves threats, blackmail or retaliation, that is a matter for qualified counsel, appropriate authorities and emergency services, not for a conversation you have alone.
