# C034: Performance Extraction — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that the reward is always just one more push away, and a reflex to give more effort for small crumbs of praise rather than ask for what you are already owed.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A pull toward offers framed as a once-only chance, and a fear of missing out that outweighs asking what it pays.
- A habit of working extra unpaid hours, telling yourself the promised step up is nearly here.
- An automatic sentence, "just one more year and it'll pay off," said again each time the finish line moves.

**A point in a life.** The middle years of a consulting career.

**Where it happens.** A mid-sized consultancy with a partner promotion cycle.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a senior engagement manager at a mid-sized consultancy, thirty-three years old, and what you want is a seat at the partner table. You have been told, by two different partners, that you are one cycle away.

**Two cycles ago, a quiet dinner**

It is after a client win, a quiet restaurant, the partners' glasses catching the candle. The first partner leans back and says, "Honestly, next cycle should be your year." The second raises her glass: "You're basically there." You nod and say thank you, and you do not ask what the year would look like. Nobody at the table mentions a date.

**Last year, the first partner's office**

Last year you billed above your utilisation target by six points. You ran the firm's hardest engagement, a restructuring for a client that had already fired another team, across three time zones, with a junior who left in month two. You took one week of leave in eighteen months and spent half of it on a call. When you say, in a quiet moment, that you have had a recruiter's call and might listen, the first partner puts down his pen. "Eighteen months of what you've put in," he says. "A week of leave. The hardest job we had. You don't walk away from that now." You hear that you would be throwing it all away, and you say you were only thinking aloud.

**January to March, your floor**

In January, at the lift doors on your floor, the same partner says it again. "Next cycle, I really think." A hand rests warmly on your shoulder. "You're close. You really are." Two sentences, a hand, and the doors open. You walk out lighter than you came in, and you do not stop to count how often you have walked out lighter.

Every Tuesday, a partner tells you how good your work is. Every second Thursday, a different partner tells you to "be patient." You start planning your week around the Tuesdays. Your Thursdays leave a dull weight in your chest, and you cannot say what you did wrong between the two.

You say yes when you are asked to lead the graduate recruiting programme, and to sit on the knowledge committee, on top of the hardest engagement. Warmth follows each yes. A partner stops at your desk and asks how you are. Then you say, carefully, that you cannot take the pitch team as well. The partner who had been so warm looks at his phone. "Understood," he says. He answers your next two emails with one word each, and you are not on the invite for the partners' dinner. You take the pitch team.

**March to July, your desk**

In March the new promotion memo circulates. Among the criteria is a line you have not seen before: "A limited number of places on the partner slate are available in any given cycle, and the committee will be selective." You ask how many places. The answer is "that depends on the firm's performance." You ask who else is being considered. The answer is "I can't discuss other people's candidacy." You feel the floor move under your chair, though nothing has been said that you could write down.

You lay it beside October's memo, two pages on the firm's template. Last cycle it asked for "evidence of origination of at least one new client relationship." This cycle, it asks for "origination of new client revenue at a level consistent with partner expectations," and "demonstrated leadership of firm-building initiatives." There is no figure. You ask a partner what level. "It's a judgement, really. We'll know it when we see it." In November you had opened a new client, eleven weeks of unbilled work, and a first-year fee that would, on last year's memo, have been enough.

In July, the results are announced. Two partners are made up. One is a peer who joined after you and who originated a large client in a way that had not been mentioned as a requirement. The other is an external hire. On Monday, you are told that you "gave a great account of yourself," that the committee was "genuinely torn," and, kindly, that the next cycle is "a clean sheet." You say thank you.

**Two years later, a Sunday evening**

Two years later it is a Sunday evening at your kitchen counter. You are three months into a job at a smaller firm, answering client emails for a project that is not even yours. On Friday your new director said that a few people would be offered a stake in the practice, "if the paperwork is in by month end," and you said yes before you thought to ask what the stake was worth. Your sister texts to ask whether the raise has come through. You tell her it is a matter of weeks, the same way you told her about the last one.

You close the laptop at nine and open it again at ten. A colleague asks whether you can take a Saturday shift. You hear yourself write, "Give it till spring. It'll come good," and you are not sure whether you wrote it to her or to yourself.

Nobody has promised you anything in writing since that first dinner, and you have not asked anyone to.

## The Name

That's **Performance Extraction**.

## Definition

Performance Extraction is the sequence where maximum effort is drawn out by a reward that is always just ahead, sustained by small signs of progress, and kept out of reach by a finish line that moves, using Future Faking, Sunk Cost Exploitation, Bread-crumbing, Intermittent Reinforcement, Conditional Love, Manufactured Scarcity, and Moving Goalposts to convert motivation from a promised future reward into uncompensated effort already delivered. It benefits whoever receives the work, and it exploits ambition and patience. It is hard to detect because promotion pipelines are competitive, partner slates are genuinely limited, and criteria do evolve. What can be lost is years of work, fair pay for it, health, and the chance to move elsewhere while still marketable.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Future Faking** — Promises for later: at a quiet dinner after a client win, "Honestly, next cycle should be your year," and in a lift two cycles ago, "You're basically there." This functions by placing the reward in a future that no one is bound to deliver, so effort is spent now against a promise. Concern rises when the promise is verbal, repeated by several people and attached to no written criteria.

2. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — Past effort used to hold you: when you mention a recruiter's call, a partner lists the eighteen months, the week of leave and the hardest engagement, and says, "You don't walk away from that now." This functions by making leaving feel like wasting everything already given. Concern rises when what has been invested is mentioned as a reason to stay.

3. **Bread-crumbing** — Small scraps of hope: a hand on your shoulder with "You're close. You really are," and in the April lift, "Next cycle, I really think." This functions by providing just enough encouragement to keep hope alive without a commitment. Concern rises when the signs arrive at moments when effort or retention is at risk.

4. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — Mixed signals: a partner praising your work every Tuesday, another telling you to "be patient" every second Thursday. This functions by alternating reward and delay so that the person keeps trying to find the rule. Concern rises when encouragement and setback do not track the person's results.

5. **Conditional Love** — Warmth tied to extra work: friendliness after each added role, then one-word emails and a missing dinner invite when you say you cannot take the pitch team too. This functions by making belonging depend on output. Concern rises when attention rises with each additional commitment and falls when the person asks about terms.

6. **Manufactured Scarcity** — A limit with no number: "A limited number of places," "that depends on the firm's performance," and "I can't discuss other people's candidacy." This functions by making the reward feel rare and competitive, justifying any extra effort and making questions seem entitled. Concern rises when the number cannot be stated or changes, or is revealed after commitments were made.

7. **Moving Goalposts** — A bar that changes: last cycle's origination of one new client becomes "revenue at a level consistent with partner expectations" plus firm-building, with no figure and "We'll know it when we see it." This functions by ensuring the target is always newly out of reach. Concern rises when the criterion changes after the work has been done and no written standard exists in advance.

## Why This Order

**Future Faking** has to come first because the whole pattern is an exchange of present labour for future reward. Without a believable future, there is no reason to over-deliver. It costs nothing to say, which is why it can be repeated by many people. It has to come before the speech about eighteen months of what you have put in, because he can only point to what you have given once he has already told you that next cycle should be your year.

> A manager says, "The corner office is yours next year." You stay late for months on the strength of one sentence. Flip it: if your manager opened with "Think of all the years you've given this team," you would ask what you have actually been promised.

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** follows because the future promise needs to be protected by the past. The more has been given, the more painful it is to stop. It has to come before the hand on your shoulder at the lift, because "you're close" only keeps you going once you cannot walk away from the hardest job and the week of leave.

> Think of a gym membership you stopped using. You keep paying because you have already paid for so many months. Flip it: if the gym kept sending "Big new classes coming soon!" before you had paid a single month, you would wait until the classes actually existed.

**Bread-crumbing** keeps the hope alive during the gaps; without it, the person would take stock. It has to come before the Tuesday praise and the Thursday "be patient," because that pattern only trains you once you are already living on small moments at the lift doors and nothing larger.

> A friend sends a heart on your birthday and a "we should meet soon" in spring. You never quite give up on the friendship, and you never quite get it. Flip it: if your friend was warm on one call and cold on the next from the very start, you would ask them, "Are we actually meeting up?"

**Intermittent Reinforcement** then does the training work: unpredictable signals tend to keep people trying longer than predictable ones, and they make it hard to judge whether the effort is working. It has to come before the partner who looks at his phone, because that cold reply only costs you something once you have built your week around the Tuesdays.

> A teacher praises your work on Tuesday and sighs at it on Thursday. You keep trying new things, unsure which one worked. Flip it: if the teacher was friendly only on days you stayed to tidy the classroom, you would notice that and ask about your actual work.

**Conditional Love** links the reward to output on a personal level. It is not only that the promotion is conditional; the warmth of the partners becomes conditional too. It has to come before the memo's limited places, because "a limited number of places" only explains a missed seat once you are already carrying the programme, the committee and the pitch team for their warmth.

> A relative is cheerful when you help with the move and short with you the week you say you are busy. Flip it: if your relative said, "Only one helper gets dinner after the move," before any mood changed, you would ask why only one.

**Manufactured Scarcity** then provides the rationale for any outcome. If few places exist, then any non-promotion can be explained by the competition, not by the shifting standard. It has to come before the October and March memos side by side, because you only compare criteria once the limited places have made any result seem like a matter of competition.

> You are told "only a few seats are left" for a school trip. When you miss one, it feels like bad luck, not a question about how many seats there were. Flip it: if the sign-up rules for the school trip changed every few days before anyone mentioned seats, you would ask the teacher to write the rules down.

And **Moving Goalposts** delivers the final control: the memo is issued each cycle, but never in a form that can be held against the last one. It has to come last, because the memo can change without being held against the last one only after the dinner, the leave, the lift, the Tuesdays, the pitch team and the limited places have all been lived through.

> A game's rules are posted each week, and each week the winning score is a different number. You can never say you were cheated, because the old rules are gone. Flip it: if the winning score kept changing and only then you heard "Just two prizes left," you would keep copies of each week's rules and ask how many prizes there are.

What weakens the sequence is written criteria obtained early, a number of places stated in advance, a promotion discussion that includes a date and a named decision-maker, and an outside benchmark such as the market for an engagement manager of your profile. The key conversion point is the second time you are told "next cycle."

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A partnership promotes on a yearly cycle, so every promise has to be a promise about next cycle. The criteria are set out in a memo reissued each year, and nothing requires the new memo to match the old one. And a slate has a limited number of places, set by how the firm did, which nobody can state in advance. A yearly cycle, a rewritten memo and a slate of unknown size: those three facts produce the order below.

Future Faking comes first because a promise about next cycle costs nothing to say, and every partner who works with a strong manager has a reason to say it. Without a believable future there is no reason to over-deliver.

Sunk Cost Exploitation comes second because by the next conversation a year of hard work already exists. When a person raises leaving, the nearest argument is what they have already given, and it happens to be true.

Bread-crumbing comes third because partners are busy, and a short line at a lift is what a busy person can give. A hand on a shoulder and "you're close" are enough to stop a person from taking stock.

Intermittent Reinforcement comes fourth because different partners give different signals on different days: praise on Tuesdays from one, patience on Thursdays from another. Nobody coordinates it, so no one can tell which of the two is the true reading.

Conditional Love comes fifth because every extra yes is a favour to a partner personally. Warmth is easy to give to someone who says yes and just as easy to withdraw from someone who says no, in a one-word reply and an invite that does not arrive.

Manufactured Scarcity comes sixth because a slate with limited places lets any outcome be explained by the competition. The number depends on the firm's performance, and other candidacies cannot be discussed.

Moving Goalposts comes last because the memo is rewritten each cycle, and a manager who compares the two finds a level with no figure attached. Nobody has to decide to change the standard; the new memo is simply the one in force.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a promise before the work, a past before an exit, crumbs before signals, warmth before a limit, a shortage before a standard. It turns a manager waiting for a seat into one who keeps a log of promises.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A yearly promotion cycle, a memo that is reissued and a slate of limited size were enough, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a senior colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Oskar Penhallow had been a senior consultant at a mid-sized firm for three years when his head of practice, Hester Yarrow, told him at a dinner after a client win that the next round should be his. Oskar worked through the winter on the firm's hardest account. When he mentioned a recruiter's call, Hester set down her glass and listed his long hours and his one week of leave, and said he could not walk away from that now. At the lift she put a hand on his shoulder and said, "You're close. You really are." Praise arrived on Tuesdays from one director, and "be patient" on alternate Thursdays from another. When he declined a third committee, Hester answered his next two emails with a single word each, and his name was missing from the invitation to the directors' dinner. He took the committee. The new promotion memo spoke of a limited number of places, and when he asked how many, Hester told him it depended on how the firm did. The criteria had changed since the last memo, and when he asked what level was meant, she said they would know it when they saw it. A colleague who joined after him was promoted. Hester told a friend that she never gave a date, only a cycle.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may take on more work in order to meet each new threshold, give up leave, sleep and relationships, and become unable to say how much they have given. They may keep a private log of promises. They may start to feel that leaving would make the sacrifice pointless.

They may feel ashamed that they still believe it. They may begin to doubt their own judgement about whether the criteria truly changed. And they may stay long after a market offer would have paid better, because the promise has become part of who they are.

## Institutional Echo

Firms with up-or-out structures, utilisation targets and a limited partner slate create the conditions for this without anyone needing to design it. Promotion memos issued each cycle with different thresholds, unstated slate sizes, and recognition tied to billable hours and origination can all work together. Where promotion discussions are held in closed committees, a candidate rarely sees the reasoning.

Many firms genuinely adjust criteria as the business changes and as the slate shrinks or grows. Whether a given firm is adjusting honestly or extracting effort depends on evidence, for example whether similar candidates over several cycles describe the same moving finish line, and whether those promoted met the written criteria in force while they worked. How promotion and compensation are structured differs between firms and should not be assumed universal.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that hard work is rewarded eventually, that the best are recognised, and that anyone who leaves early did not have what it takes. It is one of the most powerful professional beliefs, and it makes patience look like virtue.

The value being exploited is delayed gratification, which is real and honourable. The public story is a firm that grows its partners from within. The private loss, where the pattern is present, is years of the best working life spent chasing a figure that was never written down.

## Observable Indicators

1. Promotion is promised verbally by several people, with no written criteria.
2. The criteria memo changes its thresholds each cycle.
3. The number of available places is not stated, or is revealed after the work is done.
4. The person is told they are close, repeatedly, across cycles.
5. Utilisation or origination targets rise as the person approaches the criterion.
6. Those promoted did not match the criteria that were in force while others worked.
7. Requests for a figure or an example are met with "we'll know it when we see it."
8. The person is told that other candidacies cannot be discussed.
9. Encouragement is concentrated at times when the person is considering leaving.
10. Extra commitments are added as proof of readiness.
11. Past sacrifices are mentioned when the person raises concerns.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every version of the promotion criteria memo, with dates, and a side-by-side of the thresholds.
- A dated log of every promotion statement, with the name of who said it, where and in what words, preferably confirmed by a follow-up email.
- Your utilisation, origination and delivery figures for each cycle.
- The record of unbilled work, new client pursuit and firm-building duties you undertook.
- Written feedback, reviews and counselling notes mentioning partner readiness.
- Evidence of the slate size announced at any point, and when you learned it.
- Names of peers with similar experiences across cycles.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Partnership decisions are genuinely competitive and slates really are limited. Firms do revise criteria when the strategy changes. A partner may be sincerely encouraging and sincerely unable to promise. A candidate may also be a close second for good reasons. The discriminator is whether the criteria are written before the cycle, whether they are applied consistently, and whether the shortfall is explained against them.

Concern strengthens when: the threshold rises after you pass the previous one; places are announced after commitments; and promoted candidates met different criteria from those you were set.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Written Finish Line Test:**
Ask: "Where is the exact threshold, with a number, written down?"
Concern rises if: "It's a judgement."

**SPOT IT — The Slate Test:**
Ask: "How many places exist this cycle, and who decides?"
Concern rises if: no one will say.

**SPOT IT — The Version Test:**
Ask: "How does this year's memo differ from last year's?"
Concern rises if: the thresholds rose after I met the earlier ones.

**SPOT IT — The Next Cycle Test:**
Ask: "How many times have I been told it's next cycle?"
Concern rises if: more than once, with no change in the written criteria.

**SPOT IT — The Cost Test:**
Ask: "What would I do if I knew it would not happen?"
Concern rises if: I'd leave tomorrow.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the criteria in writing before the cycle opens.** "Could you share the criteria and the number of places for this cycle?"
- **Follow every verbal promise with a summary.** "To confirm what we discussed: you indicated I'd be put forward next cycle. Please let me know if I have that wrong."
- **Keep copies of each memo** and compare them yourself.
- **Set a decision date for yourself.** If the finish line moves again, you will act.
- **Test the market.** A quiet conversation with a recruiter or a former colleague gives you an outside benchmark.
- **Ask for a candid sponsor conversation.** A partner who is willing to put the commitment and conditions in writing is worth far more than one who is not.
- **Protect leave and health.** Treat them as terms of the engagement, not extras.
- **Do not threaten to leave to extract a promise.** Use real options, calmly.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A second verbal promise with no written criteria; a memo whose thresholds shifted after you met the prior ones; a slate size revealed late.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Equity, partnership terms or compensation tied to a promise that is not in writing; evidence that others were promoted on criteria you were not given; unpaid or off-contract work materially above your role. Consider a qualified employment or contract lawyer, an independent senior mentor, HR if independent of the partners involved, and, where there is a compliance concern, the firm's compliance or whistleblower channel.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Burnout with risk to health or safety, statements of self-harm by you or a colleague, or threats and intimidation linked to the cycle. Prioritise medical and crisis support, contact local emergency or crisis services if you or someone you know is in danger, and do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you share the written criteria for this cycle and the number of places?"
- "To confirm what we discussed in the lift today: you indicated I'd be put forward next cycle."
- "Last year's memo asked for one new relationship. I delivered one. What changed in this year's?"
- "What specific revenue level is consistent with partner expectations?"
- "Who is the decision-maker, and when will I hear?"
- "I'd like to understand how to be ready, in terms I can measure."
- "I'm planning my next two years. Could we agree what 'ready' means by a specific date?"
- "I've taken on the recruitment programme. How is that recorded against the criteria?"
