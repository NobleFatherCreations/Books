# C059: The Scapegoat Funnel — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that when you stand up front for a shared decision you will be left to carry it alone, and a fear that spoken permission vanishes the moment trouble arrives.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of asking for everything in writing, even small yeses, because once a spoken yes disappeared.
- A pull to stay in the background of any cause, even ones you believe in, so you are never its face.
- An automatic thought, "when it goes wrong, it'll all land on me," whenever someone calls you the right person for the job.

**A point in a life.** Midcareer, trusted with a public task.

**Where it happens.** The investor relations office of a large company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a head of investor relations, forty-five, and you have spent fifteen years being the calm voice between a company and the people who own it. You are good at the job because you are careful with words. You are known for never saying more than the company is ready to say, and never less than it needs to.

**Tuesday evening, the chief executive's office**

The chief executive calls you in with the board chair on the speakerphone. The room smells of old coffee and leather. The company is about to announce a price increase that will hit a large share of its customers, and shareholders will want to know why. They need someone to take the call. The chair says the company is lucky to have someone with your credibility. The chief executive says this is a moment for steady leadership, that the mission needs a trusted face, and that he would not trust it to anyone else. You sit up straighter. You feel chosen.

You ask whether the language has been cleared. The chief executive says that he and the chair have agreed the key points. He says you do not need to chase paper. "We are aligned. You have the room." The chair adds that legal has seen the substance, and that there is no need to burden them with the script on the eve of an announcement. Asking for a signature would sound like doubt.

**Tuesday night, your desk**

You write the Q&A that night, eleven pages, with a sleeve of crackers beside the keyboard and the cleaner's trolley rattling past your door around ten. You send it to both of them at midnight. The chief executive replies at six with a thumbs-up. The chair sends a text: "Looks good, go get 'em." You save both.

**Thursday, the shareholder call**

You sit alone in the boardroom with a speakerphone and a glass of water. The call is not friendly. A large holder asks why customers were not told earlier. Another asks whether the increase was modeled against churn. Three times you are asked about the decision process, and your palms go damp. You say it was reviewed by the board, which is what you were told. Your name is on the invitation and the recording; theirs are on neither. Afterward, the chief executive calls to say you were wonderful.

**Monday, the chief executive's office**

By the weekend the press has the story. A columnist writes that the company's explanation was "evasive." A customer group calls the call "tone-deaf." A paragraph names you: "the company's head of investor relations told shareholders the board had signed off." Your phone has not stopped since Saturday. The chief executive, the chair and someone from legal each forwarded you that paragraph within an hour of its appearing, one after another. Each asked you to explain the line. Holders' emails landed between theirs. Now you sit in his office with the phone face down, and the chair is on the speaker again.

"I'm surprised the line about board approval was used so strongly," the chief executive says, carefully. "It was never intended to be quite so definitive." The chair says she wants to be sure the board is not being characterised in a way that creates exposure. She would hate for this to look like the company hid behind a staff member.

You are quiet. You say that you have the script, and the replies. The chief executive says warmly that he does not doubt it, but that the script is yours, and it was the nature of the call that you improvise. The chair asks, softly, whether you might consider stepping back from public-facing duties while the matter settles. Then she tells you what will go out in the morning, and reads it aloud: "The company has reviewed its shareholder communications, and the head of investor relations is pursuing other opportunities." Your name is in the second sentence. Neither of theirs is.

**Two years later**

It is a Wednesday in October, and you are the part-time communications adviser to a small tenants' association. The chair of the association, a retired electrician with a kind voice, asks whether you could speak to the local radio station on Friday. You say you would be glad to help. Then you hear yourself ask whether he could put that in an email, along with the three points he would like made. He laughs, and sends it, and you save it in a folder with the date on it.

When he says you are the obvious person to stand up at the front of the campaign, your hands go cold. You offer to write the statement and let someone else read it. You tell yourself that is only sensible. You would like to be part of it. You would like to be part of it from the back of the room.

At home, in a folder with your own name on it, there is still a thumbs-up and a text. They are the only part of that year you can prove. The committee thinks you are modest, and you let them.

## The Name

That's **The Scapegoat Funnel**.

## Definition

The Scapegoat Funnel is the sequence where a person is recruited with heroic language, given only verbal approval, made the sole public face of a shared decision, and then severed from ownership when backlash arrives, so that accountability runs downward to the most replaceable figure, using Mission Framing, Verbal Authorization, Visibility Inversion, Pressure Accumulation, Narrative Severance and Sacrificial Repositioning to convert a willingness to publicly represent a cause into the loss of institutional backing when it is most needed. In corporate settings it concentrates in investor relations, communications, fractional executives and spokespeople. It is hard to detect because the recruitment is flattering and the verbal approval sounds like trust. Intent would require additional evidence; a panicked board and a genuinely loose process can produce the same outcome.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Mission Framing** — A calling, offered: on a Tuesday evening the chief executive, with the chair on speaker, calls it a moment for steady leadership, says the mission needs a trusted face, and says he would not trust it to anyone else. This functions by linking the task to identity and purpose so that it is hard to decline. Concern rises when the language of mission replaces a description of authority and risk.

2. **Verbal Authorization** — Agreement without paper: the chief executive and chair say they have "agreed the key points," legal need not see the script, and your eleven-page Q&A is approved by a thumbs-up and a text reading "Looks good, go get 'em." This functions by creating approval that is hard to prove and easy to recast. Concern rises when sign-off is requested and offered only informally.

3. **Visibility Inversion** — One name on the line: you take the shareholder call and answer three questions about the decision process, and your name, not theirs, is on the invitation, the recording and the article. This functions by placing the most public risk on the most junior owner. Concern rises when the decision-makers stay out of view while a staff member speaks.

4. **Pressure Accumulation** — Everyone at once: a columnist calls the explanation "evasive," a customer group calls it "tone-deaf," and within an hour the chief executive, the chair and someone from legal each forward you the paragraph naming you and ask you to explain the line. This functions by raising the cost of dissent just as the person is most visible. Concern rises when each stage brings greater pressure and less support.

5. **Narrative Severance** — A line walked back: the chief executive is "surprised" the board-approval line was used so strongly, says it was never intended to be quite so definitive, and the chair wants the board not characterised in a way that creates exposure. This functions by separating the decision from the statement made about it. Concern rises when leaders describe your words as improvisation.

6. **Sacrificial Repositioning** — A departure announced: the company's statement says it "reviewed its shareholder communications" and that the head of investor relations is "pursuing other opportunities," with your name in the second sentence and neither of theirs. This functions by moving the story from the decision to the person. Concern rises when the departure is announced as the response to the issue.

## Why This Order

**Mission Framing** has to come first, because an experienced professional will ask for paper if the role is described as a task. Described as a calling, the role has a feeling attached, and the feeling answers the questions that paper would ask. It has to come before the talk of alignment and no paper, because asking for a signature only feels like doubt once you have been told the mission needs you and no one else.

> A neighbour says, "You're the one everyone trusts. Will you chair the street party?" It feels like an honour, so you don't think to ask about money or permits. Flip it: if your neighbour handed you the party money and said 'no need for paperwork', before ever calling you the one everyone trusts, you would ask in writing who pays and who handles the permits.

**Verbal Authorization** follows because it is the load-bearing wall. A script approved by a thumbs-up and a text is approval that can later be described as encouragement. It is believable because it is how trusted colleagues do work. It is also the one step that the target can test, by asking for a written sign-off, and the one that most often fails to be requested because it feels like distrust. It has to come before your name on the call invitation, because you stand up on Thursday on the strength of a thumbs-up and a text that nobody has to own.

> A friend says, "Just book it, we'll sort the money out later." Later, they remember it differently, and all you have is a thumbs-up. Flip it: if your friend put your name on the booking as the one in charge before any money was agreed, you would ask them to confirm the cost in a written message first.

**Visibility Inversion** is what the first two have prepared. The face of the decision becomes the person who did not make it. This is the tactic that weakens independent evaluation: the person is now so exposed that stepping back appears to confirm the criticism. It has to come before the forwarded paragraph, because the three of them can only ask you to explain the line once you alone are the name the press has printed.

> A group agrees to cancel the party. You are the one at the door telling guests, so you take all the grumbling. Flip it: if the group kept sending you small nudges to deal with the guests before anyone had agreed to cancel, you would ask that the whole group stand at the door together.

**Pressure Accumulation** raises exit cost and the cost of dissent. As press and holders react, the leadership's concern for exposure grows, and a request for support starts to look like a demand. It has to come before the chief executive's surprise at the board line, because he can call it too definitive only after three people have each asked you to explain it.

> One friend complains about you, then another, then the group chat. Each message is small, and together they feel like a crowd. Flip it: if the first friend said they never told you to do it, before any complaint arrived, you would write down what was agreed and ask that friend to confirm it.

**Narrative Severance** supplies plausible deniability. No one lies; the statement is simply described as the speaker's own. It has to come before the morning announcement, because the line that you were only moving on reads as accountability once your own script is described as yours alone.

> "I never meant it like that," says the person who told you what to say. You remember it differently, and there is nothing to point to. Flip it: if you heard you were "stepping back" before the person who told you what to say had denied anything, you would ask them in writing what they actually asked of you.

**Sacrificial Repositioning** closes the loop. A departure announcement reads as accountability while leaving the decision-makers untouched. It has to come last, because the announcement only reads as a fair outcome after your name is on the call, the press has quoted you and the script is called yours alone.

> After a group mistake, the club posts that one member has "decided to move on." Everyone else carries on as before. Flip it: if the club posted that you had moved on and only afterwards a member said the mistake was never their call, you would notice the timing and ask the club in writing who decided.

The conversion point is the Monday call, when "we are aligned" becomes "your words." What would weaken the sequence is written approval of the script before the call, legal review of the substance, a clear statement of who owns the decision, and a record of the request.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A company that makes a hard announcement needs a person to speak, and the speaker has to be someone the audience finds credible. Executives and the board sit apart from the people who face the public, and legal review on the eve of an announcement is short and usually skipped for speed. A public statement also has to show accountability afterward, and the cheapest accountability is a departure. A named spokesperson, a thin approval trail and an announcement that has to look fair: those facts produce the order below.

Mission Framing comes first because a careful professional would ask for paper if the job were described as a task. Described as a calling, it carries a feeling that answers the questions the paper would ask.

Verbal Authorization comes second because approval on the eve of an announcement is quick, spoken and friendly. A thumbs-up and a text are how trusted colleagues work, and asking for a signature feels like doubt.

Visibility Inversion comes third because the call needs one voice, and that voice's name goes on the invitation and the recording. The decision was shared, but the face is single.

Pressure Accumulation comes fourth because press and shareholders react, and each leader's concern for exposure arrives on its own schedule. Three messages in an hour need no coordination to feel like one voice.

Narrative Severance comes fifth because once exposure is the worry, each person describes their own words in the most careful way. Nobody has to lie; a line approved in conversation can later be called the speaker's own.

Sacrificial Repositioning comes last because a company must show it has responded. A departure announcement reads as accountability and leaves the decision untouched.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a chosen face, an unwritten yes, a single name, a crowd of messages, a loosened line, an exit. It turns the person trusted with the room into the person who carries it.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A short approval window, a name on a call invitation and an announcement that had to look fair, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a mid-level manager before a company meeting. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Esmeralda Quigley, an operations manager of nine years, was asked by her vice president, Roderick Thaxter, to present the new overtime rules at the all-hands. "You're the one people trust to explain things," he said. "The company needs you on this." When she asked whether legal had read the slides, he told her they were aligned, and that nobody needed to slow the launch down for paper. His reply to her draft was a thumbs-up, and his note to the group read, "Great, Es has it." At the meeting her name sat alone on the first slide. The questions came hard, and she answered from what she had been told. By Friday the staff channel had posted her slide in forty replies. Roderick, a director and someone from legal each messaged her to ask her to clarify the line. The following Monday Roderick told her he was surprised the rule had been put so firmly. A short note went to staff: Esmeralda "has decided to pursue other work."

Roderick was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that every launch needs somebody to stand next to it.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to replay the call, wondering what they could have said differently. Over time, they may feel foolish for having trusted a verbal assurance, and angry at themselves for it. They may withdraw from professional networks, delay a job search from fear of how the departure reads, and be reluctant to explain it. They may also find that colleagues avoid them, not from hostility but from the fear of being next.

## Institutional Echo

Companies often rely on investor relations and communications staff to carry decisions made elsewhere, and informal approvals by senior leaders are common in fast-moving situations. A spokesperson role with no written authority can leave the individual exposed if the account is later contested. Disclosure practices, board responsibilities and spokesperson liability are matters of securities and corporate law, which vary by jurisdiction. Announcements framing a departure as a response to a controversy are used in many settings.

The honest reading is that boards do worry about exposure, that scripts are often approved informally, and that a stronger line than intended may genuinely have been used on a live call. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether similar approvals were given in writing for other communications, and whether the decision-makers acknowledged the earlier agreement internally.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that leaders take responsibility, and that a team player takes the heat for the team. "The buck stops here" is usually said by someone who intends it to stop somewhere else. A related belief says that people at the sharp end should simply trust leadership.

The value being exploited is loyalty and professional pride. The public story is a company that listened and acted. The private loss, when this pattern is present, is a career and a name.

## Observable Indicators

1. A role is offered with language of mission and trust, not authority.
2. Approval of a script or statement is given only by call, text or emoji.
3. Requests for written sign-off are deflected as unnecessary.
4. Legal or board review is described as unneeded or already done, without evidence.
5. The staff member alone fronts the public event.
6. Decision-makers do not appear on the call or in the coverage.
7. After backlash, the statement is described as the speaker's improvisation.
8. Leaders ask the person to step back "while it settles."
9. A departure announcement names the staff member and not the decision-makers.
10. Earlier assurances are described as misunderstood.
11. The person's access to colleagues and information narrows.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The final script and every earlier draft, with dates.
- Any reply, emoji, text or message that approves the script or the substance, preserved with date and sender visible.
- The calendar invite and any notes from the preparation conversation.
- The record of who attended the call and who spoke.
- Your same-day written account of the verbal instruction.
- The announcement and press coverage, with dates.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Spokespeople are often asked to carry decisions, and informal sign-off is common under time pressure. A call can go badly for reasons that have nothing to do with design, and a board can be genuinely worried about how a statement was used.

The discriminator is whether support continues when the pressure rises. A good-faith leadership group stays visible, acknowledges what it approved, and shares exposure.

Concern strengthens when: approvals can no longer be found; leaders describe your words as your own; the announcement names you and not the decision.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Paper Test:**
Ask: "Do I have written approval for what I am about to say?"
Concern rises if: all I have is a verbal assurance.

**SPOT IT — The Face Test:**
Ask: "Who else will be visible when this is announced?"
Concern rises if: nobody.

**SPOT IT — The Owner Test:**
Ask: "Who owns the decision, and will they say so in writing?"
Concern rises if: no one will.

**SPOT IT — The Distance Test:**
Ask: "Are leaders describing my words as mine?"
Concern rises if: yes, after backlash.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for written sign-off on the script** or on the key statements, in an email with a clear subject line.
- **Send a confirming email after any verbal approval:** "To confirm, you and the chair approved the board-approval line."
- **Ask who owns the decision** and who will be on the call.
- **Ask for legal and board review** of the substance, in writing.
- **Do not state facts you cannot verify.** Say, "I will confirm and come back."
- **Ask counsel of your own** before agreeing to a role that exposes you.
- **Keep a same-day note** of any instruction given verbally.
- **Do not make public statements** about the departure without advice.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Approval is verbal only; you are asked to be the sole face; legal review is waved away.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Backlash arrives and leaders distance themselves; you are asked to step back or accept a statement naming you; documents show a different approval than what is now claimed. Consider an employment or securities attorney, independent directors or an audit committee chair, a professional body for investor relations or communications if you are a member, and a securities or financial services regulator where a disclosure is in question.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, blackmail, intimidation, harassment or doxxing, or self-harm statements by anyone involved. Do not confront; preserve records lawfully, tell someone you trust, and contact emergency, crisis, counsel or law enforcement services.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before the call, could you confirm in writing that you and the chair have approved this script?"
- "Who will be on the call alongside me to speak to the decision?"
- "Has legal reviewed the substance? Could you confirm by email?"
- "To confirm our conversation, you approved the line about the board's review."
- "I would like to confirm the facts before I state them. I will come back to you."
- "I would like my adviser to see the proposed announcement before it is issued."
