# C068: The Committee Burial — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that being invited onto a panel means you are being heard, and a slow loss of urgency as meetings stretch across years while the problem stays exactly the same.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of joining every group formed to look at a problem, and feeling guilty when nothing comes out of it.
- A pull to trust an invitation to "help shape the process," and a quiet unease later about what changed.
- An automatic sentence, "it's with the committee," said for years about something that still is not fixed.

**A point in a life.** Midcareer, with a proposal you believe in.

**Where it happens.** A mid-sized manufacturing and distribution company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a sustainability manager, thirty-eight, at a mid-sized manufacturing and distribution company, and you have spent two years building a case. A set of suppliers in your tier-two network has no audited labor or emissions reporting. You've read the supplier contracts, mapped the exposure, and drafted a one-page policy change: a minimum reporting standard, a twelve-month phase-in, and a review of the three highest-risk contracts.

**February, the executive meeting room**

You present it to the executive team in a room with a long table and a jug of water that nobody touches. The response is better than you hoped. The chief operating officer calls it "exactly the kind of thinking we need." The chief financial officer asks sensible questions. The CEO says the topic deserves "the right process, with the right people at the table." You had been braced for a no, and this is not a no.

Before you reach the door, your phone buzzes with an email titled "Corporate Responsibility Working Group — Invitation to Chair." You are asked to chair. You say yes before you have finished reading. You walk back to your desk with your cheeks aching from smiling.

**March, a third-floor meeting room**

The first meeting is energetic, with coffee and a slide that says "Our shared commitment." Eleven people are in the room, among them procurement, legal, a consultant on a short engagement and a junior analyst taking minutes. You hand around your one-pager. People say it's a great starting point. Someone suggests the group should "build a shared understanding first." There's a vote on a name. You are warm and flexible, and you agree to everything that seems reasonable, because it all does.

Within three weeks the group has twenty-three members, because other functions "want to be involved." Procurement asks for a seat for each region. Legal asks that anything the group produces be reviewed by a separate panel. The consultant suggests a benchmark exercise and a stakeholder survey. These are good ideas. You say yes, because the alternative looks like obstruction. When the survey draft reaches you, its first line reads: "Given the decision to keep current supplier terms through the next cycle, how should we engage stakeholders?" You read it twice and tell yourself it is a drafting slip.

The charter arrives before the end of the month, five pages. Section 3 is titled Timeline. Phase One, scoping, three months. Phase Two, stakeholder engagement, six months. Phase Three, options analysis, four months. Phase Four, "recommendations to the leadership team," five months. Total: eighteen months. Section 5 states that the group's outputs "are advisory and subject to leadership prioritization in line with business conditions." There is a line for the chair's signature. Your one-page policy has become the contents of Phase Four. A note from the chief operating officer says, "Just needs your signature so we can all get moving." You sign at the table, because every reason not to sounds like obstruction.

**Summer, the third-floor meeting room**

The meetings are dense. The papers pile up: working papers on definitions, a taxonomy of supplier tiers, a legal memo and a glossary. Nobody disagrees with you. Everyone thanks you. Each meeting ends with an action to "revisit at the next session," and each one produces a new subgroup. You find yourself spending nearly half of your time preparing for meetings about whether to do the thing you proposed. Your own one-pager begins to sound naive to you.

**Eleven months in, the executive meeting room**

You present an interim update to the leadership team. It is a clean, honest summary. It states that complexity is high and that "more work is needed before a recommendation can responsibly be made." The leadership team accepts the update with appreciation. Two of the group's key sponsors have just been moved in a reorganization. The charter's timeline is extended by six months "to reflect changes." The recommendation is deferred pending the new operating model.

**Two years later**

It is a Wednesday in March, and an email arrives inviting you to "help design how complaints are handled" at your daughter's school. You read the first line and are already typing yes. It is your fourth such invitation this year. You tell yourself it is how things get done.

At dinner a friend asks how the supplier work is going. "In progress," you say, and pass the bread. You have said it for two years, in the same easy voice, and it has been true each time.

In your calendar a recurring invitation still reads "Working Group, ongoing." Your cursor lingers over it, then moves on. After the school meeting you will sit in the car for a few minutes with a feeling you cannot place, as if you left something on a stove. The one-pager is still pinned over your desk. The paper has curled, and the suppliers have not been audited.

## The Name

That's **The Committee Burial**.

## Definition

The Committee Burial is the sequence where advocacy is absorbed by forming a committee, using Responsive Performance, Inclusion Capture, Structural Dilution, Timeline Elongation, Complexity Saturation and Recommendation Deferral to convert trust that being included in a process means being heard into the loss of time and of the moment when a concern could still be acted on. In corporate settings it appears in working groups, advisory committees and board subcommittees, which are legitimate tools and also a familiar route for slowing a proposal. It is hard to detect because every step is cooperative, well-meant and defensible, and nobody says no. Intent would require additional evidence; committees can be slow because problems are hard.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Responsive Performance** — Enthusiasm and a promise: the chief operating officer calls the proposal "exactly the kind of thinking we need," the chief financial officer asks sensible questions, and the CEO says it deserves "the right process, with the right people at the table." This functions by giving the advocate the feeling of success without a decision. Concern rises when warm praise is not accompanied by a decision, a budget or a named owner.

2. **Inclusion Capture** — A chair's seat: two days later you receive the invitation to chair the Corporate Responsibility Working Group, you say yes, and the first meeting votes on a name. This functions by turning a critic into a custodian of the process, so that delay becomes partly their responsibility. Concern rises when the invitation arrives before any decision has been made on the substance.

3. **Structural Dilution** — A consultation around a settled answer: the group grows to twenty-three with more reviewers, and the stakeholder survey draft opens "Given the decision to keep current supplier terms through the next cycle, how should we engage stakeholders?" This functions by spreading ownership and diluting the original proposal until no one is responsible for it. Concern rises when membership growth is driven by who "wants to be involved" and not by who can decide.

4. **Timeline Elongation** — Eighteen months on paper: the charter sets four phases of three, six, four and five months, with "recommendations to the Executive Committee" last and outputs described as "advisory." This functions by moving the decision beyond the horizon in which the concern is urgent. Concern rises when the recommendation phase is the final phase and the timeline extends without cause.

5. **Complexity Saturation** — Paper that multiplies: working papers on definitions, a taxonomy of supplier tiers, a legal memo on jurisdictional variance, a data-quality workstream and a glossary, each meeting ending with "revisit at the next session" and a new subgroup. This functions by making the original question look simple-minded and absorbing the advocate's time. Concern rises when new complexity is added faster than any item is resolved.

6. **Recommendation Deferral** — More work, later: the interim update says "more work is needed before a recommendation can responsibly be made," and after a reorganisation the schedule is extended by six months with the outcome deferred pending the new operating model. This functions by turning delay into responsible caution. Concern rises when each deferral comes with a reason, and none comes with a date for a decision.

## Why This Order

**Responsive performance** must come first, because the sequence depends on the advocate believing they have won. A refusal would produce an appeal, an escalation, perhaps a public stance. Enthusiasm produces gratitude. It also records, in the firm's memory, that the idea was welcomed, which later protects every deferral. It has to come before the invitation to chair, because you only say yes at once to leading a group after the executives have called your idea exactly what they need.

> You suggest a new recycling system at work and your boss beams: "Brilliant, let's do it properly." You leave pleased, and nothing is booked. Flip it: if your boss first asked you to head a recycling group before saying anything about the idea, you would ask what time and budget the group would have.

**Inclusion capture** then transfers custody. The advocate, now chair, owns the process and is accountable for its progress. Criticizing delay means criticizing their own group. This is the point at which an adversary becomes an administrator. It has to come before the group growing to twenty-three, because you cannot object to new members and surveys once you are the chair who agreed to a shared understanding first.

> A neighbor who complains about the bins is asked to join the residents' committee. Now a complaint about the delay is a complaint about their own committee. Flip it: if the residents' committee had already swelled to dozens of members before the neighbor joined, you would ask how a bin decision would ever get made.

**Structural dilution** is what makes the group slow without any person choosing slowness. Each added member brings a legitimate interest, and each legitimate interest takes time. It has to come before the charter's timeline, because a five-page charter with an eighteen-month plan only seems reasonable to sign when twenty-three members and a legal review each need a phase.

> A dinner for two friends grows to twenty because everyone wants a say. Each wish is fair, and booking the table now takes weeks. Flip it: if the dinner was first pencilled in for many months away, you would ask your one friend, "Why not just this Friday?"

**Timeline elongation** writes this into the charter, so the delay is not a lapse but the plan. Putting recommendations in the last phase means the one output that could change anything is the one furthest away. It has to come before the working papers and glossaries, because the work only fills the months once the charter has placed your recommendation in the fourth and final phase.

> A builder gives you a schedule with your repair in the final week. No date is missed, because the date is the plan. Flip it: if the builder first handed you a thick pack of trade terms before any schedule, you would ask one simple question: "When is my repair?"

**Complexity saturation** is the deniability layer. When the work is complicated, no one can be accused of avoiding it. The advocate's energy goes to preparing materials, and the original proposal begins to look naive. It has to come before the interim update, because "more work is needed" only reads as honest once the summer's papers, subgroups and glossary have made the problem look complicated.

> You ask what a bill is for and get a seven-page breakdown with new terms. Nobody refused to answer, and you still can't find your answer. Flip it: if you were first told "We'll get back to you next month" about the bill, you would ask for a one-line answer now.

**Recommendation deferral** closes the loop. It is the one step that looks like diligence, and it produces no decision. It has to come last, because deferring the recommendation sounds careful only after the welcome, the chair's role, the added members, the long plan and the stack of papers.

> "We need a little more information before we can decide." It sounds careful, and the decision moves back another month. Flip it: if the decision had already slipped a month and then a long report arrived full of new words, you would ask for the decision in one plain sentence.

The conversion point is the moment the advocate signs the charter, which converts their proposal into Phase Four.

What weakens the sequence: asking at the outset who decides, by when, and what happens if the group recommends yes; declining chair or timeline terms that put recommendations last; and a written interim milestone that is a decision, not a report.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A company that gets a good idea from a middle manager has to route it somewhere, and a working group is the standard place. Every function has a legitimate interest and wants a seat. Charters divide the work into phases, with the recommendation at the end, and outputs are advisory and subject to priorities. Reorganizations move sponsors every year or so. A standing habit of forming groups, a seat for every interest and a calendar that keeps moving produce the order below.

Responsive Performance comes first because the leaders have no reason to say no, and a warm yes costs nothing. It records that the idea was welcomed, which later protects every deferral.

Inclusion Capture comes second because the best person to own a process is its advocate. Once chair, the person is accountable for the group's progress, and criticizing the delay means criticizing their own group.

Structural Dilution comes third because each added member brings a legitimate interest, and each interest takes time. Nobody chooses slowness. It is what a larger room does.

Timeline Elongation comes fourth because a charter has to name phases, and recommendations belong at the end. The one output that could change anything is the one furthest away, and no date is missed because the date is the plan.

Complexity Saturation comes fifth because careful work on definitions and tiers is real work. The chair's energy goes into preparing materials, and the original proposal begins to look naive.

Recommendation Deferral comes last because "more work is needed" is the one step that looks like diligence and produces no decision. A reorganization hands it a reason.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a welcome, a chair, a crowd, a long plan, a pile, a pause. It turns the person who brought the problem into the person who keeps the meetings going.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A habit of routing ideas to working groups, a seat for every function and a charter template with the recommendation at the end, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a customer-service lead at the same company. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Theodora Bangura had spent a year designing a simpler returns process when she presented it to her director, Marius Castellanos. "Brilliant," he said. "Let's do it properly." Before she was back at her desk she had an invitation to chair a returns working group. The first meeting had nine people. A month later it had twenty, and a legal panel to review anything it produced. The charter she signed ran fourteen months, with her recommendation in the final phase and a clause making the outputs advisory. Working papers followed, then a glossary, then three subgroups. At the interim update, Marius thanked her and said more work was needed. A reorganization moved two sponsors, the timeline was extended, and the recommendation was deferred pending a new operating model. Her one-page proposal was still pinned above her desk.

Marius was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that she would be too busy to be a problem.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may feel they've been given an honor and an assignment at the same moment. Over time, they may become fluent in the group's language, and find that their original proposal sounds blunt to them. They may feel responsible for the delay, since they chair the thing. They may work evenings preparing for meetings that produce no outcome, and feel guilty for being tired. They may stop proposing anything new, because a proposal means another committee.

## Institutional Echo

Working groups, advisory committees and board subcommittees are a standard way to study complex issues and are often used well. Charters that are advisory, subject to executive prioritization, and phased with recommendations last can make deferral the default outcome without anyone intending it. Interim updates that note complexity and call for further work are a familiar way of keeping an item alive and unresolved. Whether any reporting or due-diligence duty applies to supplier standards varies by jurisdiction and sector.

The honest reading is that supplier policy is hard, resources are limited, and real constraints do slow good proposals. Intent would require additional evidence: for example, whether comparable proposals reached decisions faster, and whether the group's membership and charter were set by people with a stake in the status quo.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good process produces a good outcome, and that serious problems deserve careful study. It sits beside the idea that rushing is irresponsible and that anyone pressing for speed hasn't understood the complexity.

The value being exploited is genuine: inclusion, consultation, care. The public story is an organization that listens and takes its time. The private record is a policy that did not change, a supplier network that did not improve, and a manager who was thanked for eighteen months.

## Observable Indicators

1. A proposal is praised warmly and then routed to a new group without a decision.
2. The proposer is invited to chair or coordinate the group.
3. Membership grows through requests to be involved, without clear decision authority.
4. The charter states outputs are advisory and subject to later prioritization.
5. The timeline is long, with recommendations in the last phase.
6. Further review layers are added for anything the group produces.
7. New workstreams, definitions and studies appear faster than any is closed.
8. Meetings end with "revisit next session."
9. Sponsors change and the timeline extends "to reflect changes."
10. Interim updates describe complexity and call for more work.
11. No date is set for a decision, even as the timeline is revised.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The original proposal, its date, and who received it.
- The invitation to chair, the charter in every draft, and the signed version.
- Membership lists over time, with dates of additions.
- Agendas, minutes and action logs, showing what was decided and deferred.
- Interim updates to the executive committee and the responses.
- Timeline revisions and the reasons recorded for each.
- Records of the conditions at the target of your proposal (for example, contract renewals) during the group's life.
- Your own dated note of what you were told at the start about decision authority.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Complex supply-chain and policy questions legitimately need consultation, data and phasing. A working group can produce a better proposal than one person alone.

Specific lookalikes:

- **A genuinely hard question** that needed taxonomy, legal review and data before any decision.
- **A group with decision authority** and a firm date for its recommendation.
- **Honest resource constraints** explained and shared.
- **A reorganisation** that is real and affects many projects, not just this one.

Concern strengthens when: the recommendation phase is last and the date keeps moving; membership and review layers grow while the substance of your proposal does not advance; no one is named as the person who decides.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Decision Test:**
Ask: "Who decides, and by what date?"
Concern rises if: no one is named and the charter says outputs are advisory.

**SPOT IT — The Order Test:**
Ask: "Where in the timeline do recommendations appear?"
Concern rises if: in the last phase, months or years away.

**SPOT IT — The Growth Test:**
Ask: "Why was each new member or review layer added?"
Concern rises if: because they wanted to be involved, not because they decide.

**SPOT IT — The Movement Test:**
Ask: "What has changed on the ground since the group began?"
Concern rises if: nothing has, and each meeting ends with a new action to revisit.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask before accepting the chair:** who decides, by when, with what budget, and what happens if the group recommends yes?
- **Request a decision date in writing,** and a short list of decisions the group is empowered to make.
- **Propose an early, small milestone** that is a decision, not a report: for example, a pilot on the three highest-risk contracts.
- **Keep your original proposal visible,** as a separate document, rather than letting it be absorbed into the working papers.
- **Track what changes on the ground,** using a simple dated list, separate from the group's own reporting.
- **Name the pattern calmly in the meeting:** "We have met many times. Can we agree what decision is due next, and who makes it?"
- **Seek a second route,** such as an executive sponsor, an audit or risk committee, or a board member responsible for the area, where one exists.
- **Know when to step back from the chair,** and say so in writing.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A proposal is routed to a group with no decision authority; membership and layers grow; recommendations are scheduled last.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The timeline extends repeatedly with no decision date; the harm the proposal addressed continues, such as contracts renewing; the group's outputs are labeled advisory while a decision is made elsewhere. Relevant routes may include an executive sponsor, an audit or risk committee, an independent director, a compliance officer, and a regulator where a reporting duty applies.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, retaliation involving physical danger, or evidence of serious harm to workers or others in the supply chain. Prioritize safety and emergency support, and route harm to people to the appropriate authorities and qualified counsel. Do not confront anyone, and do not delete evidence.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before I take the chair, can we agree who decides, by when, and what the group's recommendation triggers?"
- "Can we add a decision date to the charter?"
- "I'd like to propose a small pilot in parallel, so we learn while we study."
- "Can we record, in the minutes, what was decided today and what was deferred?"
- "I notice our timeline has moved twice. Can we agree on a date we won't move?"
- "Who is the executive sponsor for this, and can I update them directly?"
- "I'd like the original proposal attached to the next update, unchanged."
