# C196: The Afterlife — Corporate & Consulting

## Safety Note (read first)

This script covers monitoring and control that continues after you leave a job. Most of it is a matter for counsel and your new employer's IT team. If you believe you are being followed, if your location is being tracked in a way that makes you unsafe, or if anyone has threatened you, your family or your property, stop and contact local emergency services or a specialist support service for stalking and coercion. Do not confront the person, do not try to access or disable any system you are not authorized to touch, and do not delete anything that may be subject to a legal hold. Take advice first.

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that leaving never truly ends the danger, and a reflex to guard your location, number and accounts as though someone could still reach in at any time.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of changing passwords and checking settings, and a jolt whenever a device asks to share where you are.
- An automatic thought when an official letter arrives, "it's starting again," before you have opened it.
- A sense that a new home or job is never fully yours, as if the old trouble might arrive first.

**A point in a life.** The first months after leaving a job.

**Where it happens.** A design team between two consumer companies.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a thirty-seven-year-old head of design who has just left a venture-backed consumer brand, and the first six weeks at your new employer are meant to be a fresh start. You spent five years building the brand's design system and a team of nine. You resigned with a month's notice, finished the handover, and told the founder, politely and in person, that you wanted a new challenge. He said he understood. He said the door was always open.

**Week two, the new office**

Your new employer gave you a laptop and a phone on your first day, and a desk by a window over a courtyard full of bicycles. You brought a few things from your old life: your own cloud photo library, a personal contact list, and the phone number you have had for eleven years. The number was in fact set up through the old company's mobile plan, because the founder liked everyone to have "one number for work and life." Nobody mentioned this when you resigned.

On Tuesday your new IT lead, Farah, stops by with a coffee and a question. Why does your personal phone show a management profile from another organization? You do not know. She turns the screen toward you. The profile has been switched back on since your last day at the old office, and the old company's device tool still lists your phone. "Don't touch it," Farah says. "We'll get counsel to write to them." You call a friend still at the old company, who tells you, apologetically, that the tool also records where the phone last was, "for lost-device recovery." The policy you signed five years ago mentions this in a paragraph you did not read. You put the phone face down on the desk, and the back of your neck stays cold.

· · ·

That afternoon you write to the old company's operations lead, Brett, and ask him to release the number. He replies within the hour: it is "under review." You ask for your design archive and your own photographs back from the shared drive. Access has been "paused pending offboarding." You ask whether your personal contacts can be returned, and he says he will "check with counsel." Each answer is polite, and each one means you have to ask again. You start a spreadsheet of your requests, then stop, because it feels petty, then start again.

**Week four, your new kitchen**

The letter comes by courier at six on a Thursday evening. It is a letter on heavy paper from the old company's lawyers, headed as a hold notice. It says that the company reserves the right to claim that certain design assets created during your employment, and now "believed to be in your possession," are its property. It says that failure to comply "may leave the company with no alternative." It does not say what you have done wrong. You read it standing at the counter, next to the pasta water you forgot to drain, and your knees want you to sit down.

Attached is a device-return and handover protocol with eleven numbered steps. Return the devices. Hand over the passwords of any login "connected to the business." Preserve all material. Sign an acknowledgment that you will not "disparage the company, its leadership or its products to any person." Brett's covering note is friendly. "Once the acknowledgment is signed, we can put all this behind us." You find the pen already in your hand. You set it down on the letter and look at it there for a long time.

**The next Monday, the new office**

Your new employer's recruiter, Dana, catches you at the coffee machine and tells you, gently, that someone from the old company called one of your references "just to catch up." She says nobody said anything bad. She says the call was a little odd, more a question about how you were doing than about your work. You smile and thank her.

You know none of this is a threat. It is paperwork, a policy, a phone call, a careful letter. You know people who would say the company is protecting itself. In the ten o'clock design review you put your slides up and watch the faces around the table for something they have heard. Nobody says anything. You spend the afternoon wondering what your new team knows.

**A year later**

A year later you are still at the new firm, and the work is good. Your team has grown to six. You have moved to an apartment across town, and some of the boxes are still taped, and none of the pictures are on the walls.

On Sunday evenings you sit on the sofa with tea and go through every app on your phone, one screen at a time, turning things off. When a new map app asks whether it may see you, your thumb finds "Don't Allow" before you have read the question.

On a Wednesday an envelope with a government crest is waiting on the mat. Your stomach drops so fast you have to hold the door frame. You leave it on the table, unopened, for two days. When you open it, it is a small refund. You laugh, and your hands are still shaking.

In design reviews you show only work from this year. Your new manager asks if she can put your old case studies on the firm's site, and you say, "Maybe later," and change the subject.

You have your own number now. You have your own photographs back, most of them. You left a job, and a year on, part of you is still standing in a kitchen with a letter in one hand and a pen in the other.

## The Name

That's **The Afterlife**.

## Definition

The Afterlife is the sequence where leaving does not end the relationship: the person is monitored, her number and accounts remain in the other side's hands, legal process is used as pressure, control continues by other means, and the damage follows her into new settings, using Location Tracking, Account Control, Legal Abuse, Post-Separation Abuse and Legacy Contamination to convert hope that leaving ends the pressure into loss of privacy, money and a clean start. In consulting and venture-backed firms it can sit in device policies, accounts held in the company's name, hold letters and reference calls. It is hard to detect because every element has a legitimate version, and what separates a protective exit procedure from one that continues control is mostly its scope and its timing.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Location Tracking** — A phone still reporting where it is: the old company's management profile quietly re-enabled on the phone you still use, recording its last known location "for lost-device recovery." This functions by making departure visible to the former employer, so that leaving does not give you a private life. Concern rises when monitoring continues on a personal device after separation, or when you were never clearly told what it recorded.

2. **Account Control** — Your own accounts held: your number "under review," your design archive and photographs "paused pending offboarding," and your contacts waiting while the operations lead will "check with legal." This functions by making your own tools dependent on the other side's cooperation, so every request needs permission. Concern rises when personal items are held "pending review" with no end date.

3. **Legal Abuse** — A hold letter with an open claim: heavy paper headed as a legal hold, design assets "believed to be in your possession" claimed as the company's, and "may leave the company with no alternative." This functions by turning the legal process into a source of cost and fear, whether or not a claim would ever be filed. Concern rises when no specific asset or allegation is named, and when the letter is paired with a request to sign something extra.

4. **Post-Separation Abuse** — Control by other means after leaving: the eleven-step protocol to return devices, hand over credentials and sign a non-disparagement acknowledgment, and "Once the acknowledgment is signed, we can put all this behind us." This functions by keeping the relationship open on the former employer's terms. Concern rises when demands exceed what the agreement says, or when they keep changing.

5. **Legacy Contamination** — A reference call that follows you: someone from the old company calling a reference "just to catch up," a call the recruiter found a little odd and more about how you were doing than your work. This functions by letting the old story travel ahead of you, without any statement that can be challenged. Concern rises when unverified comments reach new colleagues or recruiters, or when your new employer is contacted without your knowledge.

## Why This Order

Monitoring (**Location Tracking**) has to come first because it is the oldest and quietest layer. It was set up when you were an employee, with your consent, and it persists because nobody removes it. By the time you leave, the former employer already knows where you are. It has to come first, because Brett's polite holding of your number only bites once the old company's tool already lists your phone and knows where it last was.

> Picture moving out of a shared flat and finding your old flatmate's app still shows where your phone is. You agreed to it once, for finding lost phones, and nobody switched it off. Flip it: if your old flatmate made you ask, one by one, for your own things the day you moved out, you would notice it at once and switch off every link between your phones.

**Account Control** follows because it turns that visibility into dependence. A person who needs her own phone number and photographs back has to ask, and a request is a relationship. It has to come before the lawyers' letter, because the hold notice lands on someone already waiting on Brett for her own number, photographs and contacts, with each request still open.

> Think of an ex who still holds the password to the account with all your photos. Every time you want one back, you have to ask. Flip it: if the ex opened with a stiff, formal warning about the photos, you would take advice at once, and every later request for the password would go through someone on your side.

Legal pressure (**Legal Abuse**) then does something monitoring and holding cannot: it changes the register. The matter moves from "some paperwork" to "a possible claim," and a claim is the one situation in which most people stop asking questions and start paying fees. The hold letter is also protective in form: it tells you not to delete anything, and the person receiving it cannot tell whether she is being protected or being pressured. It has to come before the eleven-step protocol, because the acknowledgment only looks like the way out once the heavy letter has made a claim against you feel possible.

> Imagine a neighbor's polite note about a fence suddenly arriving on a lawyer's letterhead. The same small disagreement now feels like something you would need money to answer. Flip it: if the person next door first sent a string of small fence requests, trim this, move that, and only then the letterhead, you would see where the requests were heading and take advice.

Post-separation demands (**Post-Separation Abuse**) then ride on the legal frame: one more deadline, one more acknowledgment, one more form. Each is small. It has to come before the reference call, because the odd call only unsettles you this much once the protocol and the acknowledgment are already lying on your counter beside the pen.

> It is like a breakup where the other person keeps sending one more thing to sort: return the key, sign here, one last box. Plenty of honest goodbyes have a checklist; what matters is whether the requests ever stop. Flip it: if your ex's first move after the breakup was a friendly call to your new friends, with nothing said to you, you would notice it straight away and keep every later request in writing.

**Legacy Contamination** closes the loop and supplies the deniability. A reference call needs no instruction and leaves no document. Nobody has said anything; everyone has heard something. It has to come last, because a call where nobody said anything bad only sends you searching your new team's faces once the letter and protocol have shown the old company has not let go.

> Picture starting at a new school and learning that a teacher there has "just had a chat" with your old one. Nobody tells you what was said, and you feel watched anyway. Flip it: if the chat between the two teachers came first, and only afterward your old school began sending home one more form, then another, you would see they were connected and let a parent handle every one.

What weakens the sequence is separation: your own number, your own accounts, your own adviser, and a record of every request. The key conversion point is the moment you sign an acknowledgment you have not had read by counsel.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Leaving a modern consumer company runs into three structural facts. Devices and phone numbers are enrolled in company systems on the first day, often for convenience, and nobody owns the job of removing them. Offboarding is a checklist written by people whose task is to protect the company's assets, so its default answer to any request is "not yet." And design work lives in shared drives, where the line between a person's portfolio and the company's property is blurred from the start.

Location Tracking comes first because it was installed long before anyone left. A device tool that records where a phone last was is a standard feature, agreed to in a policy nobody reads, and it persists because switching it off is no one's task.

Account Control comes second because the same enrollment that made the tracking possible also made the number, the photos and the contacts company-held. Every request to release them meets a process, and each "under review" turns a departure into a running relationship.

Legal Abuse comes third because unresolved requests and disputed assets are exactly what a company's lawyers are paid to treat as risk. A hold notice is routine paperwork for them. For the person receiving it, the register changes from asking to defending, and most people stop asking questions once a claim seems possible.

Post-Separation Abuse comes fourth because a hold notice arrives with procedures attached. Each step in a handover protocol is small and sensible on its own, and an acknowledgment clause rides along with them. Signing looks like the way to close the file.

Legacy Contamination comes last because a small industry runs on references, and a call "just to catch up" is ordinary behavior. It needs no instruction and leaves no record, yet it reaches the new employer before the person has had time to build anything there.

Put it together and the order is the order an enrollment-first, risk-averse company produces when someone senior leaves. Nobody needs to plan it. It turns a designer starting fresh into someone who audits her phone every Sunday and opens envelopes with shaking hands.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A device policy, an offboarding checklist and a cautious lawyer's routine did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a consultant who left to join a client. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ruben Ashcroft spent six years at a small consultancy before he left to join one of its clients. On his second day in the new job, his phone's settings showed it was still enrolled with the old firm, and a former colleague told him the firm's system could see where the phone was. When he asked the managing partner, Lydia Carrow, to release his number and his personal photos from the firm's drive, each request came back "in process." Three weeks later a letter from the firm's lawyers arrived at his home, warning that client materials "may be in his possession" and that the firm reserved its rights. It did not say what he had taken. Enclosed was a nine-step handover checklist and a promise not to criticize the firm to anyone, to be signed within seven days. Then his new manager mentioned, lightly, that Lydia had phoned "just to see how Ruben was settling in." Nobody told him what else was said. Ruben began checking his phone settings every night. Lydia was never confused about any of it. She told a friend that people who left her firm took a year to stop looking over their shoulder, and that she found that useful.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to wonder whether her phone is private, and stop using it for anything sensitive, including calls to the people who could help. She may feel watched in a job where she has done nothing wrong, and may start to second-guess ordinary decisions at the new company.

Over time she may hold back in her first months, avoid raising her old work, and be reluctant to name the dispute to new colleagues for fear of looking difficult. She may spend working hours on letters and paperwork and feel embarrassed by that. She may begin to see the old employer in every unexplained silence.

## Institutional Echo

Companies commonly manage devices, accounts and numbers centrally, and offboarding procedures often include device return and a request to preserve records. Used with care, these protect clients and secrets. Used without clear limits, they can extend an employer's reach onto personal devices and personal data after the relationship has ended.

Hold letters, intellectual-property reservations and non-disparagement acknowledgments are standard tools; whether any particular claim, clause or demand is valid depends on the contract and the jurisdiction. Reference calls between people in a small industry are normal, and informal ones are hard to distinguish from sound reference-giving. The honest reading is that a nervous company may be protecting itself clumsily. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same steps are applied to every departing employee.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that you can always walk away: that a resignation is a clean cut, and that a professional leaves gracefully and does not look back. A person who asks about her own phone number is "making a fuss." Industries built on reputation hold a second belief, that everyone talks, and that the ones who leave badly are remembered.

The value being exploited is the wish for a fresh start. The public story is a tidy offboarding and a warm farewell. The private cost, when this pattern is present, is a person who changed jobs and did not change anything else.

## Observable Indicators

1. A device-management profile or tracking setting remains active on a personal device after separation.
2. A personal phone number or account is held in the company's name, with no clear release date.
3. Personal files or contacts are held "pending review" without a stated end.
4. A legal hold or IP letter does not identify a specific asset or allegation.
5. The letter attaches a handover protocol with steps beyond what the agreement requires.
6. Credentials are demanded for accounts that are personal rather than business.
7. A new acknowledgment, release or non-disparagement undertaking is requested alongside the letter.
8. Deadlines change, or arrive immediately after you refuse something.
9. Your new employer or recruiter is contacted by the old company without notice.
10. Comments about you travel in group channels or reference calls without a named source.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The device policy, acknowledgment and management profile you signed or consented to, with dates.
- Screenshots of any management profile or setting visible on your own device, taken lawfully.
- Records of who holds your number and personal accounts, with plan and billing documents.
- The hold letter, the handover protocol, and every version, with envelope or email headers.
- Your requests to release items and each reply, with dates.
- Notes from your new employer about contact from the old company, including recruiter remarks, with names and dates.
- The separation agreement, resignation letter and any release or non-disparagement draft.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Employers legitimately protect confidential material, recover company devices and send hold letters when a dispute is genuinely possible. A requested device return, a preservation instruction, a request to hand over company accounts, and a reference call limited to dates and role are ordinary.

The discriminators are scope and symmetry: whether the demands stay within what the agreement says, whether the same steps apply to others, and whether the company will state which asset is in question.

Concern strengthens when: monitoring of a personal device continues after you left; the letter claims "possession" without naming an asset; a signature on a new undertaking is requested alongside the letter; the old company contacts your new employer without notice.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Scope Test:**
Ask: "Does each demand match what my signed agreement actually says?"
Concern rises if: the protocol asks for more than the agreement, and nobody can say where it came from.

**SPOT IT — The Asset Test:**
Ask: "Which specific asset or act is the company concerned about?"
Concern rises if: the letter names none, and still asks for a signature.

**SPOT IT — The Ownership Test:**
Ask: "Whose name is my number, my archive and my account in?"
Concern rises if: they are the company's, and the release is "under review."

**SPOT IT — The Echo Test:**
Ask: "Has the old company been in touch with people at my new one?"
Concern rises if: the contact was unannounced and the content cannot be traced.

## Chain Breakers

- Take the letter to your own qualified attorney before you reply, and do not sign the acknowledgment first.
- Do not delete, wipe or alter anything that may be covered by a hold. Take advice on what to preserve and how.
- Ask your new employer's IT team how to separate your personal accounts and devices from any old management profile, and let them handle it.
- Do not try to access, disable or alter anything on the old company's systems yourself.
- Ask in writing for the specific asset and the specific clause. "Could you identify the materials the company believes are covered, and the provision it relies on?"
- Ask in writing for the release of your number and personal data, with a date.
- Tell your new manager or HR what is happening, in a short factual note, so the first account they hear is yours.
- Keep a short log of each contact: who, when, what was asked.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Personal devices or accounts remain managed after you left; a letter arrives without naming an asset; requests for release go unanswered for weeks.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A claim is threatened against you; a new undertaking is demanded; the old company contacts your new employer; your offer or role is affected. Consider a qualified attorney with employment and intellectual-property experience, your new employer's counsel or HR, a licensed professional body where relevant, or mediation if the contract points to it.

**HIGH → URGENT:** You believe you are being followed, your location is being used to find you, someone has threatened you or your family, or property or accounts are taken by force or fraud. Contact emergency services and a specialist support service. Do not confront anyone or try to remove a tracking tool yourself without advice.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'd like to confirm which materials the company believes are covered, and the clause it relies on."
- "I'm taking advice on this letter and will reply by the date given."
- "Could you confirm in writing when my personal phone number and archive will be released?"
- "Could you confirm that no management profile remains active on my personal devices?"
- "I'd rather not sign a further acknowledgment until my adviser has read it."
- "I'm happy to return company devices. Could you confirm the process and give me a receipt?"
