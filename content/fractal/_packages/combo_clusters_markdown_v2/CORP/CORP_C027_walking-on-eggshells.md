# C027: Walking on Eggshells — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a habit of reading every room for the next change of mood, and a belief that staying safe means staying small, quiet and ready, with nothing left over for yourself.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of listening to footsteps, doors or the tone of a message to guess what kind of day it will be.
- A pull to stay calm and agreeable when someone pokes at you, because any reaction once became proof you were the problem.
- An automatic thought, "nobody would believe how different they are at home," that keeps you from telling anyone.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, six years into one role.

**Where it happens.** A chief executive's office on the executive floor.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're thirty-seven, executive assistant to a chief executive, and you are very good at your job. You have been with him for six years. You know which airline he prefers, who must never be seated near whom, and which board member's calls to take on the first ring.

**A Monday at seven-forty, your desk**

The executive floor is quiet and smells of the carpet cleaner they use at weekends. Every morning at this hour, before he arrives, you write the daily briefing note. It has his calendar, his calls, the open decisions. It also has a small column on the right that nobody asked for: a colour.

Green means he slept, the market was kind and the first email was good. Amber means be careful with numbers. Red means no bad news before eleven, no questions in the corridor, and keep the door closed.

At ten past eight the lift opens. You listen. The doors, his footsteps on the hard strip of floor before the carpet, whether he says your name as he passes. Today he says nothing and the door closes a little hard. You change green to amber.

Nobody shouts on this floor. Last week a vice president was corrected by name in front of her team, mildly, and she went grey. The do-not-disturb rules change without notice: in March, no passing in front of the glass wall; in May, nothing on his desk unless it is flagged. You keep the old versions in a folder called "rules." Your shoulders have started to live near your ears.

Some days he is wonderful. Some evenings he stops at your desk at six, remembers the date of your mother's surgery, asks how it went, and sees you to the lift. He says he does not know what he would do without you. On those days you feel a lightness that makes the other days feel like a misunderstanding. You never know which day you will get, so you keep your phone face up.

**A Thursday in May, his office**

At ten past nine, an amber morning, you put a flagged document in his tray and ask a question about the board pack. He says, evenly, in front of two directors, "I'm sorry, was I unclear on how I like things done?" The room waits. Your answer comes out quicker than you meant, with an edge in it. He turns to the directors with a small, tired smile. "You see what I'm working with." Your ears burn. All day you hear that edge in your own voice.

The next morning you knock, wait, and ask him, carefully, to say once what flagged means, so you can get it right. He is cool. "I'd have thought six years was enough." He has seen the briefing note, it turns out. He asks why it says amber when he feels, if anything, fine. For three days he passes your desk without your name, and the door stays shut. You do not ask again.

At the end of the month, in the same office, it is your yearly review. A coffee is waiting for you on the side table, made the way you like it, and he gives a small speech about how people underestimate how hard it is to run things. He says you are "indispensable." Then, two sentences later: "You sometimes seem to be carrying a lot of tension into the office." You thank him. Your stomach drops and your face stays pleasant.

**June, a hotel dining room**

The board dinner is in a private room with dark panelling and too many forks. Over dessert he stands with a glass and tells twelve people you are the best thing that ever happened to the office. His hand rests on your shoulder, and they applaud. Your face goes warm in a way that is almost happiness.

As the room settles he bends to pick up his napkin and says, low, near your ear, "You hovered. Don't hover." Then he is laughing with the chair.

The woman beside you, a director's partner you have liked for years, leans over. "He adores you," she says. You hear yourself start to say something small, that some mornings are hard, that he can be different upstairs. She smiles, puzzled. "But he's lovely." You hear how it sounds. You say it is nothing, and pass her the cream.

**Two years later, a different floor**

Two years later you work for someone else, a finance director at another company who is, by every measure, calm. She says good morning the same way every day. You still hear the lift.

At ten past eight you listen for her footsteps, and you can tell, you think, from how her bag lands on the desk. When her first message of the day has no full stop at the end, you spend twenty minutes wondering what you did. She had been typing on a train.

In a meeting a colleague needles you about a missed booking that was not yours. Something hot rises under your ribs. You smile, say it's a fair point and you'll look into it. Afterwards your hands shake in the toilets, and you tell yourself you handled it well.

At Christmas your sister asks what the old job was really like. You open your mouth. You think of the hand on your shoulder, the applause, twelve people who would swear he was lovely. You say, "Busy," and help with the plates.

That night you notice a document on your laptop with a column on the right. You made it in your first week here, without deciding to. Six years of reading another person's mornings taught you everything about his days, and almost nothing about your own.

## The Name

That's **Walking on Eggshells**.

## Definition

Walking on Eggshells is the sequence where unpredictable, mood-driven treatment consumes a person's attention in monitoring another's state, using Ambient Abuse, Intermittent Reinforcement, Baiting, Punishment of Boundaries, Push-Pull, and Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde to convert the need to stay safe by anticipating moods into the loss of attention and energy that would otherwise go into self-advocacy. It is hard to detect because the warm days are real, the cold days are deniable, and the monitoring looks like conscientious service. Executives under heavy pressure can be volatile for ordinary reasons. The concern is a pattern in which the assistant's work becomes managing his state, without a name for what is going on.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Ambient Abuse** — A climate of tension: he sets the unwritten rules by mood, a colour column in your briefing note, a vice president corrected by name, no-interruption rules that change from January to March to May. This functions by keeping the person in low-grade alert, so that no single event looks serious enough to name. Concern rises when you adjust your behaviour daily to a tone that no one ever acknowledges.

2. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — Warm days: he remembers your mother's surgery, walks you to the door at six and says he does not know what he would do without you. This functions by making affection unpredictable, which holds attention more firmly than steady kindness. Concern rises when you cannot predict which version you will meet and the good days keep you hoping.

3. **Baiting** — A public question: "Was I unclear on how I like things done?" in front of two directors, then he turns to them after your sharp reply and says, "You see what I'm working with." This functions by creating a moment in which any answer is wrong. Concern rises when a question seems built to produce an error.

4. **Punishment of Boundaries** — A cool answer to your question: you ask what flagged means, he says "six years was enough," skips your name for three days, and keeps the door shut. This functions by attaching a cost to ordinary requests. Concern rises when reasonable questions bring a rebuke.

5. **Push-Pull** — Praise, then criticism: "indispensable," then two sentences later "a lot of tension"; a coffee and a speech, then distance. This functions by alternating reward and withdrawal. Concern rises when appreciation and correction arrive in the same conversation.

6. **Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde** — Generous in public, cutting in private: a toast to twelve people with a hand on your shoulder, then "You hovered" in the car, and a friend who says he sounds lovely. This functions by making the target doubt which version is real. Concern rises when the contrast makes you question your own perception.

## Why This Order

**Ambient Abuse** sets the weather. Before any dramatic event, the environment is made tense enough that vigilance feels like competence. It has to come before the wonderful evenings, because the lightness of the good days only feels so strong once the closed door and the colour column have kept you braced all week.

> Picture a house where nobody ever shouts, but a cupboard door closes a little hard and a plate lands a little loud. You learn to read the sounds before you reach the kitchen. Flip it: if the people in the house were warm one evening and silent the next before any cupboard door closed hard, you would notice the swings plainly and could ask what was going on.

**Intermittent Reinforcement** then makes the vigilance addictive. If the good days were reliable, the assistant could relax; if the bad ones were reliable, she could leave. The unpredictability is what holds. It has to come before the question in front of two directors, because you only answer "was I unclear?" with an edge once guessing which day you will get has worn you thin.

> Think of a friend who answers your texts in a minute one day and not at all for three the next. You keep checking your phone. Flip it: if the friend texted "calm down, why so touchy?" before any of the slow-reply days began, you would be relaxed enough to laugh it off and show them the earlier messages.

**Baiting** supplies the occasional test, so the vigilance feels justified. It has to come before your careful question about what flagged means, because his coolness only seems deserved to you once your edge has been shown to the directors as proof.

> Picture a sibling who says "calm down" in a voice that makes you snap, and then tells the room, "See?" Flip it: if the sibling stopped speaking to you after you asked them kindly not to say "calm down", before any "See?" to the room, you would notice the cold shoulder plainly and could tell a parent.

**Punishment of Boundaries** teaches that stating needs is costly. It has to come before the review with the coffee, because the warm "indispensable" only feels like a reward once three days without your name have taught you what asking costs.

> You ask a neighbour kindly to stop parking across your drive, and from then on they stop waving. Next time, you think twice before asking anything. Flip it: if the neighbour waved warmly one week and ignored you the next before you had ever mentioned the drive, you would notice the swing on its own and still ask about the parking calmly.

**Push-Pull** keeps the cycle moving so that there is always a reason to try harder. It has to come before the toast at the board dinner, because nobody at that table can see the man of the review, and you are already unsure which version of him you read correctly.

> A friend sends hearts on Monday and leaves you on read on Tuesday. You spend Wednesday trying to guess which friend is coming. Flip it: if everyone told you how lovely this friend is before the hearts and the silences began, you would hear it, then notice the Tuesday silence for yourself and trust what you saw.

**Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde** is the mechanism of doubt: when the same person produces both, the target blames her own reading. It has to come last, because "But he's lovely" only silences you once the review has already left you unsure whether the tension is his or yours.

> Everyone's parents adore the coach who is kind on the touchline. When you say he is cold in the car park, they answer, "But he's lovely," and you start to wonder if you misheard. Flip it: if the parents had told you "But he's lovely" before the coach ever ran warm and cold with you, you would know the touchline view already and could trust what you saw in the car park.

The conversion point is when forecasting becomes the main job. What the target loses is the attention that would otherwise go to her own needs, and she stops noticing it is gone. Whether this reflects design, stress, or temperament cannot be told from the behaviour; intent would require additional evidence. What weakens it: naming the pattern, keeping a factual record, and seeking outside perspective on what is normal.

## Why the System Produces This Order

The executive assistant's role is built on a few structural facts. It is a team of two, with one person holding nearly all the power and the other holding nearly all the detail. There is no layer between them: no line manager for the assistant except the executive, and nobody else who sees both his public face and his private mornings. And the board, the company's only check on him, meets him at dinners and presentations, where he is at his best.

Ambient Abuse comes first because a single chief executive's mood is the weather of the whole floor. When rules about doors and desks are set by one person and change without notice, everyone near him learns to read signs. No act needs to be dramatic; the setting turns vigilance into part of the job description.

Intermittent Reinforcement comes second because his good days are real and his time is scarce. A senior executive's warmth arrives when the market is kind and the diary is light, which is unpredictable by nature. For the person who depends on that warmth most, the unpredictability is what keeps them watching.

Baiting comes third because the assistant is the safest person in the building to be short with. A cool question in front of directors costs him nothing, and her answer, made tired by months of watching, is visible to witnesses. One moment of edge becomes her story in other people's heads.

Punishment of Boundaries comes fourth because, in a team of two, there is nobody to appeal to. When a question about a rule is met with coolness and a shut door, the only way to restore the working relationship is not to ask again.

Push-Pull comes fifth because the yearly review is held by the same person whose mood sets the floor. Praise and a remark about tension in one conversation keep both on the record, and leave the assistant working harder to tip the balance.

Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde comes last because of who sees what. The board and its guests see the toast; only the assistant sees the mornings. Any attempt to describe the difference is heard against the public version, and the public version always has more witnesses.

Put it together and the order is the order a role built around one person's moods tends to produce. Nobody has to choose it. It turns a skilled professional into a full-time forecaster of someone else's days, with nothing left over for her own.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A team of two with no one between them, a board that only meets him at his best and a floor run on one man's moods did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a project manager in his practice. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Nadia Brennan was the newest project manager in a consulting practice led by Victor Hale. Within a month she had learned to read his first email of the day: short meant keep away, long meant a good day. Some weeks he took her to lunch and asked about her plans; other weeks he looked through her in the corridor. In a client meeting he asked her, kindly, whether she was sure she could cope with the workload, and when she answered too quickly, he raised his eyebrows at the client. When she asked if agendas could be sent the day before, she was left off the next two client calls without explanation. After her first project closed, flowers arrived at her desk; the next morning an email to the whole team described her reports as "uneven." At the practice awards he praised her in front of sixty people. When she told a colleague, quietly, that working for him was hard, the colleague said, "Victor? He's the best boss I've ever had."

Years later, Nadia heard that Victor had told a fellow partner he liked his teams "never quite sure of the forecast."

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may start to scan for signs of mood before the day starts and organise weekends around availability. Over time, they may stop trusting their own perception and feel tense even when nothing is wrong. They may stop planning, flinch at the phone, blame themselves for being anxious, and find it hard to tell anyone how much of the day is spent reading someone else.

## Institutional Echo

Executive support roles often involve close management of a principal's schedule and temperament, and a good assistant anticipates needs. Rules that shift with the principal's preferences are common and can be benign. Whether a work environment amounts to a problem that employer policy or law addresses depends on facts and jurisdiction. Intent would require additional evidence: whether the volatility is aimed at particular people, whether it eases when he is not under pressure, and whether others on the floor adapt in the same way.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that great leaders are demanding, and that supporting them is a privilege. Executives are expected to be intense, and an assistant who manages them is admired. The language that makes it sound noble is loyalty, discretion, indispensability. The public story is a trusted partnership at the top of a company. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a person whose working day has become the management of another adult's feelings.

## Observable Indicators

1. A routine of forecasting the principal's mood is built into daily work.
2. Do-not-disturb or interaction rules change frequently without notice.
3. Warmth and coolness alternate without apparent cause.
4. Reasonable requests draw public rebukes.
5. Questions seem to invite error, such as "Was I unclear?"
6. Praise and criticism arrive in the same conversation.
7. The assistant changes dress, schedule or plans according to the forecast.
8. The assistant keeps old versions of rules to defend herself.
9. Others on the floor adapt the same way.
10. Performance reviews mention "tension" in the assistant, not the environment.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Daily briefing notes, including the mood column, with dates, as far as company policy lets you retain them.
- Every version of the do-not-disturb rules and when each changed.
- Dated notes of incidents, quoted as closely as you can recall them, written the same day.
- Reviews and feedback, including comments about tension or attitude.
- Messages showing sudden changes in tone or expectations.
- Names of colleagues who have observed or experienced similar behaviour.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Executives under pressure can be abrupt, rules do change with a principal's needs, and a conscientious assistant anticipates moods. The discriminator is **whether the behaviour is predictable, repairable and accountable**: genuine stress is followed by acknowledgment or apology, and does not punish ordinary requests.

Benign lookalikes:

- **Crisis periods.** Board pressure or a bad quarter can raise tension for everyone.
- **Legitimate preferences.** Some principals want things done specific ways.
- **Honest apology.** A leader may repair after a harsh moment.
- **Role norms.** Anticipating needs is part of the job.

Concern strengthens when: ordinary questions are rebuked; warmth and coolness alternate without apology; the assistant's anxiety is described as the problem.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Forecast Test:**
Ask: "Do I plan my day around his mood?"
Concern rises if: I do, and it takes real effort every morning.

**SPOT IT — The Rule Test:**
Ask: "How often have the rules changed, and who told me?"
Concern rises if: they change often and I find out by being corrected.

**SPOT IT — The Repair Test:**
Ask: "After a harsh moment, does anyone acknowledge it?"
Concern rises if: no, and warmth returns as if nothing happened.

**SPOT IT — The Tension Test:**
Ask: "When someone mentions my tension, do they mention its source?"
Concern rises if: the source is never named.

## Chain Breakers

- **Keep a factual, dated note** of incidents and the rules in force.
- **Ask for rules in writing,** and confirm changes by email.
- **Name behaviour, not character:** "When I'm corrected in front of directors, I find it hard to do my best."
- **Find an outside perspective,** such as a peer assistant, mentor or counsellor.
- **Use HR or an independent contact** when a pattern is established.
- **Protect your time away from work.** Switch off notifications where your contract allows.
- **Check your support options,** such as an employee assistance programme, for the personal toll.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Rebukes for ordinary questions; rules that change without notice; reviews that mention your tension instead of the environment.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Behaviour is aimed at you after you set a boundary, or affects your health or performance review. Relevant routes may include HR or employee relations, an employment attorney, an employee assistance programme, an ombuds, and an employment or labour regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, physical intimidation, stalking, or anyone expressing self-harm. Contact local emergency or crisis services immediately, and tell someone you trust. Do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we agree the do-not-disturb rules in writing, so I can follow them exactly?"
- "When I'm corrected in front of others, I find it hard to do my best work. Could we handle that privately?"
- "I want to get this right. Could you tell me what you'd like different?"
- "Could we agree a signal for when you need uninterrupted time?"
- "I'd like to talk to HR about how I can best support you."
- "I need to step away at six on Fridays, and I'll make sure coverage is arranged."
