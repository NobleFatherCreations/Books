# C033: The Approval Addiction — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that you are only worth what one approving voice says you are, and a need to keep checking with that voice, through praise and sting alike, to know who you are.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of hearing a compliment and waiting for the catch, and feeling oddly steadier when it comes.
- A pull toward warm-then-cool leaders or teachers, and a sense that their rare praise is the only kind that counts.
- An automatic thought, "it's not that I did something bad, it's that I am bad," after even a small correction.

**A point in a life.** Midlife, after a courted move to a new company.

**Where it happens.** A mid-sized company run by a charismatic chief executive.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a general manager of a business unit, forty-four years old, and you were recruited. Not hired. Recruited. The chief executive spent four months courting you, flew to your city twice, and announced your arrival at the all-hands with the words "the best operator I've ever tried to hire." Your last employer gave you a card and a cake. You had imagined the first month for a long time: a desk with a view of the loading bay, a team of a hundred and forty, a P&L that was finally yours. You walked in here believing you had found someone who saw what you could do.

**September to March, his office**

For the first six weeks, he did. His assistant leaves a printed agenda on your chair before each Monday. Your one-to-one is forty-five minutes and always overruns, with the door half closed and a pot of tea cooling between you. He asks about your daughter's exams and remembers the answer the following week. He calls your plan "exactly the right instinct." Then you move the pricing review to a day that suits your team, without asking him first. The next one-to-one lasts fifteen minutes, and he types throughout without looking up. When you bring your next update in the structure he likes, the meeting runs long again and he smiles at the door. You notice which of the two you remember.

The Monday one-to-ones live in a shared notes document. He opens it at the start of each meeting and types while you talk. Some weeks the heading is "Wins" and runs to eight bullets. Some weeks it is "Concerns," and the bullets are shorter, and sharper. "Pace. Communication with finance. Optics of the Q3 plan." You hit your revenue target and got a Concerns week. You missed a milestone by nine days and got Wins. You start arriving five minutes early to read his face through the glass before you knock. By March you keep a private spreadsheet on your phone, held under the edge of the table: date, heading, minutes, whether he looked up.

**April, a leadership dinner**

At a leadership dinner, with the CFO on one side of you and the head of sales on the other, he lifts his glass and says: "If I could clone one person on this team, it would be her." A fork stops halfway to a mouth. People turn to you. You glow for three days. You tell your daughter, and then you tell your sister, and then you tell yourself again.

**The following Monday, his office**

The following Monday, in the notes document, under a bold heading, he writes: "You're brilliant, but I worry you're not quite operating at the level I recruited." You read it twice, then a third time on your phone under the table. The heat from the dinner goes out of your chest as if a window had been opened.

Then, kindly, below it: "Probably just a rough patch. Everyone has them. Even people like you." You apologize. You hear yourself apologize for a quarter that beat plan. He nods and says it is nothing to apologize for, and you leave feeling forgiven for something. On your way to the door the question in your head is not whether the update was weak. It is what is wrong with you.

**May, the pricing meeting**

In the pricing meeting you glance toward the head of the table before you give your own opinion on your own product. The price list on the whiteboard is one you built yourself. For weeks now you have rewritten your updates so that the first line carries the good news and the last line asks for his view. You stop proposing anything in the channel that you haven't tested on him privately first. You tell people you are the one who meets his standard, and the sentence comes out smooth, as if you had always said it.

**Two years later, a kitchen table**

Two years later it is a Sunday evening at your kitchen table, and you work somewhere else, under a manager who says the same thing every week: "Good work, keep going." It unsettles you more than anything he ever said. You keep waiting for the second half of the sentence. On Friday she mentions that one slide has a typo, and something in your chest unclenches. There it is.

Then, as you carry the plates to the sink, a thought arrives whole, in your own voice, and has nothing to do with a typo: something at the center of you is faulty. Last month a recruiter described a role under a leader famous for being brilliant and difficult, and you said yes to coffee before she finished the sentence. On your phone there is still a note called "things he said," with the dinner line copied at the top.

Your daughter, home for the weekend, asks how work is going. You open your mouth to say, "He's pleased," and remember there is no he. "Are you?" she says. You find you are listening for someone else's voice before you can say whether you are.

## The Name

That's **The Approval Addiction**.

## Definition

The Approval Addiction is the sequence where warmth is made conditional and unpredictable, praise and criticism are alternated without a visible logic, and the person's sense of worth is gradually fused to one authority's opinion, using Conditional Love, Intermittent Reinforcement, Pedestal Ploy, Negging, Shame Installation, and Identity Hooking to convert a natural wish for recognition into an independent-self-worth loss. It may benefit whoever holds the approval, and it exploits capable, motivated people. It is hard to detect because praise is real, feedback is partly valid, and managers do have moods. What can be lost is a stable internal measure of one's own work, and with it judgment, initiative and ease in the role.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Conditional Love** — Warmth that follows his way: a forty-five-minute one-to-one and "exactly the right instinct" for his structure, then a fifteen-minute meeting typed through without looking up after you move the pricing review on your own. This functions by making belonging feel earned each week. Concern rises when warmth tracks the manager's needs more than the person's results.

2. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — Weeks that do not follow results: "Wins" after a missed milestone, "Concerns" after a hit target, and a flame, a thumbs up or nothing in the channel, logged in your spreadsheet. This functions by making the reward unpredictable, which tends to keep people checking and trying. Concern rises when the person begins to track reactions, search for patterns and plan around them.

3. **Pedestal Ploy** — Public elevation: at the leadership dinner, in front of the CFO and head of sales, "If I could clone one person on this team, it would be her." This functions by raising the height of the fall, so that any later criticism reads as a failure to meet an ideal. Concern rises when praise is public and extravagant but never tied to specific work.

4. **Negging** — A compliment that cuts: in the notes document, "You're brilliant, but I worry you're not quite operating at the level I recruited." This functions by pairing a compliment with a small doubt, leaving the person motivated to win back the first half. Concern rises when criticism arrives in the same sentence as praise, vague and unfalsifiable.

5. **Shame Installation** — Your quarter turned into a flaw: "Probably just a rough patch. Everyone has them. Even people like you," and your apology for a quarter that beat plan. This functions by turning performance feedback into a judgment of who the person is. Concern rises when criticism targets character, level or fit instead of an observable behavior.

6. **Identity Hooking** — Your own use of his words: the email "This is what I hired her for," updates rewritten to end on his view, ideas tested on him first, and "the one who meets his standard." This functions by tying the person's self-description to his approval, so independence feels like losing who they are. Concern rises when the person starts to define themselves by his latest remark.

## Why This Order

**Conditional Love** comes first because the pattern needs a relationship in which warmth is valued. A senior recruit who has been courted is ready for it. The warmth is not false; it simply becomes a resource that can be withheld, and the person learns that it can be lost. It has to come before the Wins and Concerns headings, because those headings only keep you guessing once you know that a forty-five-minute Monday and a fifteen-minute one depend on how he sees you.

> Picture a teacher who is delighted when you copy their method and goes distant when you try your own. You start copying. Flip it: if the teacher's praise came at random from the very first lesson, you would ask them plainly what good work looks like.

**Intermittent Reinforcement** then makes the resource unpredictable. A manager who is always warm teaches nothing, and one who is always cold gets left. The mix, unlinked to results, is what trains attention. The person stops asking whether the work is good and starts asking what will earn the reaction. It has to come before the toast at the leadership dinner, because "clone one person" only lifts you as high as it does after months of guessing which Monday's warmth you would get.

> Think of a friend who sometimes replies to your photos in a minute and sometimes never. You find yourself checking, and you stop enjoying the photo. Flip it: if your friend called your photos the finest in the whole group before ever going quiet, you would enjoy it lightly and keep posting for your own fun.

The **Pedestal Ploy** raises the stakes. Public praise creates a standard and an audience. The higher the pedestal, the more any doubt registers as a fall. It has to come before the note about operating at the level he recruited, because that note only stings as much as it does in the days after three days of glowing from the dinner line.

> A coach calls you the best player on the team in front of everyone. After that, a missed catch feels like a public event. Flip it: if the coach said, "Good catch, for someone so slow," before any praise in front of everyone, you would laugh it off and ask what to work on.

**Negging** is how the fall is delivered without conflict: criticism wrapped in compliment, easy to deny, hard to answer. It has to come before you apologize for a quarter that beat plan, because you only apologize for beating plan once his note has told you that you are brilliant and also falling short of what he recruited.

> A relative says, "You look lovely, for someone who has been so tired lately." You thank them, then spend the evening checking a mirror. Flip it: if you already thought "Something is wrong with me" before your relative said a word, you would talk about that thought with someone you trust.

**Shame Installation** converts these episodes into identity. When the question moves from "was that update good" to "am I the kind of person who is good," the person begins to carry the evaluator inside. It has to come before you say you are the one who meets his standard, because you only describe yourself in his words once you ask what is wrong with you, not what was wrong with an update.

> You get a slightly low mark and, instead of thinking "that essay was weak," you think "I am not clever." Flip it: if you had already called yourself "the top student" before any mark came back, you would remind yourself a mark is about one essay.

**Identity Hooking** closes the loop. Once the person describes themselves in his language, the approval is no longer something he gives. It is something they need in order to be who they believe they are. This also supplies plausible deniability: nothing was demanded, nothing was threatened. It has to come last, because "the one who meets his standard" can be said so smoothly only after the cold fifteen minutes, the headings, the dinner line, the note and the apology have all been lived through.

> You begin to say "I'm the reliable one in our family." Now missing a call feels like not being yourself. Flip it: if one missed call made the reliable one in the family think "I'm useless," you would tell yourself it was just one call.

What weakens the sequence is an independent measure: results written down against targets agreed in advance, a mentor or peer outside the line, and an outside benchmark for what is reasonable. The key conversion point is the first time the person checks the channel for a reaction before the work is done.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. In a company the chief executive's attention is the scarcest thing there is, and it can only be given in small amounts: a Monday meeting, a reaction in a channel, a line at a dinner. A recruit who was courted for four months arrives expecting warmth, and the weekly one-to-one runs from a shared notes document in which the executive writes while the other person talks. Scarce attention, a weekly meeting and a document only one person types: those three facts produce the order below.

Conditional Love comes first because a courted recruit values the warmth, and a chief executive's warmth can only be given where there is time. A short meeting follows a change nobody asked him about, and the person learns the warmth can be lost.

Intermittent Reinforcement comes second because attention is rationed unevenly. A busy executive cannot be warm to everyone every week, and the headings in the notes document follow his week, not the person's results.

Pedestal Ploy comes third because dinners and all-hands are where an executive speaks to a room. A line about cloning one person is a natural thing to say aloud, and it sets a height that the person now has to stay at.

Negging comes fourth because the way down from a public high is rarely a direct criticism. A sentence that praises and faults in one breath is easy to type into a notes document and hard to answer.

Shame Installation comes fifth because a person told, after a quarter that beat plan, that they are below the level recruited has to explain it somehow, and the nearest explanation is themselves.

Identity Hooking comes last because after enough of these episodes the person has a vocabulary for who they are, and the vocabulary is his: the one who meets the standard. Nothing was demanded, so nothing can be refused.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: warmth before it can be lost, unevenness before it can be guessed at, a public height before a fall, and a standard before a self. It turns a recruit who arrived confident into one who tracks reactions.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A chief executive's thin calendar, a shared notes document and a recruitment announcement were enough, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a new hire. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Nadia Fairweather joined a mid-sized firm as head of customer operations, and the managing director, Cyrus Delacroix, gave her long Monday meetings that overran with tea going cold. He remembered her son's name. When she moved a staff meeting to suit her team without telling him, her next one-to-one lasted ten minutes and he typed throughout. After that the notes document opened some weeks with "Wins" and some with "Concerns," and the headings did not match what she had delivered. At the firm's all-hands he said that if he could clone one person, it would be her. For three days people stopped her in the corridor. On the next Monday, in the document, he wrote that she was brilliant but he worried she was not operating at the level he had hired, and added, "Probably a rough patch. Even people like you." Nadia apologized for a quarter that had beaten plan. Within weeks she was rewriting her updates to open with good news and close with a question for him, glancing at him before speaking in meetings, and telling colleagues that she was the one who met his standard. Cyrus told a friend that he liked to keep new hires guessing which Monday they would get.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may begin to pre-test ideas privately, soften their own judgment, and spend attention on reading his mood. They may keep records of reactions, find themselves elated by a thumbs up and flattened by silence, and notice that the difference no longer matches the facts of the work.

They may lose track of what they think. They may rehearse a Monday meeting all weekend. They may hesitate to take a better offer elsewhere because they are unsure whether they are as good as they were told. And they may feel embarrassed to describe any of it, since it sounds like oversensitivity.

## Institutional Echo

Organizations where promotion, sponsorship and standing rest on a single senior person's impression can make this pattern routine. Rating systems that rely on narrative comments, executive channels where reactions are visible, and one-to-one notes written by only one party can all amplify variable feedback. Where criteria for "operating at level" are unwritten, a criticism cannot be tested.

Many leaders also give mixed feedback for honest reasons: tough standards, a changing business, an unclear sense of their own mood. Whether a given leader is calibrating or conditioning depends on the evidence and cannot be read from a single meeting. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the feedback tracks measurable results and whether peers describe the same rhythm.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that great leaders have high standards and that the best people crave their approval. It is wrapped in the language of excellence, of "keeping people sharp," of the demanding mentor whose rare praise means more because it is rare.

The value exploited is real. People do grow under people who believe in them, and ambition does include a wish to be seen. The public story is a leader who develops talent. The private loss, where the pattern is present, is that a capable adult hands the question "am I any good" to someone whose answer changes.

## Observable Indicators

1. Praise and criticism alternate week to week without a link to documented results.
2. Public superlatives are not tied to specific work.
3. Criticism is delivered in the same sentence as praise and is not specific enough to act on.
4. Feedback concerns level, character or fit, not observable behavior.
5. The person tracks reactions such as emojis, response times or tone.
6. One-to-one notes are written and held by one party.
7. Criteria for the level or standard are unwritten or change.
8. The person rewrites updates to seek approval before submitting.
9. Ideas are tested privately with the senior person before they are shared.
10. Warmth cools after the person acts independently or sets a boundary.
11. The person apologizes for outcomes that met targets.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The shared one-to-one notes document and its version history, with dates, as you lawfully have access to it.
- Your own dated record of what was said in each meeting, written soon afterward.
- Your results against agreed targets, kept separately from the feedback.
- Any written feedback, board messages or performance documents that describe your work, and comparisons with the verbal feedback.
- A note of channel reactions only as a factual log, if it helps you see the pattern.
- Written criteria for your level, role or bonus, and any change to them.
- Names of peers who receive similar feedback.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Warm and critical feedback in the same week can be honest management. A chief executive may be giving real praise on Tuesday and real concern on Monday because the numbers moved. A busy leader may react to an update with an emoji or nothing at all for innocent reasons. High standards and a recruiter's enthusiasm are normal. The discriminator is whether the feedback tracks measurable results, whether it is specific, and whether it is consistent for others in comparable roles.

Concern strengthens when: praise and criticism run opposite to results; the person starts to arrange work around a reaction; and criticism targets character or level while the numbers are met.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Result Test:**
Ask: "Does the feedback follow what I actually delivered?"
Concern rises if: wins weeks and concerns weeks do not match results.

**SPOT IT — The Specific Test:**
Ask: "What exactly should I do differently, and how will we know it worked?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a mood, a level or a worry.

**SPOT IT — The Reaction Test:**
Ask: "Am I checking for a response before I finish my work?"
Concern rises if: yes, and my day is shaped by it.

**SPOT IT — The Pedestal Test:**
Ask: "When I was praised, what was the praise for?"
Concern rises if: it was for who I am, not for what I did.

**SPOT IT — The Own Judgment Test:**
Ask: "Can I say whether my quarter went well without asking him?"
Concern rises if: no.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write down results against agreed targets.** Keep this separately from feedback, and update it weekly.
- **Ask for specifics in writing.** "Could you tell me which of the two Q3 assumptions concerned you, so I can respond to it?"
- **Co-own the notes.** "Could we both have edit access to the one-to-one document, and I'll send a summary after each meeting?"
- **Ask for criteria.** "How is operating at level defined for my role, and is it written somewhere?"
- **Find an outside benchmark.** A mentor, peer or executive coach with no stake in the answer, or a trusted ex-colleague.
- **Slow the reflex.** Decide your view of an update before you look for the reaction.
- **Separate the person from the work in your own words.** "That quarter missed on pricing" is a statement about a quarter.
- **Do not confront the person with the tracked emoji log.** It is a private aid to your own clarity.
- **Preserve other options.** Keep your network and a current CV in good order.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Feedback follows mood more than results; you are tracking reactions; criticism arrives vague and personal; your own judgment is eroding.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Written feedback begins to contradict your documented results; your standing, bonus, equity or role is placed on a vague "level" judgment; others describe the same rhythm; you are told not to discuss the matter with others. Consider a qualified employment lawyer, an independent board member or lead director, or HR where independent of the person involved.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, retaliation of a severe kind, or signs of self-harm in you or a colleague. Prioritize safety and emergency or crisis support, tell someone you trust, and do not confront the person. A therapist or peer support can help with the personal toll, which is separate from the commercial and legal questions.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you tell me specifically which part of the plan concerns you, so I can respond to it?"
- "I'd like to keep a shared summary of our one-to-ones. I'll send you my notes after each one."
- "I hit the Q2 numbers and I'm hearing concern. Help me understand how the two fit."
- "How is operating at level defined for this role? Is it written?"
- "I'd rather we talk about the pricing assumption than about whether I'm meeting the level."
- "I'd value a regular, written feedback rhythm, so I'm not guessing."
- "Is the feedback I'm getting consistent with what the rest of the leadership team is hearing?"
- "I'm going to take a day to think before I reply."
