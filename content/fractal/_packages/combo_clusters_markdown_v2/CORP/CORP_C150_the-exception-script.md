# C150: The Exception Script — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that you are the special one to whom the usual warnings do not apply, and a need to keep earning that special place by staying close.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic thought when you hear how others were treated, "it's different with me," before you compare the stories.
- A pull toward people who say you are unlike anyone they have met, and a feeling that it must be fate.
- A habit of holding on for a promised future, even while the present keeps getting smaller.

**A point in a life.** The peak years of a finance career.

**Where it happens.** A founder-led, fast-growing company.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a CFO, you're forty-nine, and you have spent twenty years learning what a "special arrangement" costs. You are not easily flattered. You keep a spreadsheet of your own mistakes, and its first column never changes: believed a promise before reading the paper.

**March, a corner table**

A recruiter called, with the careful voice of someone reading a speech they like. A founder-led company, growth stage, a board that wanted discipline. The founder wanted to meet "before anyone else does." You almost said no. You said yes for a coffee. The coffee became dinner. He chose the restaurant, a quiet place with a corner table that smells of rosemary and warm bread, and he knows things about you that you have not put in your résumé: the turnaround you led, the way you handled the audit, the reason you left your last company. Three more dinners follow. At midnight he sends a link to an article about operators who change companies. He introduces you to a board member who says you are "the one he has been telling us about." He tells you about his father, and a mistake he made at twenty-six. Your shoulders come down for the first time in a year.

The word he uses is partnership. "I don't want a finance person," he says. "I want a co-builder." He leans in. "I don't do this with everyone. I don't do this with anyone." You think of telling your husband it feels right. It does.

Over the main course of the last dinner he says, "I've been looking for someone like you for three years," and then says it again in a different way: "It wasn't an accident the recruiter called you." He says you will sit on the executive committee. You feel the sentence more than you hear it.

**Friday, your study**

The offer letter arrives on a Friday, generous and unusually warm. His cover email promises a seat on the board in eighteen months, and equity that "will reflect what you're really worth." Base and bonus are clear. The equity paragraph says an option grant "to be approved by the board and documented following the next financing." There is a paragraph titled Special Arrangement: a retention-based true-up "reflecting the Company's commitment to your long-term success," at the founder's discretion. The vesting schedule is attached as "indicative." You read it twice at the desk by the window, with the radiator ticking.

The signing deadline is Tuesday, so the founder can "announce at the offsite." When you tell him, on a video call, that you would like your lawyer to read it, he says, kindly, and the warmth in his face dims by a degree, "Of course. I just hope this doesn't make it feel like the usual thing. You and I aren't the usual thing." You hear yourself say it is no trouble, and that you will keep it short.

You ask what the last CFO was offered, and hear instead that the last one "wanted everything in writing too," and that you are different.

**Monday night, your kitchen**

Since then you have not asked for the cap table, or the last two board decks, or the last CFO's terms. The lawyer's email sits unopened, because you are not sure you want to be the person who sends it back with questions. You used to read a cap table before you read an offer letter. You sleep badly. When your husband asks how the terms look, you say they are generous, and change the subject before he can ask for a number. You notice that you have started saying "we" about a company whose numbers you have never seen. You rehearse the conversation with the lawyer so that it will sound like trust, and then you do not have it. You tell yourself that every question you don't ask is a kindness.

The letter lies on the kitchen table, and the pen beside it is the good one. You sit down in front of it, and get up again, and put the kettle on, and sit down again. At eleven you go to bed without reading it.

**Two years later**

You are fifty-one, the finance lead at a smaller firm, and it is a Thursday in November at a fundraising dinner. A man you met an hour ago, the owner of a regional chain of garden centers, takes the empty seat beside you and says, "You're not like the other finance people I've met. I can tell." He says, "It's strange. It feels like we were supposed to run into each other." A warm feeling begins in your chest, and you let it.

Across the table someone is telling a story about the owner's last two controllers, who both left within a year, and one of whom, she says, was treated "pretty badly, I heard." You hear it, and you think, with no effort at all: that was them, not me. You do not ask a single question about it.

The waiter refills your water twice. Someone raises a glass to the new store. He offers you a role, in a few words, on a napkin: a seat at the table when the company is ready, a share of ownership when the new store opens, a future that is a year off, and a year after that. You say you will think about it. You already know you will say yes. The last time you counted, your nights at your desk outnumbered your nights at home. You fold the napkin and put it in your jacket, next to the other one.

## The Name

That's **The Exception Script**.

## Definition

The Exception Script is the sequence where a person is persuaded that they are uniquely special and exempt from what happened to those before them, so that ordinary caution is set aside. It can use Love Bombing, Exclusive Meaning, Manufactured Destiny, Future Faking, Conditional Love and Comparative Devaluation to convert the desire to feel uniquely valued into the loss of ordinary caution that "exceptional" status was used to bypass. It is hard to detect because the flattery is specific, the appeal may be sincere, and the person's own track record makes the story plausible. Intent would require additional evidence: founders do court executives enthusiastically, turnover can have honest causes, and a discretionary arrangement can be offered in good faith.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Love Bombing** — The attention before the paper: a corner-table dinner where he knows your turnaround and audit story, three more dinners, a midnight article, and a board member who calls you "the one he has been telling us about." This functions by creating gratitude and attachment before the evaluation stage. Concern rises when the volume of attention is greatest before documents are shared.

2. **Exclusive Meaning** — The language of partnership: "I don't want a finance person. I want a co-builder," and "I don't do this with everyone. I don't do this with anyone." This functions by placing the relationship outside the normal categories of employer and employee, so normal safeguards seem out of place. Concern rises when ordinary professional steps are described as unneeded for "us."

3. **Manufactured Destiny** — The sense of fate: "I've been looking for someone like you for three years," "It wasn't an accident the recruiter called you," and a place on the executive committee. This functions by turning a business choice into a calling. Concern rises when your role is described in terms of fate and not of defined responsibilities.

4. **Future Faking** — The promises with no numbers: a board seat in eighteen months, equity that "will reflect what you're really worth," an option grant "to be approved," a Special Arrangement true-up at the founder's discretion, and an "indicative" vesting schedule. This functions by pricing the present in the currency of a future not yet written. Concern rises when the promises are specific in tone and vague in number, date and approval.

5. **Conditional Love** — The change in warmth: a Tuesday signing deadline for the offsite, and when you ask for your lawyer, "I just hope this doesn't make it feel like the usual thing." This functions by tying affection to compliance. Concern rises when review, questions or delay change the temperature.

6. **Comparative Devaluation** — The ones who came before: the last CFO "didn't understand the vision," the one before "couldn't handle the pace," "The last one wanted everything in writing too. That was the start of it," and "this time is different." This functions by explaining away turnover and setting your performance against theirs. Concern rises when previous people are described only by their faults, and you are not told what they were offered.

## Why This Order

**Love Bombing** has to come first because the rest requires a state of attachment, and nothing builds it faster than informed, specific attention. The founder who knows your audit story is hard to treat as a vendor of terms. Before a document appears, you've been persuaded of a bond. It has to come before his claim of a rare partnership, because a founder who already knows your audit and your last company is hard to treat as a seller of terms.

> Picture someone you have just met remembering the name of your childhood dog. Before you know anything about them, you feel known. Flip it: if someone you had just met called you "my one true kindred spirit" before learning a thing about you, you would laugh it off and ask how they could know.

**Exclusive Meaning** then removes the frame that would let you evaluate the bond. In a normal hiring process the questions are standard, and counsel is routine. If the relationship is a partnership beyond the usual, then asking for the usual feels like a category error. It has to come before the claim that the call was no accident, because a partnership he says he offers no one else is what makes fate sound like a compliment.

> A shopkeeper says "you're not a customer, you're family." Asking for a receipt starts to feel strange. Flip it: if the shopkeeper had said "you were always going to walk in here" before treating you as anything special, you would still ask for your receipt.

**Manufactured Destiny** lifts the stakes: this is not a job but a path, and a person on a path does not haggle over a paragraph. It has to come before the vague grant and the true-up, because a person who has been told it was meant to be is slow to ask for a number on the equity.

> A new friend says "it's like we were meant to meet." It is lovely, and it is hard to ask what they want from fate. Flip it: if the new friend promised a lovely trip together "one day soon" before any talk of fate, you would kindly ask when and how.

**Future Faking** supplies the substance, and it is deliberately formless: a grant to be approved, a true-up at discretion, an indicative schedule. The value is placed in the future where no one can check it. It has to come before his dimming warmth, because a grant to be approved later and a true-up at his discretion are exactly what you would be asking your lawyer about.

> Picture a friend who says "I'll make it up to you when my ship comes in." The promise is warm, and you cannot check it until the ship docks. Flip it: if your friend went a little cool each time you asked about money, before any promise about the future, you would ask straight out what was wrong.

**Conditional Love** is the enforcement. It is rarely stated; it appears as a change in warmth when the lawyer is mentioned or the deadline questioned. It has to come before the answer about the last CFO, because you only ask what the last CFO got after his warmth has dimmed at the mention of a lawyer.

> Think of a host whose smile cools by a degree when you say you must leave early. You cannot point to anything, and you stay another hour. Flip it: if the host had said you were far better company than the last guests, before you mentioned leaving, you would still leave when you meant to.

**Comparative Devaluation** is the explanation of why this time is different, and it works by disarming the one piece of evidence that would otherwise raise a flag: the turnover. It has to come last, because hearing that you are different from the last CFO only reassures you after the warmth has cooled and the lawyer has been made to feel unusual.

> A new partner says of the exes, "they never understood me." You feel special, and you do not ask why there were so many. Flip it: if your new partner praised you above the exes first and later went cool whenever you made your own plans, you would ask why the warmth had a condition.

The conversion point is the moment you sign without having seen the cap table, because you believe you are the exception. What would weaken the sequence: asking for the same documents anyone would receive, a comparison of the arrangement with what the predecessors were offered, and an independent reading of the offer by counsel and an accountant who have no stake in the outcome.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A founder-led company hiring a senior executive produces this order without a plan. Three facts about the setting do the work. The person being hired is scarce and the founder needs a yes before the offsite. The offer is built from terms that depend on future events, so the value sits in the future. And a predecessor left, and the reason is awkward to explain.

Love Bombing comes first because the rest needs attachment, and nothing builds it faster than specific attention. A founder who knows your audit story is hard to treat as a seller of terms. Before a document exists you have been persuaded of a bond.

Exclusive Meaning comes second because a normal hiring process has standard questions and routine counsel. If the relationship is a partnership beyond the usual, asking for the usual feels like a mistake of category.

Manufactured Destiny comes third because it lifts the stakes from a job to a path. A person on a path does not haggle over a paragraph, and the person who is slow to sign starts to look like someone who missed the point.

Future Faking comes fourth because the substance has to be formless: a grant to be approved, a true-up at discretion, a schedule marked indicative. Value placed in the future cannot be checked today.

Conditional Love comes fifth because enforcement is rarely stated. It appears as a change in warmth when the lawyer is mentioned or the deadline is questioned.

Comparative Devaluation comes last because the predecessor's departure is the one piece of evidence that could raise a flag. Describing the last one as someone who wanted everything in writing turns a warning into a contrast.

Put it together and the order is the one this setting needs: a company that must close a hire before the offsite, on terms that cannot be priced today. It turns the person at the center into someone who is certain they are the exception and has stopped reading.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A deadline before an offsite, an offer built from future terms, and a predecessor who left did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a job candidate. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Cormac Ostwell, a senior engineer weighing a move to a start-up, was invited by its founder, Winifred Osei, to a quiet dinner at a corner table. She knew the name of his first team and the project he had never gotten credit for. Three dinners later she told him, "I don't do this with anyone else." She said the recruiter had not called him by chance. The offer letter arrived with a title, a salary, and a line about "equity to be agreed after the next round" and "a bonus at the founder's discretion." When Cormac said his lawyer would like to read it, her smile cooled for a moment, and she said she hoped he would not turn this into "the usual hiring thing." When he asked what the last engineering lead had been offered, she said that one had wanted everything on paper, and that Cormac was nothing like him. Winifred was never unsure what she was doing. She told a friend that Cormac would sign by Tuesday, because he believed he was special.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may feel unusually valued and unusually reluctant to ask for ordinary things. Over time they may find themselves explaining the arrangement to friends in terms of the founder's character, not the clauses. They may rehearse reasons why the turnover does not apply to them. They may delay asking for the cap table, because the question feels like a breach of the special bond. If the promises later narrow, they may blame themselves for being naive, and feel embarrassed to describe it, because they were warned by their own experience and signed anyway.

## Institutional Echo

Executive offers that reference equity "to be documented" after a financing, discretionary retention amounts and indicative vesting schedules can leave key terms undefined at signing. Courtship processes run by founders directly, with tight signing windows tied to events such as an offsite, can compress review time. Recurring turnover in a role, explained as a series of individual failings, can function to protect the structure from scrutiny.

The honest reading is often that founders are enthusiastic, that equity is genuinely papered after financings, and that previous executives may have been a poor fit. Intent would require additional evidence: whether the previous executives were offered the same arrangement, whether papering ever followed the same promises, and whether the discretionary terms were exercised.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that the right person gets recognized, and that the truest deals are built on being chosen. We admire the founder who sees potential, and we distrust the executive who negotiates. Around it sits the belief that sophisticated people do not need paper, and that to ask is to doubt.

The value exploited is among the most natural: wanting to be seen as exceptional by someone who matters. The public story is a founder who recruited the right operator. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is that the operator stopped doing what twenty years of experience told her to do.

## Observable Indicators

1. Attention, praise and personal disclosure peak before any written terms exist.
2. You are told you are different from named or described predecessors.
3. The relationship is described as a partnership beyond ordinary employment.
4. The seat has a history of turnover, explained only by predecessors' faults.
5. Equity is "to be approved" or "documented later," with no number, date or approval step.
6. A discretionary "special arrangement" has no defined trigger, amount or date.
7. The signing deadline is set around the founder's calendar.
8. Review by counsel is met with disappointment or comment on trust.
9. Requests for the cap table, board materials or predecessors' terms are deferred.
10. The vesting schedule or key terms are described as "indicative."

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every draft and version of the offer letter, with the dates received, and each change request and reply.
- The wording of the equity and special-arrangement paragraphs, and any message describing them.
- Messages describing you as different from predecessors, with dates.
- Deadline communications, with the date, who set it and what review you requested.
- Requests for the cap table, board approvals, vesting schedule and financing timeline, and any replies.
- Notes of dinners and calls, written the same day, recording quoted promises.
- The names of recruiters, board members or others who heard the same commitments.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Founders recruit with real enthusiasm. Offers are often tentative before a financing closes, and the equity of a senior hire is legitimately papered after board approval. Warmth in a courtship is normal and good.

The discriminator is **whether the warmth survives the request for paper**. Genuine enthusiasm welcomes the cap table, counsel and a written grant mechanism. This pattern reacts to those requests as if they were a betrayal.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Honest turnover.** Previous executives may have been poorly matched, and the founder may say so candidly.
- **Real discretion.** A retention arrangement may truly be at the board's discretion.
- **Legitimate timing.** A deadline may reflect a real announcement and a real board schedule.

Concern strengthens when: key terms remain undefined while attention intensifies; counsel is treated as a lack of trust; and predecessors' terms cannot be discussed.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Predecessor Test:**
Ask: "What were the last two people in this role offered, and what happened to it?"
Concern rises if: the answer is only about their faults.

**SPOT IT — The Paper Test:**
Ask: "Is every special promise written with a number, a date and an approval step?"
Concern rises if: the warmest promises are the least defined.

**SPOT IT — The Counsel Test:**
Ask: "How does the founder react when I say my lawyer will read this?"
Concern rises if: the temperature changes.

**SPOT IT — The Exception Test:**
Ask: "Would I accept this offer if it were offered to everyone?"
Concern rises if: the arrangement only makes sense because I am special.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the standard documents** before you sign: cap table, option plan, board approval process, financing timeline.
- **Have an attorney and a tax or accounting adviser read the offer** and describe in plain terms what is and is not guaranteed.
- **Convert every special promise into a clause** with a trigger, an amount and a date, or treat it as absent.
- **Ask about predecessors in a neutral way:** "What was the structure for the previous CFO's equity, and how did it work out?"
- **Ask to talk to a director or investor** who is independent of the founder.
- **Slow the timeline.** "I can sign when counsel has finished. If the announcement can't move, we can announce later."
- **Notice your own reaction.** Write down what you would advise a client in the same position.
- **Do not rely on verbal commitments,** and do not resign a current position before the paper is final.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Equity or special terms promised without a document; a deadline tied to an event; counsel greeted with disappointment. Ask for the paper and slow the timeline.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Refusal to share the cap table, board approval path or predecessors' terms; pressure to resign your current role before signing; signs that the equity may not be approved. Relevant routes may include an independent employment or corporate lawyer, an independent director, an accountant, and a securities regulator where equity or offering questions arise.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats about references or reputation, retaliation after you asked questions, intimidation, or anyone describing self-harm. Prioritize safety and emergency support, contact qualified counsel, and do not confront the person or negotiate under threat.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'm very interested. I'd like my lawyer to review the offer, and I'll respond by next Friday."
- "Could you share the cap table and the plan under which my grant would be issued?"
- "The special arrangement paragraph has no amount or date. Can we define the trigger and the figure?"
- "When does the board approve the grant, and what happens if it doesn't?"
- "I'd like to understand what the previous CFO's arrangement was, and how it worked."
- "I'd prefer to sign when the documents are final, even if the announcement moves."
- "Can I speak with an independent director before I decide?"
