# C199: The Captured Referee — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that every body meant to judge fairly is already on the other side, and a sense that reporting a wrong is pointless because nothing ever happens.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic sentence when told to raise a concern, "there's a policy for that, and it never works."
- A wariness of experts and critics who seem independent, and a wondering who briefed them first.
- A habit of watching what happens to the person reported, and deciding from that whether to speak at all.

**A point in a life.** Late in a long career.

**Where it happens.** A company pension scheme's trustee board.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a fifty-five-year-old operations lead who has worked for the same company for twenty-nine years, and three years ago your colleagues elected you as a member-trustee of the company pension scheme. You ran for it because you believed in it. You have watched people retire on the scheme and you know that it is what stands between some of them and a very thin old age. You read the papers properly. You are, in the dry language of the board, "diligent."

**The spring meeting, the boardroom**

The trustee board meets quarterly in a room on the executive floor, with a long table, a jug of water and a view of the car park. There are seven trustees: three nominated by the company, two elected by members including you, one from the pensioners, and the chair, a retired finance director with a long history at the firm. The scheme's investment adviser sits in the corner. The company's chief financial officer attends "for context" and leaves before the voting.

In the spring the paper asks you to approve moving a portion of the scheme's assets into a property fund managed by a firm in which the company's chief executive holds an interest. It says "the company has confirmed its comfort." Somewhere on page eleven there is a line about the executive's "ownership interest in the manager's parent." You ask whether this is a conflict that should be reviewed. The chair thanks you. He says it is exactly the kind of question a good trustee asks, and proposes a conflicts review and a small working group to carry it out. Your shoulders drop. You have been heard.

**The next week, the corridor**

The working group is formed the next week: the chair, a company-nominated trustee, and one of the two elected members, a colleague you have known for years, who has been kind to you. He catches you in the corridor outside the canteen, holding his tea. He says he'd like to "help keep this constructive," and mentions that the chief financial officer was glad to hear he would be involved. He suggests that you give him your concerns in writing so that he can "carry them in the room." You do, and you feel the relief of putting them down. At the working group he carries them softly. By the end they are the working group's "points to be noted."

**A month later, your desk**

The review needs an independent opinion. The scheme has used the same law firm for years, and the firm is instructed through the company's legal department, which is where the engagement letter comes from. It is also paid through a budget the company approves. You ask the chair, by email, what information the expert was given, and by whom. He replies that it is covered by privilege. You ask whether you can see the instruction letter. He says he will take advice on that. The opinion arrives, four pages, politely worded. It concludes that "on the information provided, the proposed arrangement is consistent with the trustees' conflicts policy, and no material conflict arises that cannot be managed by disclosure." You read the first sentence of it three times, then the last.

**The next meeting, the boardroom**

At the next meeting the chair places the letter on the table next to the conflicts policy. He points to the clause on disclosure and management. "We have followed our policy," he says. "We have taken independent advice." There is a nod around the table. The pensioner trustee, who has been quiet, says that she is not an expert. The adviser says that the proposal seems reasonable. The chair moves to a vote. Your mouth is dry, and there is nothing left on the table to object to.

You vote against. The motion passes five to two, and nothing else happens. No one asks you to leave. The minutes are accurate. The assets move. You walk back to your desk past people who will retire on it.

**A year later, the staff event**

A year later, at the staff year-end event, the chief executive thanks the trustees for their "thorough governance," and the room applauds. You clap a beat late. A colleague who once asked you how to raise a concern with the board tells you, over the buffet, that she has decided not to bother. You tell her you understand. You don't tell her to try. When the visiting pensions consultant gives her talk afterwards, you spend the whole of it looking for the name of whoever paid for her slides, and when a younger colleague mentions a rota that doesn't add up, you ask him first what happened to the last person who mentioned one.

You no longer take the quarterly papers home to read twice. When a colleague close to retirement asks whether the scheme is in good shape, you say "the board is looking at it," and hear how that sounds. You wake at four and go over the count of the vote. By now you are the only person in the building who remembers that the question was ever open, and you cannot find a single place left to take it.

You still read the papers properly. The word for it in the minutes is "diligent," and you have started to hear it differently.

## The Name

That's **The Captured Referee**.

## Definition

The Captured Referee is the sequence where influence is gained over the body meant to provide oversight, outside structures are aligned to enforce the result, opposition is quietly guided, an aligned expert closes the debate, the policy is cited as proof, and everyone sees nothing happen, using Institutional Capture, Controlled Opposition, Captive Expertise, Policy as Proof and Visible Impunity to convert belief that oversight bodies are neutral into loss of any route to an independent hearing. In corporate settings it can sit in trustee boards, audit committees and ethics panels. It is hard to detect because every step is procedurally correct, and because no one person has to intend the result.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Institutional Capture** — The board is shaped by the company: three company-nominated trustees, a chair from the firm's finance history, the chief financial officer attending "for context," and a paper stating "the company has confirmed its comfort." This functions by making the referee dependent on the party being refereed. Concern rises when those overseeing a matter have an interest in its outcome, and when the interest is disclosed only in a footnote.

2. **Controlled Opposition** — A friendly colleague offers to "carry" your concerns: he asks for them in writing, mentions the chief financial officer was glad he would be involved, and they come out as "points to be noted." This functions by absorbing dissent into the process so that it appears heard and loses its edge. Concern rises when an objection is softened on its way into the record, or when an objector is invited into a group that then dilutes the concern.

3. **Captive Expertise** — An opinion commissioned through the company's legal department and paid from its budget: privilege is cited when you ask what the expert was given and to see the instruction letter. This functions by replacing a contested question with an authority that seems unchallengeable. Concern rises when the instruction, the information given and the fee route are not visible to the people being asked to rely on the opinion.

4. **Policy as Proof** — The chair lays the opinion letter beside the conflicts policy and says "We have followed our policy. We have taken independent advice," and the table nods. This functions by treating procedure as a substitute for substance, so that "we followed the policy" ends the discussion. Concern rises when a policy is cited to close a question it was meant to open.

5. **Visible Impunity** — Applause instead of account: the motion passes five to two and the assets move, the chief executive thanks the trustees for their "thorough governance" to applause, and a colleague tells you she has decided not to bother raising a concern. This functions by showing everyone watching that raising the matter led nowhere. Concern rises when outcomes carry no consequence and no further route is offered.

## Why This Order

**Capture** must come first, and it is almost never an event. It is a composition: who sits, who attends, who chairs, who writes the paper. Once the body's makeup favours one interest, later steps can be fully procedural and still deliver the same outcome. It has to come before the colleague who offers to carry your concerns, because the working group is chosen from the same table, so a kind elected member can sit inside it beside the chair and a company nominee.

> Picture a football match where one team's coach also picks the referee and the linesmen. Every call can be by the book and still go the same way. Flip it: first the coach says he will pass your complaint to the referee, and it comes back as "a fan mentioned a few things"; only then do you learn he also picked the referee and the linesmen.

**Controlled opposition** follows because the most dangerous voice is the one that cannot be outvoted by sheer numbers. A trustee who asks the question loudly can be recorded as dissenting. A trustee whose concern is "carried" is not recorded at all. It is turned into an ordinary agenda item. The colleague need not be acting in bad faith; kindness is enough. It has to come before the law firm's opinion, because the review needs a finding to carry weight, and your concerns, softened into "points to be noted," leave the working group free to commission one.

> A friend says "give me your complaint and I'll pass it to the landlord." It comes back as "a tenant mentioned a few things." Your name and your anger are gone from it. Flip it: first your friend says the landlord has asked "a surveyor we trust" to look at your complaint, and the report sounds independent; only then does she hand it on as "a tenant mentioned a few things."

**Captive expertise** supplies the thing neither of the first two can: legitimacy from outside. A conflicts review that ends in the working group's judgement is a company talking to itself. The same conclusion in an expert's letter is a finding. The expert does not need to be wrong. It is enough that the question put, and the facts supplied, were chosen. It has to come before the letter beside the policy, because the opinion, concluded on "the information provided," is what the chair sets next to the conflicts policy as proof the matter has been handled.

> You ask a mechanic if your car needs a repair, and he sends you to "a garage we trust" that he pays. The second opinion sounds independent, and you stop asking. Flip it: first the mechanic points to a framed certificate on his wall saying "all work is checked," and you stop asking; only then does he send you to "a garage we trust" that he pays.

**Policy as proof** then makes the finding portable. The letter and the policy together become a ritual phrase: "we followed our policy and took independent advice." It has to come before the vote passing and nothing following, because the nod around the table and the pensioner trustee's "not an expert" only follow once the letter and the policy sit side by side.

> A restaurant hangs its safety certificate by the door. The framed paper is there, so nobody asks whether the kitchen is clean. Flip it: first you report a dirty kitchen and nothing changes for the restaurant; only then does it hang a framed safety certificate by the door, and nobody asks any more.

**Visible impunity** closes the loop. The trustee who voted against sees that nothing happens, and so does everyone else. It also protects the structure from review, since "nothing happened" reads as "nothing was wrong." It has to come last, because only once the vote has passed and no one has asked you to leave does everyone watching learn what reporting a concern achieves.

> You report a noisy neighbour, and nothing changes for them. The next time something bothers you, you don't bother to say so. Flip it: first nothing changes for your noisy neighbour after you report them, and you stop saying anything; only later does the building put up a framed notice about noise rules, and nobody asks whether it works.

What weakens the sequence is separation: an adviser instructed independently of the company, a request for the instruction letter, and a record of the dissent in the minutes. The key conversion point is the moment the letter is placed beside the policy.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. It falls out of what a company pension board is built to produce. Seats are filled by nomination and election, and the company nominates and hosts. Experts are instructed through the company's own legal department and paid from a budget it approves. A conflicts policy exists, and meetings run on a quarterly calendar, so any concern arrives as one item among many.

Institutional Capture comes first because it is a composition, not an event: who sits, who attends, who chairs, who writes the paper. Once the body's makeup favours one interest, later steps can be fully procedural and still deliver the same outcome.

Controlled Opposition comes second because the most awkward voice is the one that cannot be outvoted by numbers. A trustee who asks a question loudly can be recorded as dissenting. A trustee whose concern is "carried" by a kind colleague is not recorded at all; it becomes an ordinary agenda item. The colleague need not act in bad faith. Kindness is enough.

Captive Expertise comes third because the review needs legitimacy from outside. A conflicts review that ends in the working group's own judgement is a company talking to itself, and the same conclusion in an expert's letter is a finding. The expert does not need to be wrong. It is enough that the question put, and the facts supplied, were chosen.

Policy as Proof comes fourth because a letter and a policy together become a portable phrase: "we followed our policy and took independent advice." It ends discussion without requiring anyone to show that the policy works.

Visible Impunity comes last because the vote is the first time everyone sees what happens. Nothing happens to the trustee who dissented, and nothing happens to the arrangement, and "nothing happened" reads as "nothing was wrong."

Put it together and the order is the order a conflicted board needs: a composition, a quiet channel, an outside voice, a ritual phrase, and a lesson for the watchers. What it turns the person into is someone who stops reporting, and who assumes that any body meant to judge is already settled.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A board built by nomination, an adviser instructed through the company and a conflicts policy that exists on paper are what this setting produces, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a staff representative. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Rhiannon Teague was elected by her colleagues to her company's ethics committee. It had five members: three managers put forward by the directors, herself, and one other elected representative, with the finance director attending "for context" and leaving before the votes. When the committee's paper proposed a supply contract with a firm part-owned by a director's family, she asked whether it was a conflict. The chair thanked her and promised a review. Another elected member, a kind colleague, offered to "carry her concerns in the room" if she wrote them down. They came back as "points to be noted." The review's opinion came from the company's usual law firm, instructed through the legal department, and it said that on the information provided no conflict arose that could not be managed by disclosure. The chair laid the opinion beside the committee's policy and said they had followed it and taken independent advice. Rhiannon voted against. It passed four to one. Nothing happened to anyone. The next week a colleague who had meant to bring a complaint to the committee told her she had decided not to bother.

Hugo Lathbury, the head of legal, was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that committees were easiest when everyone had been heard.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt whether her question was reasonable, because everyone else appears satisfied. She may notice that she prepares less for meetings, because preparation seems pointless. She may feel responsible for the members she represents, and helpless at the same time.

Over time she may stop asking at all, or ask only in private. She may feel isolated from colleagues who see the meeting as ordinary. She may feel tension between her duty as a trustee and her career in the company, which pays her wages and holds her job.

## Institutional Echo

Trustee boards, audit committees and ethics panels commonly mix nominated and elected members, executives who attend by invitation, and advisers who are engaged through the sponsoring organisation. In such arrangements the question of who instructs the adviser, who pays, and who sets the question is a real governance issue, and good schemes address it openly.

Conflicts policies that rely on disclosure and "management" rather than recusal can let a conflicted matter proceed on paper while the relevant interest stays at the table. What trustees and directors owe, and what any particular policy requires, varies by jurisdiction and by the scheme's own documents. The honest reading in many cases is a well-meaning board following habit and advice. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same adviser has repeatedly reached conclusions favourable to the sponsor, and whether any dissent has ever changed an outcome.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that oversight bodies are neutral: that someone, somewhere, is checking, and that if a matter had been wrong, the board or the auditor would have caught it. "If it were a problem, the board would have caught it" is one of the most repeated sentences in organisational life.

Beneath it lies a respect for procedure and expertise that is largely earned. The values being exploited are real: trust in process, in professionals, and in the idea that decisions made carefully are decisions made well.

The public story is a board that took independent advice and followed its policy. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a pensioner who was told that the question had been asked and answered.

## Observable Indicators

1. Members of the oversight body have a financial or reporting link to the party under review.
2. An executive of the interested party attends the meeting, and leaves before the vote.
3. A paper discloses a conflict in a footnote or a single line.
4. A working group formed to review a concern is composed mainly of those aligned with the matter.
5. A concern raised in the meeting appears later in softened form in the record.
6. The independent expert is instructed and paid through the interested party.
7. The expert's instructions and information are not shared with the objecting member.
8. A policy and an opinion letter are cited together as the end of the matter.
9. A request for documents is answered by a claim of privilege or a promise to take advice.
10. A vote passes and no further route for review is offered.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The trustee conflicts policy, the scheme's rules and any terms of reference, with versions and dates.
- Board papers, agendas and minutes you are entitled to hold, including drafts of minutes.
- Your own dated notes of what was said and decided, and a written record of your dissent.
- The conflicts review's terms of reference and membership.
- The expert's engagement letter, instruction, fee arrangement and final opinion, so far as you are entitled to see them.
- Records of who attended and who left before the vote.
- Your written requests for documents and the replies.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Boards legitimately use experienced advisers, sponsors legitimately nominate trustees, and conflicts are often manageable through disclosure. A policy that is followed, an expert who is chosen by a panel, and a vote that goes against you are not this pattern.

The discriminators are independence, visibility and consequence: who chose and paid the expert, whether the instruction is visible, and whether any objection has ever altered an outcome. Genuine oversight welcomes a second opinion.

Concern strengthens when: the adviser is instructed only through the interested party; your request to see the instructions is refused; the working group includes the people whose conduct is under review; your dissent is not recorded as you stated it.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Appointer Test:**
Ask: "Who appointed the people deciding this, and what do they gain or lose?"
Concern rises if: the reviewers answer to the party being reviewed.

**SPOT IT — The Instruction Test:**
Ask: "Who instructed the expert, what were they told, and who pays?"
Concern rises if: nobody will show the instruction, and the paying party is the interested one.

**SPOT IT — The Policy Test:**
Ask: "Does the policy answer the question I asked, or only a narrower one?"
Concern rises if: the policy is cited as proof without addressing the substance.

**SPOT IT — The Consequence Test:**
Ask: "If I had been right, would anything have changed?"
Concern rises if: no route to an independent hearing exists, and everyone knows it.

## Chain Breakers

- Ask in writing for the terms of reference, the expert's instruction letter and the fee arrangement, and note the reply.
- Ask that your dissent and its reasons be recorded in the minutes in your own words, and check the draft.
- Request that the conflicted individuals step out of the review and the vote, and ask the chair to record the response.
- Ask for a second opinion from an adviser instructed independently of the company, such as through the trustees alone.
- Take your own legal advice on your duties as a trustee before the next vote, rather than relying only on the board's adviser.
- Keep your notes factual and dated. Do not circulate accusations by email or in group channels.
- Speak to the members you represent only within what confidentiality allows, and take advice on what that is.
- Do not take or copy papers you are not entitled to hold, and do not access systems you are not authorised to use.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A conflict is disclosed only in passing; the review is led by aligned members; your questions about instruction or fees are deferred.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The expert's independence is in doubt; your dissent is not recorded; a material decision proceeds on a conflicted vote; you face pressure about your job after raising it. Consider your own qualified pensions or governance attorney, an independent or professional trustee, the scheme's independent member representatives where they exist, an audit or compliance function, a pensions or financial regulator, or a whistleblower channel.

**HIGH → URGENT:** You receive threats or intimidation, there are signs of fraud in progress, or anyone mentions self-harm. Contact emergency services or the relevant authority, keep your evidence, and do not confront anyone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before we vote, could we see who instructed the expert and what information they were given?"
- "I'd like my dissent and my reasons recorded in the minutes."
- "Could the trustees with an interest in the manager step out of this review and the vote?"
- "I'm not questioning the policy. I'm asking whether it answers this question."
- "I'd like a second opinion from an adviser instructed by the trustees alone."
- "I'm taking my own advice on my duties before the next vote. Could the vote be deferred until then?"

## Reflection

If the only people who can say the question was answered are the people who were asked about themselves, who is the question answered for?
