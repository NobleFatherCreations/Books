# C016: The Dependency Web — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that you cannot manage money, news, friendships or your own sense of self without the system around you, and a need to keep giving to it so you can stay inside.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A pull toward groups that offer to handle everything for you, and a sense of relief when someone else holds the details.
- A habit of checking with others before you trust anything you have read or heard on your own.
- An automatic thought, "I wouldn't know where to begin out there," whenever the idea of standing alone comes up.

**A point in a life.** The first year of founding a company.

**Where it happens.** A small import business in a new country.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're thirty-three, a founder nine months into a country you chose and barely know. You import specialty ceramics from small workshops back home and sell them to restaurants and hotels here. You speak the language well enough to order lunch and badly enough to be careful with a contract. A bowl from the first workshop rode in your carry-on, and it sits on your desk, glazed the green of river stones.

**The first months, the advisor's office**

You did not arrive alone. A former colleague of your father's introduced you to an advisor before you boarded the plane, and he met you at the airport with a printed sign and a coffee. Now you sit across his wide desk while he slides things to you one at a time: the keys to a rented office above a stationery shop, the card of a bank officer, the name of a law firm, the papers for your visa sponsorship through a company he says he "has a long relationship with." "Think of me as your local operating system," he says, and you laugh, and you mean it when you say thank you. The lease sits in an entity he recommended, and his fee, a modest monthly retainer plus a participation in "facilitated revenue," runs through the same accounts as payroll. You read the engagement letter once. Everything else is going so well that it seems ungenerous to read it twice.

· · ·

In month five he brings you a document called the Operating Protocol: three pages, with a table titled Signing Authority. Nearly every line for contracts, banking changes, filings and hiring reads "Company, with Advisor concurrence." Payroll already needs two approvers, and the second on file is him. "This is just so we are protected if something happens to you," he says. "It's how a family handles the keys." You sign it at his desk while his assistant waits to file it. Your signature on the page looks smaller than usual.

· · ·

As you stand to leave, he mentions, with concern, that some people in the expatriate group "talk too freely about their business." By the next month two friends from that group have stopped being invited to his dinners. A supplier back home tells you he has asked them to route everything through his office. When you ask the lawyers a question, the reply arrives copied to him. In his office one afternoon you say you are thinking of a second opinion on the retainer, and he goes quiet, and the meeting ends early.

**Month nine, your office above the stationery shop**

The shop below smells of pencil shavings, and the floor hums when its printer runs. You need a customs document for a shipment held at the port. You open the drive he set up, where every lease, licence and customs filing lives. The folder says Access Requested. Three days later the reply is polite: it is being reorganised. You take the Protocol from the drawer and turn to its second table, Drive and Access Permissions, which you signed without reading. Beside most folders your name says Viewer and his says Owner. You sit very still with the bowl in your hands.

That evening he climbs the stairs with two coffees. He tells you about the founder who once left him without notice and nearly broke him. "I'm not saying you would. I'm saying I know you wouldn't," he says. Last week, in front of the bank officer, he called you "my younger brother," and you felt the word in your chest like warmth. You say, of course. It is good to be known as someone who would not.

· · ·

The same week you count the calendar on the wall beside your desk. Your visa renewal is eleven weeks away. Your runway is sixteen. The quarterly transfer falls in the same fortnight, and when you ring the bank, the officer asks whether the advisor has approved it. All three dates pass through him. You write them in red and look at them until they blur.

Through all of it you have been looking after him. He mentions, quietly, that his own business is in a hard season and that you are the engagement he is proudest of, and so on Sunday evenings you sit at your desk and ring him to ask how he is. You listen for an hour. You pay for the dinners. You tell him it will pass. He has not asked about your week in months, and you have stopped offering it.

On one of those Sunday calls you say, carefully, that you would like to talk about the protocol. He pauses. "After everything we've built," he says, "I thought we were past needing a protocol between family." You hear his voice at the desk in month five, the pen in your hand. You apologise before you have hung up.

**Three years later**

It's a grey Monday. The advisor is two years behind you now; a lawyer you found on your own took eight months to untangle the leases and the logins. This morning you are signing up for a founders' collective that promises to "take care of the back office, the banking and the visas, so you can focus on what you love." You feel the old relief before you have finished the form. Somebody else will hold the details. You tick the box that gives them access to everything.

That afternoon a supplier emails a new price list. It is clear and correct, and you forward it to two people and wait for their replies before you believe the numbers. When a news story about import duties scrolls past, you message a friend to ask whether it is true.

On Friday a former colleague suggests you run the business alone again: your own bank, your own lawyer, your own name on every lease. You laugh and say, "I'd need someone to show me where the doors are." It is out before you have thought it.

The bowl is on your new desk. You can run a supplier on another continent and read invoices in three languages, and you no longer believe you can stand in a room by yourself.

## The Name

That's **The Dependency Web**.

## Definition

The Dependency Web is the sequence where one advisor becomes the channel through which a founder's money, paperwork, contacts, information and self-image all pass, using Financial Enmeshment, Financial Dependence by Design, Isolation, Information Control, Identity Hooking, Forced Dependency, Emotional Parasitism, and "We're Family" Erasure to convert a newcomer's need for stability into the loss of independent capacity to operate or leave. It is hard to detect because each piece of help is real and each dependency has a sensible reason, and the structure shows only when the founder tries to move one strand. A single trusted advisor can be a genuine, efficient arrangement; the concern is help arranged so that exit costs more than anyone said.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Financial Enmeshment** — Lease, visa and retainer in one place: an office lease in an entity he recommended, a visa sponsored through a company he has "a long relationship with," and a retainer running through the same flows as payroll. This functions by blurring where the company ends and the advisor begins, so separating costs time and money nobody priced. Concern rises when you cannot draw who holds which contract and account without asking him.

2. **Financial Dependence by Design** — An Operating Protocol with a Signing Authority table: nearly every line for contracts, banking, filings and hiring reads "Company, with Advisor concurrence," and payroll's second approver is him. This functions by tying the founder's autonomy to staying in his good standing. Concern rises when the approval structure arrived after the relationship began, was framed as protection, and has no end date or reversal process.

3. **Isolation** — Other expatriates described as people who "talk too freely about their business," suppliers routed through his office, lawyers' replies copied to him, and a second opinion on the retainer met with silence. This functions by removing the reference points a founder would use to test whether the arrangement is typical. Concern rises when outside input is discouraged in advance.

4. **Information Control** — The drive he owns: your request for a login meets "set up properly so nothing breaks," a later request meets "reorganised," and the access table shows you as Viewer and him as Owner. This functions by putting the founder's own records one step away, so every question needs his answer. Concern rises when you cannot find your own lease or visa papers without his help.

5. **Identity Hooking** — "My younger brother" in front of the bank officer, the founder who left him, and "I'm not saying you would. I'm saying I know you wouldn't." This functions by attaching self-image to staying, so asking for documents feels out of character. Concern rises when you rehearse reassurances of loyalty before an ordinary commercial question.

6. **Forced Dependency** — Visa renewal in eleven weeks, runway of sixteen and the quarterly transfer all waiting on his approval. This functions by making the cost of leaving concrete and near. Concern rises when every deadline on your calendar points back to one person.

7. **Emotional Parasitism** — His hard season and the engagement he is proudest of, while you call him on Sundays, listen for an hour, pay for dinners and tell him it will pass. This functions by making the founder responsible for the advisor's feelings, so an exit becomes a harm to a friend. Concern rises when his hardship appears just as you raise a boundary or fee question.

8. **"We're Family" Erasure** — "It's how a family handles the keys" at signing, then "I thought we were past needing a protocol between family" when you ask to talk about it. This functions by erasing the role, fee and contract boundaries that protect both sides while the signed obligations stay in force. Concern rises when family appears exactly when you ask for terms, documents or time.

## Why This Order

**Financial Enmeshment** comes first because it has to be built while trust is high and scrutiny is low. In the first months nobody asks who holds the lease or whose entity sponsors the visa, and enmeshment looks like help. It creates the substrate for everything after: money and paper in the same place. It has to come before the Operating Protocol, because a signing table only looks like tidiness once the lease, the visa and his fee already run through his accounts.

> Picture a friend who sets up your phone plan, your bank app and your flat share all under their own name "to keep it simple." In the first weeks it is a relief. Flip it: if your friend handed you a page of rules to sign before setting up a single account, you would ask to take it home and read it first.

**Financial Dependence by Design** formalises it. An Operating Protocol reads as tidiness, and its timing matters: it arrives after the founder is operationally reliant, so signing is easier than refusing. It has to come before the warning about the expatriate group, because that warning only lands once you have signed papers you would rather not have discussed by people who talk freely.

> You have lived for months in a house where your housemate pays the bills. When they hand you a tidy new house-rules sheet, signing seems easier than starting the conversation. Flip it: in your first week, if your housemate said, "Best not tell your friends about our arrangements," you would ask why and keep talking to your friends.

**Isolation** then shields the structure from comparison. A founder abroad has few reference points, and discouraging the expatriate group or a second lawyer removes the people who could price the arrangement. It has to come before the locked drive, because the Viewer and Owner columns only go unnoticed once the friends and the second lawyer who might have asked about them are gone.

> A friend says your old college mates "gossip too much." Within a year you can't tell what an ordinary arrangement looks like. Flip it: if your friend kept your important papers out of your reach from the start, you would ask your college mates whether that seemed normal.

**Information Control** is its technical counterpart: without the drive, the lease and the filings, outside advice has nothing to work with. It has to come before the coffee and the story of the founder who left, because "I know you wouldn't" lands on you once you cannot even see your own customs papers.

> Imagine asking a friend to look over your house papers, when the papers sit in a cupboard that only the landlord has the key to. Flip it: when the landlord says, "You're far too easygoing to need copies," before anything is locked away, you would smile and ask for copies anyway.

**Identity Hooking** is the conversion point. Everything before it is structural and could be unwound by a determined person. This step changes the question from "is this good for me?" to "what kind of person questions it?" It has to come before the three dates on the calendar, because those dates only trap you once asking anyone else for help has come to feel like being someone who would leave.

> Someone tells you, warmly, "You're not the sort to go looking for a better deal." Asking a question now feels like becoming a different person. Flip it: if everything you needed this month had to pass through that one person's hands first, you would find a second person to ask and compare deals yourself.

**Forced Dependency** then arrives as a coincidence of calendar. It can be coincidence; intent would require additional evidence. It has to come before the Sunday calls, because looking after him feels like the only safe thing for you once the visa, the runway and the transfer all pass through him.

> Three bills fall due on the same Monday, and each one needs your housemate's signature. Nothing is wrong, and it is the kind of week where you cannot manage alone. Flip it: picture your housemate leaning on you about their worries every night before any bill arrives; you would gently say you need some evenings for yourself.

**Emotional Parasitism** makes leaving personal. It has to come before the word family closes the protocol talk, because "between family" only silences you after months of Sunday hours spent on his hard season.

> A friend's hard year takes up every Sunday call. By the time you think of leaving, you feel you are abandoning someone. Flip it: if your friend called you "practically a sister" on the very first Sunday call, you would notice the label and still say when a call runs too long.

**"We're Family" Erasure** supplies deniability: the founder who asks to review the protocol meets wounded surprise, while the signed paper stays in force. It has to come last, because "between family" only works on you once the money, the papers, the friends and the Sunday calls already make a question feel like betrayal.

> A cousin says, "Do we really need a written agreement? We're family." Asking for one is now a small insult. Flip it: if your cousin brushed off the written agreement first and only later leaned on you through a hard year, you would still ask for the agreement in writing.

What weakens it: holding your own keys, naming your own counsel, keeping peers outside his circle, and asking in month one who owns which contract.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A founder working abroad needs someone local for almost everything: a lease, a bank account, a visa sponsor, a lawyer. Facilitators who supply that help are often paid a share of the revenue they make possible, so the engagement is worth most to them while it lasts. And foreign founders tend to know one small community, where everyone's business travels fast.

Financial Enmeshment comes first because help has to start before anything else can. In the first month nobody asks whose entity holds the lease or sponsors the visa; the facilitator's own channels are simply the fastest ones, and his fee runs where the money already runs.

Financial Dependence by Design comes second because a facilitator paid on revenue needs controls that protect that revenue. A signing table with his concurrence on every line reads as good governance, and it arrives once the founder already works through his accounts.

Isolation comes third because other people are the only outside measure. Expatriate groups compare fees, lawyers give second opinions, suppliers talk. Anyone in a position to compare is, from the facilitator's side, a risk to the engagement, and warnings about gossip are the ordinary way small communities sort themselves.

Information Control comes fourth because whoever sets up the shared drive sets the permissions. Owner and Viewer are defaults, chosen once and rarely revisited, and without the documents no outside adviser has anything to work with.

Identity Hooking comes fifth because a relationship this close needs a story about the people in it. Loyalty is the founder's virtue in that story, and it is offered as a compliment, in front of the bank and over coffee.

Forced Dependency comes sixth because immigration, cash and banking all run on fixed calendars. When one person sits at the centre of all three, their dates eventually line up, and for a few weeks nothing can move without him.

Emotional Parasitism comes seventh because a facilitator's business has its own hard seasons, and the founder is the person he talks to most. Care flows to whoever seems to need it, and the founder is too busy to notice that it only flows one way.

"We're Family" Erasure comes last because family language is how the relationship has always described itself. When a written question finally arrives, the most available answer is the oldest one.

Put it together and the order is the order in which a helper's role grows when nobody draws its edges: money, paper, people, files, self, calendar, care, and finally a word that dissolves the rest. It turns a founder who crossed the world alone into someone who checks the drive at dawn.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A facilitator's fee model, a visa calendar and a small expatriate community did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a freelance consultant. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Leah Moreau moved across a border to freelance, and a practice manager, Anton Sule, began placing her with clients. He arranged her flat through his property company and set up her invoicing through his firm's accounts. A few months in, he gave her a services agreement: every client contract and payment needed his co-signature. He warned her off the local freelancers' association, saying its members poached each other's clients, and she stopped going. Her client files sat on his system, where she could view them but not download them. He introduced her to clients as "the most loyal consultant I've ever worked with" and told her she was not the type to chase a better rate. In one month her work permit renewal, her rent and her largest invoice all came due, and each needed his signature. Most evenings he rang her to talk through his own difficult year, and she listened for an hour. When she finally asked for a written breakdown of his fees, he sounded hurt: "Since when do we need paperwork? We're family." Much later she saw a note he had written about his consultants, with her name at the top under the words "no flight risk."

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The founder may begin to run small decisions past the advisor even when no approval is needed. Over time, the person may lose track of which documents they have seen and which they have only been told about, and put their thin local knowledge down to inexperience rather than routing. They may stop mentioning friends to him and him to friends, rehearse a conversation about the protocol many times without having it, and hesitate to tell family back home, because they came to show it could be done.

## Institutional Echo

Company formation, banking introductions, visa sponsorship, legal referral and document hosting are sometimes bundled through one intermediary, which can be efficient and legitimate. The same bundle can leave no one but the intermediary holding the full picture. Signing authority lists requiring a third party's concurrence can function as risk control or as a lever, depending on who chose them, with what information, and whether they can be reversed. Obligations attached to a sponsor, lease holder or introducer depend on immigration, corporate and contract rules that vary by place. Most often an overstretched advisor builds a convenient system without thinking about the founder's exit. Intent would require additional evidence: whether document requests are resisted, whether other clients describe the same structure, and whether his economics depend on the client staying.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good relationship needs no paper, and a newcomer should be grateful to whoever opens doors. Hospitality toward strangers is a sacred duty in many cultures, and loyalty is its return. Both are honest, which is why questioning the structure feels disrespectful. The language that makes it sound noble is family, honour and protection. The public story, a seasoned mentor welcoming a young entrepreneur, can be completely true. The question is whether the founder can also leave on ordinary terms.

## Observable Indicators

1. One person holds or must approve banking, legal, visa, lease and document access at once.
2. The signing authority list requires the advisor's concurrence for ordinary actions, with no end date.
3. The founder is a viewer, not an owner, of the company's own shared drive.
4. Document or access requests meet delay, "reorganisation" or "setting it up properly."
5. Suppliers, lawyers or contacts are asked to route communication through the advisor's office.
6. Outside advisors or peers are described in advance as indiscreet, biased or unnecessary.
7. Family language appears when terms, fees, documents or time are requested.
8. The advisor's own hardship or past clients who left are raised when alternatives come up.
9. Visa, banking and runway deadlines converge on dates he controls or influences.
10. A request to review the protocol is met with hurt or withdrawal rather than a revision process.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every version of the Operating Protocol, signing authority table and drive permissions table, with dates received and signed.
- A screenshot or export of drive permissions (Owner, Editor, Viewer) and a log of your access requests and replies.
- A one-page map you draw yourself of which entity holds the lease, bank relationship, visa sponsorship and each contract.
- Visa sponsorship papers you lawfully hold and the dated renewal timeline.
- Messages using family, honour or past clients in connection with a request for documents, terms or time.
- Records of redirected supplier or lawyer communication, and names of other founders who used the same advisor.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

One advisor handling banking contacts, legal referral, visa sponsorship and documents is common for newcomers and often the best option. The discriminator is **reversibility and transparency considered together**: a genuine integrated service can be explained on one page, lets the client see and export their own records, names how each function would be handed over, and welcomes outside review.

Benign lookalikes:

- **Real security practice.** Dual approvals and restricted administrator rights can protect a small company.
- **Genuine hospitality.** Warm family language can be sincere.
- **Legal constraints.** Sponsorship, lease and licensing structures sometimes require a local entity or named party.
- **Early-stage dependence.** A founder with no local knowledge is dependent at first; that is what the engagement is for.

Concern strengthens when: the founder cannot obtain their own records on request; questions about the protocol are met with family language or hurt instead of documents; the advisor's story of a past client who left arrives when you raise alternatives.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The One-Page Map Test:**
Ask: "Can I draw, on one page, who holds my lease, bank access, visa sponsorship, contracts and drive?"
Concern rises if: the answer is mostly one person and I cannot finish the page without asking him.

**SPOT IT — The Owner Test:**
Ask: "Am I an owner of my company's own records, and could I export them tonight?"
Concern rises if: no, and a request to change that meets delay or a reason it is not the right time.

**SPOT IT — The Second Opinion Test:**
Ask: "What happens if I say my own lawyer will read this protocol?"
Concern rises if: the response is hurt, a story about someone who left, or a suggestion outsiders will not understand.

**SPOT IT — The Family Word Test:**
Ask: "When does the word family come up, and what is it attached to?"
Concern rises if: it arrives with each request for terms, documents or time.

## Chain Breakers

- **Draw the one-page map now and update it quarterly.** An hour's work that turns a feeling into a fact.
- **Hold your own keys.** Ask to be owner or administrator of your company's drive, bank portal and licence accounts, with the advisor as an additional user; if not possible, ask for the reason in writing.
- **Have independent counsel read the signing authority table before you sign it**.
- **Ask for a reversal clause:** how and when signing authority returns to the company alone.
- **Keep two or three relationships independent of the advisor,** such as a founder community or former colleague.
- **Price your exit while not trying to leave.** Write down what moving the bank, lease and visa sponsor would cost, and update it.
- **Separate warmth from paperwork:** "I appreciate everything. Separately, I would like copies of the lease and sponsorship papers for my files." Do not enter any account you are not authorised to use.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Document or drive-access requests are delayed or answered only verbally; family language arrives when you ask for terms; you cannot complete the one-page map without him.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** You cannot act for the company without his concurrence and cannot obtain a reversal; records you need for a renewal or filing are withheld; fees cannot be reconciled with deliverables. Relevant routes may include an attorney with corporate, contract and immigration experience, an independent accountant, a professional licensing body where a recommended professional holds a licence, and a small-business protection authority.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Anyone connected threatens your visa status, safety or family; passports, funds or documents are withheld to compel you to stay; blackmail, intimidation or self-harm statements appear. Contact local emergency services, your embassy or consulate where relevant, crisis support and qualified counsel. Do not confront the person, do not negotiate under threat, and do not delete evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "I would like to be an owner of our shared drive. What is the process?"
- "Could you send a one-page summary of who holds the lease, bank relationship and visa sponsorship?"
- "I would like my own lawyer to read the Operating Protocol before we go further."
- "I value what you have done. Can we add how signing authority returns to the company?"
- "I am not saying anything is wrong. I would like copies of my own documents."
- "Can we agree a review date for the protocol?"
