# C095: The Proxy War — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that conflict comes at you sideways through people you care about, and a reflex to give in before anyone near you pays for a disagreement they had no part in.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of avoiding any disagreement with someone powerful, fearing a friend or colleague will feel the cost.
- A pull to trust a go-between's account of a conflict over your own talk with the other person.
- An automatic thought, "nobody ever said it was about me, but it always is," when routine changes keep landing on you.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, running a company you built.

**Where it happens.** A venture-backed technology company's boardroom.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a co-founder and chief technology officer, forty, and for six years the company has been, in a very real sense, the thing you built. You wrote the first version of the platform. You hired the first twelve engineers. You and the chair of the board had a good relationship once, back when she led the seed round and called you on Sundays to ask how you were holding up.

The disagreement was about hiring. You wanted to keep a small senior team through the next year, and she wanted to add a layer of management and bring in a new head of engineering. You each said your piece in a board meeting, civilly. She said she would think about it. You thought that was the end of it.

**March, your office**

You hear about the next move from the company's outside counsel. He calls on a grey Tuesday morning, courteous and a little apologetic, to say the board would like to tidy up some governance. He asks whether you would be open to an updated shareholder consent process. It sounds routine. You say you will read whatever he sends, and you do, and it contains nothing you can object to.

That same week an investor you barely know sends you a warm note: "Heard you're stretched. Anything I can do to take weight off?" You thank him. It is the first time he has ever written to you.

At Monday's check-in the chief of staff, sitting on the edge of your sofa with a laptop on her knees, mentions that the chair has asked for a "risk lens" on every function. The finance lead, she says, is only tidying slide formatting. A leadership coach starts next week, on a programme the board has funded for the whole founding team. Each errand is small, and each is reasonable. You say it all sounds sensible, because it does.

**Wednesday evening, your office**

The board deck for the quarter arrives while the cleaners are vacuuming the corridor outside. Your engineering section has been restructured since the draft you approved. The slide on velocity now leads with a chart that starts at a different baseline. On the org slide, the engineer you hired first, the one who built the payments core, no longer has a box. Her name sits in a footnote: "role under review." You message the finance lead to ask who edited the deck, and the reply says they were "some formatting consistency changes." You call the engineer. HR rang her at five, she says, and her voice is very even. You wrote none of it, and she is the one who paid.

**Three weeks later, your office**

The investor mentions, lightly, on a call, that he has "heard concerns about engineering throughput." You ask from whom. He couldn't say. You ask counsel again who wanted the governance change, and he says, "It's coming from the board generally." After her first session the coach, sitting across your desk with a closed notebook, tells you what she is "hearing from people" about you, and does not say who.

Then the 360 report comes back. The summary says your colleagues see you as brilliant but hard to scale, and it uses the phrase key-person dependency twice. It is the same phrase from the deck. You notice. You wonder, briefly, whether you are being paranoid.

You call the chair. For the first time in six months she picks up. She is kind. "I wasn't involved in the coaching," she says. "And I haven't seen the 360. I'd never interfere with that. Honestly, I think you're doing a heroic job." You thank her. The call is two minutes. You put the phone down feeling worse than before it rang. Counting them up that evening, you have been spoken to by counsel, an investor, a coach, the finance lead and the chief of staff. None of them is attacking you. Each has said something reasonable. Each has used a phrase you have heard before. Then the minute of the last board session arrives: the chair was not in the room for the item on engineering leadership; she recused herself, to avoid, as the minute says, "any perception of a conflict."

**Eight months later, your kitchen**

Eight months later you are awake at five in your kitchen, in a different house, at a different company you advise, reading an email from a new board member that begins, "Quick thought on the roadmap, happy to chat." You answer yes before you have read the second line. In your head you have already conceded whatever you imagine it will ask for. At midnight the engineer who built the payments core texted to say she has taken a job elsewhere and there are no hard feelings. You were relieved, and now you are ashamed of how relieved.

Your new head of finance says she heard from a recruiter that the board member is unhappy with you. You do not call him. You take her account, softened, and you rewrite your own plan for the quarter around it. You have not argued with anyone who holds a vote in eight months. When you catch yourself apologising to your own phone, you set it face down.

On Sunday evening you open the first version of the platform, the code you wrote alone, and read it for a while, the way you used to. It still runs. You cannot remember the last time you wanted something and said so.

## The Name

That's **The Proxy War**.

## Definition

The Proxy War is the sequence where a conflict is conducted entirely through third parties, using Flying Monkeys, Proxy Recruitment, Punishment by Proxy, Triangulation, Smear Campaigns, and Plausible Deniability to convert trust that a third party's account of a conflict is neutral into the loss of any direct resolution unmediated by someone with their own agenda. It may benefit a person who wants an outcome without owning the confrontation, since each step can be attributed to someone else, to process, or to routine. It exploits the target's reasonable assumption that counsel, investors, coaches, and colleagues are acting for their own stated reasons. It is hard to detect because every change can be explained as ordinary governance. The target may lose a direct line to the person they disagree with, their standing with the people they depend on, and the ability to settle the matter plainly. Coordination would require additional evidence; many boards and companies change at once for unconnected reasons.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Flying Monkeys** — Messengers carry what the chair does not say herself: outside counsel asks to "tidy up some governance," and an investor you barely know writes "Heard you're stretched. Anything I can do to take weight off?" This functions by delivering pressure through people the target has reason to trust. Concern rises when several intermediaries raise the same subject within weeks, and each says it comes from "the board generally."

2. **Proxy Recruitment** — Small separate errands: the chief of staff adds a "risk lens" the chair asked for on every function, the finance lead only tidies slide formatting, and a coach is brought in on a board-funded programme. This functions by drawing people into the conflict without telling them it is one. Concern rises when intermediaries appear unaware of each other and each acts on a narrow instruction.

3. **Punishment by Proxy** — Harm lands on someone you care about through the paperwork: in the restructured deck the engineer you hired first has no box, only a footnote reading "role under review," and HR has already rung her. This functions by attaching consequences to routine processes rather than to a decision. Concern rises when consequences reach valued relationships or roles and the explanation is always "process."

4. **Triangulation** — Messages come through others: the investor "heard concerns about engineering throughput" from nobody he can name, counsel says "It's coming from the board generally," and the coach says what she is "hearing from people." This functions by creating a version of the conflict in which the target must answer to people who were not part of it. Concern rises when the target is repeatedly told what others think rather than being told directly.

5. **Smear Campaigns** — Phrases about your relevance, not your skill: the 360 summary says "brilliant but hard to scale" and uses "key-person dependency" twice, the same words as the deck. This functions by recasting a founder's contribution as a risk to be managed. Concern rises when the same phrase appears across the deck, the coach's report, and informal remarks.

6. **Plausible Deniability** — A kind call, a recusal, a minute: the chair says "I wasn't involved in the coaching" and "I think you're doing a heroic job," then steps out of the engineering item "to avoid any perception of a conflict," and the minute has no sentence she wrote. This functions by ensuring that the person who benefits cannot be tied to any single act. Concern rises when the direct conversation is always warm and brief, and nothing in writing connects the chair to the changes.

## Why This Order

**Flying Monkeys** come first because the chair needs messengers before she needs allies. A request that comes through counsel is not a challenge; it is governance. The messenger's neutrality is the asset, and the target has no reason to doubt it. It has to come before the investor's note and the coach, because those errands only look like ordinary business once counsel's polite call has made the whole matter sound like governance.

> Your landlord never calls. A friend of the agent drops by to say, "Just so you know, they're thinking of tidying up the lease." Flip it: an agent you do not know asks a friend of yours for one small favour about the lease, and you phone the landlord yourself to ask what it is for.

**Proxy Recruitment** prepares the field. Each recruit is given a narrow, defensible task, which means none has to believe they are in a conflict. This is also what makes the pattern difficult to see from inside: the investor, the finance lead, and the coach may each be acting in good faith. It has to come before the edited deck, because the finance lead, the chief of staff and the coach must already be in place for the engineer's lost box to appear from no one in particular.

> Each person is asked for one small favour: check the spelling, print the sheet, mention it at lunch. None of them thinks they are part of anything. Flip it: the day after you disagree with someone, one of the people at your lunch table finds their hours quietly cut, and you ask them straight away who decided it.

**Punishment by Proxy** comes next, because by now the structures are in place. A restructured slide, a framing in a report, a recusal from a vote, each is an effect without an author. This raises the target's exit cost, since the consequences touch the company they built and the colleagues who respect them. It has to come before the answers that leave out who spoke, because you only start chasing the source once the engineer's changed position has shown you that something is wrong.

> After you quarrel with your sister, her husband's hours at the family business are quietly cut. Nobody says the two things are connected. Flip it: after you quarrel with your sister, a friend tells you she heard from a cousin that her husband is upset with you, and you phone your sister herself to ask.

**Triangulation** weakens independent evaluation. Once the target is hearing about the conflict from others, they must reconstruct it from rumour and tone, which takes time and energy. It has to come before the 360 summary, because you only meet its borrowed phrase as a verdict after weeks of failing to find out who is speaking.

> "I've heard you're a bit much" comes from a friend, who got it from a cousin, who "wouldn't say who." You spend weeks rebuilding the chain. Flip it: the phrase "a bit much" is already passing from mouth to mouth about you, and when a friend repeats it to you, you ask who she heard it from and then ask that person.

**Smear Campaigns** then supply the vocabulary: a phrase that survives in many mouths and gradually becomes the room's settled view of the founder. It has to come before the chair's kind call, because the call only reassures you once a settled phrase about you sits in a report that she says she has not seen.

> "Great at the job, but a bit old-school" is repeated at coffee, in emails and in a review. Soon people use it instead of your name. Flip it: a colleague at coffee says kindly, "I'd never say a word about you," and you ask her straight out whether anyone has been saying anything in emails or in your review.

**Plausible Deniability** closes the loop. The kind call, the recusal, and the clean minute ensure that if the target raises the matter directly, the response is warm confusion. The key conversion point is that kind call: it feels like reassurance, and it takes away the one direct line that might have resolved things. It has to come last, because the kind call and the clean minute only close the matter once the deck, the report and the people carrying them exist for her to deny.

> A kind phone call, a polite "I'd never interfere," a meeting note with nobody's name in it. If you complain, you get only warm confusion. Flip it: first you get the kind call, the polite "I'd never interfere" and the note with nobody's name, and only then do you start hearing that colleagues call you hard to work with; you go back to the caller and ask where it began.

What would weaken the sequence: a written, direct request to the chair; asking each intermediary who requested their involvement; and a neutral person, such as an independent director, who can bring the parties into a single conversation.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A venture-backed company is built from structures that nobody sits at the centre of. A board chair holds authority over leadership but has no operational hands, so she works through counsel, finance, HR and advisers. Every quarter produces a deck, a review and a minute, and each of those has an editor and a deadline. Governance work is dull and delegated, so a request that arrives through it looks like procedure. And board minutes record who recused themselves, because that is what keeps a record clean. Those facts are enough to produce what happened to you.

Flying Monkeys come first because a chair cannot call a founder with every question, and a request from outside counsel carries no personal weight. The messenger's neutrality is the asset, and its cost is that you have no reason to doubt it.

Proxy Recruitment comes second because the work needs many hands: an investor with a welcome note, a finance lead with a formatting job, a coach on a funded programme. Each is given a narrow task and none needs to know the larger shape. That is why every one of them can say, truthfully, that they did nothing wrong.

Punishment by Proxy comes third because by now the structures are in place and decisions can be made inside them. A restructured slide or a role under review is an effect with no author, and it lands on the colleague closest to the founder, which is where it hurts most.

Triangulation comes fourth because once effects exist, questions follow, and the answers arrive through intermediaries who each pass them on. Hearing concerns from several sources, none of whom will name a source, turns the founder into a researcher of their own case.

Smear Campaigns come fifth because the same sources now need a shared vocabulary. A phrase in a deck can reappear in a review, because the same people read both. It becomes the room's settled view of you without anyone having said it to your face.

Plausible Deniability comes last because the chair's own cleanliness depends on it. A kind call and a recusal are exactly what a careful person does anyway, and they leave a record in which nobody is responsible.

Put it together and the order is the order this setting needs: a messenger, many small hands, an effect, a hunt for the source, a phrase, and a clean record. What it turns you into is a founder who stops calling the one person who might explain it.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A quarterly board cycle, a chain of delegated tasks and a tidy minute did most of it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a colleague. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Tomasz Morwick ran the sales team at a logistics software firm and had disagreed, politely, with the chief commercial officer, Constance Idowu, about splitting his territory. The next day the firm's outside lawyer rang to say the board would like to tidy up the sales compensation policy, and asked whether he would mind a new sign-off step. A recruiter he had never met wrote that he looked stretched and offered to take weight off. Over the following days his chief of staff asked for a risk lens on his pipeline, an analyst began re-running his forecasts, and a coach arrived for the team, paid from Constance's budget. A week later his best account manager, Amira, was taken off the flagship client, her role marked under review. When Tomasz asked who had decided, the analyst said it was formatting. He heard from two people that there were concerns about forecast quality, and neither could say whose. The coach's report called him strong but hard to scale, the exact phrase from the analyst's slides. He called Constance. She picked up at once, said she would never interfere, and told him he was doing a wonderful job. The minute of the next meeting recorded only that the matter had been discussed.

Constance was never confused about what she was doing. She told a friend that the best arguments are the ones you win without ever being in the room.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The target may begin to question their own judgement, wondering whether each incident is real or imagined. Over time, the person may become reluctant to trust any colleague who is also in touch with the chair, which narrows their support network.

They may also grow reactive, treating every routine email as a signal, and others may read that as the instability the phrase described. They may lose sleep, rehearse a confrontation they cannot hold, and feel acutely aware that the person they most want to talk to is the one who will not be in the room.

## Institutional Echo

Boards, investor networks, and leadership development programmes can serve as channels for conflict without anyone intending it. Board materials are often edited by several hands, and framing choices in a deck can shape discussion. Coaching and 360 processes depend on who briefs the coach and who selects the respondents. A recusal can be a genuine conflict-management step and can also remove a person from the record of a decision.

Investor intermediaries may carry concerns that are their own. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the coach was briefed on the board's view, who proposed each deck change, and whether the same phrase can be traced to a single source.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that serious people don't have conflicts; they have processes. A mature leader is expected to let others handle friction, to avoid "making it personal," and to trust that structures are neutral. The vocabulary is "governance," "professionalism," "scalability."

The value being exploited is genuine: boards do need processes, and conflicts of interest do need recusals. The public story is a well-run company that managed a transition with care. The private cost is a founder who learned of their situation from five voices, none of which was the one that mattered.

## Observable Indicators

1. Several intermediaries raise the same topic within a short period, each citing "the board generally."
2. A change to your materials is made by someone other than the author, without notice.
3. The same distinctive phrase appears in a deck, a coaching report, and informal remarks.
4. Requests that would normally come from the chair arrive via counsel, an investor, or staff.
5. The chair is warm in direct contact and never addresses the substance.
6. A coach or consultant appears to have been briefed on the conflict's themes.
7. Recusals or absences remove one participant from items concerning you.
8. You are repeatedly told what others think, without a direct statement by any of them.
9. Explanations for changes are always "formatting," "consistency," or "process."
10. Your own requests for a direct meeting are postponed or redirected.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Each version of the board deck, with metadata or timestamps showing who edited what, within what you are lawfully authorised to see.
- The 360 report and the list of respondents, plus any brief given to the coach, if you are entitled to it.
- Notes of each intermediary conversation: date, who said what, and who they said asked.
- Governance proposals from counsel, such as consent process drafts, and the request that triggered them.
- Board minutes you are entitled to hold, including recusal entries.
- A running list of repeated phrases and where each was first seen.
- Written requests you made for a direct meeting with the chair and the responses.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Boards do act through counsel, investors do give feedback, and coaches do produce reports that echo what the board has said, because boards often fund the programme and describe their concerns to the coach. A recusal may be exactly right.

Benign lookalikes specific to this pattern:

- **Routine governance work.** Consent processes and board materials are updated regularly.
- **Shared diagnosis.** Different people can independently arrive at "key-person risk," which is a common concept in start-ups.
- **Coaching briefs.** A coach is often briefed by a sponsor without ill intent.
- **Chair's restraint.** Staying out of a process can be a sincere attempt to avoid interference.

Concern strengthens when: the same phrase and change requests trace back to one person; direct conversations about substance are always avoided; and intermediaries each say they were asked to raise the matter.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Messenger Test:**
Ask: "Who asked you to raise this with me?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "the board generally" or nobody can say.

**SPOT IT — The Phrase Test:**
Ask: "Where else have I heard this exact wording?"
Concern rises if: it appears in a deck, a report, and a conversation, all tracing back to one person.

**SPOT IT — The Edit Test:**
Ask: "Who changed my section, and who requested it?"
Concern rises if: the editor points elsewhere and the request has no author.

**SPOT IT — The Direct Line Test:**
Ask: "Can I put the issue to the person it concerns, in writing?"
Concern rises if: the reply is warm, brief, and never addresses substance.

## Chain Breakers

- **Request the conversation in writing.** "I'd like thirty minutes with you on the engineering plan, with an independent director present if you prefer."
- **Ask intermediaries one neutral question.** "Who suggested this?" Record the answer without arguing.
- **Lock your own materials.** Ask that changes to your sections be tracked and approved by you before circulation.
- **Brief the coach yourself.** Ask what the coach was told, who selected respondents, and how the report will be used.
- **Bring in an independent director or lead director.** Describe the pattern with dates and ask for a joint conversation, not a verdict.
- **Avoid recruiting your own proxies.** Do not respond in kind through investors or staff; it hands the other side a narrative.
- **Seek independent legal advice about your position as a founder.** Shareholder rights and duties are jurisdiction-specific.
- **Look after yourself.** A founder peer group or therapist can provide a reality check apart from the boardroom.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Two or more intermediaries raise the same subject; materials you authored are changed without notice; or a report echoes a phrase from the board.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The changes affect your role, compensation, voting rights, or authority; your written request for a direct meeting is repeatedly declined; or the process appears designed to produce a result before you can respond. Routes include an attorney with corporate experience, independent board members or a lead director, and mediation if the shareholder agreement provides for it.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, coercion, intimidation, or demands for your signature under pressure that includes threats; any self-harm statement by anyone involved. Prioritise safety, contact local emergency services or crisis lines, and consult qualified counsel. Do not confront anyone. Do not delete evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'd like to discuss this directly with the chair. Could we set up thirty minutes this week?"
- "Who asked you to raise this? I'd like to take it to the right person."
- "Could you send the version history of the engineering section, and the reason for each change?"
- "I'd like to see the respondent list and the brief for the 360 before it's used."
- "I'm happy to have an independent director join the conversation."
- "Could the consent changes be sent in writing so my counsel can review them?"
- "I'd like to be present for the discussion of engineering leadership, or to submit my view in writing."
