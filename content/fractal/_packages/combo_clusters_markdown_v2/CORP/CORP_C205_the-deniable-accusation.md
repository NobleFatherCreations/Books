# C205: The Deniable Accusation — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that a question asked about you can damage you as much as a claim, and a fear that speaking up will bring blame and legal trouble onto you.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- An automatic sentence when you are hurt, "what did I do to bring this on?" before asking what the other person did.
- A habit of staying silent in public about something that happened to you, in case a lawyer's letter follows.
- A wariness of people who say they are "just asking," and of claims that shrink whenever you push back.

**A point in a life.** Retirement, after a long audit career.

**Where it happens.** An industry association's ethics committee.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a volunteer ethics committee chair at an industry association, fifty-four years old, a retired audit partner who spent thirty years being the quiet person in the room who read the footnotes. You took the role because you believe small professional communities stay honest only if someone who knows how things are done will say so. In March, on a panel, you are asked about fee transparency in private funds, and you answer in general terms: that some fee structures are described in ways that make it hard for investors to see what they pay, and that members should ask for a full breakdown before they commit. You mention no firm, and when the moderator asks whether you have a particular fund in mind, you say no. One fund's partners are in the front row, not clapping. The name card from that panel still leans against your desk lamp.

**Nine days later, your study**

Nine days later the association's members' mailing list, eleven hundred people, receives an open letter on the letterhead of the fund's outside counsel. It's polite, even gentle. It doesn't say you were wrong. It asks.

"We note with interest the remarks made by the committee chair. We wonder whether the chair would care to clarify whether she holds, or has held, any financial interest in competing arrangements. We wonder whether her views on fee practice were shaped by her own earlier professional disappointments. We wonder whether a volunteer officer who has made such remarks has considered the effect on the livelihoods of members who rely on the association's good name. We would be glad to hear her answers to these questions."

There are seven questions in all. None of them is a statement. You read them four times looking for a sentence to correct, and there isn't one. By lunchtime a former colleague has replied-all: "Interesting questions. I hadn't thought about that." Your jaw aches, and your sandwich stays in its wrapper.

**The following fortnight, your study**

You write a short reply to the list, careful, factual: you hold no interest in any fund, your remarks were general, and no fund was named. Within the hour a second letter arrives. Counsel thanks you. They clarify that the earlier letter was only an invitation to dialogue, that no one has alleged misconduct, and that the fund simply believes in respectful discussion of industry practice. The sweeping suggestion has gone and a harmless one stands where it was. On Friday a short note from counsel reaches the list, thanking members for their "continued interest" and attaching the first letter again, "for convenience." Your careful reply sits in eleven hundred inboxes, and it looks like the louder thing.

A week later there's a third letter, attached to the agenda for a members' call. It observes that you have, in the past two years, left a position in a large firm and accepted several consulting engagements, and asks whether members should "consider the context in which such a volunteer comes to speak." It asks why you chose a public forum rather than a private channel, why you did not first raise your concerns with the fund, and whether you were aware that others might feel targeted. You read it for the word "fees." It isn't there. The subject of the letter is you.

**That Thursday, your kitchen**

On Thursday a fourth document arrives by courier at your home. It is a demand to cease and desist from "continuing to make or repeat statements that are or may be defamatory," and it requests a written undertaking within fourteen days. It does not specify which statements. It courteously reserves all rights.

You sit at your kitchen table with the pages, and the tea at your elbow goes cold. You leave a message at a solicitor's office and are told somebody will ring on Monday. You read the page until the word "defamatory" stops looking like a word.

**Two months later, your desk**

Two months later you've been asked, politely, whether you still intend to speak at the panel. You've written four replies. In each one you've removed every sentence a stranger could lift out and hold up, and what's left says nothing. At the members' reception the week before, a young auditor asked what you really thought about fee disclosure, and you looked at the ring of faces behind her and said it was a big subject. You catch yourself going back over March, hunting for the sentence that deserved all of this.

You used to read footnotes for a living; now you read your own sentences that way. You wake at four and compose replies to a list you haven't written to. At dinner with your brother you begin to describe your week and finish with "never mind."

The panel is next month. You turn the name card face down on the desk, and then face up again.

## The Name

That's **The Deniable Accusation**.

## Definition

The Deniable Accusation is the sequence where an accusation is delivered as a question so that no one has to own it, a sweeping implication retreats to a harmless claim whenever it is challenged, attention is shifted from the issue to the complainant's choices and character, and the threat of litigation is used against anyone who continues, using Just Asking Questions, Motte-and-Bailey, Victim Blaming and Legal Abuse to convert a belief that fair questions deserve fair answers into lost reputation and the ability to speak publicly. It benefits whoever would prefer a concern not be heard, and it exploits a decent instinct: that a reasonable person answers reasonable questions. It is hard to detect because each step can be described as civility, clarification, context or protecting one's rights. In a small professional community, where reputation travels by mailing list and reference call, it can quickly narrow what a person can safely say.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Just Asking Questions** — An open letter of "we wonder whether" questions: on the fund's outside counsel's letterhead, it goes to eleven hundred members and asks about your financial interests, your "earlier professional disappointments" and the livelihoods of members, with seven questions and no statement. This functions by letting an implication reach many readers while the sender asserts nothing that can be contradicted. Concern rises when the questions are sent to third parties rather than the person, when none is about the substance of the original remark, and when the form requires a reply to a premise.

2. **Motte-and-Bailey** — A second letter retreats after your reply: counsel thanks you, says the earlier letter was only "an invitation to dialogue" and that no one has alleged misconduct, then on Friday attaches the first letter again "for convenience." This functions by pairing an ambitious, damaging claim with a modest, defensible one, so that criticism always lands on the modest one. Concern rises when the sweeping version reappears in other forums after the harmless version has been offered.

3. **Victim Blaming** — A third letter turns to you: attached to the agenda for a members' call, it cites your left position and consulting engagements, and asks why you chose a public forum, why you did not first raise concerns with the fund, and whether you knew others might feel targeted. This functions by changing the subject from the practice to the person who named it, so the complainant is the one being examined. Concern rises when the questions about the speaker outnumber, or replace, any engagement with the substance.

4. **Legal Abuse** — A courier delivers a fourth document to your home: it demands you cease "continuing to make or repeat statements that are or may be defamatory," asks for a written undertaking within fourteen days, names no statement, and "reserves all rights." This functions by using the cost and uncertainty of litigation to make continued speech feel unsafe, whatever the merits. Concern rises when the demand is non-specific, is sent after a measured public response, or is circulated to others.

## Why This Order

**Just Asking Questions** has to come first because it is the only form in which the accusation can be launched without being owned. A statement can be rebutted; a question can only be answered, and answering accepts its premises. The first letter also makes the audience a jury: eleven hundred members now know there are questions. It has to come before the second letter, because only once eleven hundred members have read the questions does your careful reply give counsel something to retreat from.

> Picture a coworker saying in the team chat, "Not saying anyone took the money from the tip jar, but has anyone looked in your bag?" Nobody has accused you of anything. Everyone is now looking at your bag. Flip it: first the coworker tells the team chat you took money from the tip jar, and when you ask, says, "I only said tip jars go missing"; only then does he ask, "Has anyone looked in your bag?"

**Motte-and-Bailey** comes second because the first move will draw a response, and a response needs somewhere to go. When you answer the insinuation, the sender retreats to the motte: no one has alleged anything. The retreat is not a concession; it preserves the original impression while making your answer look like an overreaction. The bailey, the suggestion of motive, stays in the minds of those who read the first letter and not the second. It has to come before the letter about your career and your choice of forum, because the retreat leaves the first letter's insinuation in place, and members are already reading about you, not the fees.

> A neighbour tells the street you leave your bins out to annoy people. You knock, and they say, "I only said bins can be a bother." By the weekend they are telling the street again. Flip it: first you tell the neighbour the street talk is about you, and she asks why you leave the bins out where people pass; only then does she say, "I only said bins can be a bother."

**Victim Blaming** follows because, once the substance has been successfully obscured, the only visible subject is the person. Her career, her timing, her platform: each can be presented as context. The shift is the key conversion point. When readers start discussing why she spoke, the fees are no longer being discussed. It has to come before the demand letter, because once members are discussing why you spoke, a formal document can land on a person who is already being treated as the subject.

> You complain that a shop sold you a faulty kettle, and the reply asks why you bought it online and whether you read the reviews. Somehow you are now talking about your shopping habits and not the kettle. Flip it: first a stiff letter from the shop's lawyer lands on the mat about the kettle, and you ask a solicitor whether it has teeth; only then does a reply ask why you bought it online.

**Legal Abuse** is last because it works on the person who has not yet stopped. Questions, retreats and context are all deniable. A demand letter is the first step that is not, and its power lies largely in the cost of finding out whether it has teeth. It also lends the earlier steps cover, since a party with legal advice can call everything before it measured. It has to come last, because the demand letter relies on the earlier letters having made the insinuation, the retreat and the questions about you, so that its vagueness reads as one more serious step.

> A stiff letter from someone's lawyer lands on the mat. You are sure you did nothing wrong, but you cannot tell whether it has teeth, and you cannot settle until you know. Flip it: first the stiff letter from someone's lawyer lands on the mat and you take it to a solicitor to find out whether it has teeth; only then does a second page ask why you spoke up in the first place.

What weakens the sequence: replying to the substance rather than the premises; asking the sender to state what, specifically, is alleged; taking independent legal advice before further public statements; and laying the versions of each letter side by side.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. It falls out of what an industry association is built to produce. A members' mailing list puts a message in front of eleven hundred people at once, and nobody on it can check the sender's motive. Letterhead from an outside law firm makes anything look serious. And a volunteer officer has no institution behind her, only her own time and savings.

Just Asking Questions comes first because it is the only form in which an insinuation can be launched without being owned. A statement can be rebutted; a question can only be answered, and answering accepts its premises. The first letter also makes the audience a jury: eleven hundred members now know that there are questions.

Motte-and-Bailey comes second because the first move draws a response, and a response needs somewhere to go. When you answer the insinuation, the sender retreats to the safe position: no one has alleged anything. The retreat is not a concession. It keeps the original impression alive while making your answer look like an overreaction, and the suggestion of motive stays in the minds of those who read the first letter and not the second.

Victim Blaming comes third because once the substance has been obscured, the only visible subject left is the person. Her career, her timing and her platform can each be presented as context. When readers start discussing why she spoke, the fees are no longer being discussed.

Legal Abuse comes last because it works on the person who has not yet stopped. Questions, retreats and context are all deniable. A demand letter is the first step that is not, and its power lies largely in the cost of finding out whether it has teeth. It also lends the earlier steps cover, since a party with legal advice can call everything before it measured. A letter like this is the point at which qualified legal advice, not a public reply, is what the situation needs.

Put it together and the order is the order a members' list needs: a question, a retreat, a change of subject, and a formal threat. What it turns the person into is someone who rereads their own sentences for what a stranger could lift out of them, and chooses silence.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A members' mailing list, a law firm's letterhead and a volunteer with no institution behind her are what this setting produces, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a newcomer. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Ottilie Ireby was an associate in her second year when she remarked at a firm-wide forum that some client invoices were hard for anyone to read. The next day an email reached the whole firm, in the name of an outside adviser, putting seven questions about her: whether she had once applied for a role in billing, whether her views had been shaped by her last review, whether she had considered the livelihoods of colleagues who relied on the firm's good name. She replied, briefly and factually. A second note thanked her, said that no one had alleged anything, that the first email had only been an invitation to talk, and attached the first email again, "for convenience." A third asked why she had chosen a public forum, and whether she had first spoken to the department concerned. It did not mention invoices. Then a letter from the firm's solicitors told her to stop repeating statements that "may be defamatory," without naming any. She rang a solicitor of her own, who agreed to see her on Monday.

Silas Brandreth, the head of the department, was never confused about what he was doing. He told a friend that nobody ever lost a good argument by asking a good question.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

The person may find themselves repeatedly rehearsing what they said, searching for the sentence that could be misread. Over time, they may begin to withdraw from forums where they were once comfortable, and to soften remarks they previously made without hesitation. They may spend hours answering questions that may function as unanswerable.

They may feel isolated as colleagues who privately agree become publicly silent. They may take on legal costs and anxiety out of proportion to anything they said, and wonder whether the original concern was worth it. Some may speak less.

## Institutional Echo

Association mailing lists and investor circles can become channels for this pattern without anyone planning it. Open letters from counsel addressed to a membership, rather than the individual, are used as reputation instruments in some disputes. Volunteer officers often have no institutional legal support and no indemnity, while the party sending the letter does. Associations may lack a procedure for handling a member's complaint about another member's conduct that does not pass through a listserv.

Some jurisdictions have protections against litigation aimed at deterring speech on matters of public interest, but these vary widely and need qualified review. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether more than one critic received the same sequence, and whether the questions were ever put to the person directly first.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a good-faith question is always innocent, and that a person with nothing to hide welcomes scrutiny. There is real value in that: open questioning is how honest communities check themselves. The language of dialogue and context sounds noble, which is why it can be borrowed.

The value being exploited is fairness itself: the sense that if someone asks, you owe them an answer. The public story is a respectful exchange of views between professionals. The private story, where the pattern is present, is a person who raised a concern about fees and spent the next months being asked about herself. Nothing here argues against asking hard questions of anyone, including an ethics chair. It argues for noticing who is the subject of the questions, and who is being asked to prove a negative.

## Observable Indicators

1. A letter or message sets out a series of questions about a person's motives, interests or history rather than the substance of their remark.
2. The questions are sent to a membership, mailing list or investor group rather than to the person.
3. No assertion appears that could be directly confirmed or denied.
4. After a factual reply, the sender restates the position as only an invitation to dialogue.
5. The original insinuation reappears in other forums or conversations.
6. Follow-up communications focus on the speaker's career, timing, platform choice or feelings of others.
7. The speaker is asked why they did not raise the matter privately first, without reference to whether they had a safe route to do so.
8. A demand letter does not identify the statements complained of.
9. A short deadline for a written undertaking is combined with a general reservation of rights.
10. Colleagues begin to reply with "interesting" or ask what the context is, without engaging with the substance.
11. An offer to meet privately is conditioned on stopping public remarks.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every version of every letter, with the date and time received, the sender, the recipients on the list and the headers you lawfully hold.
- The exact words of your original remark, from your notes, slides or a transcript or recording you lawfully hold or that the host published.
- Your own replies, as sent, with dates.
- A side-by-side comparison of the sweeping version and the retreating version of each claim.
- Any association policy on complaints, conduct, communications or indemnity for volunteer officers.
- The date and manner of delivery of any legal demand, and the deadline stated in it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Questions about an ethics officer's independence are legitimate, and so is a firm's right to defend its reputation or to seek advice about a statement it believes to be false. A fund can sincerely believe it was unfairly singled out. A letter from counsel is not itself evidence of bad faith. The discriminator is **whether the questions are about the substance, are put to the person, and stop when answered**.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Genuine conflict inquiry.** A disclosure request tied to a specific role or vote, made to the person directly.
- **Fair correction.** A specific, identified statement that is factually wrong, with a request to correct it.
- **Ordinary legal caution.** A measured letter that names a statement and invites correction.

Concern strengthens when: the questions go to a wide audience and not the person; a broad claim returns after a narrow one is offered; the subject moves from the practice to the person; and a demand names no statement.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Question Test:**
Ask: "Is there any statement in this I could confirm or deny, or only questions about me?"
Concern rises if: "Only questions, and they were sent to everyone but me."

**SPOT IT — The Retreat Test:**
Ask: "When I answered, did the claim shrink to something harmless, and did the bigger version later come back?"
Concern rises if: "It shrank when I replied and returned in other rooms."

**SPOT IT — The Subject Test:**
Ask: "Are people now discussing the practice I raised, or why I raised it?"
Concern rises if: "Nobody is discussing the fees any more."

**SPOT IT — The Demand Test:**
Ask: "Does the legal letter say which statements are complained of, and why?"
Concern rises if: "It does not, and it still wants an undertaking within days."

## Chain Breakers

- **Do not answer the premises.** Reply to the substance of your original remark, briefly, once, or reply only through counsel. Each additional answer to an implied question widens its scope.
- **Ask the sender to state what is alleged.** "Could you identify the specific statement and why you consider it inaccurate?" A specific allegation can be addressed; a series of wonderings cannot.
- **Get independent legal advice early,** before any further public statement or any undertaking, from a lawyer with relevant experience in the right jurisdiction.
- **Keep a version log.** A simple dated table of each letter and its claim. The retreat works only if no one lays the versions side by side.
- **Tell a trusted peer, the association's officers or an independent board member** what is happening, so you are not the only holder of the record.
- **Ask what support the association offers** volunteer officers, including any indemnity or insurance.
- **Do not escalate publicly with new accusations.** Public counter-claims can create their own exposure.
- **Do not post private correspondence without advice.**

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A letter or message to a wider audience poses questions about your motives rather than your claim; the claim retreats and returns; colleagues begin to ask about your context.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A demand or threat of proceedings arrives; the letter names no statements; contact is made with your employer, clients, members or references; or an offer to stop is tied to your silence. Consider a qualified attorney, the association's independent board or ethics body, a professional licensing body where relevant, or a securities or financial-services regulator where the original concern is about an offering or fee disclosure. Do not sign any undertaking without advice.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats of violence, stalking, doxxing, blackmail or intimidation, harassment of family, or anyone expressing thoughts of self-harm. Prioritise safety: contact local emergency services, crisis lines and qualified counsel or law enforcement. Do not confront anyone and do not negotiate under threat.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you identify the specific statement you believe is inaccurate, and what you would like corrected?"
- "I stand by what I said, which is on the record. I will not be answering questions about my motives."
- "I'm happy to discuss fee transparency in general terms, and in a forum where all members can read both sides."
- "Please direct further correspondence about this to my counsel."
- "I have taken advice and I will respond to a specific written allegation if one is made."
- "I'd like the association to confirm its procedure for complaints and the support available to volunteer officers."
- "Please share the questions with me directly, rather than through the members' list."
