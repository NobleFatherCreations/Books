# C039: The Reward Maze — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a need for scores, points or praise to tell you a thing was worth doing, and a belief that you are only as good as your last measured result.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of losing interest in anything you enjoy once nobody is counting or watching how well you do it.
- A pull toward places with clear rankings and prizes, and a lost feeling wherever nobody hands out scores.
- An automatic question, "but what do I get for it?", that crowds out whether you actually want to do it.

**A point in a life.** The early years of a sales career.

**Where it happens.** An open-plan sales floor at a software vendor.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're an account executive at an advisory software vendor, thirty years old, and you got into this because you like helping a client see their problem clearly. In your first year you spent three hours with a head of finance who was drowning in manual reporting, and when the new system went live she emailed you: "This changed my team's year." You printed it. It is still in your drawer. What you want now is a good year and a bonus tier that lets you stop doing sums at the supermarket.

**January, the sales floor**

The open-plan floor has a leaderboard on a screen above the coffee station, refreshing every few minutes. Under it runs a new dashboard, introduced this month, that everybody calls the Board. Points for calls, for demos, for pipeline created, for closed revenue, for logging notes on time. Points convert to a monthly rank, and rank converts to a bonus tier. Every call you make now has a number attached before you have finished it.

Your first closed deal of the year rings the gong. People clap, you get a badge, your name is read out at the all-hands, and your face stays warm for the rest of the day. Then three weeks go by with nothing: the same calls, the same care, no gong, no badge, no mention. You keep dialing as if the next one will ring.

By the end of the month the multipliers pay unevenly. On a sticky note you work out that one deal can pay three times as many points one month as the next. A long careful call with a head of finance can earn you almost nothing. You stop being able to predict what a Tuesday is worth, so you try every kind of Tuesday.

**March, the sales floor**

In March you are third on the leaderboard, and the screen above the coffee station shows your name. Colleagues glance up when you walk past it. At the Monday huddle your manager says, in front of the team, "Look at what happens when someone really commits." Your stomach lifts, the way it does when a number goes up. A colleague ranked eleventh looks at his coffee.

The rules keep moving. In January closed revenue counted double. On the last Thursday, at 4 p.m., a rocket emoji announces that pipeline now counts triple and closed revenue only 1.2. The month's calls have already been made. Then a multiplier appears on a short list of named accounts, and the list is edited while you are on the phone to one of them, so the account you have chased for two weeks stops counting in the middle of the call.

**May, the sales floor**

By May you shape your days around the Board. You split a large deal into two so that it lands in a high-multiplier month. You make calls you would not have made, only to log them, and you check the Board between every one. You chase a small strategic account that you privately think is a poor fit, because it carries a multiplier. At the refresh you drop to ninth, and nothing about your work is different. Your manager says, kindly, "Rankings go up and down. It's a good thing. It keeps us honest." You hear yourself say, "I know." You ask what the multipliers will be in June. He says they are "still being finalized."

**June, a family dinner**

At a family dinner someone asks what you do and you say, before you think, "I'm a top-three seller." Then, because you are ninth, you add, "Someone who really commits." Your sister smiles. You hear it, and you do not recognize whose sentence it is.

**Two years later, a Saturday**

You left the vendor last spring for a small company with no dashboard, and in nine months nobody there has asked you for a number. The first month felt like holding your breath. By the fifth you could no longer tell which of your days had been good, so you made a spreadsheet and gave each one a grade.

This morning the head of finance from your first account phones. She is retiring, and she wants an hour of your time to talk through how her team might use less of the system, not more. Nobody will pay you for it. You hear yourself ask, "Does it go on anyone's sheet?" There is a pause on the line. "No," she says gently. "It's just a favor."

You talk for an hour. You like it more than you expected. When she hangs up, you stand in the kitchen with the phone warm in your hand and take the printed email out of the drawer where you keep the chargers, the paper gone soft: "This changed my team's year." There is no number on it. You read it twice.

Then you open the spreadsheet and give the hour a grade, because it is the only way you will know whether it was a good one.

## The Name

That's **The Reward Maze**.

## Definition

The Reward Maze is the sequence where an institutional points system turns work into a stream of measurable rewards, varies the rewards unpredictably, makes a person's worth depend on rank, and shifts the rules, using Token Economy, Intermittent Reinforcement, Variable Ratio Reinforcement, Conditional Worth, Moving Goalposts, Performance Addiction, and Identity Hooking to convert responsiveness to measurable rewards and recognition into loss of intrinsic motivation and a self-directed sense of purpose. It benefits those who want output on the metrics, and it exploits ordinary competitiveness. It is hard to detect because incentive plans and leaderboards are standard sales tools, and because the person experiences the pull as their own ambition. What can be lost is the reason the person did the job, judgment about client fit, and a settled sense of what counts as good work.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Token Economy** — A points dashboard: points for calls, demos, pipeline created, closed revenue and notes logged on time, converted to a monthly rank and then a bonus tier. This functions by translating work into countable tokens, so that what is counted starts to define what is valued. Concern rises when the points can be earned for activity that does not serve the client.

2. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — A gong, a badge and a mention at the all-hands for the first closed deal, followed by three weeks of the same calls and nothing. This functions by making reward unpredictable, which tends to keep people attentive and checking. Concern rises when the person monitors the dashboard in off hours.

3. **Variable Ratio Reinforcement** — Uneven payouts: a sticky-note calculation showing the same deal worth three times as many points in one month as the next, and a good conversation earning almost nothing. This functions by making the payoff uncertain and the effort-to-reward ratio unreadable, which can sustain repeated attempts. Concern rises when the person cannot predict what a given effort will earn.

4. **Conditional Worth** — Rank shown as a verdict: your name third on the screen, and the manager saying in front of the team, "Look at what happens when someone really commits." This functions by tying standing to the latest score. Concern rises when recognition is attached to the number and not to the quality of the client outcome.

5. **Moving Goalposts** — Rules that arrive late: multipliers announced at 4 p.m. on the last Thursday with a rocket emoji, and the strategic account list updated mid-month. This functions by changing what counts after the effort has been spent. Concern rises when the rules change within the period they govern.

6. **Performance Addiction** — Working for the screen: splitting a deal, logging calls you would not have made, a poor-fit strategic account chased for its multiplier, and refreshing the dashboard in bed. This functions by making the pursuit of the next reward the aim, regardless of the original purpose. Concern rises when the person works to the metric against their own professional judgment.

7. **Identity Hooking** — Rank as self-description: "I'm a top-three seller," then "Someone who really commits," said at a family dinner without thinking. This functions by tying the person's self-image to a position on the leaderboard. Concern rises when losing rank feels like losing oneself.

## Why This Order

**Token Economy** comes first because the rest needs something to count. Once work is converted to points, the points can be varied, delayed and re-weighted. This is also where the shift begins from "what does the client need" to "what scores." It has to come before the gong and the silence, because a gong can only ring unpredictably once your calls, demos and notes have all been turned into numbers on the Board.

> At school, a sticker chart turns "tidy your room" into "get a sticker." You start tidying only what gets counted. Flip it: some days you tidy your room and a sticker appears; other days, for the same tidying, nothing. You would keep tidying for the room's sake and wonder what the sticker is even for.

**Intermittent Reinforcement** and **Variable Ratio Reinforcement** follow because unpredictable rewards tend to hold attention more strongly than predictable ones. The gong rings sometimes; the multipliers pay unevenly. The person cannot learn a rule, so they keep trying all of them. It has to come before the sticky-note sums, because you only start working out what a deal pays after one gong and three silent weeks have left you unable to guess what a Tuesday is worth. It has to come before the Monday huddle, because the leaderboard can only judge you once the uneven pay has you trying every kind of Tuesday and your name is climbing the screen.

> Think of checking a phone that sometimes has a message and sometimes does not. You check it far more than a phone that always does. Flip it: the same glance at your phone brings three messages one day and one the next. You would notice you cannot tell which glance mattered, and decide when to check by the clock instead.

> A game that pays out three coins one day and one coin the next for the same moves keeps you playing, because you cannot tell which move mattered. Flip it: your standing in the game is read out in front of the table before you play a single move. You would still play for the moves, and ask what the coins have to do with who you are.

**Conditional Worth** then supplies the social stakes: a public leaderboard is a daily verdict, and warmth from a manager follows it. It has to come before the late-month announcements, because a manager's praise in front of the team gives you something to lose when the formula changes at 4 p.m.

> A teacher reads out test scores in rank order. For the rest of the term, you carry your position around like a name tag. Flip it: the teacher tells you that the way test scores count will change whenever she likes. You would ask who decides the next rule, and what any position could mean if it can move.

**Moving Goalposts** keeps the learning from settling. If the person ever decoded the system, it would stop controlling them; so the multipliers change late in the month. It has to come before the calls you make only to log them, because you start working for the Board once the rules have changed after the month's calls are already made.

> You learn the rules of a card game, and the night you start to win, someone adds a new one. Flip it: while you learn the card game, you keep glancing at whether you are winning more than you look at your own cards. You would stop, and play the hand in front of you for its own sake.

**Performance Addiction** is what this produces: attention and effort directed at the dashboard itself. It has to come before the dinner table, because you only answer a family's question with your leaderboard place once you have spent months checking the Board between every call.

> You glance at the step counter on your wrist more often than you glance at the view, and you take a few extra steps in the kitchen just to see it tick. Flip it: you start thinking of yourself as a person who always gets a high step count, and a quiet day on your wrist feels like a bad day for you. You would wonder why a counter gets to say who you are.

**Identity Hooking** closes the loop by turning rank into self-description. At that point the system no longer needs to reward or punish; the person does it to themselves. It has to come last, because only after months of calls made for the Board can a leaderboard place feel like the true answer to what you do.

> After a year of being "the fast one" on the team, a slow day feels like you are not yourself. Flip it: you are "the fast one" on the team, and a slow day feels like you are not yourself. Only after that do you start checking each morning how the team reacts before checking how you feel.

The conversion point is the moment an action is chosen because of its points and not its merit. What weakens the sequence is a stable, published formula, a measure of client outcomes alongside points, and a manager who values judgment that cost points.

## Why the System Produces This Order

A sales floor like this one is built on three facts. Revenue is a number that arrives late and lumpy, so the company watches the activity that comes before it. A bonus budget is fixed and has to be divided, so it needs a ranking to divide it by. And a dashboard is cheap to build and instantly visible, so whatever it can count becomes what the floor looks at.

Token Economy comes first because a dashboard needs a currency before it can show anything. Calls, demos and notes are easy to count, so they become points, and points convert to a rank and a tier. Nobody has to want the work to turn into numbers; the screen only knows numbers.

Intermittent Reinforcement comes second because deals close when they close. A gong that rings only when a contract signs rings sometimes and then not for weeks. Nobody plans the gaps; the calendar of a sales cycle makes them.

Variable Ratio Reinforcement comes third because the same effort lands in different months. A multiplier that pays more where the company needs more makes identical conversations worth different amounts, and nobody is left able to predict a Tuesday.

Conditional Worth comes fourth because a bonus budget has to be explained, and the easiest explanation is a public ranking. A manager who thanks the top of the screen is only reading what the screen says, and the warmth follows the number.

Moving Goalposts comes fifth because the company's priorities shift faster than the compensation plan can be rewritten. Changing a multiplier on the last Thursday is quicker than changing a plan. Each change is reasonable, and each lands after the month's work is done.

Performance Addiction comes sixth because attention goes where the feedback is fastest. A dashboard refreshes every few minutes and a client changes their mind once a quarter, so the dashboard is where the person looks.

Identity Hooking comes last because a person who is measured every day and thanked by the measure starts to describe themselves in its terms. No one asks for that; it is what a visible rank does to a name.

Put it together, and the order is the order a sales floor needs: a currency, an uneven payout, a public verdict, a moving formula, a screen to watch, and a rank to introduce yourself with. What it turns the person into is someone who cannot tell whether a thing was worth doing until a number says so.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A bonus budget, a quarterly forecast and a dashboard did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a new hire. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Corin Vasquez joined a customer-success team in March, hired by Delphine Marchetti, who ran it. In his first week a screen went up that gave every call, renewal and logged note a number. When he closed his first renewal she rang a bell across the floor and read his name aloud. Then, for three weeks, whatever he did, nothing happened, and he caught himself listening for the bell. Some months a renewal moved the screen a great deal; other months the same renewal barely moved it, and nobody could tell him why. At the Monday meeting Delphine put the standings on the wall and said, "Corin is what happens when someone really commits," while the person in tenth place studied the table. On the last Thursday of each month a message announced what would count from then on, always after the month's work was done. By fall Corin was logging calls he did not need to make and checking the screen at lunch. When he dropped to ninth, Delphine said it kept everyone honest. At a friend's wedding he introduced himself by his place on the board. Delphine told a colleague that it was easy to make people forget why they had joined.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may check the dashboard compulsively, plan around multipliers, and take actions that do not serve clients. They may feel elated on a good rank and flat on a bad one, with little connection to the quality of their work.

They may stop caring about the problems that first attracted them. They may feel guilty about gaming the system and guilty about not doing it. And they may find it hard to explain what they actually do well, except in terms of the Board.

## Institutional Echo

Sales organizations commonly use leaderboards, points, tiers and variable commission multipliers. Plans that change monthly, lists of strategic accounts that can be amended mid-period, and bonus tiers tied to relative rank can make outcomes hard for a participant to forecast. Many such systems are designed in good faith to focus effort on strategic priorities.

Whether a given plan motivates or manipulates depends on how stable the rules are, how transparent the formula is and whether client outcomes count. Legal treatment of changes to compensation plans, commission rights and notice varies and needs qualified review. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether late changes consistently reduced payouts.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that what gets measured gets done, that competition brings out the best, and that high performers are motivated by winning. It is a deeply modern belief, and it makes every number look like fairness.

The value exploited is the pleasure of progress. The public story is a team energised by transparency. The private loss, where the pattern is present, is that a person whose work once meant something finds that the meaning has been converted into a score they cannot predict.

## Observable Indicators

1. Work is converted into points that can be earned for activity unrelated to client benefit.
2. Rewards such as badges, gongs and mentions appear at irregular intervals.
3. Multipliers vary widely from month to month for similar work.
4. Rules or lists change within the period they govern.
5. The formula is not published in full or cannot be reproduced from the dashboard.
6. Rank is displayed publicly and used in recognition.
7. Bonus tiers depend on relative rank rather than absolute results.
8. People split, time or choose deals to fit the multipliers.
9. People check the dashboard outside working hours.
10. Managers talk about commitment in terms of rank.
11. Client outcomes are not part of the score.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The incentive plan document, the dashboard rules and every announcement of a multiplier change, with dates.
- Screenshots of the dashboard and leaderboard at month-ends, lawfully taken from your own access.
- Your own calculation of what a given deal would earn under each month's rules.
- The strategic account list in each version and its change dates.
- Written confirmation of commission or bonus calculations and any disputes.
- Notes on deals shaped by the multipliers, and the client outcome.
- Comparisons with colleagues' experience, where shared voluntarily.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Incentive plans legitimately steer effort, and sales leaderboards can be fun and motivating. Multipliers may be changed to reflect new strategy, and relative ranking is common. The discriminator is whether the formula is written and stable within a period, whether it can be reproduced, and whether client outcomes count.

Concern strengthens when: rules change mid-period; the payoff for similar effort varies without explanation; and professional judgment about client fit is penalised.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Formula Test:**
Ask: "Can I calculate my score from the published rules?"
Concern rises if: no.

**SPOT IT — The Mid-Period Test:**
Ask: "Did the rules change after the period began?"
Concern rises if: yes, and more than once.

**SPOT IT — The Client Test:**
Ask: "Would I have done this if it earned no points?"
Concern rises if: no.

**SPOT IT — The Refresh Test:**
Ask: "How often do I check the Board when I'm not working?"
Concern rises if: more than I'd like to admit.

**SPOT IT — The Self Test:**
Ask: "Does my rank change how I see myself?"
Concern rises if: yes, strongly.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the formula in writing,** with the multiplier schedule for the whole period.
- **Do your own arithmetic** and keep a record.
- **Write down what a good result is for the client** beside the points.
- **Decide your rules for yourself.** For instance, no splitting deals to fit a month.
- **Limit dashboard time** outside work.
- **Raise changes in the period.** "Could you confirm whether the new multiplier applies to deals already in progress?"
- **Ask for client outcome measures** to be part of the plan.
- **Do not game the system in ways that harm clients or breach policy.** That can create separate risks.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Rules change within a period; your own calculation differs from the dashboard; you are acting against client fit to earn points.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Commission or bonus owed under the written plan is reduced after a late change; the plan cannot be produced; a dispute about calculations is unresolved; colleagues report similar issues. Consider an employment or contract lawyer, HR or sales operations leaders independent of your manager, and, where relevant, a labor regulator.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Pressure to misstate sales, falsify records or harm clients to earn points (fraud in progress), threats, or signs of self-harm in you or a colleague. Prioritize safety and counsel, use a compliance or whistleblower channel where appropriate, do not confront, and do not delete evidence.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you share the multiplier schedule for the whole period, in writing?"
- "Does the new multiplier apply to deals already in progress?"
- "My calculation of this deal's points differs from the dashboard. Could we reconcile them?"
- "I'd like client outcomes to be part of how we measure this."
- "I've decided not to split deals to fit the month's multiplier."
- "Could the strategic account list be fixed at the start of the quarter?"
- "I'd like to understand how rank translates into bonus tier."
- "I did what the client needed. Can we look at how that is recorded?"
