# C035: The Burnout Machine — Corporate & Consulting

## Before the Story

This chain can install a belief that rest is a kind of failure and tiredness a sign of weakness, and a reflex to answer every new emergency because stopping would let down the people you serve.

If you never see how it got in, it can follow you long after, as:

- A habit of feeling guilty on a day off and checking messages until you find a reason to go back.
- A pull toward roles where everything is always urgent, because calm feels like you are not doing enough.
- An automatic sentence, "I'm fine, I'm just tired," spoken for years while you take on the next task.

**A point in a life.** Mid-career, leading a major project.

**Where it happens.** An IT consultancy's delivery team.

*You won't be told what this chain is called, or what is inside it, until the story ends.*

## Cold Open

You're a delivery director at an IT consultancy, thirty-eight years old, and all you want is to get this programme over the line and sleep in your own bed. The client is a large retailer, your team is nine people, and your name is on the plan. You have never missed a go-live.

**Weeks one and two, the programme war room**

A data migration failed on Saturday. Everyone came in, and by Monday morning it was solved. Then the room stayed open. It has a whiteboard nobody has erased, a pile of charging cables, and a rota taped to the glass, a spreadsheet printed in colour that lists who covers the escalation line each night and each weekend. Your name is on it more than anyone's.

You answer the escalation line at midnight, at three, at Sunday lunch. Each time the rota is reprinted, and each time somebody says, "just until we're through this."

A week later the client's integration partner changes an interface without telling anyone, and there is another escalation. You start a private tally of how often the word "just" is used. By Friday it stands at nine.

**Week three, the managing director's office**

The targets arrive on a slide. Nineteen deliveries in twenty on time. Any escalation answered in under fifteen minutes, around the clock, with nearly every team hour billed to the client. You ask him how all three can hold with a team that is also covering the rota.

He says, "You're the one who knows how to make it work." You laugh, because it is plainly a compliment. Then you turn the printout over and do the sums on the back while he waits. The same nine people cannot be on the night line and billed all day. You write it down twice. He doesn't look at the page.

**Week eight, the programme war room**

The response target becomes ten minutes for severity ones, and the on-time count restarts from a baseline the client has moved twice. Each change comes on a slide, in the same font, with the word "refinement" in the title.

The managing director comes in at four and stands by the glass with his hands in his pockets. "This is what we're made of," he says to the room. "This team doesn't walk away from a client." Four people look up from the rota and look down again.

You wait until the room empties and tell him you have not had a weekend since the start. He does not argue. "I know what you're giving," he says, and his voice drops. "I think about it every day." Through the glass a junior engineer wipes her eyes with her sleeve. "But the team looks to you. I wouldn't ask if I didn't believe in you."

You say yes again, and you have a reason ready.

On Friday the next phase plan lands, with the figures you hit in weeks six to eight as its starting line. "The team has shown what it can do," says the cover note. Nobody mentions that the rota was how you did it.

**Week fourteen, the programme war room**

A priority one defect arrives at 6:40 on a Friday evening. For four days it is priority one, and then nobody mentions it again. On Monday a new one arrives, and by lunch another. You have not had one clear hour to ask what the programme is actually for.

On Wednesday your 360 review lands: thirty pages of colour charts, most of it warm. Page nineteen has a line in bold. "Resilience: development area. Stakeholders note signs of fatigue and reduced composure under sustained pressure." Underneath: "Strong delivery lead; would benefit from building stamina." You read it seven times, with the rota behind you on the glass.

**Two years later**

Two years later it's a Sunday in June, and you are on a hired sun lounger beside a lake, on the first week of leave you've taken in three years. Your phone lies face down on your chest. You have turned it over eleven times since breakfast. There is one message, from a colleague: nothing needed. You find a reason anyway, and by noon you're in the car park with the laptop open on the bonnet, tidying a deck nobody asked for.

Your partner walks over with two coffees. "You all right?"

"Nothing wrong," you say. "Long week." You have said it for so long that it comes out before the question is finished.

Last month a recruiter offered you a role with fixed hours and no rota. You turned it down. It felt like being put on a shelf. You took the one with a night line again.

Your sister sent a photo of her garden with a note: Come and sit. You told her you will, after the next release. You haven't sat in it once.

## The Name

That's **The Burnout Machine**.

## Definition

The Burnout Machine is the sequence where people are depleted by relentless, shifting and contradictory demands, held in place by duty and guilt, and then described as personally lacking when exhaustion shows, using Ritualized Exhaustion, Impossible Metrics, Moving Goalposts, Duty Scripts, Guilt-Tripping, Performance Extraction, and Strategic Chaos to convert a sense of duty and a wish to be seen as dedicated into loss of rest and of the right to name exhaustion without blame. It benefits whoever receives the output, and it exploits conscientious people. It is hard to detect because real crises, real deadlines and real client pressure exist in this sector, and because the person often chooses each step. What can be lost is health, time, standing and the ability to argue that the workload was the cause.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Ritualized Exhaustion** — A room and a rota: the programme war room with its unerased whiteboard, a colour rota on the glass listing the night and weekend escalation line, and your name on it more than anyone's. This functions by turning depletion into a routine and a badge of commitment. Concern rises when the emergency arrangement has no end date and becomes normal operations.

2. **Impossible Metrics** — Three targets at once: ninety-five percent on-time, a fifteen-minute response around the clock and eighty-five percent utilisation, answered with "You're the one who knows how to make it work." This functions by setting targets that cannot be reached together, so any shortfall is the person's. Concern rises when no one can show how the three targets are meant to coexist.

3. **Moving Goalposts** — Targets that change: in week six "ten minutes for severity ones," and in week eight the on-time rate counted from a revised client baseline that moved twice. This functions by making the finish line recede after effort has been given. Concern rises when the standard changes without a change in resources.

4. **Duty Scripts** — Team and identity as the reason to stay: "This is what we're made of," and "the team looks to you. If you step back, they'll feel it." This functions by placing rest in conflict with loyalty. Concern rises when duty language appears at the moment someone raises a limit.

5. **Guilt-Tripping** — A personal plea: "I know what you're giving. I think about it every day... I wouldn't ask if I didn't believe in you," beside a junior in tears and a client lead's bad quarter. This functions by making refusal feel like harm to others. Concern rises when a request for rest is met with a statement about who will be affected.

6. **Performance Extraction** — Extra effort turned into the baseline: the next phase plan starts from the figures your nights and weekends produced, with "The team has shown what it can do." This functions by drawing out effort above the contract without matching compensation or relief. Concern rises when the extra becomes the expected.

7. **Strategic Chaos** — Constant emergencies: a priority one at 6:40 on Friday that lapses after four days, a new crisis the day after your name drops off the rota, then a review calling your fatigue a development area. This functions by keeping the environment unpredictable, so that urgency never ends and planning is impossible. Concern rises when urgent items repeatedly turn out not to be urgent.

## Why This Order

**Ritualized Exhaustion** comes first because everything else rests on tired people. A rested person can ask whether the targets add up. A depleted one is less able to. The crisis is real at first, which is what makes the ritual feel justified, and the justification is what lets it become permanent. It has to come before the three targets, because a team already covering the night rota has no spare hour to ask whether all three figures can hold together.

> After a week of broken sleep, you agree to things at breakfast that you would have questioned at lunch. Flip it: at breakfast, fully rested, you are handed a list of terms that cannot all be kept. You would read it at the table and question it before agreeing to anything.

**Impossible Metrics** follows because depletion needs a measure to be turned against the person. If three targets cannot all be met, then failure is guaranteed and the shortfall can be personal. It has to come before the moving baselines, because the client's shifting baseline only bites once the on-time and response figures are the numbers your name is judged by.

> You are asked to be quick, cheap and perfect. Whichever one slips, the fault is yours. Flip it: before anyone sets you a task, you are told that what counts as quick has changed twice this week. You would ask for the current meaning in writing before you agreed to any task.

**Moving Goalposts** keeps the person running: whenever they approach the line, it shifts. It has to come before the speech about this team, because a plea to stay only lands on people who have just watched the response target shrink beneath them.

> You are almost at the end of a run when someone says, "Actually, the route is a mile longer." Flip it: at the start of a run, someone says, "People like us never stop." You would ask how long the route really is before you agreed to anything.

**Duty Scripts** and **Guilt-Tripping** then make stopping costly. By this stage the person has given a lot, and the vocabulary of team, trust and belief turns rest into betrayal. It has to come before the personal appeal, because the plea about believing in you needs the room to have heard first that this team does not walk away. It has to come before the phase plan, because the plan's starting line counts the weekends you gave only after you have said yes to giving them once more.

> A parent says, "This family sticks together." Next time you want a weekend to yourself, it feels like leaving. Flip it: a parent says, "After everything I've done, you'd leave me on my own this weekend?" before anyone has said what sort of family this is. You would ask what was needed and say what you could give.

> A friend says, "I wouldn't ask if I didn't think you'd come through." You hear it as a debt, and cancelling feels cruel. Flip it: your friend assumes you will come, as you did the last three times, before asking anything at all. You would say you never agreed to be the regular, and ask what they need this time.

**Performance Extraction** is the economic result: what was an extraordinary effort in week one is the baseline by week eight. It has to come before the Friday defects, because each new emergency is staffed from the same nine people whom the new starting line already assumes will stay on the rota.

> You cover a colleague's shift once, as a favour. Soon the rota simply has your name on that shift. Flip it: before you cover any shift, new gaps keep opening on the rota every hour. You would ask why they keep appearing, and say yes only to a shift you had chosen, once.

**Strategic Chaos** is the stabiliser, and it supplies the plausible deniability. When there is always a new fire, no one has to claim the pattern is deliberate. Crises seem to come from the world. Only their timing, and their lapses, hint at design, and intent would require additional evidence. The key conversion point is when the review describes the effects as the person's personal trait. It has to come last, because the review can only call the tiredness your own trait when nonstop defects look like the world's doing and the rota already looks normal.

> A kitchen with a new spill every five minutes leaves no time to ask why the tap keeps running. Flip it: you walk into a kitchen where a new spill appears every five minutes, and only later does mopping become your regular job. You would go and look for the tap first.

What weakens the sequence is an end date for the emergency, a resource plan that matches the target, a rota that rotates, and a record of how often urgent items prove not to be urgent.

## Why the System Produces This Order

Nobody has to design this sequence. A consultancy sells fixed-scope programmes with promised dates, penalty clauses and thin margins. It measures whether people earn their keep by how many of their hours are billed. And a client crisis arrives with the whole weight of the relationship behind it. Put those facts together and the story above follows.

Ritualized Exhaustion comes first because a crisis is the cheapest moment to ask for extra hours, and a rota is the cheapest way to cover them. Nobody has the budget for a second team, so the same nine people stay.

Impossible Metrics comes second because targets are sold to the client before the staffing is planned. Response time, delivery date and billed hours are written into three documents by three groups, and nobody adds them up until the team is already inside the contract.

Moving Goalposts comes third because a client's baseline changes whenever the client's own priorities change, and the firm will not reopen a signed contract. Definitions flex inside slides instead, where a change looks like a refinement and not a renegotiation.

Duty Scripts comes fourth because a team in a long crisis needs a reason to keep going, and "this is who we are" is the reason that costs nothing. It is also the one story that works on a whole room at once.

Guilt-Tripping comes fifth because managers are measured on keeping the programme staffed, and the person holding it together is the person with the most to lose by stopping. Appeals follow the scarce person, in private, after the group message has gone out.

Performance Extraction comes sixth because plans are built from the last phase's actuals. What a team delivered under pressure becomes what the spreadsheet says it can deliver, and the spreadsheet carries the figure forward without the rota that produced it.

Strategic Chaos comes last because a programme at the edge of its capacity throws up defects, and the severity label is chosen by whoever is most exposed to the client that day. There is always another fire. The review cycle, which looks at individuals, meets afterwards and finds tired people.

Put it together and the order is the order a stretched delivery model needs: tired people first, targets that cannot close, baselines that move, a loyalty story, a personal appeal, a baseline raised by past effort, and a steady supply of emergencies. It turns a conscientious lead into someone who treats rest as something to be earned after the next release.

## On Purpose

Nobody orchestrated what you just read. A fixed-price contract, a client who kept changing its mind, and a rota that grew out of one weekend did it, and the people inside were doing their jobs. This is what it looks like when one person does it knowingly, to a new team lead. The tactics are the same and so is their order. Only the chooser has changed.

Tobias Nwosu was six months into his first role as a team lead when the practice head, Marguerite Vance, asked him to cover the escalation line for one weekend after a failed release. The weekend became a rota with his name on it, and she called it "just until we're through this." In the third week she gave him three targets on a single page: every call answered within ten minutes, delivery on the date in the contract, and nearly all of his team's hours billed. Tobias said it would take two teams. She said he was the one who made things work. By week six the date in the contract had become "the date on the revised plan," and the response time had shrunk. In a team meeting Marguerite told everyone that people like them did not walk away from a client. Afterwards, when Tobias said he had not had a weekend off, she lowered her voice and said she wouldn't have asked if she didn't believe in him more than anyone she had hired. The following Friday his weekend hours appeared in the new plan as the baseline. Urgent tickets kept arriving from her inbox, each marked top priority, none mentioned by Tuesday. His review said stamina was a development area.

Marguerite told a friend over lunch that Tobias had become easy to schedule.

From the inside, the person in the middle can't tell the difference, and doesn't need to. The pattern is what to watch for, whether or not anyone intends it. The way out is the same either way: slow it down, and bring someone in.

## Individual Level

Over time, the person may sleep less and eat on the move, find concentration harder, and become short with people they care about. They may take on more because refusing feels disloyal. They may feel guilty for being tired and unable to say why.

They may begin to accept the description of themselves as low in resilience, even as the evidence of their own output sits in the delivery reports. They may delay seeking medical or professional support because there is always another escalation. And they may stay in the room long after it would have been wise to leave.

## Institutional Echo

Service firms that sell responsiveness, bill on utilisation and staff programmes thinly can make this routine. Standing war rooms, around-the-clock escalation rotas and targets that depend on client baselines they do not control are all found in delivery work. Review systems that measure "resilience" or "composure" can attribute the results of sustained load to the individual.

Whether a given crunch is a genuine emergency or a staffing model is hard to tell from inside. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the emergency has an end date, whether staffing was adjusted, and whether others in the same room are scored the same way. Working-time and rest rules differ by jurisdiction and role and need qualified review.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that dedication is measured in hours, that great professionals sacrifice for the client, and that exhaustion is the price of importance. It is told with pride in consulting and technology, and it hides the arithmetic.

The value exploited is conscientiousness. The public story is a team that rises to the occasion. The private loss, where the pattern is present, is that the people most committed to the work are the ones whose bodies pay for the plan, and the language of resilience puts the bill in their name.

## Observable Indicators

1. A temporary emergency arrangement continues for weeks without an end date.
2. A rota spreads night and weekend cover unevenly.
3. Targets that cannot all be met at the same time are set together.
4. Definitions or baselines change without changes in resources.
5. Urgent items repeatedly turn out not to be urgent.
6. Duty or team language appears when someone raises a limit.
7. A request for rest is answered with who will be affected.
8. Extra effort becomes the expected baseline.
9. A review attributes fatigue to the person's resilience or composure.
10. Crises arrive soon after the person reduces their load.
11. Staffing is not adjusted despite repeated requests.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The war room rota spreadsheet in every version, with dates and the number of nights and weekends worked by each person.
- Targets set, with the slide or email that stated them, and every change to a definition or baseline.
- Your timesheet or hours log, and messages sent outside working hours.
- A log of escalations: time raised, stated severity, outcome, and whether it proved urgent.
- Requests you made for staffing, rest or relief, and the answers.
- The 360 review and the underlying comments, with dates, and your written response.
- Any medical or wellbeing notes you choose to keep, held privately.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Delivery crunches are real. A failed migration or a regulatory deadline can justify weeks of long hours, and many professionals choose them knowingly for good reasons. Clients genuinely change baselines. Feedback on composure can be fair. The discriminator is an end date, adjusted resources, fair rota distribution, and a review that considers workload as a cause.

Concern strengthens when: the emergency has no end date and staffing does not change; urgent items repeatedly lapse; and exhaustion is attributed to the person in a review while the load that produced it is not mentioned.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The End Date Test:**
Ask: "When does this arrangement stop?"
Concern rises if: no one will name a date.

**SPOT IT — The Arithmetic Test:**
Ask: "Can all three targets be met with this team?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that I will make it work.

**SPOT IT — The Real Urgency Test:**
Ask: "Of the last ten urgent items, how many were?"
Concern rises if: most were not.

**SPOT IT — The Duty Test:**
Ask: "Did the language of the team appear when I raised a limit?"
Concern rises if: yes, every time.

**SPOT IT — The Review Test:**
Ask: "Does my review mention the workload that produced my fatigue?"
Concern rises if: it names my resilience and not the load.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for an end date in writing.** "Could we agree when the war room returns to normal operations, and what the exit criteria are?"
- **Put the arithmetic on the table.** "Here are the three targets and the team's capacity. Which should take priority?"
- **Keep an honest escalation log.** It tells you what is genuinely urgent.
- **Ask for the rota to rotate** and for cover rules that include everyone.
- **Protect rest as a work condition, not a favour.** Block time and tell people.
- **Write your response to the review.** Add the workload data next to the resilience comment.
- **Seek independent support early.** A doctor, a therapist or a trusted peer, as appropriate to your own situation.
- **Do not accept a development plan built on stamina** without discussing resourcing.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A temporary arrangement passes its stated period; metrics are redefined with no new resources; urgent items repeatedly lapse; a review links fatigue to personality.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Rota or hours are materially beyond your contract or local working-time rules; your pay, bonus or progression is tied to a resilience rating; you are discouraged from taking sick or rest leave; colleagues report similar patterns. Consider an employment lawyer, HR or employee relations where independent of the managers involved, a union or representative where one exists, and the relevant labour or health-and-safety authority.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Collapse, fainting, chest pain or other physical symptoms, or statements about self-harm by you or a colleague. Seek emergency or crisis support immediately, tell someone you trust, and do not wait for the programme to finish. Do not confront the person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we agree an end date for the war room and what has to be true for it to close?"
- "The three targets cannot all be met with the current team. Which should take priority?"
- "I've been on the rota for most nights over eleven weeks. I'd like the cover to rotate from Monday."
- "I'd like the 360 comment to reflect the workload that came with the programme."
- "I'm happy to support the severity-one cover, but I need two nights a week off the rota."
- "Could we log which escalations proved urgent, so we staff the rota to match?"
- "I'm taking this weekend off. Here is the cover plan."
- "I'd like to discuss resourcing before a stamina development plan."
