# C033: The Approval Addiction — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a twenty-six-year-old conservatory violinist, and for fourteen months you have been the strings arranger in a celebrated mentor's studio, a room with oatmeal-colored acoustic panels and a wall of framed records you stopped noticing around month three. He found you the way people like you are found: one email, one audition, one afternoon when he listened to your arrangement of a verse you had written for a quartet and said, "Where have you been hiding?" You want to stay in this room and become the arranger people ask for by name. Tonight a former classmate's offer of a recording project, outside the studio, sits unanswered in your phone.

In the first months, when a chart pleases him he turns his whole chair toward you. Lunch gets ordered with your name on it, and on Friday nights you are asked to the late listening at his house, where the good records come out. When a chart is flat he does not look up. The lunch order goes around the room without you. Your seat on Friday is filled. The warmth is not loud, and it is precise, and you start learning its edges the way you learned the fingerboard.

Then the Tuesdays start. You hand in a string chart he praised on Monday. He reads it in silence and says, "I thought you had more than this." You say it is the same approach as yesterday. "Yesterday I was being generous." The room laughs a little, kindly, the way rooms do. You laugh too, and write it down, because writing things down is what you do. A chart he calls "sublime" on Wednesday is "student work" on Friday. In the mornings you listen for the weather in his greeting: a warm "good morning" and you can breathe, a flat one and your hands go cold before your coat is off. That night, with a chart open, you stop asking whether it is good. You ask whether he will like it.

There was a Thursday, in the first month, you keep returning to. He stopped a session to say, to the whole room, "That. That is what I mean. Listen to what she did with the second violins." Engineers looked over. A pop vocalist you grew up listening to said you were the reason the bridge worked. At the end of the night he drove you to the train station himself, in front of everyone. The assistant engineer who used to share her fries with you began taking lunch at her desk. You were too grateful to ask why.

Now he says things lightly. Over your bar sixteen, grinning: "Lovely instinct. Wrong instrument for this brain." The room laughs. Over a harmonic choice, with a wink: "Conservatory reflex." They are easy to dismiss in the moment and you do dismiss them. But before every chord you now check what he would hear in it, and your own ear comes second.

After each session he writes notes in the margins of a shared file, and the file goes to the label contact and the vocalist's manager. The notes are quick and funny. At the bottom, beside nothing at all: "Gifted but fragile. Needs a firm hand." You read that line on a train, and your face goes hot in front of strangers. The people who hire you have read it too.

In the kitchenette, over the espresso machine he is proud of, he says softly, "You remind me of myself at your age. I don't say that often." When you're ready, he says, he will write you a reference letter that "will open every door in this city." A letter like that depends on knowing someone's character, he says, and he is still learning yours. You nod. You do not ask when. You do not ask what ready means. You notice that you are already planning your behavior to deserve it.

You have stopped playing the quartet verse at home, the one that started all this. You eat standing up, and wake before the alarm to check whether he has written. When your mother asks how the work is, you say what he said about you. You cannot remember, with real effort, what you thought of your own playing before you met him.

You open a reply to your former classmate. You write "I'd love to," and delete it. You write "Let me check with" and stop, thumb above the screen, because you are asking a man's permission to be asked.

## The Name

That's **The Approval Addiction**.

## Definition

The Approval Addiction is the sequence where praise and criticism from a powerful, admired figure are alternated until a person's sense of worth depends on that figure's opinion, using Conditional Love, Intermittent Reinforcement, Pedestal Ploy, Negging, Shame Installation and Identity Hooking to convert the need for approval from an intermittently warm authority into the loss of an independent basis for self-worth. It benefits whoever relies on the person's effort, availability and loyalty, and it exploits real features of the sector: mentorship that does open doors, session notes that are normal, reference letters that matter. It is hard to detect because genuine mentors also praise, criticize and gatekeep. Intent would require additional evidence beyond the pattern.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Conditional Love** — Warmth, attention and belonging arrive when the work pleases the mentor and withdraw when it does not. This functions by teaching that closeness is earned by each delivery rather than given by the relationship. Concern rises when the warmth tracks the mentor's mood or needs more than the quality of the work.

2. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — "Sublime" on Wednesday, "student work" on Friday, with no stable standard in between. This functions by making rewards unpredictable, which tends to make people watch the source of reward more closely and work harder to earn it. Concern rises when identical work gets opposite reactions and the person cannot name what changed.

3. **Pedestal Ploy** — Public praise in front of engineers and executives, "that is what I mean," a drive to the station. This functions by raising the person high enough that the fall is costly, and by creating a debt of gratitude that is hard to question. Concern rises when praise is public and showy but never converts into pay, credit or written commitments.

4. **Negging** — Backhanded lines delivered lightly: "Wrong instrument for this brain," "conservatory reflex." This functions by planting doubt under cover of humor, while the person is expected to laugh along. Concern rises when the barbs target the very skills that make the person valuable, and objecting is treated as lacking humor.

5. **Shame Installation** — "Gifted but fragile. Needs a firm hand," written where others will read it. This functions by attaching a flaw to the person's identity, rather than to a piece of work, and making it visible to people who matter. Concern rises when comments describe the person rather than the music, and the notes circulate beyond those who need them.

6. **Identity Hooking** — "You remind me of myself," "I'm still learning your character," the letter held as a prize. This functions by fusing the person's self-image with the mentor's endorsement, so that leaving feels like losing who they are. Concern rises when the person cannot picture their career without this one figure's approval.

## Why This Order

**Conditional Love** goes first because everything else needs a relationship in which warmth already matters. If the mentor were simply unkind, the target would leave. A first phase of genuine attention builds the reservoir that later withdrawal will draw down. It has to come first, because the Tuesday "I thought you had more than this" hurts only after lunch with your name on it and Friday listening at his house; start with the swings instead and you would treat him as moody and take the outside project.

> Picture a new friend who is thrilled to hear from you at first and always has time. When they finally go quiet for a week, you notice it more than you ever would have noticed a friend who was lukewarm all along. Flip it: if your new friend went quiet for a week right at the start, before any thrilled messages, you would shrug and get on with your other friends.

**Intermittent Reinforcement** comes next because it turns that warmth into something to chase. Steady praise is taken for granted and steady criticism is escaped; praise that arrives unpredictably keeps attention locked on the source. It has to come before the Thursday he praises you to the whole room, because that moment stays precious only while his praise is uncertain; the other way round, a public compliment would just be a good night, and you would still answer your classmate's offer.

> It is like a vending machine that sometimes gives you two bars of chocolate. You keep pressing the button long after you should have walked away. Flip it: if the vending machine flashed "lucky winner" before it ever gave you two bars, you would laugh at the sign and walk away.

**Pedestal Ploy** then raises the stakes of the fall, since the more public the elevation, the more expensive any loss of standing becomes, and it plants gratitude that makes later questions feel ungrateful. It has to come before "Wrong instrument for this brain," because the joke stings only from the man who drove you to the station in front of everyone; the other way round, you would hear it as a cheap line and trust your own ear on bar sixteen.

> Say your boss names you "employee of the month" in front of the whole office. Now you have more to lose, and asking them for anything feels rude. Flip it: if your boss made a joke at your expense before naming you employee of the month, you would hear it as a cheap line and shrug it off.

**Negging** is what weakens independent evaluation. A small barb, delivered with a laugh, is easy to dismiss in the moment and hard to argue with later. It trains the person to doubt their own ear before they doubt his. It has to come before "Gifted but fragile" in the shared file, because you already doubt your own ear when that line reaches the people who hire you; the other way round, you could ask him to remove it and send your best chart to the label contact yourself.

> An uncle says, "Nice haircut. Brave." Everyone laughs, you laugh too, and for the rest of the day you keep touching your hair. Flip it: if your uncle told the family you can't take a joke before his "brave" remark, you would ask him what he meant and leave your hair alone.

**Shame Installation** raises the exit cost, because a label like "fragile," written in notes seen by the people who hire you, makes leaving look risky. It has to come before "You remind me of myself at your age," because the promised reference feels like rescue only once the file calls you fragile; the other way round, the praise would stand alone, and you could answer your classmate without asking anyone's permission.

> Imagine a school report that calls a child "difficult" and goes to every teacher they will meet next. The label arrives before they do. Flip it: if a teacher said "you're just like me at your age" before any report called you difficult, you would take the kind words and still choose for yourself.

**Identity Hooking** closes the loop and supplies the plausible deniability: a mentor can always say he was invested in the person's growth. It has to come last, because a reference "when you're ready" holds you only after months of chasing his warmth; earlier, you would ask him plainly when the letter is coming and take the recording project in the meantime.

> Think of a coach who tells you, "You're just like me at your age." After that, quitting the team feels less like leaving a team and more like turning your back on yourself. Flip it: if the coach said "just like me" before anyone had called you difficult, you would have taken it as a kind word and felt free to quit.

The key conversion point is the first time the person asks "will he like it?" before "is it good?" After that, the mentor no longer needs to apply the pattern; the person applies it to themselves. What weakens the sequence: independent feedback from trusted peers, written standards for the work, and a reference or credit arrangement that does not depend on one person's mood.

## Individual Level

The target may begin to scan for the mentor's mood before they begin work, and to feel physical relief or dread at a greeting. Over time, the person may stop trusting their own judgment about their playing, and defer to remembered phrases from the notes.

They may start to turn down outside projects, or to ask permission to accept them. They may feel gratitude and shame at once, and find it hard to explain to friends why a place where they were so praised feels so small. They may also come to believe that the reference letter is the measure of their worth, so that deadlines and delays in it feel like verdicts.

## Institutional Echo

Mentorship in studios and writing rooms often runs on informal arrangements: shared notes files, verbal promises about credits or letters, and gatekeeping over introductions to labels and managers. A shared notes file that is read by third parties can turn private feedback into reputation.

Where an arranger's pay, credit and future references all depend on one senior figure, there may be no neutral place to ask what the standard is, and no written record of what was promised. Genuine mentors also write blunt notes and withhold references until they know someone well, so the same documents can describe either good or harmful practice. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the standards stayed consistent, whether credit and pay followed the praise, and whether others describe the same cycle.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that great art comes from great teachers who are hard on you, and that the harshest critic is the one who believes in you most. Around it sits the belief that talent needs to be broken to be refined, and that gratitude for being chosen is a debt that never closes.

The value being exploited is a good one: the wish to learn from someone extraordinary. The public story is the apprentice who became a star because a master saw something in them. It can be true. It is also the story told about the apprentices who stayed, and rarely about the ones who stopped playing.

## Observable Indicators

1. Reactions to near-identical work vary widely without a stated reason.
2. Praise is delivered publicly and is not followed by pay, credit or a written commitment.
3. Humorous barbs target the skill the person was hired for.
4. Notes describe the person's character ("fragile," "reflex") rather than the music.
5. Session notes are shared with third parties who do not need them.
6. A reference letter is promised, but timing and criteria are never stated.
7. Warmth in greetings, access or invitations changes after disagreement.
8. The person is discouraged from outside projects, or must ask first.
9. The mentor says "I don't do this for many people" in connection with a favor.
10. Peers describe a similar warm-then-cold cycle.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The annotated session notes and the version history of the shared notes file, showing who could see which comments and when.
- Dated copies of the charts or arrangements and the reactions each received, for comparing reactions to similar work.
- Any written or messaged reference to the promised reference letter, including timing, criteria and conditions.
- Messages praising the work, kept beside messages criticizing similar work.
- Records of credits, pay and invoices for your arranging work, and any discussion of credits.
- Messages in which outside projects were discussed, approved, discouraged or declined.
- A dated private journal of the cycle, noting what changed between reactions.
- Names of peers or engineers who witnessed public praise or put-downs.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Real mentors are demanding, uneven, funny and blunt. Honest harsh feedback, a high bar and a refusal to write a reference before knowing someone well are all ordinary. A mentor who explains standards, is consistent between similar pieces, welcomes outside work and gives credit and pay on schedule is not running this pattern.

- **Honest quality variation.** Some charts are weaker, and a mentor's reaction may honestly differ.
- **Direct feedback culture.** Some studios are blunt with everyone, and the bluntness is not aimed.
- **Cautious references.** A mentor may reasonably wait to write a letter.
- **Your own sensitivity to critique.** Early-career feedback can feel larger than it is.

Concern strengthens when: the standards are different for the same work; barbs are aimed at the person, not the work; the mentor's warmth and the letter are tied to compliance.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Same-Chart Test:**
Ask: "If I handed in the same work on another day, would the answer be the same?"
Concern rises if: it plainly would not, and no one can say what changed.

**SPOT IT — The Outside Ear Test:**
Ask: "What would a trusted peer outside this room say about this piece?"
Concern rises if: you feel unable to ask anyone else, or you assume they would be wrong.

**SPOT IT — The Letter Test:**
Ask: "Do I know what the reference requires, and by when?"
Concern rises if: the criteria are never stated and the letter is raised whenever you hesitate.

**SPOT IT — The Margin Test:**
Ask: "Do the notes describe my music, or me, and who else reads them?"
Concern rises if: the notes label your character and circulate to people who hire you.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the standard in writing.** "What would make this chart a yes?" A real standard can be stated.
- **Keep your own record** of which pieces got which reactions, with dates.
- **Seek outside ears.** A teacher, a peer, a section player you trust outside the studio. Consistent feedback from them is a benchmark the mentor's mood is not.
- **Ask for notes to be limited** to the people who need them, in a calm, practical tone.
- **Ask about the reference directly,** once: "What would you need to see, and roughly when?" Then make a list of other references.
- **Keep credit and pay separate from praise.** Ask for credit and fees in writing before the session.
- **Maintain at least one outside project or relationship** so that your sense of your work does not run through one person.
- **Avoid public confrontation or retaliatory leaks** of the notes. Preserve and consult first.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Reactions vary for the same work; barbs about your character appear in notes; the letter is mentioned whenever you hesitate; you stop outside projects or ask permission for them.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The letter, credit or work are made explicitly conditional on compliance; you are told your career depends on staying; notes with character labels are circulated to people who hire you; others describe the same pattern; credit or pay for your arranging is withheld. Relevant routes may include an independent entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, and, where credits are disputed, a performing rights or collective management body.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to your reputation or livelihood if you leave; stalking or unwanted contact; any sexual pressure or exploitation; withholding of belongings, documents or access; talk of self-harm or signs of a mental-health crisis. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront the mentor or sign anything alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Can you tell me what would make this chart a yes? I'd like to aim at it."
- "I got a very different reaction to a similar approach last week. Can we talk about what you're hearing?"
- "I'd like to keep the notes between us and the people who need them for the session."
- "I'm grateful for the offer of a reference. What would you need to see, and roughly when?"
- "I have an outside project and I'd like to take it. I'll make sure my studio commitments are covered."
- "Can we confirm my credit and fee for this arrangement in writing before we start?"

## Reflection

If every kind word and every cruel one disappeared tomorrow, what would you say about your own playing?
