# C059: The Scapegoat Funnel — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a festival spokesperson, thirty-five, standing in the press tent at 6:40 on the first morning with a cold coffee and a laminated schedule. You have been the voice of this festival for six years: warm, plain, unrushed. People trust you because you have never told them anything that was not true. What you want is for the weekend to go well, and for that to stay the case.

The chief of operations calls. The second day has to be called off: a ground-conditions report, a contractor problem, nothing anyone wanted. "This is going to be a hard day," she says. "We need someone steady. I can't think of a better face for the festival." Under the weight of it you feel a small, honest pleasure at being trusted with the hard part. You say yes before you have finished deciding.

You ask the sensible questions. Has legal approved the wording? Have the artists been told? Refunds? Is the safety consultant's recommendation in writing? Each gets one quick, warm sentence. Legal is aware. The management teams are being informed. "You have my full backing. Say it just as you'd say it yourself." You draft a statement on your phone and read it to her over the speaker. "Perfect. Go." Nothing comes in writing. You think of sending it to her, but there is no time, and sending it would feel like not believing her.

At 8:15 you stand at a lectern in front of nineteen cameras. You say that Saturday's programme will not go ahead, that the decision was taken in the interest of safety and that refunds are being worked out. You say you are sorry. Your voice does not shake. By nine the statement is the top item on three music sites, with your name and your photograph. Nobody else is named. No company, no board, no one who took the decision.

You try to reach the chief of operations. She is in a meeting. The festival's own account posts nothing. An executive asks the press team whether "the spokesperson's statement" was "properly cleared," and you watch the question travel round the tent like a draft. By noon a thousand posts quote you, and a headline says the festival "cancels without notice." By afternoon the refunds are not finalized and the artists' managers are angry that they heard it from you. A reporter tells you that someone said, off the record, that the wording "went further than we'd intended."

At six the festival posts a short statement of its own, signed by the organizing company, regretting "any confusion caused by earlier communications." It does not use your name. It does not need to. You read it twice, standing, and your throat tightens. There is nothing in it to object to, because it points at no one.

The chief of operations calls at last. She is very sorry. It has been a terrible day for everyone. The board is looking at how the announcement was handled, she says, and perhaps you should not speak to the press for a while. She is sure it will all be fine. You ask who approved the wording. There is a pause. "I think we all assumed you'd cleared it."

You look at your phone. There is no email and no message. There is only the voice note you recorded to yourself at 6:50, pacing, to remember the call. You play it again, because you have started to wonder whether you remember the call at all. Your own voice says, "She said say it just as you would." You have not eaten since the coffee. You leave the replies unopened, and then the messages from friends that begin "Are you okay?" You used to say your name into a microphone without thinking about it.

You are standing in a tent full of folded chairs, the laminated schedule still in your hand, Saturday still printed on it. It is still your face on every screen.

## The Name

That's **The Scapegoat Funnel**.

## Definition

The Scapegoat Funnel is the sequence where a person is recruited with language of trust and mission, given verbal-only approval, made the single public face of a shared decision and then severed from ownership when backlash arrives, using Mission Framing, Verbal Authorization, Visibility Inversion, Pressure Accumulation, Narrative Severance and Sacrificial Repositioning to convert willingness to represent a decision into the loss of institutional backing at the moment it is needed. In live music it concentrates around festival cancellations, lineup changes, ticketing and safety announcements, where a spokesperson, tour manager or junior staff member is put at the front of a decision made by others. It is hard to detect because every step can look like trust and urgency. Intent would require additional evidence, and a confused organization under pressure can produce the same shape without a plan.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Mission Framing** — "We need someone steady," "you're the best face for the festival." This functions by recasting a risky role as an honor, which makes declining feel like disloyalty. Concern rises when praise arrives at the same moment as an unusual, high-exposure task.

2. **Verbal Authorization** — Approval by phone, in person or by voice, with no written confirmation. This functions by granting permission that cannot later be proved. Concern rises when written confirmation is deferred for time, or its request is treated as mistrust.

3. **Visibility Inversion** — The spokesperson alone is named, photographed and quoted, while decision-makers, the organizing company and the board remain invisible. This functions by making one person the apparent author of a collective decision. Concern rises when the organization's own channels say nothing while the individual's name is everywhere.

4. **Pressure Accumulation** — Mounting backlash, internal questions about clearance, reporters, managers and an unfinished refund plan. This functions by raising the cost of the position until the organization's interest moves toward separation. Concern rises when internal questions begin to ask who cleared it, rather than what to do.

5. **Narrative Severance** — The organization states that the individual's communications caused confusion, or uses language that distinguishes "the spokesperson's statement" from its own. This functions by redefining the statement as hers. Concern rises when the organization's later wording contradicts what you were told to say.

6. **Sacrificial Repositioning** — A suggestion to avoid the press, a review of "how it was handled" and the implication that responsibility lies with the messenger. This functions by moving accountability downward to the visible and disposable role. Concern rises when you are asked to step back without any recorded decision that explains why.

## Why This Order

**Mission Framing** has to come first because it controls consent. A person told the task is risky might ask for written cover. A person told they are the best face for the festival hears trust, and trust is hard to answer with a request for a signature. It has to come first, because once you have said yes to being the steady face at 6:40, asking for written approval feels like doubting her; start with the spoken go-ahead instead and you would more likely ask her to email the wording.

> A neighbour says, "You're the only one I'd trust to sort this out with the landlord." Being trusted is a good feeling, and asking her to put it in an email would feel rude. Flip it: if your neighbour just said "tell the landlord I agreed" before saying she trusted you, you would ask her to put it in an email.

**Verbal Authorization** is what makes the rest possible. A written approval would be an anchor: it would show who decided. A verbal one is an authorization that can be remembered differently later, which is the plausible deniability the sequence needs. Importantly, the request for writing is awkward by design, because asking for it appears to doubt the person who just praised you. It has to come before the lectern, because nineteen cameras turn a spoken "say it just as you'd say it" into your words alone; the other way round, you could stop before 8:15 and ask for the approval in writing.

> A friend says on the phone, "Just tell the host I said the dog can come." Later the host says nobody mentioned it, and your friend says, "I don't remember saying that." Flip it: if the host was already looking only at you about the dog before your friend's phone call, you would ask your friend to tell the host herself.

**Visibility Inversion** turns a collective decision into one face. The key conversion point is the lectern: from that moment the public sees a person where it ought to see an organization. It has to come before the backlash builds, because the thousand posts need one named face to quote; the other way round, anger at an unnamed cancellation would land on the organizing company, and you could point to its decision.

> Five people agree on the price for a cake stall, and one of them stands behind the table. Every cross customer only ever meets her, and she cannot say who set the price. Flip it: if customers were already cross about the cake price before you took your place behind the table, you would ask the other four to stand there too.

**Pressure Accumulation** then works on the organization, not the person. As backlash grows, the cheapest relief is separation. Nobody has to design this; they only have to follow the incentive. It has to come before the six o'clock statement, because a line about "earlier communications" only seems settled once the headlines and angry managers already point at you; issued first, you would ask at once which communications it meant.

> When a plan in a group chat goes wrong and people get annoyed, you can watch the messages change, one by one, to "I only went along with what Sam said." Flip it: if someone posted "the confusion came from an earlier message" before anyone got annoyed, you would ask straight away which message, and who sent it.

**Narrative Severance** supplies the language of separation. It has to come before the call telling you to stay away from the press, because that call leans on a public statement already blaming the earlier wording; the other way round, you would ask her, in writing, who approved it.

> A notice at work says, "Any confusion was caused by the earlier email." It never says who wrote it, or who asked for it, so there is no one for you to ask. Flip it: if everyone at work already looked at you before the notice blamed "the earlier email," you would ask in writing who wrote that email and who asked for it.

**Sacrificial Repositioning** completes the funnel by moving responsibility to the lowest point. It has to come last, because "I think we all assumed you'd cleared it" only sticks once your face is the only one in the coverage; earlier, on the 6:40 call, you would have asked her to confirm the wording in a message.

> The whole family helped carry the table to the garden. When it tips over, everyone looks at the one who was standing nearest, and you hear yourself begin to explain. Flip it: if everyone looked at you before anyone had said how the table tipped, you would see it for what it is and remind them the whole family carried it.

What would weaken the sequence: a written confirmation of wording and authority before anything is said publicly; an agreed list of who else is named; a shared statement issued under the organization's name; and an internal record of the call, kept lawfully and from the first minute.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt their own memory of a call that was clear when it was made. Over time, the person may find that people who said they had their back stop answering, and may experience professional isolation in a small world.

They may feel shame about an announcement that they believed was true, and anger about having been trusted into it. They may withdraw from public roles. And they may carry the absence of paper as a kind of private injury, because the proof exists only in their own voice.

## Institutional Echo

Festivals and live events often announce difficult news through a single designated person, under time pressure and with approvals given by phone or chat. Where press statements are drafted quickly, with legal, artist and sponsor stakeholders informed separately, clearance can become unclear. Employment terms covering a spokesperson's public statements and duties vary.

The honest reading is that in a genuine crisis people do act on verbal instructions, and that confused attribution is common. Organizations also genuinely differ on whether a statement matched their intent. Intent would require additional evidence: whether wording was actually approved, whether the organization's own channels were silent by plan, and whether earlier crises were handled the same way.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that "the show must go on" and that a professional takes the hit. Around it stand beliefs that loyalty means absorbing blame, that the trusted face is a privilege and that a person who asks for it in writing is not a team player. "We trust you" is a compliment that also moves the risk.

The value being exploited is a good one: the willingness to stand up and tell the public something hard. The public story is a steady voice at a lectern. The private story is a voice note in a pocket.

## Observable Indicators

1. A high-exposure task is offered with unusual praise and urgency.
2. Authorization is verbal, and a written version is deferred.
3. Legal, artist and sponsor approvals are described but not shown.
4. Only the spokesperson is named in coverage; decision-makers are not.
5. The organization's own channels stay silent while the individual is quoted.
6. Internal questions turn to who cleared the statement.
7. Off-the-record remarks say the wording "went further than intended."
8. The organization later issues a statement that distances itself from earlier communications.
9. You are asked not to speak to the press.
10. A review of "how it was handled" is announced without scope.
11. People who gave verbal reassurance become hard to reach.
12. Colleagues say "we assumed you'd cleared it."

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Any voice note, note-to-self or calendar entry created at the time of the call, with timestamp.
- The draft statement in every version, and any message in which it was sent to anyone.
- Call logs, showing who called, when and for how long.
- Your job description, contract or engagement terms covering public statements.
- Press coverage, with timestamps, showing who was named and who was not.
- The organization's official posts and statements, with times.
- Names of anyone in the press tent who heard the instruction or the call.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

In a real crisis, instructions often arrive by phone, and a spokesperson is often the proper face of a decision. Being named in coverage is part of the role.

The discriminator is **whether the organization stood behind the wording it asked for**. In a benign case, the organization owns the decision publicly, even when it is unpopular. In the pattern, it separates from the person at the moment of backlash.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Honest disagreement** about what was said on a rushed call.
- **A clear, shared decision** announced by one designated person.
- **A genuine overreach,** where the statement went further than any instruction.
- **A fast-moving situation** with no time for written approval.

Concern strengthens when: the organization's own channels say nothing while one person is exposed; the organization's later wording contradicts the original instruction; and a review of "how it was handled" targets the messenger.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Paper Test:**
Ask: "Is the wording and the authority to say it written down anywhere?"
Concern rises if: it exists only as a phone call.

**SPOT IT — The Name Test:**
Ask: "Whose name will the public see besides mine?"
Concern rises if: nobody else's.

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "Will the organization post under its own name?"
Concern rises if: it stays silent while I am quoted.

**SPOT IT — The Praise Test:**
Ask: "Why does the praise arrive at the same moment as the risk?"
Concern rises if: refusing is being treated as disloyalty.

**SPOT IT — The Clearance Test:**
Ask: "If someone asks who approved this, what will they say?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "we assumed you had."

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for written confirmation before speaking.** "Can you send me the wording and a one-line confirmation that it is approved? I'll read it exactly." If time is short, send it yourself: "Confirming my instructions from our 6:40 call..."
- **Send your own summary immediately after any verbal instruction,** addressed to the person who gave it.
- **Ask that the statement be issued under the organization's name** and that a decision-maker be named or present.
- **Stay within the approved wording,** and say so if challenged.
- **Keep a lawful, dated record** of the call: a note to yourself written at the time.
- **Do not speculate publicly or respond to online attacks.**
- **Take your record to an independent employment or entertainment attorney, a union or guild representative, or a communications professional,** before agreeing to step back or sign anything.
- **Ask for the scope and the decision-maker of any review** in writing.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A high-exposure task is given verbally; the organization is silent while you are quoted; someone asks who cleared the statement.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The organization contradicts the wording it asked you to use; you are asked to step back or sign a statement; a review targets you; your reputation or income is affected. Relevant routes may include an employment or entertainment attorney, a union or guild representative, a labor office or an arbitration or mediation service named in the contract.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Any threat, harassment, doxxing, stalking or fear for your physical safety arising from the backlash, or if distress is affecting your health or you are having thoughts of harming yourself. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person and do not engage with the people involved.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before I go out, can you send me the wording and a line confirming it's approved?"
- "To confirm my instructions from our call this morning..."
- "I'd be glad to make this statement. Can the festival post under its own name at the same time?"
- "Who else will be named or present for this announcement?"
- "I said what I was asked to say. I'm happy to walk through the call with you."
- "Please share the scope of the review and who is conducting it."
- "I'd like to take advice before agreeing to anything."

When you were asked to be the face, who agreed to stand behind you?
