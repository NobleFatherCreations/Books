# C133: Competence Undermining — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a thirty-two-year-old assistant producer, and you are in a control room at two in the afternoon with a headache you have carried since the second week of the album. The desk is covered in cold coffee cups and the long strip of console tape where you have written, in your own capitals, the names of the tracks.

You are very good at this. You know it the way you know your own handwriting. You are the one who heard that the bridge on the third song needed to drop half a step, who rebuilt the drum chain when the first one fell apart on the night before delivery, who found the vocal comp that made the artist cry. You are also, as the producer likes to say in front of guests, "my right hand." He is charming. When you first joined, he told the engineer, "Watch her, she's got ears." You walked on air for a month.

Now the credits page for the album sits open on the screen. It is a draft, and you have been asked to proofread it. Under Produced By there is one name. Under Additional Production there is a second, which is not yours. Under Assistant, in the small print, there is yours, between the tea runner and the courier. You say, carefully, "Should I be on additional production? I rebuilt the whole second half of the record." He doesn't look up. "I'll sort you out in the notes. You know I'd never forget you." Your face is warm. You nod.

"Let me handle it," he says, and puts a hand on your shoulder. "The label's very particular about the credits page. Bless her, she'd do the whole thing herself if I let her." He says it so easily that you almost forget you asked for something.

At a listening party the artist says, "This record is the best thing we've made." The producer touches your shoulder and tells the table, "She keeps the wheels on." There is laughter. It is gentle. You laugh. Afterwards he adds that you are better at the admin than the rest of them, and you go home pleased and cannot say why your jaw aches.

The next week he opens the delivery email and sighs that he has never understood the label's portal. "I'll only break it. You do it." Then it is the stem list. Then the follow-ups, which are his to chase. By Friday his tasks are in your inbox, and he forwards your finished email to the label with a note that says "just confirming the details, as per our chat." The replies thank him. He copies you.

The session notes, which you have kept for every day of the sessions, are a shared document. Over the next weeks, small things happen to them. Someone summarizes your entries into a tidy list in which no names appear. "Dropped bridge by a half step" has become "mix adjustments." The line about the comp has lost its subject. When the label asks who found the half-step, he says, "Honestly, a team effort. We got lucky with that one."

When you propose a mix decision at the session, he tells you, kindly, that it's "a lot to take on." When you take on a smaller piece of work, he says, "You'll get there." Nobody says what getting there would look like. You stop being sure what size the thing is that you are supposed to be ready for.

When another assistant producer on a different project finishes an album in a month, he mentions it, twice. Late one night you open the session notes again, looking for a line you wrote about the vocal. It isn't there. You do not remember writing it either. For a moment, you honestly cannot tell which of the two things is the truth: that you did the work, or that you were helped through it.

You have stopped putting your hand up in sessions. You write your ideas on the console tape and tear them off before anyone reads them. You lie awake at three rehearsing how to ask about the credit, and by morning you have talked yourself into thanking him instead. When someone asks what you did on the record, you say, "A bit of everything," and change the subject.

The credits page is due tomorrow. There is a blank line, and a cursor, and a man in the next room who calls you his right hand.

## The Name

That's **Competence Undermining**.

## Definition

Competence Undermining is the sequence where a person's belief in their own ability is steadily eroded so that they come to doubt that they can function without the system that diminishes them, using Credit Theft, Infantilization, Negging, Weaponized Incompetence, Sabotaging Success, Impossible Metrics and Comparative Devaluation to convert the desire to be seen as capable into the loss of credit for one's own work and demonstrated ability. In the entertainment and music industry it can concentrate in studios, producer-assistant relationships, writing rooms, label departments and any hierarchy where credits, session notes and recommendation matter more than the work itself. It may benefit the person who takes the credit and the structure that relies on unrecognized labor. It is hard to detect because each act is small, warm and plausible, and because the credit is often fixed in documents the junior person is asked to proofread. What can be lost is credit, confidence and a career built on both.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Credit Theft** — The assistant's contributions are credited upward: one name under Produced By, the assistant's rebuilt drum chain and key decisions folded into "mix adjustments." This functions by removing the evidence that the work was theirs, so achievements cannot be claimed. Concern rises when the credits page and the session notes diverge from what the assistant remembers doing, and no one explains why.

2. **Infantilization** — The assistant is treated as unable to handle things alone: "let me handle it," "bless her," "it's a lot to take on." This functions by presenting protection as care, so the person comes to accept that someone else must decide on their behalf. Concern rises when the offered help is not asked for and always results in the helper's name replacing the assistant's.

3. **Negging** — Backhanded compliments undercut the praise: "she keeps the wheels on," "you're better at the admin." This functions by pairing recognition with a quiet limit on what the person is allowed to be recognized for. Concern rises when compliments are public and warm and always describe maintenance rather than creation.

4. **Weaponized Incompetence** — The senior person performs helplessness about tasks that are theirs, so the burden falls on the assistant: the delivery email, the detail work, the follow-ups. This functions by making the junior person do the unseen work and then letting it be sent under another name. Concern rises when the senior person is clearly capable in other contexts and only becomes unable where the work would be credited to someone else.

5. **Sabotaging Success** — Achievements are actively undermined: key notes edited, successes summarized away, a good decision recast as luck or "a team effort." This functions by preventing accomplishments from accumulating into a record. Concern rises when the person's best work seems to disappear from the documents that describe it.

6. **Impossible Metrics** — The standards for "taking it on" are unclear or unreachable: bigger responsibilities are described as too much, smaller ones as proof of limitation. This functions by guaranteeing that whatever is done will fall short of an unstated bar. Concern rises when the bar moves after each success or cannot be written down.

7. **Comparative Devaluation** — Others are held up as measures of the assistant's inadequacy: "a different assistant finished an album in a month." This functions by installing the idea that the person is slow or less capable, without any shared yardstick. Concern rises when the comparisons are repeated, are unsourced, and are made to someone who cannot answer them.

## Why This Order

**Credit Theft** has to come first because everything else is built on the absence of a record. If the assistant's work is credited clearly and in writing, there is a standing answer to every later doubt: the page says they did it. Take the page away and the person is left with a memory, which is the easiest thing in a studio to doubt. It has to come first, because "let me handle it" only sounds like a favour once your name already sits in small print between the tea runner and the courier; start with the hand on your shoulder instead and you would simply ask to see the credits page yourself.

> Picture a group project where one classmate hands in the poster with only their own name on it. Later, all you have to prove it was you is what you remember. Flip it: if that classmate offered to hand in the poster for you before you had finished it, you would say thanks, no, and write your own name on it.

**Infantilization** then makes the taking feel kind. "Let me handle it" is the sentence that turns a transfer of credit into a favor. The assistant is thanked, not robbed, and it becomes almost impolite to ask for the page back. It has to come before the joke that you "keep the wheels on", because that line only lands at the listening party once the credit has been taken off your hands as a kindness; the other way round, you would hear it while still asking for additional production by name.

> At the bank, an uncle takes the pen from your hand and says, "I'll do it, you've got enough on." It feels like care. It is also how you stop being the one who signs. Flip it: if your uncle joked at the bank that you only manage small things before taking the pen, you would hear the dig and sign the form yourself.

**Negging** prepares the person to accept a lower place. Praise that always describes maintenance trains the listener, and everyone else in the room, to hear "assistant" when they hear the person's name. It has to come before the label portal lands on you, because "I'll only break it" only makes sense once the room already hears you as the one who is good at admin; the other way round, you would answer that the follow-ups are his to chase.

> A colleague says, "You're so good with the boring stuff." It sounds like praise. Before long you are the person who gets the boring stuff, and nothing else. Flip it: if your colleague said they couldn't do the boring stuff before praising you for it, you would say it is their job too and share it out.

**Weaponized Incompetence** follows because it needs that prior framing: a senior person can only get away with helplessness if the junior person is already seen as the one who copes. It also moves unseen labor downward. It has to come before the half-step becomes "a team effort", because your work only blurs once his notes go out over your finished emails and the session notes lose their names; the other way round, the label would have your own emails and you could point to them.

> Think of a housemate who can never quite work the washing machine. You end up doing all the laundry, and after a while you stop asking for help. Flip it: if your housemate called your clean laundry "lucky" before ever failing with the washing machine, you would laugh and show them which buttons to press.

**Sabotaging Success** is the step that weakens independent evaluation. By now the person's best work has been recast as luck or support, and the documents confirm it. It has to come before "you'll get there", because a bar with no size only sticks once your bridge and drum chain have been written down as luck; the other way round, you could point to the session notes and ask exactly what more he needs to see.

> You cook a lovely dinner and someone at the table says, "Lucky it turned out." The next time you cook, you wonder which one it was, you or luck. Flip it: if someone said your cooking was "not quite there" before you made the dinner, you would ask exactly what good looks like and cook it anyway.

**Impossible Metrics** raises the exit cost: the person cannot demonstrate readiness because readiness is never defined. It has to come before the talk of the other assistant's one-month album, because that comparison only hurts once "getting there" has no clear size; the other way round, you would ask him what that album's credits and schedule actually were.

> You ask your boss what it would take to be promoted and hear, "Show more initiative." When you do, you hear, "Not like that." There is no bar to clear, so you can't clear it. Flip it: if your boss praised a colleague who "just gets it" before you ever asked about promotion, you would ask what that colleague actually did.

**Comparative Devaluation** closes the loop, since it supplies a shadow rival the person can always lose to. It has to come last, because a rival you never meet only holds once the credit, the notes and the bar have all gone soft; earlier, you would still have the draft page and your own session notes in hand, and you would ask for your credit in writing.

> A parent says, "Your cousin tidied his room without being asked." The cousin isn't there to be asked, and you lose to him every time. Flip it: if the cousin was mentioned before you had ever been told what a tidy room means, you would just ask what tidy looks like and do that.

The conversion point is the moment the person cannot tell whether they did the work or were helped through it. After that, the system no longer needs to take; the person will give up credit themselves. What weakens the sequence is a credits agreement made before the work, session notes that are dated and shared, a written description of what each role will receive, and a trusted peer who saw the work done.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to second-guess work they know was theirs, and to describe their contributions with qualifiers: "I just helped," "it was a team thing." Over time, they may stop volunteering ideas, or offer them only as questions.

## Institutional Echo

Credit pages, session notes and liner-note proofs are fixed at the end of a project, often by a few people, and often under time pressure. Where junior roles are asked to proofread or draft the documents that decide their own credit, they may be unable to correct them without risk. Where a producer's recommendation is the main path to the next job, the junior person's dependence can function as a reason not to raise credit. Where agreements leave credit to "customary practice" or to the producer's discretion, credit questions can turn on goodwill. Where reputational stakes are high, summaries of work may reduce individual contributions to "the team".

The honest reading is that credits are genuinely hard: roles overlap, contributions blur, and some assistants are given real teaching and real opportunity. Intent would require additional evidence: for example, whether other assistants working with the same producer report the same pattern, whether notes were edited after the fact, and whether credit was discussed with the assistant before delivery.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth that hides the pattern is that the studio is a master-apprentice tradition, and that paying dues is the road to recognition. People say "everybody starts as the assistant," "you'll get your turn," and "the producer's name sells the record," and each is partly true.

The public story is the single name on the sleeve and the genius in the control room. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is a body of work that cannot be shown to the next employer because no document says it was theirs.

## Observable Indicators

1. Credits drafts omit or downgrade contributions the assistant remembers making.
2. Session notes are summarized or edited so that names and specific decisions drop out.
3. Requests to correct a credit are met with "let me handle it" and no written follow-up.
4. Praise in public describes support, maintenance or admin rather than creative decisions.
5. The senior person is unable to perform tasks that would be credited to the assistant, but able to perform comparable ones elsewhere.
6. Delivery emails and administrative tasks are routinely assigned to the assistant but sent or forwarded under another name.
7. Larger responsibilities are described as too much, while smaller ones are described as limits.
8. No written description exists of what would count as readiness for a bigger role.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Drafts of the credits page, liner notes and metadata, with dates, and any changes made to them.
- Your own dated session notes, in the original form, and any later summaries that omit entries.
- Emails, messages or voice notes in which you describe a decision, a fix or an idea at the time you made it.
- Stems, project files, bounce versions and session files with date stamps that show your changes.
- Requests you made about credit, and the exact answer, including "let me handle it," with the date.
- Delivery emails, forwards and approvals showing who drafted what and who sent it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Credits and recommendations are often handled carelessly, and not every omission is a pattern. Lookalikes specific to this combo include:

- **Genuine ambiguity of role.** Production and assistant roles overlap, and honest people disagree about what counts as production.
- **Real mentoring.** A senior producer who takes on tasks to protect a junior person from risk, and who names their contribution clearly.
- **A rushed credits page.** Errors that are corrected when raised.

Concern strengthens when: credit questions are met with warmth and no written answer; entries in shared notes change after the fact without explanation; and the assistant's higher-level contributions are consistently described as support.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Page Test:**
Ask: "Does the credits page match what my session notes and project files show I did?"
Concern rises if: it doesn't, and the response to the gap is "let me handle it" with no written reply.

**SPOT IT — The Compliment Test:**
Ask: "When I'm praised in public, what exactly is the praise for?"
Concern rises if: it is always for keeping things running and never for what was decided or made.

**SPOT IT — The Admin Test:**
Ask: "Who is unable to do this task, and is that true of the same task elsewhere?"
Concern rises if: the helplessness appears only where the work would be credited to someone else.

**SPOT IT — The Bar Test:**
Ask: "Can anyone tell me, in writing, what I would have to do to be credited as additional producer?"
Concern rises if: no one can, or the answer changes each time.

## Chain Breakers

- **Date and keep your own notes.** Keep a private log of what you decide and do each session, with times, in a format you control and lawfully hold.
- **Ask for credits in writing before the work, not after.** "Could we confirm in an email what credit each of us will receive on this record?"
- **Answer "let me handle it" with a short summary.** "Thanks. For my notes, I understand the credit is additional production. Could you confirm?"
- **Request the credits draft early.** Ask to see it before the label does, and send your correction in writing, politely.
- **Seek a witness.** An engineer or musician who saw you work can confirm the sequence of decisions.
- **Ask for a written role description and a readiness definition.** "What would make me ready for this next step, and by when?"
- **Get independent advice on credit clauses.** An entertainment attorney with no tie to the producer or label can read the credit provisions.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A credits draft that omits your work; session notes that change without explanation; your credit question answered with "let me handle it" more than once; promised references that do not arrive.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The credits are finalized without your contribution after you raised it in writing; you are told to stay silent for the sake of your career; entries in shared notes are altered after your correction; you are asked to sign a release or agreement covering credit. Relevant routes may include an entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, an artist advocacy or legal-aid clinic, a collective management or performing rights body for registration questions, and the label's formal channels.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to end your career, blackmail, sexual pressure, stalking, or any attempt to withhold documents or travel papers: contact emergency services or the relevant helpline, tell a trusted person where you are, and do not confront the person. If sustained doubt in yourself has become low mood or thoughts of self-harm, contact a doctor or a crisis line.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before we lock the credits, could you email me what credit I'll have on the record?"
- "I'd like to see the credits draft when it's ready, so I can check my details."
- "For my notes: I rebuilt the drum chain and proposed the half-step change on the bridge. Could we make sure that's reflected?"
- "Thanks for offering to handle it. Could you confirm in writing what the outcome will be?"
- "I'd be glad to help with admin, and I'd like it to be sent under my name when I wrote it."
- "Can we agree what I'd need to do to be credited as additional production on the next one?"
