# C028: The Frog in Boiling Water — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a merch manager, twenty-six, on a regional tour, and what you want is simple: sell shirts, vinyl and enamel pins at the table, count the float at the start of the night and again at the end, and go home with a fair day rate and a story. Every night the tour accountant prints a settlement sheet and you sign at the bottom. Your pen lives in the float tin.

Week one, the crew is short, and you are asked to load the merch in with the backline. It is a favor and takes twenty minutes. You smile, you do it, and nobody mentions it again. Week two, you are asked to stay until the last fan has left, because the band likes to meet people. Week three, you set up before soundcheck. Week four, you count the support act's stock into your inventory sheet. It will "only take a minute," and it takes forty. Each request comes from a tired, friendly person in a hurry, and each fits inside what a flexible person says yes to. Your back aches in the van afterward, and you decide that is just what loading is.

In week five you ask, lightly, whether running a card reader for the support act's items is really yours. "It just makes sense," the tour manager says. "That's just the job." He says it pleasantly, already walking away. Nobody has called anything a favor since the third week. That night at the settlement desk, a folding table backstage with a laptop and a printer that jams, the sheet has the usual columns: gross sales, venue merch fee, credit card processing, net. Beneath them are new lines: "stock handling," "table staffing," and one that says only "admin." The next night they are still there. You sign at the bottom, because the sheet is the thing everyone signs.

In the private tab of the spreadsheet you started in week two, the nightly nets have gone down in a slow staircase. No single step is alarming. You say something, gently. "Is the staffing line something we could look at?" The tour manager laughs, warmly. "Oh, it's nothing, honestly. It barely moves things." You ask the accountant about "admin," and she shrugs over the jammed printer. "Just covering a cost we hadn't planned for. It's temporary." Each answer is reasonable. You walk away feeling like someone who counts pennies.

By week nine you have bought a second set of boxes because the first split. You turned down the summer job, the one at the camp, with an email that took you three drafts. You told your family the tour was your big step. The van leaves at six in the morning, and your contract runs to the end of the leg, so leaving now would mean leaving all of it at once.

After load-out you try one more time. You say you are tired and could the lines be reviewed. The tour manager puts a hand on your shoulder. "You've come this far. You've been amazing out here. We'd hate to lose you." Nine weeks of late nights sit in your chest, and stopping now would make every one of them count for nothing. "I know," you say, and you hear that it is your sentence as much as his.

The next day the curtain behind the table is yours to hang. The tarp for the van is yours to roll. Nobody thanks you for either. Nobody has thanked you in weeks, and you cannot say when you stopped waiting to be thanked. When you open your mouth at the table, someone looks at a phone.

You sleep in the van with your cap over your eyes and wake before the alarm anyway. Dinner is whatever is left in the green room. The notes on your phone that used to be small scenes from the road, the story you came out here to get, are all numbers now. You tell your family the tour is going great and send them photos of the crowd, never the table. You have stopped adding up the settlement sheets in your head, because the total makes you feel foolish for every one you signed.

Tonight the sheet is on the folding table with a new line already at the bottom. The pen is in the float tin. You pick it up.

## The Name

That's **The Frog in Boiling Water**.

## Definition

The Frog in Boiling Water is the sequence where duties, deductions and expectations rise by steps so small that none triggers alarm, using Boundary Testing, Normalization, Minimization, Commitment Traps, Sunk Cost Exploitation, and Ambient Abuse to convert a worker's adaptability into a tolerated state of worsening conditions. In touring it concentrates in merch, crew and session roles where scope is informal, pay is by the day or by percentage, and settlement documents change line by line. It is hard to detect because each change is small, comes with a reason, and is asked of someone who wants the tour to succeed. What can be lost is the baseline sense of what is normal or acceptable, along with money and time. Intent would require additional evidence, because tours do change, costs do appear, and generous people do ask for help.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Boundary Testing** — Loading in the backline, staying for the last fan, setting up before soundcheck, counting the support act's stock. This functions by asking for a little more than the last time and observing whether it is accepted, so that each yes moves the line. Concern rises when each request is slightly larger than the previous one and no one agrees to a defined scope.

2. **Normalization** — "That's just the job." "It just makes sense." New lines on the settlement sheet that appear and then stay. This functions by turning each addition into the new default, so the original baseline fades from memory. Concern rises when changes are never announced as changes and are described as how things have always been.

3. **Minimization** — "It's nothing." "It barely moves things." "It's temporary." This functions by shrinking the apparent size of each step so that raising it feels petty. Concern rises when the person who raises a concern is made to feel small, and when the temporary line is still there next week.

4. **Commitment Traps** — The second set of boxes, the turned-down summer job, the van at six in the morning, a contract that runs to the end of the leg. This functions by increasing what the worker has invested and what leaving costs. Concern rises when changes arrive after the worker has committed money, time or opportunity.

5. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — "We'd hate to lose you." "You've come this far." This functions by converting past effort into the reason to stay, so that objecting feels like wasting what has been given. Concern rises when the appeal is to what has been invested rather than to what is fair now.

6. **Ambient Abuse** — The tarp that is now your job, the curtain that is yours to hang, the absence of thanks. This functions by a steady low pressure that follows any questioning without being tied to it. Concern rises when mild coldness appears after a question and nobody says why.

## Why This Order

**Boundary Testing** has to come first because the whole sequence depends on the first yes. A request that is twenty minutes of work gives the other side information: this person will say yes, and will not mention it. Everything later is built on that. It has to come first, because staying until the last fan leaves only feels like part of the job once you have already loaded in for free; start there instead and you would ask what it pays.

> Picture a new neighbour who asks to borrow your ladder, then your drill, then your parking spot for a weekend. Each ask is small. By the third one, you have said yes without anyone ever asking what you were willing to give. Flip it: if the neighbour treated your parking spot as theirs before asking for the ladder, you would ask why and say no.

**Normalization** turns each yes into a floor. Once the favor has been done three times it is no longer a favor, and the fourth request is not a request at all. The settlement sheet is the most elegant form of this: a new line that appears once and is never removed becomes part of the document, and the document is what everyone signs. It has to come before 'it barely moves things', because the staffing line only seems small once favors have already become 'just the job'; the other way round, you would ask why the line exists before signing the sheet.

> Imagine you cook dinner for a houseguest twice, as a kindness. By the third night they sit down at the table and wait, and nobody remembers who decided it was your job. Flip it: if the houseguest called cooking dinner "only a little thing" before you had ever cooked, you would ask why and let them cook instead.

**Minimization** protects the process from challenge. A staircase is only visible from the side. Described one step at a time, each increase is trivial, and anyone who objects is cast as someone who cannot see the bigger picture, although it is the bigger picture that matters. It has to come before you turn down the camp job, because the nightly nets only look harmless while each line is called nothing; the other way round, you would show your spreadsheet before giving up the summer offer.

> It is like a friend who says "it's only a few pounds" every time the group bill is split unevenly. You would feel petty bringing up a few pounds, even once the pounds have added up to a month's shopping. Flip it: if the friend asked you to sign up for a year of group bills before any "only a few pounds", you would add it up first and say no.

**Commitment Traps** raise the cost of leaving at exactly the moment the picture becomes clear. By the time the total is visible, the worker has spent savings, turned down alternatives and told the family. It has to come before 'you've come this far', because nine weeks only feel too costly to walk away from once the camp job is gone and your family told; the other way round, you would leave at the next city.

> Say you sign up for a year at a gym, buy the kit, and tell all your friends you are getting fit. When the first month shows you it is not for you, quitting means wasting the money, the kit and the announcement. Flip it: if someone said "you've come this far" before you had signed up for the gym, you would laugh and skip the year-long plan.

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** then makes the case for staying in the worker's own voice: after all of this, I cannot leave now. It has to come before the silence at the table, because missing thanks only stops mattering once you already tell yourself you cannot leave; the other way round, you would raise the curtain and tarp work at the next settlement.

> It is the feeling of sitting through a terrible film because you have already paid for the ticket and watched an hour of it. Flip it: if the people beside you sighed and glared before the film had even begun, you would notice it and simply move seats or leave.

**Ambient Abuse** supplies the background pressure that keeps questions from becoming a conversation. It has to come last, because silence and glances at phones only stop your questions once the extra lines are already normal; earlier, in week one, you would have asked plainly about any new charge on the sheet.

> Think of a workplace where nobody ever says thank you and every request comes with a sigh. You do not complain, because there is nothing you could point to, and you would only be asking for kindness. Flip it: if the sighs had started on your very first day, before you felt you couldn't leave, you would point them out and ask plainly for basic kindness.

What weakens it: an agreed written scope and settlement format before the first show; a private ledger that shows the whole staircase; asking about each new line when it first appears; and a trusted outsider who looks at the total. The key conversion point is the moment a favor becomes an unwritten duty.

## Individual Level

The worker may find that their sense of a normal day has shifted without their noticing. Over time, they may stop counting extra hours and tasks, and be surprised when they finally see the total.

They may feel embarrassed to raise a small deduction after accepting a series of small additions, and may tell themselves they should have said something earlier. They may begin to feel tired and slightly resentful without a clear source. They may keep working because leaving feels like wasting everything already given.

## Institutional Echo

Tours often work with flexible scope and day-rate or percentage arrangements, which makes incremental change easy. Where settlement sheets are produced by one party and signed nightly, new deduction lines can enter without a renegotiation. Merch deals with venues and artists can involve fees and splits whose terms are specific to each arrangement. Whether an employee or contractor relationship exists, and what protections apply, is jurisdiction-specific.

The honest reading is often that touring costs do arise, that small teams stretch roles, and that a request to stay late is part of a collaborative tour. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether additions consistently ran one way, whether deductions were explained in advance, and whether the same pattern has affected others.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that you pay your dues, that everyone pitches in, and that a good team member does not count the minutes. "We're a family" and "it's just for now" are the sentences that turn a changing contract into a mood.

The value exploited is a worker's willingness to be flexible, which is among the best things about people on the road. The public story is a band and its merch table, a happy small operation. It does not show the staircase on the settlement sheet.

## Observable Indicators

1. Duties expand beyond the original description without written agreement.
2. Requests arrive as favors and become expectations.
3. New deduction or fee lines appear on settlement sheets with brief or no explanation.
4. Lines described as temporary persist.
5. Concerns are answered with phrases such as "it's nothing" or "it barely moves things."
6. Changes are announced after the worker has committed money or time.
7. Appeals to loyalty or past investment are made when a concern is raised.
8. Mild coldness or extra chores follow a question.
9. Settlement sheets are presented for signature without time to review.
10. The worker cannot say what the original scope and settlement format were.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The original job description, offer message or agreement showing scope, pay and settlement method.
- Every nightly merch settlement sheet, in order, with dates and the lines that changed.
- A private ledger of your own counts, hours and tasks, kept as you go.
- Messages that request additional duties or announce new deductions, and your replies.
- Purchase records for supplies or stock you paid for.
- Names of others who had similar changes in scope or settlement.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Tour budgets move. Costs appear, small teams stretch, and a generous worker may help gladly. Most additions are exactly what they claim to be.

The discriminator is **direction, transparency and reversibility**. Genuine adjustments go both ways, are announced before they apply, and are removed when the reason ends.

- **Real unplanned costs.** A genuine new expense can be shared openly.
- **Shared workload.** Everyone may be doing more on a short-staffed tour.
- **Fairly agreed trade-offs.** Extra duties for extra pay are a deal, not a creep.
- **Honest accounting changes.** A line may simply clarify an existing cost.

Concern strengthens when: changes run one way and only toward more duties or lower net; temporary lines remain; objections are met with appeals to loyalty.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Baseline Test:**
Ask: "What was I hired to do, and what am I doing now?"
Concern rises if: the difference is large, unwritten and entirely in one direction.

**SPOT IT — The Staircase Test:**
Ask: "If these changes had been listed together on day one, would I have agreed?"
Concern rises if: the answer is no.

**SPOT IT — The Temporary Test:**
Ask: "Which of the temporary lines has ever been removed?"
Concern rises if: none has.

**SPOT IT — The Loyalty Test:**
Ask: "When I raise a concern, is the reply about the numbers or about how much I have already given?"
Concern rises if: the reply appeals to loyalty and sunk effort.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write the scope down early.** A short message confirming duties, hours, day rate and settlement format is a baseline.
- **Keep your own ledger.** Counts, hours, tasks and every line on every sheet. The staircase becomes visible from the side.
- **Ask about each new line when it appears,** calmly: "Could you tell me what the stock handling line covers, and when it ends?"
- **Sign nothing you have not had time to read.** Ask for the sheet before the end of the night.
- **Price your extras.** "Happy to load in the backline; that's outside my scope, so I'd like to add it to my day rate."
- **Check the total, not the step.** Once a week, compare today to your first week.
- **Get independent advice** on your arrangement, particularly on deductions and classification.
- **Keep your exit realistic.** Know what you would lose by leaving now, and what staying costs.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Duties growing without a written scope; new deduction lines with no explanation; temporary lines that persist; concerns met with minimization.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Deductions that materially change pay; changes announced only after you committed money or time; consequences for questioning; others reporting the same. Routes may include an independent attorney, a union or guild representative, a labor or wage authority in the relevant jurisdiction, and a creators' legal-aid group.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Pay withheld so that you cannot get home, passports or belongings held, threats connected to raising a concern, unsafe working conditions, or signs of crisis. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you confirm in writing what my duties and day rate are for the rest of the run?"
- "Could you tell me what the stock handling line covers, and when it ends?"
- "I'm glad to help with the load-in. Since it's outside my scope, can we add it to my day rate?"
- "I'd like a few minutes with the settlement sheet before I sign."
- "Last week's sheet had two fewer lines. Can you walk me through what changed?"
- "I'd like to keep a written record of what we agreed. Here is my summary; please correct me."
