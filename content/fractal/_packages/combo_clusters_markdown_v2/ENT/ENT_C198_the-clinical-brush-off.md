# C198: The Clinical Brush-Off — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're an orchestral oboist, forty-one, and your hands are the only instrument you cannot replace. For nineteen years you have sat in the second row of the woodwinds, making reeds at your kitchen table at midnight, shaving cane with a knife you have owned longer than your marriage. You know your hands the way a surgeon does. That is why you know, in the spring, that something has changed. It starts as an ache in the base of the right thumb after a long Mahler rehearsal. By June you are waking at four in the morning with the thumb curled and numb, and you count rests in the second movement for the chance to shake your hand out below the stand. In September you tell the personnel manager, quietly, that you would like to see someone. He is relieved. "We have a good occupational-health arrangement for exactly this."

The clinic is on the second floor of an administrative building two streets from the hall. The waiting room has a plant, a rack of leaflets on back pain, and a framed poster about hydration. The doctor is pleasant. He has been the orchestra's contracted adviser for years. He examines the hand for twelve minutes, presses the thumb joint, asks you to make a fist, and says, "Hmm. Nothing alarming there." You say that you wake at night with it numb. "That's very common in people under pressure." You say that it hurts most after playing. "Well, you do play a lot." The thumb throbs against the arm of the chair while he writes one line.

You say you are worried about your career. "I understand. But let's not jump to the worst case. You're a professional. You know how it is: a big season, a new principal, a lot going on." Your voice catches on the word *career*, and he lifts his pen. "You sound quite wound up about this. Let's keep it calm, shall we, and then we can think clearly." You hear yourself defending your calm, in a short appointment, about a hand that is numb at four in the morning. "I'd like a scan," you say, evenly. He nods as if you had asked for a second helping. "We can arrange an ultrasound. But I think we'll find nothing."

The scan is done on a Friday. The technician is quiet. You get dressed and wait to be told. No one tells you. Eleven days later a letter arrives, on the orchestra's letterhead and the clinic's, signed by the doctor. The first paragraph says that imaging showed "no significant abnormality." The second says that "symptoms are likely to reflect stress and the demands of a busy performing schedule" and recommends "relaxation strategies and a graded return to normal activity." The last line says that, as results are normal, "no further occupational-health follow-up is indicated at this time, and this file is now closed." You read the word "closed" four times.

You carry the letter to your car and sit in the car park with the engine off, the letter on the steering wheel. The ache in your thumb is exactly where it was this morning. You think: maybe it is stress. You think: maybe I am the sort of person who makes things bigger. You remember the words "difficult" and "fragile" floating through green rooms for as long as you have been in the business, and the colleague who played through a bad shoulder for two seasons and was rewarded with a solo. You tape the thumb before every rehearsal. You tell the personnel manager that the clinic said it's nothing, and he says, "Wonderful, I'm so glad."

You still make reeds at midnight, but you stop earlier, and you hold the knife differently. You wake at four and do not wake your spouse. You have stopped shaking the hand out below the stand and do it in the pocket of your coat in the corridor, where no one sees.

On Monday the play-through of the Strauss goes fine, and you do not quite notice what you have agreed to: that you will say nothing, play through, and not ask again.

## The Name

That's **The Clinical Brush-Off**.

## Definition

The Clinical Brush-Off is the sequence where a person's symptoms are treated as fragility, a normal result is used to end the conversation, concern is recast as catastrophizing, a catch-all explanation closes the file, and the person stops asking for help. It uses Minimization, Tone Policing, Accountability Evasion via Diagnosis / Trash-Can Diagnosis and Need Concealment to convert trust in professional care into lost timely treatment and lost trust in one's own body. In performing arts it is hard to detect because performers are used to pain, the clinic is often contracted by the employer, and a normal result feels like good news. This script helps recognize the pattern. It cannot say what any symptom means, and anyone with pain, numbness or loss of function should see a qualified clinician and consider a second opinion.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Minimization** — "Nothing alarming," "you do play a lot," a twelve-minute exam, a quiet reassurance that the worst case is unlikely. This functions by reducing the seriousness of what the person has described before it has been assessed. Concern rises when symptoms that affect work are treated as ordinary without an explanation of what was checked.

2. **Tone Policing** — "Let's not jump to the worst case," "I understand, but," the suggestion that worry is the problem. This functions by turning the person's concern into the thing being treated, so the content of the report is replaced with a comment on how she says it. Concern rises when emotion is named more than symptoms are explored.

3. **Accountability Evasion via Diagnosis / Trash-Can Diagnosis** — A letter attributing symptoms to "stress and the demands of a busy schedule" after a normal scan. This functions by supplying a broad, unfalsifiable explanation that closes the file and moves the cause from the workplace or the body to the person. Concern rises when a catch-all label is used without examination of the specific complaint, and no next step is offered.

4. **Need Concealment** — The oboist tapes the thumb, tells the manager it is nothing and does not ask again. This functions by teaching the person that a need is a risk, so she hides it, and the system never sees it. Concern rises when people stop reporting symptoms, plan around them in silence, or say, "I don't want to be seen as difficult."

## Why This Order

**Minimization** has to come first because it sets the register of the whole encounter. Once the doctor has said "nothing alarming" and the exam has lasted twelve minutes, every later statement from the performer has to push uphill against a verdict already delivered. It has to come first, because after 'nothing alarming' your worry about your career sounds out of scale; start with 'let's keep it calm' instead and you would say calmly that a numb thumb at four a.m. needs a scan.

> You tell a mechanic your car is making a noise. He listens for ten seconds and says, "Nothing to worry about, cars do that." You drive away with the noise. Flip it: if the mechanic told you to calm down before he had even listened to the noise, you would calmly ask him to look at the car properly.

**Tone policing** is what stops her pushing. If she raises a night symptom and is told that it is common under pressure, the discussion moves from the thumb to her state of mind. She is invited to defend her calm, which is a poor use of a short appointment. It has to come before the letter blaming 'stress', because being told you sounded wound up makes that word seem to fit; the other way round, you would ask what the scan showed and for a second opinion.

> You tell your landlord about the damp and he says, "Let's stay calm, no need to panic." Now you are proving you are calm, and the damp is not being discussed. Flip it: if your landlord blamed the damp on your own habits before any "stay calm", you would ask him to come and look at the wall.

The **trash-can diagnosis** is what closes the loop. A normal scan is a real result, and it is easy to hear it as the answer. The letter takes a narrow finding, that one test showed no obvious abnormality, and stretches it into a statement about cause. The word "closed" does what the examination did not: it ends the inquiry. It has to come before you tape the thumb in secret, because a letter naming stress makes the ache feel like your own failing; the other way round, you would still take the thumb to your own doctor.

> A shop can't find the fault in your kettle, so the manager says, "It's the way you use it." The kettle failed, and somehow the habit is yours. Flip it: if you had stopped mentioning the broken kettle before the manager blamed your habits, you would remember it is the shop's job and ask again.

**Need concealment** is the downstream product. Having been told, in effect, that the problem is her, she conceals the problem. It has to come last, because playing through in silence follows twelve minutes, a 'keep it calm' and a closed file; earlier, you would tell the personnel manager the thumb still wakes you and ask for another opinion.

> You stop mentioning your sore back at work, because the last time you did, people started calling you fragile. You keep lifting. Flip it: if you had gone quiet about your back before anyone said it was your own fault, you would have seen no reason to and told someone it hurts.

That is the key conversion point, because from here the system can report that no one has complained. What weakens the sequence is asking exactly what was examined and what was not, asking in writing whether a normal result rules anything out, requesting the report and images, and, most of all, seeing a second qualified clinician with no tie to the employer. A person does not need to be sure she is right to deserve a second look.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt her sense of her own body. Over time she may change her technique to hide a symptom, avoid telling colleagues, and accept an explanation she does not believe. She may take on more tape, more rest and more silent workarounds. She may wait longer before seeking help the next time, and may feel ashamed of having asked. A loss of trust in her own signals can last longer than the symptom.

## Institutional Echo

Many orchestras and touring companies use contracted occupational-health or wellness providers, which can place the clinician in a dual position between the performer and the employer. Where a clinic's findings go to management, or where a closed file is a convenient outcome for scheduling, a normal-result letter can serve both patient and schedule without anyone planning it. Performer injury, workload and recovery are areas where medical claims need subject-matter review. Reporting rules, confidentiality and rights to a second opinion are jurisdiction-specific. Intent would require additional evidence, because a busy clinic can be rushed and honest.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that artists suffer for their art and that a professional plays through. A second says that the tough player is the valuable one, and that complaints show weakness. A third is the reasonable belief that medicine is objective, and that a clean scan means a clean bill. The value being exploited is trust in the people who care for performers. The public story is a supportive orchestra with a clinic on call. The private cost, when this pattern is present, is a player who learns that the surest way to keep her place is to say nothing.

## Observable Indicators

1. A short examination is followed by a reassurance that does not state what was and was not checked.
2. Symptoms that interfere with work are described as common or expected.
3. The person's emotion or "pressure" is named more than the symptom is examined.
4. A normal result is presented as ending inquiry.
5. A letter attributes symptoms to stress or workload without reference to the specific complaint.
6. The letter closes the file and offers no review date.
7. Findings are shared with the employer more fully than with the person.
8. The person is told, or senses, that raising it again would be unhelpful.
9. Colleagues describe similar brush-offs.
10. The person starts hiding a symptom or reducing reporting.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The letter, its date, and any earlier or later correspondence from the clinic.
- Your own dated symptom diary: what you felt, when, during which repertoire or rehearsal, and how it changed.
- Any written referral, scan request, scan report or image copy you are entitled to request.
- A note of what the clinic told you at the visit, written the same day.
- Records of who the clinic reports to and what was shared with management.
- Playing schedule, workload and rehearsal changes around the onset.
- Medical or wellness accommodation requests and replies.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Many performer symptoms are minor and resolve with rest, and a normal scan is genuinely reassuring. A busy clinic may offer a short, honest reassurance. A recommendation of relaxation and graded return may be correct. Stress can contribute to physical symptoms, and naming it is not a brush-off. Doctors also cannot treat what is not found.

The discriminators are explanation, follow-up and openness to review. A sound clinical conversation says what was tested, what was not, what could still be present, what to do if symptoms continue, and when to come back.

Concern strengthens when: a catch-all explanation is offered without a specific plan; the person is told not to return; employer interests appear in the clinical decision; symptoms persist or worsen while the file stays closed.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Question Test:**
Ask: "Did anyone tell me exactly what was examined, and what was not?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "everything's fine" and no one can say which tests answer which questions.

**SPOT IT — The Door Test:**
Ask: "If my symptoms continue, who do I go to, and when?"
Concern rises if: the file is closed and no next step exists.

**SPOT IT — The Label Test:**
Ask: "Does this explanation fit my specific symptom, or would it fit anyone?"
Concern rises if: it would fit anyone.

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "Have I stopped telling people about this?"
Concern rises if: I now plan around the symptom quietly, because asking again feels risky.

## Chain Breakers

- Treat a persistent symptom as worth a second look by a qualified, independent clinician, such as your own doctor or a specialist in performers' health. This script cannot say what is wrong.
- Ask the clinic in writing for your report, images and the reasoning behind the letter.
- Ask: "What does a normal result rule out, and what does it not?"
- Ask who receives clinical information, and what is shared with management.
- Keep a dated symptom diary and note any change in function, numbness, weakness or night waking.
- Ask for the file to remain open for review, or for a referral to another provider.
- Tell one trusted colleague or your union or guild representative, and ask how others have been handled.
- If the symptoms get worse, or you have sudden weakness, severe numbness, loss of grip or other alarming signs, seek urgent medical care.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Symptoms continue after a "normal" letter; the file is closed without a plan; you find yourself hiding the symptom.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Function worsens; the employer is receiving fuller findings than you; workload is increased despite a documented concern; colleagues describe similar brush-offs. Consider an independent specialist, a performing-arts health service, your own doctor, a musicians' union or guild representative, an occupational-health regulator or complaints route, and an employment or injury adviser.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Sudden weakness, severe numbness, loss of function, signs of a medical emergency, or distress and thoughts of self-harm. Contact emergency services or a crisis line. Do not wait for a letter, and do not rely on the employer's clinic alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Thank you for the letter. Could you tell me which tests were done and what they can and cannot rule out?"
- "My symptoms are continuing. What should I do if they persist?"
- "I'd like a copy of my report and images, please."
- "I'd like to see a second clinician outside the orchestra. Can you send my records?"
- "Please keep my file open until I've had that review."
- "Could you tell me what information goes to management?"
- "I'm not asking to stop working. I'm asking to be sure what this is."
