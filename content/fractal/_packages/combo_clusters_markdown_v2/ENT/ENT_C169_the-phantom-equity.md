# C169: The Phantom Equity — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a graphic and merch designer, twenty-nine, and for the last fourteen months you have been the visual identity of a small indie imprint. Every cover, every shirt, every tour poster, the little hand-cut logo in the corner of the release pages: yours. You work from a hot desk in a shared coworking space, three days a week, at a rate you quoted in the first week, when the founder said the sentence that made the rest of it feel reasonable.

He said it over coffee. "I can't pay you what you're worth yet. But I don't want a vendor. I want a founder. You're going to be a founding contributor, with a real stake in what we're building. We're all in this together." You said yes before he finished. You had spent six years designing for bands who paid in exposure and thanks. The imprint pays about a third of your usual price for a campaign. You do the difference in your head as ownership, and it feels like a good deal, and nobody argues with it, because it sounds like belonging.

You ask about paperwork, once, in the second month. He is warm about it. "Totally. We'll paper it properly when we close the round, so we only do it once. Lawyers charge by the hour and it all gets restated at the raise anyway. It's a formality, I promise." Every Friday the imprint holds a sync, and somewhere in the middle he shares a tab called Founding Contributors. Four names. Yours is third. Next to yours, where the others show a percentage, the cell says PENDING, in grey italics. He scrolls past it quickly, the way you would scroll past a cell that was only waiting for a formula.

In month four you ask what the grey cell is waiting for. He sends a long, patient message about pools, vesting cliffs, conversion mechanisms, and how your stake needs to be sized after the new money goes in. You read it three times. It sounds like someone who knows what he is doing, and you do not want to be the person who asks him to explain it a fourth time. You still have no page.

In month five, the round slips. "Investors want to see the second release first." In month eight the second release lands, and the paperwork now waits on the next raise. You put another quarter of covers, shirts and posters into the pile, and the date you were waiting for is somewhere behind you.

In month ten, a new name appears in the tab, with a percentage and a footnote that says fully diluted, pre-round. Yours still says PENDING, but the footnote is longer now. The founder says the pool has to be reshaped for the incoming lead, that everyone's number will be smaller than anyone expected, and that this is actually good news, because a smaller slice of something much bigger is the whole point. On the call everyone says congratulations, and you say it too.

Today is Friday. You are at the hot desk with your headphones around your neck, and the spreadsheet is on the screen in front of you. Someone on the call asks, almost as a joke, when the paperwork lands. And he says, kindly, without hesitating, "Right after the next funding round. It's literally the first thing." You have been told that sentence, in some form, six times. Last month a different imprint asked you for a full campaign at your normal rate, in cash, no stake. You said you could not take it, because you were committed here. Leaving would mean leaving the grey cell behind, and there is nothing signed that you could carry out of the door.

You wrote down the number you did not charge. You have never added it up. You do not add it up now, because if you add it up, you will have to ask what the grey cell is worth. You have stopped sending your portfolio to anyone else, and when a friend asks what you own of everything you have made, you change the subject. On Sundays you open the spreadsheet to look at the cell, though nobody has touched it, and you close it again.

Somebody laughs. The founder says, "Okay, quick wins." You nod. You open the next file, a shirt design for the spring run, and you start working.

## The Name

That's **The Phantom Equity**.

## Definition

The Phantom Equity is the sequence where a promise of deferred ownership is used to buy discounted design, production or writing work, using Alignment Framing, Paper Deferral, Complexity Shield, Milestone Migration, Dilution Disguise and Exit Forfeiture to convert a creative worker's belief in a future stake into below-market labor and an unpapered claim. It benefits whoever receives the work without carrying an enforceable obligation. It is hard to detect because every step has an ordinary explanation: rounds slip, lawyers are expensive, equity is technical. The worker may lose the gap between market price and pay, and a stake that may never have existed on paper. Intent would require additional evidence, since many early promises are sincere and simply never formalized.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Alignment Framing** — The offer is described as partnership: "founding contributor," "not a vendor," "all in this together." This functions by recasting a discounted rate as shared sacrifice, so asking for market pay feels like leaving the team. Concern rises when the partnership language is frequent but no document states the stake, the vesting or what was given up.

2. **Paper Deferral** — The documents are promised for later, tied to an event outside the worker's control. This functions by letting work begin on trust while the only record is a conversation and a spreadsheet the other side controls. Concern rises when months pass, work keeps being delivered, and each request for a draft meets a new reason to wait.

3. **Complexity Shield** — Questions are answered with explanation instead of a document: pools, cliffs, conversion, post-money sizing. This functions by making a second question feel like admitting ignorance, and by trading a page the worker could show an adviser for fluency. Concern rises when the explanations grow longer while the paper stays absent, or a one-page term sheet is declined as premature.

4. **Milestone Migration** — The trigger for the paperwork keeps moving: the round, then the second release, then the next raise. This functions by keeping the finish line visible and never reachable, so effort already spent argues for one more cycle. Concern rises when each new trigger arrives as the last expires and none is written down with a date.

5. **Dilution Disguise** — Whatever appears is smaller than described, and the shrinkage is presented as success. This functions by turning a shrinking promise into good news before any figure exists to compare with the original words. Concern rises when others' percentages are shown while the worker's stays blank, or the original verbal terms are called misremembered.

6. **Exit Forfeiture** — Leaving means leaving the stake behind, because nothing signed could carry it out the door. This functions by making the unpapered promise the largest reason to stay and the past discount a reason not to look at the bill. Concern rises when departure is called abandoning the team, the stake is said to lapse, or a release is demanded before any payment.

## Why This Order

**Alignment Framing** comes first because it changes what the transaction is. Before it, the designer is a vendor with a price. After it, she is a believer with a future, and the discount is a contribution rather than a concession. Nothing else works until the price is emotionally off the table. It has to come first, because "we'll paper it at the round" only sounds fair once you see yourself as a founder rather than a vendor at a third of your price; start with the delay instead and you would ask for your full rate until a page existed.

> A friend asks you to help them move and says, "You're basically family." Helping for a slice of pizza feels like belonging, not like a favour you could have put a price on. Plenty of real partnerships start warmly; what matters is what is written down once the warmth has been spent. Flip it: if your friend said "we'll sort out what I owe you later" before calling you family, you would ask to agree it now.

**Paper Deferral** is the cheapest way to hold that frame. A document would have to state a number, a vesting condition and an end date, and each could be tested against the discount she is giving. A promise to document later lets the work start with nothing to test, and it gives the best possible deniability: no one has refused anything, they have only said not yet. It has to come before the long message about pools and cliffs, because a lesson can only stand in for an answer while there is no page to point to; the other way round, you would hold a signed number and could ask him to explain just that line.

> A landlord says, "We'll sort out the lease after you've moved in." You carry the boxes up the stairs with nothing in your hand to check. Flip it: if the landlord gave you ten minutes on tricky legal words before you moved in, you would ask for the lease in your hand first.

The **Complexity Shield** protects the deferral from its natural enemy, the direct question. The answer is not no, it is a lesson. That turns a request for a page into a request for an education she is embarrassed to need, and moves the conversation onto ground where the other side is more fluent. Independent evaluation weakens most here, because the safest-looking move is to stop asking. It has to come before the round slips to the next raise, because the date can only move quietly once you feel too unsure to ask a fourth time; the other way round, you would see month five's slip and ask plainly for a written date.

> You ask a garage what you are being charged for and get ten minutes on valves and gaskets. You leave feeling you should have known, and still without a figure. Flip it: if the garage said "we'll give you the figure after the next job" before any talk of valves, you would ask for the figure now.

**Milestone Migration** raises the cost of exit while the door still looks open. Each time the trigger moves, she has given another quarter of work, so her sunk cost grows as fast as her patience. It has to come before the new name with a percentage, because a smaller-than-expected slice only sounds like good news once eight months of covers and shirts are already in; the other way round, you would hear about the reshaped pool early and ask for your number before the next quarter.

> A boss says, "Finish this project and then we'll talk about your raise." You finish it, and there is a new project. Flip it: if your boss said the raise would be shared among more people before you finished the project, you would ask for your own number in writing.

**Dilution Disguise** covers the one moment the pattern could break, the day a real number must be shown. By announcing in advance that everyone's number will be smaller, the eventual figure reads as ordinary instead of as a gap against what was said. It has to come before you turn down the cash campaign, because staying only feels worth it while the grey cell might still hold a real share; the other way round, you would take the paid work and ask him to send your number when it existed.

> A cake is cut into twice as many pieces, and the host says, "Isn't it great that so many people came?" Your slice is thin, and you are the one saying thank you. Flip it: if the host said leaving early means no cake before cutting it thinner, you would laugh, say thanks, and go when you liked.

**Exit Forfeiture** closes the loop and needs no one to say anything. Because the stake exists only as a promise, it cannot go with her. The more she has given, the more it costs to walk away from a document that was never created. It has to come last, because the PENDING cell only keeps you after the founder talk, the delays, the lessons and the shrinking slice; earlier, you would add up the number you did not charge and ask for your stake in writing.

> You have been collecting stamps on one café's loyalty card for a year. Leaving means leaving the stamps, so you keep going back for coffee you no longer want. Flip it: if the café had made each stamp worth less before you had a year's worth, you would see the card for what it is and drink coffee elsewhere.

What weakens the sequence: a rough one-page term sheet in the first month, a request for the cap-table legend, a running tally of the rate difference, and an independent adviser who says plainly what exists and what does not.

## Individual Level

The designer may begin to turn down paid work because she feels committed, and then count the refusal as proof of belonging. Over time, she may stop pricing her own hours, because a number would make the arrangement look like a loss rather than an investment.

She may ask less each month, not because she trusts more but because each question costs more, and she may defend the founder in the same sentences he gave her. The pride is real, and pride and arrangement have become hard to separate.

## Institutional Echo

Early-stage imprints and creator ventures often rely on deferred compensation because cash is genuinely short. A promised stake can be sincere, and informal arrangements that were never formalized are common. The same structure can function as a retention device when the promise stays out of any document and contributors rely on a spreadsheet one party controls.

A cap-table sheet shown at a weekly meeting creates a sense of record, but it is not an agreement and records only what its owner types.

The honest reading is that fundraising does slip, pools are reshaped for incoming investors, and small founders avoid legal fees until a raise. Intent would require additional evidence: whether similar promises went to other contributors, whether drafts were ever prepared, and whether triggers changed without a record. How equity or profit shares are treated at law varies by place.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that creative people are rewarded in the end, and that those who believed early and paid in sweat will be remembered. Beside it sits the older idea that exposure and belonging are a form of wage, and that asking for your price inside a scene built on friendship is a failure of loyalty.

The value being exploited is the real wish to own what you help create. Nothing here argues against taking a stake in a good project, or working below your rate for work you believe in. People have done both and been well served.

The public story is a founding team in front of a mural, four names on a slide. The private story is a grey cell that has been grey for fourteen months.

## Observable Indicators

1. A stake, "founding" status or "backend" is described verbally, with no document stating its size, vesting or end date.
2. The rate paid is well below the worker's usual price, and the difference is described as an investment.
3. Documentation is promised for after an event the worker does not control.
4. The triggering event changes more than once, with no written record.
5. An ownership sheet shows others' percentages while the worker's entry says pending or is blank.
6. Questions about terms get long technical explanations and no page to review.
7. A one-page summary is declined as premature or as a sign of distrust.
8. New contributors or investors appear in the sheet before the worker's entry is resolved.
9. The promised stake is described as reduced, with the reduction presented as good news.
10. Outside paid work is discouraged or declined because of the commitment.
11. Any mention of leaving is answered with talk of loss, or a request to sign a release.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The first messages offering the stake, "founding" status or "backend," with dates.
- Every message promising paperwork "after the round" or any other trigger, in order.
- Screenshots, taken lawfully from a meeting you were invited to, of any contributor tab.
- A running log of hours, your normal rate, and the difference.
- Invoices sent, invoices paid, and any you chose not to send.
- Project files and version histories showing what you delivered and when.
- Names of other contributors who were promised stakes, and what they were told.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Equity-for-work is a legitimate way to build a small venture, and a sincere promise can fail for ordinary reasons.

- **A real, documented founding deal.** A contributor agreement exists, states the share, and the worker has had it reviewed.
- **Genuine funding delay.** The round slips, and the founder keeps the worker informed, shares drafts and offers interim written terms.
- **A good-faith restructure.** An investor requires the pool to be reshaped, and everyone sees the same figures at the same time.
- **Honest informality.** A tiny team that never reached a lawyer, and welcomes the request to write it down.

Concern strengthens when: the paperwork trigger moves more than once without a written record; the worker's entry stays blank while others receive percentages; a one-page written summary is refused.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The One-Page Test:**
Ask: "Can we put what I get, what I give, and when it ends on one page this month?"
Concern rises if: the answer is a reason to wait, or an explanation instead of a page.

**SPOT IT — The Trigger Test:**
Ask: "What exact event releases the paperwork, and by what date must it happen?"
Concern rises if: the event has already changed once and no date is offered.

**SPOT IT — The Pending Cell Test:**
Ask: "Why does my row say pending when others show figures, and what number belongs there today?"
Concern rises if: the figure is called unknowable, or likely to be smaller.

**SPOT IT — The Walk-Away Test:**
Ask: "If I stopped today, what would I keep, in writing?"
Concern rises if: the honest answer is nothing, or leaving is described as abandoning the team.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for a one-page term sheet now.** Rough is enough: a share, a trigger date, what happens on departure. The point is a thing you can show an adviser.
- **Price the work openly.** Invoice at your normal rate with the discount shown as a line, so the record states what you gave.
- **Keep a dated tally.** Hours, rate, difference. Five minutes a month turns a feeling into a figure.
- **Ask who benefits from the delay,** and whether a short interim written agreement can cover the wait.
- **Get independent advice before the next delivery.** Choose an entertainment attorney or adviser with no tie to the imprint, and ask what you hold and what you do not.
- **Do not sign a release or "thank you" document in exchange for payment without advice.** Read what it takes away first.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** The paperwork trigger has moved once; your row stays pending while others show figures; explanations arrive without a document; you are declining paid work because of the promise.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A reduced stake is announced with no written comparison to the original words; a release is offered in return for any payment; you are asked to stop work or lose the stake; other contributors describe the same sequence; the imprint is about to be sold, merged or funded while your claim is unwritten. Relevant routes may include an independent entertainment or commercial attorney, an artist legal-aid clinic, a creators' guild or union where one exists, and mediation if the arrangement names it. Whether an unwritten promise can be relied upon is jurisdiction-specific.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Any threat tied to your work, name or files; blackmail over unreleased material; stalking or harassment after you raise the issue; or distress so severe you cannot tell whether to keep going. Do not confront. Move to safety, tell a trusted person, and contact emergency services or a local crisis line.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before I start the spring run, can we put the founding-contributor terms on one page, even roughly?"
- "What exact event triggers the paperwork, and by what date should it happen?"
- "My understanding is that my stake is [X], vesting on [Y]. Is that what you have in mind?"
- "Could you send the legend for the pending cell in my row, and the figure that belongs there today?"
- "I will invoice at my normal rate with the founder discount shown as a line, so we both have a clean record."
- "I would like my adviser to read the draft before I deliver anything else. Is that all right?"
