# C022: The Sunk Cost Prison — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a thirty-eight-year-old heavy-metal frontman, sitting on an overturned bass cabinet in the rehearsal space you have rented since you were twenty-nine, with a green-covered ledger open on your knee and your phone on speaker on the amp beside you. The label's A&R rep is on the line, and she is being lovely. What you want is the one thing you have wanted for nine years: for the record to come out.

The ledger is yours. You began it in the first year because your drummer said that if you were going to do this, you should know what it cost. Nine years of entries in your cramped hand. Rent on the room. Strings. Drum heads. Two rounds of van repairs. Studio days paid in cash. A mastering engineer, a photographer, and a long line of entries in a smaller hand from the lean year when you drove for a delivery app between sessions. At the bottom of the last page is a total, and when you look at it your stomach drops. The advance, three years ago, was modest, and it was less than you had spent. "All that work deserves to be heard," the rep said then, and she says it again now. You look at the total and cannot find a way to answer a sentence like that.

With the advance came a delivery commitment letter, one page, which you read at a coffee shop and signed that afternoon because she had said, kindly, "This just says we both want a great record." It obliges you to deliver an album "of commercially satisfactory quality" by a date the label may extend at its discretion, and an option follows delivery. It does not say what satisfactory means or who decides. The pen was still warm in your hand when you handed it back. A great record was the plan.

Eighteen months ago you delivered the first mixes. She called that same afternoon. "We're so close," she said. "This is it. One more push and we're there." Before you hung up she added, "You're the best." You told the drummer to hold the spring free, and you turned down a month of paid shows to keep it clear. You drove home with the windows down, and you were singing.

The notes came on a call. The songs were heavy, which was good, but the singles needed to be "more accessible." You rewrote and delivered again. A different executive had joined by then, and the new priority was a "more modern sound." You recut the vocals twice. "Honestly," she said, pleasantly, "I'll know it when I hear it." In the spring the label's marketing person thought a feature would help. You could have a feature, but the record would need to be re-sequenced and some songs replaced. Each note arrived like a favor, and each time you said thank you. Somewhere in there you stopped writing for a sound and began writing for a face you imagined across a desk.

Today, on the speaker, she says: "I want to give you a heads up. The commercially satisfactory piece is under review. The team feels we're not quite there on the first single. I'm not saying it's a problem. We're just reflecting." Then, softer: "We're all in this together. I'd hate for all that work not to see the light of day." Your chest goes tight at the thought of the record never appearing. You think of the five people at the label who have gone to bat for you. You feel the heat of an ungrateful man who was about to ask a question.

You could ask what satisfactory means. You could ask for it in writing. You could ask when the date is. But you have heard her answer nine times in nine ways, each one sounding like generosity, and none of them ever told you anything. "I know it's been a long road." So you say, "Sure. Send me the notes." She thanks you for being such a pro. You notice that you have been smiling at a phone for twenty minutes.

You have not picked up a guitar for pleasure since the spring. When your drummer texts to ask how it's going, you write "close" and put the phone face down. You wake at three and do sums in the dark. You skip the shows of bands you used to drive hours to see, because someone will ask. You cannot remember how the first demo sounded before anyone had an opinion about it.

When the call ends the room is quiet except for the compressor humming on the far wall. You pick up the pen, and your hand knows how to add a line. You write the date and the word "call," and in the column where the money goes you leave the space empty. You have thought of leaving twice this year, and both times the thought ended with the same sentence: I can't walk away from nine years.

## The Name

That's **The Sunk Cost Prison**.

## Definition

The Sunk Cost Prison is the sequence where years of investment become the bars that keep an artist in a deal, using Sunk Cost Exploitation, Commitment Traps, Future Faking, Moving Goalposts, FOG, and Learned Helplessness to convert the artist's reluctance to write off the past into a loss of clear judgment about the future. It benefits the party whose delivery standard, option and approvals remain undefined, and it exploits the ordinary human difficulty of treating money and years already spent as gone. It is hard to detect because the work continues, the encouragement is real and each revision request sounds reasonable. What the person may lose is clear-eyed evaluation of the future independent of the past. Intent would require additional evidence, since creative development genuinely takes time and executives do change their minds in good faith.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — The ledger of nine years, the advance that was smaller than the spending, the repeated reminder that "all that work" deserves to be released. This functions by making past investment the argument for continued investment, whatever the prospects. Concern rises when the past is cited as a reason to accept terms that the future alone would not justify.

2. **Commitment Traps** — The delivery commitment letter, signed quickly, with an undefined standard and a date that may be extended at the label's discretion; the option language that follows delivery. This functions by creating obligations that are easy to enter and costly to exit, with the exit conditions held by the other party. Concern rises when the standard for acceptance is undefined and decided by one side, and when the artist is unsure what happens if the record is not accepted.

3. **Future Faking** — "We're so close." "One more push and we're there." "You're the best." This functions by holding out an imminent release and a better future, so that waiting seems to be the rational choice. Concern rises when the promised milestone recedes after each delivery and no date is attached to the promise.

4. **Moving Goalposts** — More accessible singles, then a more modern sound, then a feature, then re-sequencing. This functions by redefining success after the artist has met the last definition. Concern rises when the changes are in direction rather than refinement, when each is announced by a different person, and when none is put in writing.

5. **FOG** — Fear of the record never appearing, obligation to a team that believes in you, guilt for questioning generosity: "I'd hate for all that work not to see the light of day." This functions by keeping attention on feelings rather than on the terms. Concern rises when every query about terms is answered with warmth and none with a date, a standard or a document.

6. **Learned Helplessness** — The artist does not ask what satisfactory means because the last nine answers did not help. This functions by teaching that effort and questions do not change outcomes, so the person stops trying. Concern rises when the artist has stopped asking, stopped negotiating, and describes the situation as simply how it is.

## Why This Order

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** has to be first, although it is often the last thing noticed. The pattern only works on someone who has already invested, so the investment is the ground everything stands on. Here it begins before the label appears: the nine years of self-funding are the raw material. It has to come first, because the one-page letter only feels like a lifeline once the ledger total outweighs the advance; start with the letter and you would ask what 'commercially satisfactory' means before signing.

> You have been fixing up an old car for years. Every time you think of selling it, you add up what you have spent, not what it is worth today. Flip it: if someone asked you to sign a vague promise about the old car before you had spent anything on it, you would ask what it means and stay free to sell.

**Commitment Traps** then convert investment into obligation. The delivery commitment letter is the structure. It looks like enthusiasm on paper, while its key term is left empty. An undefined standard is more powerful than a strict one, because it cannot be met or disputed. It has to come before 'we're so close', because that promise only keeps you waiting once you have signed to a standard nobody defined; the other way round, you would answer 'one more push' by asking for the date in writing.

> You agree to "tidy up the garden" for a neighbour, with no word on what tidy means. A signed, one-line note is easy to give. It is impossible to finish. Flip it: if your neighbour kept saying "nearly there" before you had agreed to anything, you would ask what tidy means before giving any note.

**Future Faking** supplies the reason to stay. If the standard is unknowable but the release seems close, waiting looks sensible. The promise needs no date; "so close" does the work. It has to come before the shifting notes, because each rewrite only feels like the final push once release seems close; the other way round, the third new request would make you ask who decides and by when.

> A builder says, "Nearly done, just a few more days." You keep the spare room empty for months because the day is always just ahead. Flip it: if the builder kept changing the plans before saying "just a few more days", you would ask who decides and by when.

**Moving Goalposts** is the operative mechanism. Because the standard was never defined, it can shift with each new executive and each new opinion, and each shift looks like help. This is the key conversion point: the artist stops delivering to a standard and starts delivering to a person. It has to come before the warm, worried call, because 'under review' only frightens you once the standard has moved again and again; the other way round, you would simply ask her to put the standard in writing.

> You finish your essay to the brief. The teacher asks for a different tone, then a different length, then a different topic. By the end you are guessing at the teacher's mood, not answering the question. Flip it: if the teacher sounded kind and worried at once before changing the brief, you would ask plainly what the brief actually is.

**FOG** weakens independent evaluation. By surrounding each shift with warmth and mild alarm, it makes the artist uncomfortable asking for specifics. It has to come before you stop asking, because giving up only feels sensible once every question would sound ungrateful; the other way round, you would still ask what satisfactory means and when the date is.

> A manager says "We're all rooting for you" in the same breath as "I'd hate for this to fall through." You'd feel rude asking what exactly is wrong. Flip it: if you were told nothing you do matters before any "we're all rooting for you", you would laugh it off and ask what exactly is wrong.

**Learned Helplessness** closes the loop, and it supplies plausible deniability. By the end, the artist has stopped asking and stopped pushing. Nobody forced anything. The artist simply concluded that asking was pointless. It has to come last, because the empty money column only follows nine answers that told you nothing; earlier, after the first changed note, you would have called your drummer and asked whether to keep the spring free.

> You phoned the help line nine times and got nine different answers. The tenth time, you don't call. Flip it: if you had felt like not calling before getting any of the nine answers, you would notice it, ring anyway and ask for one clear answer.

What weakens the sequence: a defined acceptance standard before signing, independent advice on the option and delivery language, written notes after every call, and a periodic exercise of pricing the future as if the past had not happened.

## Individual Level

The artist may begin to do the arithmetic of the past obsessively and the arithmetic of the future not at all. Over time, the person may become reluctant to look at the ledger, may measure every decision against what has been spent, and may start to see walking away as proof of wasted years. They may stop asking questions that have not been answered before, and describe the label's changes as "just the process." They may lose pleasure in the songs themselves, since each is now a deliverable. They may feel ashamed of the length of time, and silent about it, which prevents anyone from saying that nine years in is not an argument for a tenth.

## Institutional Echo

Development deals, delivery commitments and option periods can operationalize this pattern without any plan. Where delivery clauses use terms such as "commercially satisfactory" without defining the standard or the decision-maker, the artist's compliance cannot be tested. Where A&R staff change during a project, new priorities can reset the target. Where the label's discretion to extend dates is one-sided, the artist's uncertainty can continue for years.

The honest reading is that records do need revision, executives do change, and an A&R who says "we're so close" may believe it. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether the standard has been described in writing, whether the changes have been in direction rather than polish, and whether the same sequence has occurred with other artists.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that persistence is virtue and that quitting is failure. Alongside it sits the idea that the one who keeps going will be rewarded and that every struggling artist is nearly at the break. The value being exploited is perseverance. The language that sounds noble is paying your dues. The public story is the overnight success after a decade of work. It is honest in some cases and tells nothing about those who kept going because they could not bear to stop.

## Observable Indicators

1. The delivery standard is described only in general terms such as "satisfactory" or "accessible."
2. Acceptance decisions are made by one party, with no stated criteria.
3. Feedback changes direction after each delivery rather than refining the previous one.
4. Different executives give different standards.
5. Notes are given on calls but are not put in writing.
6. The delivery date can be extended at one party's discretion, and is.
7. Milestones such as "so close" recede after each delivery.
8. The artist's past spending is mentioned when terms or exit are discussed.
9. Questions about the standard are answered with warmth and without detail.
10. The artist has stopped asking about terms.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The delivery commitment letter and the full recording agreement, with dates, including any option and extension language.
- A dated log of every delivery and every response, with the name of the person giving feedback.
- Written notes or emails after calls; if there are none, your own summary sent the same day.
- Your ledger of spending, with receipts and invoices where you have them.
- Any message describing what "commercially satisfactory" means, or declining to.
- Records of changes in label staff or A&R during the project.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Records genuinely need revision, and a long development can end in a better album. The discriminator is whether the target is becoming clearer or vaguer over time.

Benign lookalikes: constructive notes that refine rather than redirect; a delay caused by a real release-window problem; a new executive with a legitimate and communicated change of strategy; a clearly defined standard that you have not met.

Concern strengthens when: the standard has never been defined in writing; each delivery produces a different direction; and the extension of dates is used without a stated reason.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Definition Test:**
Ask: "What exactly does 'commercially satisfactory' mean, and who decides?"
Concern rises if: no one will put it in writing, or the answer changes with the person asked.

**SPOT IT — The Direction Test:**
Ask: "Are the latest notes refining the last version, or pointing somewhere new?"
Concern rises if: each round redirects the record and the previous round's work is discarded.

**SPOT IT — The Clean-Slate Test:**
Ask: "If I had spent nothing so far, would I sign this today?"
Concern rises if: the only honest reason to continue is what has already been spent.

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "When did I last ask a hard question about terms, and why did I stop?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that questions never changed anything.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the standard in writing.** "Could you confirm in an email what 'commercially satisfactory' means for this delivery, who decides, and by when?"
- **Send a written summary after every call.** "Per our call, my understanding is that the notes are... Please let me know if I have missed anything."
- **Have an independent entertainment attorney read the letter and the option language,** including what happens if the album is not accepted.
- **Price the future as if the past were free.** Write down what the next year would cost, what you could realistically gain, and what other routes exist, and set it beside the ledger.
- **Ask for a definite date or a clear exit path** for acceptance, rework or release of the recordings.
- **Look at the ledger with someone.** A trusted peer or accountant can see the ledger without the shame.
- **Keep other work alive.** Material, shows and a small audience outside the deal protect your judgment.
- **Do not agree to another round without a written brief,** including the criteria on which acceptance will be decided.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** The standard is undefined and the direction changes after delivery; notes are given only by phone; questions about terms are met with warmth and nothing else.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Several rounds of redirection with no written criteria; options or extensions are used to extend obligation without acceptance; further investment is requested of you; other artists describe the same experience. Relevant routes may include an entertainment attorney with no tie to the label, a royalty auditor or forensic music accountant, a musicians' union or guild representative, or a mediation process named in the agreement.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, harassment or intimidation connected to the dispute; threats to blacklist that are paired with threats of harm; coercion tied to your health or safety; or signs of a mental-health crisis from years of strain. Contact emergency services or local crisis support, and tell a trusted person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you confirm in writing what 'commercially satisfactory' means for this delivery and who makes the call?"
- "I'd like a written summary of these notes so I can be sure I've understood them."
- "Before the next revision, can we agree in writing on what acceptance looks like?"
- "I'd like my attorney to look at the delivery letter and the option language before I begin another round."
- "Is there a date by which the label will either accept the album or release it back to me?"
- "I'm happy to keep working. I need a defined target to work toward."

## Reflection

If a stranger handed you the ledger and asked whether to spend one more year, what would you tell them, and why does it sound different when it is yours?
