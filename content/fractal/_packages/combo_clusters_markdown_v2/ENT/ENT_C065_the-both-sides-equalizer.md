# C065: The "Both Sides" Equalizer — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a bass player, thirty-one, and for six years you have been the quiet one in a band that is no longer quiet. You load the van first. You know which amp hums. You write the bass lines, and over six years they have become a good part of what people hum back at the shows. What you want is small: your name back on the writing line of the release forms, where it was dropped twice as "just a clerical thing." You have asked twice, in writing. The answers were warm and vague. Then, the night a headline slot fell through, you wrote an email at two in the morning with one sentence you would not stand behind in daylight.

Three days later the band's management office calls a meeting. It is a nice room: a glass wall, a bowl of green apples, a framed platinum record that is not your band's. The manager, who has known you all since the van days, says everyone is here because everyone cares. A mediator sits at the head of the table with a legal pad, calm and kind, with no stake in anything except a good outcome. "This has been a hard season for both of you," she says. The word "both" lands in the room like a coat on a chair. You came to talk about credits. She says that can be part of it, later.

First, she says, she wants to understand "the dynamic." Each of you gets the same number of minutes. Each of you is asked to name one thing the other does that is hard. The frontman says you are "reactive." You say you were not credited, were mocked in front of the crew, and were asked in a group text whether you were "still in this band or just visiting." She writes it all down, evenly. Objecting to the fairness of it would sound like asking for more than your share.

Afterwards the manager walks you both through a one-page Mutual Conduct Agreement. You agree to "communicate respectfully," to "raise concerns first through management," and to "treat the contents of this process as confidential." It says that "both parties acknowledge that the tension arose from shared communication challenges." It says nothing about credits or release forms. You ask whether the credits can go on the page. "That's a separate business conversation." The frontman says, gently, that he is happy to sign, "if it helps us move on." Everyone looks at you. The pen is already on the table. You think about what it will look like if you are the only one who will not sign something that says "mutual." You sign. He signs.

The mediator's notes are typed up and clipped to the folder: two matching columns. His column quotes your two a.m. email in full, the one sentence included. Yours has three short lines, "credits," "visiting," "excluded," for six years of small things nobody wrote down. The columns are the same length. You look from one to the other and cannot find the place where it stopped being even.

At the door the manager hands you a note. The file is private and not to be discussed outside the process. You think of showing the agreement to a lawyer, then of the rest of the band, then of your sister, and each one is now a breach of something you signed.

"Thank you," the manager says, warmly. "This is what a healthy band looks like." A copy goes into the shared drive.

You still load the van first. At soundcheck you play the lines and say nothing else, and you have not brought a new one to rehearsal since. You reread the email at night, hunting the sentence, and wake at four with the word "both" in your head. When your sister asks how the band is, you say "fine" and ask about her week. You no longer trust your own account of those six years.

On the drive home you keep both hands on the wheel and the radio off. A bass line comes to you at a red light, and you let it go.

## The Name

That's **The "Both Sides" Equalizer**.

## Definition

The "Both Sides" Equalizer is the sequence where a one-directional harm is recharacterized as a two-way conflict, using Conflict Reframing, Procedural Symmetry, Mutual Agreement Fabrication, File Equalization, Privacy Weaponization and Closure Manufacture to convert a person's trust that a resolution process weighs facts into the loss of recognition of who was actually harmed. It can function as the opposite of fairness while looking like fairness itself: identical treatment of unequal situations. It is hard to detect because genuine mutual conflict is common in bands and a balanced process is often the correct one. What the person may lose is the record that one side was harmed, along with credit, standing and the ability to raise the original issue. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Conflict Reframing** — "This has been a hard season for both of you." This functions by changing the question from what one person did to what is going on between two, before any facts are weighed. Concern rises when the framing is set before anyone has described what happened, and when the original grievance is relabeled as a dynamic.

2. **Procedural Symmetry** — Equal minutes, matching columns, one hard thing each. This functions by giving identical procedure to unequal situations, so that the form of fairness stands in for its content. Concern rises when the process measures the two parties' contributions by quantity and does not ask whose conduct came first or how large it was.

3. **Mutual Agreement Fabrication** — A Mutual Conduct Agreement that says both agreed that tension came from shared challenges. This functions by creating a signed document in which agreement appears to exist where only one party gave ground. Concern rises when the signing is urged within the meeting, when the pen is already on the table, and when the document records a conclusion nobody reached.

4. **File Equalization** — Two matching columns, one email quoted against months of unrecorded slights. This functions by placing the one written outburst beside the many unwritten harms, so the file seems balanced. Concern rises when the record contains only what was written down and weights it as if everything else were not there.

5. **Privacy Weaponization** — "The file is private and not to be discussed outside the process." This functions by preventing the person from checking what happened to the other side, comparing notes or seeking outside advice. Concern rises when confidentiality extends to the existence of the process and the terms, not only to unreleased work.

6. **Closure Manufacture** — "This is what a healthy band looks like." This functions by declaring the matter resolved because a document exists. Concern rises when the issue that started the conflict, credits in this case, is left out of the closure and labeled a separate business matter.

## Why This Order

**Reframing** has to come first because it sets the unit of analysis. Once the room accepts that the problem is two people's friction, every later fact is weighed as a contribution to that friction rather than as a claim about one person's conduct. Nothing false has been said. The most important choice has simply been made quietly and early. It has to come first, because equal minutes for "the dynamic" only feel fair once the meeting is about "both"; start with matching minutes instead and you would use yours to put the dropped credits and release forms on the table.

> One housemate leaves the dishes for months. The other finally snaps, and the landlord says, "There's been some tension in the house." Now it is about two people, and the dishes have vanished from the story. Flip it: if the landlord gave you each two minutes before calling it "tension in the house," you would use yours to talk about the dishes.

**Procedural Symmetry** then makes the frame feel fair. Equal time and matching columns are what fairness looks like from the outside, and objecting to them sounds like a request for special treatment. It has to come before the Mutual Conduct Agreement, because "shared communication challenges" reads as a fair summary only after two equal turns; handed the page first, you would see no credits on it and ask to add them before the pen moved.

> At a flat meeting everyone gets two minutes, the one who always cooks and the one who never does. If you say "this isn't equal," you sound like you want more than your share. Flip it: if you were handed a note to sign before your two minutes, you would see nothing on it about who always cooks and ask to add it.

**Mutual Agreement Fabrication** is what makes the frame permanent: a signature turns a facilitated discussion into a joint statement. Because the signer wanted to be a good bandmate, the signature carries moral weight too. It has to come before the typed notes, because two matching columns look agreed once both names sit under "mutual"; the other way round, you would see your two a.m. email quoted in full and could refuse to sign.

> You and a neighbour sign a note saying the fence dispute "came from misunderstandings." You wanted to be a good neighbour. Now your name sits under a sentence you would not have written. Flip it: if you saw your one cross message about the fence written down before signing the note, you could refuse to put your name under it.

**File Equalization** quietly uses the weakest point of the person who was harmed: the single message written in anger. Months of unrecorded conduct have no document, so the one that exists balances the page. It has to come before the confidentiality note at the door, because the uneven columns are fixed in the folder before anyone else may look; the other way round, you could show the folder to a lawyer or your sister and ask whether it looks even.

> A friend has been late for a year and nobody wrote it down. The one time you snapped at him is in his phone, and now that is the only record anyone can show. Flip it: if your friend asked you to keep it between you two before showing his phone, you could still ask someone else whether the record looked fair.

**Privacy Weaponization** then closes off the ways to correct the record. You cannot compare agreements, seek a second opinion or tell the rest of the band. It has to come before "this is what a healthy band looks like", because calling it finished holds only when you have already agreed not to discuss it; the other way round, you could still ask a lawyer or your bandmates before the copy reached the shared drive.

> "Let's keep this in the family," someone says. Now you cannot ask your cousin what she was told, or ask a friend what she thinks. Flip it: if someone said "that's all sorted now" before "let's keep this in the family," you could still ask your cousin and a friend what they think.

**Closure Manufacture** finishes it, because a matter that is called closed is hard to reopen without looking like the one who cannot let go. It has to come last, because the healthy-band line lands once the agreement is signed and the file is private; earlier, with the credits still unsettled, you could say plainly the matter is not closed and ask for that in writing.

> The person who bent your bike wheel says, "That's all sorted now." If you bring it up again, you are the one making a fuss. Flip it: if "that's all sorted" came while you could still talk to anyone about the bent wheel, you would see it for what it is and say it isn't.

What weakens the sequence: taking the draft agreement to independent counsel before signing, asking for the original grievance to be listed in writing, and separating "mutual conduct going forward" from "what happened before." The key conversion point is the sentence "both parties acknowledge."

## Individual Level

The person may begin to wonder whether they were in fact at fault, because their own single email is now in a file next to the other person's. Over time, they may start measuring their words in every message and group text, and may lose some of the ease that made them good in the band.

They may find themselves defending the process to others, since they signed it. They may avoid raising the credits again because doing so would appear to break an agreement that includes the word "mutual." They may feel grateful that the band did not break up and resentful that nobody asked why it nearly did, and they may not know how to explain both.

## Institutional Echo

Management offices often act for the band as a whole, and sometimes for one member's career more than another's, which can leave a mediator or facilitator with an unavoidable conflict of interest. Band agreements, leaving-member clauses and conduct clauses are frequently drafted for symmetry, and symmetry is easy to defend even when the underlying facts are not symmetrical. Confidentiality in mediation is common and often legitimate, and the extent to which it can bind a person to silence about their own grievance depends on the document and the place.

Label business affairs, booking agents and publicists often prefer a quiet resolution to a public one, especially when a tour is on sale. Whether a given process was fair depends on who selected the facilitator, who paid, and what was left out.

The honest reading is that many band disputes really are mutual and that a balanced agreement is often the right outcome. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the party with more power chose the facilitator or whether the original grievance was ever recorded.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that fairness means treating everyone the same, and that anyone who asks for more than symmetry is being difficult. Around it sits the band-as-family story: we don't take sides, we're all in this together. It makes asking who started it sound like a betrayal.

The value being exploited is evenhandedness, one of the best things about collective work. Nothing here argues against mediation. The public story is a band that sorted out its differences like adults, and that may be wholly true. It is simply not a story about whose credits were removed.

## Observable Indicators

1. A conflict is described as "both of you" before the individual accounts are heard.
2. A facilitator is arranged and paid for by the party with more influence, or by the management that represents both.
3. Equal time and matching columns are used regardless of how much each person has to report.
4. A written agreement states that both parties share responsibility without a finding.
5. The original grievance, such as credit or pay, is deferred as a separate matter.
6. A single written outburst is cited against many unrecorded events.
7. Signing is encouraged during the meeting itself.
8. Confidentiality covers the existence and terms of the process, not just unreleased work.
9. The matter is described as closed or healthy once the document is signed.
10. Attempts to raise the original issue afterward are described as breaching the agreement.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The draft and signed versions of the agreement, with dates, and any changes between them.
- The facilitator's identity, who arranged and paid for the process, and any prior relationship with the parties.
- Your own notes of the meeting, written the same day, including who spoke first and what was left out.
- Your record of the original grievance: credit forms, release forms, split sheets and the dates of each.
- The email or message that was cited against you, along with earlier messages showing the context.
- Any request to see or correct the process file, and the response.
- Messages from bandmates, crew or management about the meeting, before and after.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Many band conflicts are truly two-sided, and a balanced agreement can be generous and wise. Equal process is not proof of an equalizer.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Real mutual conflict.** Both members conducted themselves badly, and both said so.
- **Neutral facilitator.** Chosen jointly and paid by both.
- **Legitimate confidentiality.** Protecting unreleased work or band business.
- **Pragmatic compromise.** A person chooses to settle the conduct question and keep the credit question for later, with eyes open and advice.

Concern strengthens when: the facilitator is arranged by one party's side; the original grievance is excluded from closure; the signer was not given time or independent advice.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Direction Test:**
Ask: "Does anything in this document say who did what, or only that tension existed?"
Concern rises if: no one's conduct is described, and the grievance that started it is absent.

**SPOT IT — The Chair Test:**
Ask: "Who chose the facilitator, who is paying them, and who do they work for the rest of the year?"
Concern rises if: the answer is the manager or the party with more influence.

**SPOT IT — The Weight Test:**
Ask: "Is the one thing I wrote down being weighed against the many things nobody wrote down?"
Concern rises if: the file contains one outburst and no record of the earlier events.

**SPOT IT — The Pen Test:**
Ask: "Was I invited to take this home and have someone read it?"
Concern rises if: the pen was on the table and the answer was "let's just sign."

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for the draft in advance.** Request any agreement a few days before the meeting, and do not sign in the room.
- **Take it to independent counsel.** An entertainment attorney with no tie to management or the other band members, or a union or guild representative if you have one.
- **Put the original grievance on paper first.** A short, dated, factual note about credits, pay or conduct, sent to the facilitator and management, before any "mutual" text.
- **Ask who chose and paid the facilitator.** Request a neutral if the answer is the management office.
- **Separate the tracks.** Offer to sign a forward-looking conduct statement while the credit question is recorded as an open item in writing.
- **Check the confidentiality language.** Ask what is covered, for how long, and whether you may consult a lawyer and a support person.
- **Do not retaliate in writing.** Keep your own messages calm, specific and brief.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** You are told to sign in the meeting; the original grievance is deferred; the facilitator is arranged by the other side; a single message of yours is quoted back to you.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The agreement or its confidentiality term is used to stop you from raising credit or pay; your work or income is affected after you raise it; you are told the matter is closed while credits remain unrecorded. Relevant outside support may include an independent entertainment attorney, a union or guild representative, a royalty auditor for pay or splits, a collective management or performing rights body for registration questions, and a recognized mediation service named in a contract, with availability that varies by place.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, stalking, any physical danger or any thought of self-harm. Prioritize safety, emergency services and crisis lines. Do not confront anyone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could I take the draft home and have my lawyer read it before I sign?"
- "I'd like to add the credit question to the page as an open item, so it isn't lost."
- "Who selected the facilitator, and who is paying for the process?"
- "I'm happy to agree on how we communicate going forward. I'd also like the earlier concerns recorded."
- "Could the document say who raised what, instead of only that both of us did?"
- "What exactly does the confidentiality clause cover, and may I speak to my own advisor?"
- "I'd like a copy of whatever goes into the file about this meeting."

## Reflection

If a document says you both agreed, whose memory of what happened is it written from, and where is yours?
