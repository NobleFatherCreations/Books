# C030: The Impossible Standard — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a library-music composer, thirty-four, working from a spare room with acoustic foam on one wall and a window you keep meaning to clean. You deliver sixty-second cues to a sync agency, pieces called "Hopeful Dawn" and "Corporate Momentum," built to sit under a car commercial or an explainer video. It is a craft and you are proud of it. What you want this month is for the agency to take your next batch, and the one after, so the rent stops being a number you check at night.

Their notes arrive by email, and you read them like weather. The first is generous: "Love the energy! Could the intro be a touch more cinematic?" You make it more cinematic. Two days later: "It's a bit too cinematic. Can it be more organic and intimate, but still big and commercial?" You send back a cue that is all four, and you are pleased with it. The reply comes that evening from a different person on their team, copying the first. "Thanks! Can it feel fresh without straying from the reference?" The reference is attached. It has nothing in common with the brief. You open the whole chain, forty-one messages, and read from the bottom up. Near the middle, plainly: "Please keep the arrangement exactly as is, and make it feel completely different." You laugh. Then you do not laugh. You reopen the project file and listen to your own cue, hunting for what you did wrong.

On Tuesday the approval finally comes. "Great! Perfect." You let out a breath you did not know you were holding. On Wednesday morning: "We've decided to go another way on the brief. Can you adapt it to the new one by Friday?" The cue you delivered is now a draft for a different request. You read "Perfect" again, as if the word might have changed.

You pull up your agreement. It says you will supply tracks "to the agency's reasonable satisfaction." Two lines in a longer document, and you signed with the quiet confidence of someone who thinks reasonable is a fixed point. You write, politely, asking what reasonable satisfaction would look like for this one. The answer comes within the hour, with a smiley face: the client will know it when they hear it, and it needs to be distinctive but familiar. There is no line in it that you could hold up and say, here, I met this. Your cursor sits over the reply button, and you decide asking again would only sound like you cannot hear.

You accept a call. The head of music is warm. "This one's just... not there yet. You know what I mean? I know you can do it." You do not know what he means. You say yes anyway, because the alternative is to say you do not know, which would be the very thing he is hinting at. Your jaw is tight when you hang up. You catch yourself no longer asking what is wrong with the brief, and asking, instead, what is wrong with you.

The rounds multiply. You are paid on acceptance, and nothing is accepted, so every round is yours. On Friday at five a new brief lands for Monday. A note promises "a few small tweaks": a new key, a new tempo, no drums, which means the whole cue rebuilt from the first bar, at two in the morning, with your own money in the cello session you booked.

Then the warm call comes after a round they like. "Love the direction. So close. You're our first call for the spring slate." You glow for a day. After the next round, the one they do not like, the exclamation marks disappear and the replies shrink to a single line. When the slate is circulated, your name is not on it.

At midnight you write to a colleague: "I think I'm just not good at this anymore." You delete it, because you do not want to be a person who says that. You have thought it for weeks. You have stopped playing the piano on Sundays for yourself. When you listen to your old cues, the ones you were proud of, you cannot hear whether they are good. You turned down a friend's dinner so you could rebuild one more intro.

On Sunday you lift the piano lid, play one chord for nobody, and listen to it ring out.

## The Name

That's **The Impossible Standard**.

## Definition

The Impossible Standard is the sequence where contradictory demands coexist, success is redefined after it is achieved, and failure is framed as personal deficiency rather than the system's design, using Double Binds, Moving Goalposts, Impossible Metrics, Shame Installation, Overcorrection Demands, and Conditional Love to convert a creator's legitimate wish to meet professional expectations into a belief that effort can never be enough. In library and sync work it concentrates in revision cycles, delivery commitments with undefined standards and feedback chains passed through several people. It is hard to detect because every note sounds reasonable and many are, and because the worker is rewarded for responsiveness. What can be lost is unpaid time, income and the sense that any effort can be sufficient. Intent would require additional evidence, since clients genuinely change their minds and feedback chains genuinely contradict themselves.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Double Binds** — "Keep the arrangement exactly as is, and make it feel completely different." "Fresh, but not straying from the reference." This functions by presenting two demands that cannot both be met, so that any response violates one. Concern rises when the contradictory instructions appear in the same message and when failure is attributed to the composer rather than the brief.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — Approved on Tuesday, "another way" on Wednesday. This functions by redefining success after it has been achieved, so that completion never arrives. Concern rises when approval does not close a stage and when the standard changes without the brief changing.

3. **Impossible Metrics** — "Reasonable satisfaction" with no stated criteria; "distinctive but familiar"; "just not there yet." This functions by setting a standard that cannot be measured or reached, so that satisfaction stays in the evaluator's hands. Concern rises when no objective criteria exist and when the evaluator cannot say what would be accepted.

4. **Shame Installation** — "I know you can do it," "it's just not there yet." This functions by locating the problem in the composer's ability rather than the process, so that the person begins to say it about themselves. Concern rises when feedback shifts from the work to the worker, and when the worker begins to apologize for the standard.

5. **Overcorrection Demands** — Round after round of revisions, a new brief on Friday, a "few small tweaks" with large consequences. This functions by requiring effort far beyond the issue raised, so that revision itself becomes the punishment. Concern rises when the volume of revisions is out of proportion to the notes and no one tallies the unpaid hours.

6. **Conditional Love** — The warm call, the "love the direction," the "so close." This functions by tying warmth and future work to an unattainable standard, so that approval feels like a prize. Concern rises when affection and the promise of more work rise and fall with each round.

## Why This Order

**Double Binds** come first because they establish the impossibility. A single contradictory note could be an honest mistake. But a pattern of contradictions teaches the composer that the problem cannot be solved by skill, and, crucially, makes the composer search for the solution inside themselves. It has to come first, because Wednesday's new brief only feels like your fault once the forty-one emails have left you hunting your own cue for the mistake; start with the new brief and you would just quote a new fee.

> Imagine a parent who says "tell me the truth" and then gets angry at the truth. After a few rounds you stop asking what to say and start asking what is wrong with you. Flip it: if your parent kept changing what they wanted before ever asking for the truth, you would ask them plainly what they actually want.

**Moving Goalposts** then prevent resolution. If the first approval were final, the contradictions would end. Redefining success after it is achieved means the target is always a step beyond. It has to come before the 'reasonable satisfaction' answer, because 'know it when they hear it' only sounds acceptable once 'Perfect' has already turned into a draft; the other way round, you would ask for written criteria before the first round.

> You finally clear out the garage, and are told it was never the garage that mattered, it is the shed now. Every time you finish, the finish line has moved. Flip it: if you were told the rules for a clear garage were secret before you started, you would ask to see them written down first.

**Impossible Metrics** supply the cover: if criteria were stated, the composer could point to them. A "reasonable satisfaction" clause lets the evaluator remain the only judge. It has to come before 'I know you can do it', because that line only turns inward once no standard exists to point to; the other way round, you would ask the head of music which part missed the brief.

> Think of a game where the other player keeps the rulebook in their pocket. They say "that doesn't count," and you cannot check. Flip it: if the other player said "I know you can do better" before the game even started, you would laugh and ask to see the rulebook.

**Shame Installation** is the conversion. When the process cannot be blamed, the person is. Phrases like "I know you can do it" sound like faith and work like a quiet accusation. The composer begins to internalize the failure. It has to come before the Friday rebuild, because unpaid rounds only feel deserved once you think the problem is you; the other way round, you would call a new key and tempo a new cue and ask to be paid.

> A teacher says kindly, "I know you can do better," and gives no mark and no reason. You go home thinking something is wrong with you, not with the question. Flip it: if the teacher asked you to redo the work three times before saying "I know you can do better," you would ask what exactly needs changing.

**Overcorrection Demands** then monetize it: the more the composer feels at fault, the more unpaid revisions they accept. It has to come before the spring slate warmth, because 'You're our first call' only glows once endless rounds have left you grateful for another; the other way round, you would ask to see the slate in writing.

> It is like apologising for a late reply and then being asked to write the whole email again, three times, as a kind of penance. By the third go you are grateful to be allowed. Flip it: if they were extra warm to you before asking for the email three more times, you would enjoy it and still say once is enough.

**Conditional Love** closes the loop. Warmth and future work arrive just after an acceptable round and leave just after a failed one. The composer learns that being valued is possible but must be earned again each time. It has to come last, because missing from the slate only stings once the warm calls have taught you to earn your place each round; earlier, you would have shrugged and offered your cues to another agency.

> Picture a friend who is delighted to see you when you have good news and a little distant when you do not. You start arriving with good news ready. Flip it: if your friend had gone distant on your no-news days before ever asking you to redo anything, you would have seen it for what it is.

What weakens it: written, objective criteria before work begins; a defined number of revision rounds; a single person who consolidates feedback; and outside benchmarks from other composers. The key conversion point is when the composer starts to ask what is wrong with them, rather than what is wrong with the brief.

## Individual Level

The composer may begin to doubt their ear, second-guess work that was fine, and apologize for revisions that were not their error. Over time, they may find it hard to tell good work from accepted work.

They may stop trusting their own sense of completion, write more cautiously, and work late for the next round in the hope that this one is the one. They may feel ashamed and avoid telling anyone. They may start to dread opening email. This is a reasonable reaction to a standard that moves.

## Institutional Echo

Sync and library arrangements can involve delivery standards expressed as satisfaction rather than specification. Where several people in an agency pass notes from a client, contradictions can arise and no one owns them. Where revisions are unlimited and unpaid, the cost of changing course falls on the composer. Whether a delivery clause based on reasonable satisfaction can be relied on, and how disputes are handled, is jurisdiction-specific.

The honest reading is often that clients truly are unsure, that briefs shift, and that agency staff pass notes along in good faith. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same pattern affected other composers and whether approval was ever treated as final.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that great work is never finished, that the client is always right, and that a real professional absorbs revisions without complaint. "Make it pop" and "just a few tweaks" turn an open-ended standard into a virtue.

The value exploited is the composer's pride in craft and wish to deliver. The public story is a seamless soundtrack under a polished advertisement. It does not show the forty-second message.

## Observable Indicators

1. Feedback contains contradictory instructions in the same message or chain.
2. Approval is followed by a request to redo or rethink the same work.
3. Delivery standards are described only as satisfaction or similar terms with no stated criteria.
4. Notes arrive from multiple people with no consolidation.
5. Feedback addresses the composer's ability rather than the work.
6. Revisions are numerous and unpaid, with no cap.
7. A brief changes while the deadline stays.
8. Praise precedes new demands.
9. The composer cannot say what would count as finished.
10. The composer begins apologizing for the standard.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The full revision-notes email chain in order, with dates and senders.
- The original brief and every updated brief.
- The delivery commitment and satisfaction language in your agreement, and its draft history.
- Version files with timestamps, showing what was delivered when.
- A log of hours spent on each round.
- Written approvals and messages that reopen approved work.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Sync work is subjective, clients do change their minds, and feedback chains truly contradict themselves without anyone intending it. Many revision requests are exactly that.

The discriminator is **whether the contradictions are acknowledged and resolved, and whether approval is allowed to close a stage**.

- **A genuinely changing brief.** Clients revise their needs midway.
- **Honest ambiguity.** Creative language is imprecise.
- **Competent but crowded notes.** Several reviewers can contradict one another.
- **Fair revision norms.** A reasonable number of rounds is standard practice.

Concern strengthens when: contradictory notes are never reconciled; approvals are repeatedly reopened; the composer is blamed for the brief.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Contradiction Test:**
Ask: "Can I satisfy every note in this email at the same time?"
Concern rises if: no version could.

**SPOT IT — The Finish Test:**
Ask: "What would count as finished, in writing?"
Concern rises if: no one will say.

**SPOT IT — The Approval Test:**
Ask: "Has an approval ever stayed an approval?"
Concern rises if: approved work keeps returning.

**SPOT IT — The Blame Test:**
Ask: "Is the feedback about the work, or about me?"
Concern rises if: it is about my ability while the brief is never questioned.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for consolidated notes.** "To avoid conflicting changes, could you send one set of notes from a single contact?"
- **Name the contradiction, calmly, in writing.** "Note 2 asks to keep the arrangement and note 4 asks to change it. Which should I follow?"
- **Set a revision cap and price extras.** "This covers two rounds. Further rounds will be billed at my day rate."
- **Get criteria first.** "Could we agree on two or three reference points and success criteria before I begin?"
- **Treat approval as a milestone.** Reply: "Thanks for approving. I'll treat this cue as delivered; further changes would be a new request."
- **Keep your hours log,** so unpaid time becomes visible.
- **Compare with outside benchmarks:** other composers, a mentor, an independent adviser. Ask what is normal in your corner of the market.
- **Have the satisfaction clause reviewed** by independent counsel before the next agreement.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Contradictory notes in one chain; approval reopened; criteria undefined; unpaid revisions rising.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Payment withheld pending unreachable satisfaction; the clause used to refuse payment for delivered work; repeated reopening of approved cues; other composers reporting the same. Routes may include an independent attorney, a composers' or writers' guild or association, an agency's formal dispute channel, or a creators' legal-aid group.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats connected to payment or future work, harassment, signs of crisis or self-harm, or intimidation related to a dispute. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "To avoid conflicting changes, could you send one consolidated set of notes?"
- "Note 2 and note 4 seem to pull in opposite directions. Which should I prioritize?"
- "Could we agree on success criteria and a number of revision rounds before I start the next version?"
- "Thanks for approving this. I'll treat it as delivered; further changes would be a new request."
- "I've spent additional hours on revisions beyond our agreed rounds. Could we discuss how to cover them?"
- "Could you share what 'not there yet' would look like, ideally with a reference?"
