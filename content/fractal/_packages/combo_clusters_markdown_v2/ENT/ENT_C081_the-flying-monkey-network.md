# C081: The Flying Monkey Network — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a thirty-four-year-old lighting designer, and your career is built on the fact that people call you when something is going wrong at four in the afternoon and the doors open at seven. You like the crew. You know the riggers by their children's names. Three weeks ago you did something you had rehearsed for a year. After a run of touring dates where programming hours were scheduled after load-out and logged as "flex time," you sent a complaint to the production company's compliance address and asked that it be handled confidentially. It was about hours and pay, not people. The confirmation came back the same afternoon, on the company's template: "Thank you for contacting us. Your submission has been received and will be reviewed in confidence. Reference number: 4471. Please do not discuss the details of your submission with colleagues while the review is pending." You saved it to a folder you named "Taxes," and for the first time in a year you felt safe.

On Monday morning, at load-in, a rigger whose daughter's name you know leans in while you coil a cable. "Sorry about the whole hours thing," he says, quietly. You have told no one. You say thanks, because there is nothing else to say, and you spend the day wondering who else is sorry.

That afternoon the phone rings. It is a production manager you worked with two summers ago, a good guy, who once drove you to an airport at five in the morning. "Hey! Just checking in. How are you doing? Heard things have been, you know, a lot." You say you're fine. He says, "I'm not asking about anything." On Wednesday your old head electrician rings, and says he is "just a messenger, really." You like both of them. You cannot be short with people who are only checking in.

On Tuesday it is the tour accountant, who is lovely. She wants to talk about "how the room is feeling." You know I'm in your corner, she says. By Wednesday evening you have counted four. On Thursday a text arrives from a booking agent you barely know: "Hope everything's okay! Thinking of you." Each one is a little more pointed than the last, and each is easier to answer than the one before, so you answer all of them, and by Thursday night you have nothing left for the next.

On Friday you write to the compliance address, as you should. "A number of colleagues appear to know about my submission. Was it shared?" The reply, in the same template: "Submissions are handled in confidence. We are not aware of any disclosure. We cannot comment on individual reviews." You read it twice and have nowhere to put it.

Replaying the week, you notice that no one said hours or pay. The manager's line comes back: "I just hate to see people burn bridges over stuff that works itself out." You did not tell him there was a bridge. The accountant: money is tight, nobody wants to be the one who makes it harder. The electrician says you have been going about it "the wrong way." The agent finishes the thought: "Would hate to see the season get messy." Somewhere in the week you became the person who disturbed the crew.

You hear yourself say, to the next person who calls, "I'm sure it'll all be fine," in a voice trying to be calm for them. Every call said the same thing in different words, and not one of them said it out loud: drop it. Dropping it begins to feel like a kindness you could do them.

You no longer pick up when a warm name lights your phone. You eat at your desk so you will not pass the crew in the kitchen. You rebuild the lighting plot at night, a thing you could always fix, and you check the confirmation more than once a day. You have started to doubt whether what you wrote was careful at all.

You open the folder called "Taxes." The confirmation says the review is pending. Reference number 4471. It does not say who knows.

## The Name

That's **The Flying Monkey Network**.

## Definition

The Flying Monkey Network is the sequence where a complaint made in confidence reaches people it was never meant to reach, who then press the complainant to drop it without any institution appearing to do so, using Complaint Leaking, Proxy Recruitment, Social Pressure Layering, Plausible Deniability, Community Reframing, and Withdrawal Pressure to convert trust that a confidential complaint will stay confidential into loss of control over who knows about it and when. It can function as a way to apply institutional pressure without institutional fingerprints. It is hard to detect because each caller is a friend or colleague acting in apparent good faith, and because the institution can truthfully say it asked no one to call. The person may lose their sense of safety in reporting, working relationships, bookings, and the practical ability to see the complaint through. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Complaint Leaking** — The existence or substance of a confidential submission becomes known to people outside the review. This functions by activating a community that would otherwise not know. Concern rises when people who were never told about your complaint refer to it, or to its tone, within days of filing.

2. **Proxy Recruitment** — Friends, former colleagues, assistants and collaborators are drawn in, sometimes without realizing their role, to "check in." This functions by sending pressure through trusted voices rather than through a manager or a lawyer. Concern rises when several people with no stake in the matter contact you in the same period, each saying they are "just a messenger."

3. **Social Pressure Layering** — Contacts accumulate across days, each a little more pointed: concern, then worry about bridges, then talk of the season. This functions by making the pressure feel like the weight of a whole community instead of one request. Concern rises when the calls converge on the same message from different angles and escalate in tone.

4. **Plausible Deniability** — The institution says it knows nothing about any disclosure and offers a template reply. This functions by separating the organization from the effect of the pressure, so there is no one to hold responsible. Concern rises when written questions about the leak are answered with generic confidentiality language and no investigation of how it happened.

5. **Community Reframing** — The complaint is recast as a threat to the shared peace: "messy," "burning bridges," "going about it the wrong way." This functions by turning a workplace concern into a disruption of the community, so the complainant becomes the problem. Concern rises when the discussion shifts from what you reported to how you reported it.

6. **Withdrawal Pressure** — Every contact carries the same implicit message: drop it. This functions by making withdrawal feel like the generous, reasonable choice. Concern rises when you are praised for dropping the matter and faulted for pursuing it.

## Why This Order

**Complaint Leaking** is first because the whole sequence depends on information that should have been sealed. Without it, there are no callers, and nothing to which a community can respond. It also turns the person's strongest protection, confidentiality, into the vector of exposure. It has to come first, because the rigger's "sorry about the whole hours thing" needs the complaint already known; start with the friendly calls instead and they would have nothing to refer to, so you could just thank people and carry on.

> You tell the school office something in confidence. At pick-up the next day, another parent mentions it, kindly. Flip it: if another parent rang "just to see how you are" before you told the office anything, you would just thank them and carry on.

**Proxy Recruitment** must come next because direct pressure from an institution would itself be evidence. Using friends and colleagues replaces an order with a favor and an authority with affection. Many proxies do not know they are part of anything. They are simply told the person is "having a hard time." It has to come before the four calls in one week, because the build-up works only when each caller is someone you like, such as the man who drove you to the airport; the other way round, calls from the company itself would read as pressure, and you could forward them to compliance.

> Your cousin rings "just to see how you are." You learn later that he had been chatting with someone who asked him to. Flip it: if four people rang in one week before your cousin's call, it would feel like pressure, and you could ask who had asked them to.

**Social Pressure Layering** turns single nudges into a climate. One call is a check-in; four calls in a week is a message. The layering also exhausts the target's ability to answer each one without seeming ungracious. It has to come before the compliance template reply, because by Friday four calls have worn you down too far to push past "we are not aware of any disclosure"; the other way round, you would send your written question fresh and ask for a named person to follow up.

> One neighbour asks how you are, which is nice. Four do in one week, and each is a bit more careful in the way they ask. By the fourth, you are tired of being gracious. Flip it: if you heard "nobody has told anyone anything" before the four careful visits, you would ask in writing who had been told.

**Plausible Deniability** protects the system. Once pressure has been delivered through proxies, the institution can truthfully say it did not instruct anyone, and it can answer the target's written question with a clean template. This is the step that makes the pattern hard to prove, and it is why intent is genuinely difficult to establish here. It has to come before the talk of burned bridges and a messy season, because the crew framing sounds like friends' own worry once compliance has said it told nobody; the other way round, you would hear "burn bridges" first and ask compliance in writing who had been told.

> You ask the shop manager whether anyone told the staff to be cold with you. "We haven't told anyone anything," she says, and she may be right. Flip it: if people said you'd upset everyone before the manager said "we haven't told anyone," you would ask her in writing who had been told.

**Community Reframing** changes the moral frame. By now the target is being asked to consider the feelings of the crew, the season and the budget, and the original topic, hours and pay, has vanished from the conversation. The complainant has become the person who disturbed the peace. It has to come before "I'm sure it'll all be fine", because dropping it feels kind only once hours and pay have turned into the crew's feelings; the other way round, you would be asked to drop a pay question and could simply say no.

> You report a broken lift in your building. Soon people are saying you have upset the whole floor. Nobody is talking about the lift. Flip it: if you were asked to just drop it before anyone said you'd upset the floor, you could simply say no and keep reporting the lift.

**Withdrawal Pressure** completes the loop: when dropping it feels like generosity, it can be accepted as the person's own decision. It has to come last, because letting it go feels like a gift to the crew only after the calls and the reframing; earlier, with reference 4471 still fresh, you would ask compliance for a date and keep the review open.

> After a week of kind visits you say, "Honestly, forget it." Everyone thanks you, and you feel like the bigger person. Flip it: if you said "forget it" before anyone told you how upset everyone was, you would see the kind visits for what they are and keep your question open.

What would weaken the sequence: telling no one outside a protected circle; filing through a channel that logs access; replying to every caller with the same short, calm sentence; and bringing in an independent adviser before the third call. The key conversion point is Proxy Recruitment, because pressure that comes from friends is much harder to name than pressure that comes from management.

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt whether the complaint was worth it, and may feel guilty toward the friends who called. They may rehearse explanations for people who never asked for one. Over time, they may stop answering calls from colleagues they used to trust, which can look like the withdrawal the callers were hoping for. They may lose sleep going over who told whom, and feel foolish for having believed the word confidential. Some people drop the matter, then carry the quiet resentment of having done so for years.

## Institutional Echo

Production work is a small, reputation-driven network in which crew members move among the same promoters, venues and tour managers across seasons. A confidential submission to a compliance address, an HR contact or a shared inbox passes through several hands, and the people with access are not always bound by a formal confidentiality process.

Where intake confirmations ask the complainant not to discuss the details with colleagues but make no equivalent commitment about the recipients, the asymmetry is built into the document. Where the same people who hear a complaint also hire for the next run, the line between review and relationship is thin. Where reviews are slow and communication is minimal, a rumor can move faster than a process.

The honest reading is that many calls of this kind are exactly what they seem: friends who care, a gossipy industry, or a coincidence of timing. Intent would require additional evidence, such as how the callers learned of the complaint and whether their messages converged on the same ask.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that the industry is one big family and that family handles things internally. Beside it sits the belief that a good professional does not make waves, and that anyone who goes outside the group has chosen to betray it. The value being exploited is solidarity: crew members really do depend on one another for work, safety and the next job. The language that makes it sound noble is "keeping the peace," "not airing dirty laundry," and "looking out for you." The public story is a tight-knit crew that takes care of its own. The private cost is a person told, by everyone who loves them, to be quiet.

## Observable Indicators

1. People you did not tell refer to your complaint, or to your "situation," soon after filing.
2. Several unrelated contacts reach out within the same week.
3. Callers describe themselves as "just checking in" or "just a messenger."
4. The conversations converge on the same message: let it go, don't burn bridges.
5. The pressure is framed as concern for you, the crew, the season or the budget.
6. Callers cannot describe the substance of your complaint but have a view on how you handled it.
7. A written question about disclosure gets a generic confidentiality reply.
8. Your tone is described as the problem rather than your content.
9. Work offers or social invitations quietly thin out.
10. Praise follows any sign that you might withdraw.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The intake confirmation, reference number and date, saved outside work systems you do not own.
- A dated log of each call or message: who, when, what they said, and what they said they knew.
- Your written question about disclosure and the reply.
- Texts, voicemails and call records that show the clustering of contact.
- A short list of who knew of your complaint before filing, and whom you told.
- Notes of any change in bookings, invitations or crew assignments after filing.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Friends do check in. People in a small industry hear things, and a call from a former colleague can be pure kindness. A good-faith mediator may also encourage informal resolution, and some complaints are indeed better resolved quietly.

The discriminator is whether the contact respects your choice. In a benign case, the person asks how you are, accepts your answer, and does not keep returning to the same suggestion.

Concern strengthens when: several contacts arrive within days of filing; they carry the same message; and they know details you shared with no one.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Knowledge Test:**
Ask: "How does this person know about my complaint?"
Concern rises if: nobody told them, and nobody can say who did.

**SPOT IT — The Chorus Test:**
Ask: "Are different people delivering the same message in different words?"
Concern rises if: yes, and each says they are acting on their own.

**SPOT IT — The Reframe Test:**
Ask: "Has the conversation moved from what I reported to how I reported it?"
Concern rises if: my manner is discussed more than my content.

**SPOT IT — The Template Test:**
Ask: "Did my written question about disclosure get an answer, or a template?"
Concern rises if: the reply says nothing about how people found out.

## Chain Breakers

- **Use one calm line with every caller:** "I appreciate you checking in. I'm not going to discuss it, and I'd rather leave it to the process."
- **Do not confirm, deny or explain** the complaint to any proxy, even a friend.
- **Log every contact** the day it happens: who, when, what was said.
- **Put the disclosure question in writing** to the review body and ask who has access to the submission.
- **Get independent advice early** from an employment or entertainment attorney, a union or guild representative, or an advocacy service, before the pressure peaks.
- **Do not retaliate** against callers, and do not confront the person you suspect leaked it.
- **Keep your own support circle small and outside the production,** including at least one trusted person who has no stake in your next booking.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Anyone you did not tell refers to your complaint; two or more contacts in a week carry the same message; a written question about disclosure gets only a template.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Evidence that the submission was shared; work offers that disappear after filing; pressure that crosses from persuasion to consequence. Relevant routes may include an employment or entertainment attorney, a union or guild, a labor or workplace regulator, or an external ombuds.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, stalking, intimidation, harassment, any concern for physical safety, or severe distress including thoughts of self-harm. Do not confront anyone. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and preserve messages.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Thank you for checking in. I'm not going to discuss it, and I'd like to leave it to the process."
- "I haven't told anyone about it. Can I ask how you heard?"
- "Please don't pass anything on from me. I'm happy to talk about something else."
- "Who has access to my submission, and can you confirm how it was handled?"
- "I'm going to take advice before responding to anything more on this."
- "I appreciate that you care. I'd like you to respect my decision."

## Reflection

When everyone who asked you to let it go was kind, how will you tell whether you are being protected or being managed?
