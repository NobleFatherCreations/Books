# C008: Bait and Switch — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a twenty-five-year-old singer with a day job and a drawer full of demos, and what you want is to be heard once by someone who can open a door. The credit card you keep for emergencies lives in your wallet, behind your library card. You have never used it for anything you wanted.

The email has a gold crest in the corner. *Invitation-Only Artist Showcase. Selected talent will perform before A&R, publishing and management professionals.* It is signed by the selection committee, and you are one of twelve. Your name is spelled correctly. You read it eleven times, standing in the stairwell at work, and your hands are not quite steady on the phone. You tell your sister you are not nervous. You are so nervous you cannot eat.

The night itself is a hotel ballroom made to look like a club. A velvet rope, a low stage, a bar that opens early. There are perhaps forty people in the room, and few of them look at the stage. You sing three songs and give the last one everything you have. When you finish, there is polite applause and one person at the back who does not stop. Afterward a man puts a card in your hand. "You were incredible," he says. "Let's talk." You are so relieved you cannot remember what you answer. The card is warm from his pocket.

On Monday, a call. The voice is friendly, almost shy. The executives, it says, were a mix of the organizer's partners, friends and sponsors, and the evening was designed to get your name in front of the right people. The next step is a development track with more performances, which will help build momentum. "You've already done the hard part," the voice says. "This just keeps it going." Your shoulders come down from around your ears. It sounds like what you hoped the email meant.

A revised fee schedule arrives that afternoon. You lay it beside what you have been given so far, and the steps are plain on the page. The invitation became a package, with an invoice a week before the show: a participation fee, a "stage and production contribution," a photography charge, a line for "executive access." The package has become a track, and the track a monthly performance fee. The amounts are larger than the invoice. You had agreed to each step, one at a time, each with a reason attached, and you do not find the place where you could have said no.

You count what you have already spent. The outfit you have always imagined wearing. A session player who learned four songs in two evenings. A rehearsal room with a window. And the credit card, run the week before the show, after a friendly voice said, "We don't want you to feel pressured, but spots are filling, and we'd hate to give yours away." The receipt is in a drawer. You know exactly what each thing cost, and your stomach tightens at the total.

At the bottom of the schedule: *Participation in the development track is recommended for artists wishing to remain visible to the original audience.* You think of the one person who clapped. You write back that you need to think. The reply is gentle: *Totally understand. You've already put so much into this, the outfit, the band, that night. It would be such a shame to walk away from all of it now. We just can't hold your place much longer, and several artists from your night have already confirmed.* It is the first time you have heard the word *visible* as something you could lose.

You have stopped singing in the shower. You wake at five and run the total again. When your sister asks how it went, you say "good" and change the subject to her kitchen. You used to be sure of what you had heard in that room. Now you replay the clapping and cannot tell if it was one person, or two, or something you wanted.

You stand at your kitchen counter with the fee schedule. You can see in it every dollar you have already spent, arranged like a staircase, and the next step is one more dollar than the last.

You pick up the card and begin to dial.

## The Name

That's **Bait and Switch**.

## Definition

Bait and Switch is the sequence where what was promised bears little resemblance to what is delivered, but the substitution is staged slowly enough that by the time the gap is visible, leaving costs more than staying, using Catfishing, Future Faking, Moving Goalposts, Commitment Traps and Sunk Cost Exploitation to convert trust in stated representations into money, time and opportunity already committed. In pay-to-play showcases and development programs, the audience, the "selection" and the access promised are the bait, and the fee schedule is the switch. What may be lost is savings, months of an artist's early career, and confidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Catfishing** — The gold crest, the "selection committee," the promise of A&R, publishing and management in the room. This functions by presenting an image of organic industry interest that the underlying terms do not commit to. Concern rises when the invitation describes an audience in aspirational terms but names no one, and the invoice arrives after the invitation.

2. **Future Faking** — "Selected talent will perform before...", "help build momentum," "remain visible to the original audience." This functions by selling a described future that no one is contractually bound to deliver. Concern rises when benefits are described in the future tense and obligations are described in the present.

3. **Moving Goalposts** — The invitation becomes a package, the package becomes a development track, the track becomes a monthly performance fee. This functions by fragmenting one large change into smaller ones, each with a reasonable explanation. Concern rises when each stage changes what is being sold and the price, and the earlier stage is described as the first step and not the whole thing.

4. **Commitment Traps** — The outfit, the session player, the rehearsal room, the credit card, the "spots are filling." This functions by growing the artist's exposure faster than their information. Concern rises when the cost is paid before the audience is verified and refunds are not offered.

5. **Sunk Cost Exploitation** — "Artists wishing to remain visible to the original audience." This functions by converting the artist's prior investment into the reason to invest again. Concern rises when the next fee is justified by what has already been spent and not by what is delivered.

## Why This Order

**Catfishing** has to be first because the image of the room has to exist before the terms are read. The crest, the committee and the promised executives make the invoice feel like an admission price rather than a purchase of an audience. Most artists read the invitation and skim the line items. It has to come first, because the gold crest and the selection committee make the Monday call sound like the start of something; start with talk of a development track instead and you would ask who was actually in the ballroom.

> Picture a flyer for a "grand opening" with a glossy photo of a full restaurant. You come for the photo, and you skim the small print about the cover charge. Flip it: if the flyer only promised "great things coming soon" before showing any glossy photo, you would read the small print about the cover charge first.

**Future Faking** is the temporal condition that makes the first step safe. Nothing in the invitation is false at the time of sending: the room may include real professionals, and some of them may be genuinely interested. This is why the pattern is hard to tell apart from an ordinary, if overhyped, showcase. It has to come before the revised fee schedule, because "this just keeps it going" makes each new charge feel like momentum; the other way round, you would see the monthly fee first and ask what the showcase actually led to.

> A shop says "new stock arriving soon." That is true, or nearly true, and it is enough to keep you coming back to the shop. Flip it: if the shop raised its prices step by step before ever saying new stock was coming, you would notice the rises and shop somewhere else.

**Moving Goalposts** is the working mechanism. A single message that said "this is a paid audience and a monthly program" would be refused. Delivered as an invitation, then a package, then a development track, each change comes with a plausible reason and each can be accepted separately. It has to come before the outfit and the session player, because spending feels safe while each step arrives with its own reason; the other way round, you would see the total and keep your card in your wallet.

> Think of a gym that said "free trial," then "starter plan," then "membership." You never saw the whole price at once, so you never said no to it. Flip it: if the gym asked you to sign up for a whole year before the free trial, you would ask to see the full price and probably say no.

**Commitment Traps** turn disappointment into captivity. The artist builds most of the trap herself: she buys the outfit and the session player before the audience is verified. The organizer does not control those costs, which is part of why they are effective. It has to come before "you've already put so much into this", because that line needs the outfit, the band and the card receipt to point to; the other way round, you would hear it before spending and say no.

> You bought the tickets, booked the hotel and told your family you were coming. Backing out of a trip like that now feels like losing the whole thing. Flip it: if someone said "think of everything you've put into this trip" before you bought any tickets, you would laugh and decide freely whether to go.

**Sunk Cost Exploitation** closes the loop and supplies deniability. The artist attends, sings well and receives applause, so the event can be recorded as satisfaction. Few complaints follow. It has to come last, because the total in your drawer weighs most once the fees have climbed step by step; earlier, you would write back that you are not joining the track.

> After a long wait in a queue that turned out to be the wrong one, you still say "well, it wasn't terrible" because you do not want to feel the hour was wasted. Flip it: if you had seen it was the wrong queue before deciding to join it, you would simply walk to the right one with no hour to protect.

The key conversion point is the Monday call. What weakens the sequence is asking, before paying, who the audience is, whether the fee is the only cost, and what the organizer earns if the artist is signed or not.

## Individual Level

The artist may find herself unable to prove what she was told, because the invitation no longer reads as it did. Over time she may re-narrate the evening as a success to avoid facing the fee, and defend the organizer to friends.

She may accept each new charge in isolation and never total them. She may feel embarrassed, then stubborn, then silent. Her trust in the next opportunity may shrink, and her faith in her own judgment with it.

## Institutional Echo

Showcases, festivals and development programs can be structured so that unsigned artists subsidize the audience that is meant to discover them. Where fees cover production and marketing, the event's revenue can come from participants more than from any downstream signing. Where an organizer's audience includes affiliates, sponsors and staff, "industry attendance" may be hard to verify.

The honest reading is that many showcases are legitimate, that costs are real, and that a showcase can generate a real connection. Intent would require additional evidence, most usefully whether attendees were identified in advance, whether fees increased after the event, and whether prior participants describe a similar sequence.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that talent has to be discovered, that you pay your dues, and that exposure is payment. Around it sit the sayings that everybody does it this way and that real artists take risks. The value exploited is hope, which is the best thing an unsigned artist has.

The public story is a young singer under stage lights and a room full of people who matter. The private story, sometimes, is a staircase of fee lines. Nothing here argues against playing live shows. It argues for knowing whose room you are in.

## Observable Indicators

1. An invitation that names no specific attendees but describes them in aspirational terms.
2. A fee schedule sent after the invitation and after the artist has committed emotionally.
3. A package described as covering "the room, the audience and the marketing."
4. A line item such as "executive access" with no definition.
5. Pressure to confirm quickly because spots are filling.
6. Cards and offers after the event that lead to a paid program rather than a contract.
7. A revised fee schedule larger than the original.
8. Attendees who are the organizer's partners, sponsors or staff.
9. Refund and cancellation terms that are absent or vague.
10. A monthly or per-show fee described as "recommended" for remaining visible.
11. Questions about attendance answered with general reassurance.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The original invitation email and any marketing page, as received, with the date.
- The showcase package invoice, the revised performance fee schedule and every payment confirmation.
- A list of costs you incurred in preparation, with receipts: outfit, session player, rehearsal room.
- Photographs of the venue, the audience and any signage.
- Names and cards of anyone who approached you, and what they said and offered.
- Messages about spots, deadlines and "recommended" participation.
- Names of other artists on the bill and what they were told.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Showcases cost money to run, and paying for a stage is not by itself a sign of a trap.

- **Open paid events.** Fees clearly described, no promises about who attends, no pressure.
- **Real industry attendance.** An audience that includes working professionals, even if few.
- **Honest marketing exaggeration.** An enthusiastic invitation that overstates, not one that conceals.
- **Legitimate development programs.** Costs itemized in advance with deliverables defined.

Concern strengthens when: the audience cannot be described in writing; fees rise after the event; the next step is justified by what has already been spent.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Audience Test:**
Ask: "Who exactly will be in the room, and can you confirm it in writing?"
Concern rises if: the answers are general and nobody will name anyone.

**SPOT IT — The Invoice Test:**
Ask: "Why am I being asked to pay for a room that is meant to discover me?"
Concern rises if: the explanation is that this is how everyone gets seen.

**SPOT IT — The Timing Test:**
Ask: "When did each cost appear, relative to my commitment?"
Concern rises if: the larger costs arrived after the earlier ones were paid.

**SPOT IT — The Next-Step Test:**
Ask: "What would I get for the next fee that I did not get for the last?"
Concern rises if: the answer is visibility.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask the specification question before paying.** "Can you tell me who has confirmed attendance and in what capacity?"
- **Screenshot the invitation and the page** as they stand when you decide.
- **Total the costs on paper** before agreeing to a next step: what you have spent, what is next, what is promised in return.
- **Perform, take the cards, go home.** Make no decision in the room.
- **Talk to artists who have done it** before. Ask what happened after the night.
- **Ask for the refund and cancellation terms** in writing.
- **Do not pay a recommended next fee** because of the last one. Decide on what is delivered.
- **Get independent advice** before signing any development or management agreement.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A fee schedule that arrives after the invitation; attendees who cannot be named; a revised fee higher than the first.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Significant sums paid for access that was not delivered; several artists describing the same sequence; refusal to refund or explain. Relevant routes may include an entertainment attorney, a consumer-protection office, a card issuer's dispute process and an artist advocacy group.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats of reputational harm, blacklisting or legal action unless you pay; pressure on someone who is a minor; any form of coercion or extortion; financial hardship with self-harm risk. Prioritize safety and emergency support. Do not confront.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before I commit, can you tell me in writing who has confirmed attendance, and in what role?"
- "What does each line in the invoice buy, and what is refundable?"
- "Can you send the full terms, including the development track, before I decide?"
- "I'd like to speak with two artists who participated last year."
- "I'm not able to pay for the next stage because of the first. What is being delivered?"
- "I'll take time to review this with an adviser. If the place goes, that is fine."
- "What do you earn if I sign with someone I meet at this event?"

## Reflection

They came to see you, or you paid to be seen? Who in that room knew the difference?
