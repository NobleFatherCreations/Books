# C047: The Confusion Protocol — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a composer, forty-one years old, with a catalog of film and concert music that is respected in your own country and almost unknown in this one. Your English is good enough for a hotel and a grocery store, and not yet good enough for a sentence with six clauses and a cross-reference. You came to this city because a publisher here wanted to represent your work, and the first agreement, a year ago, was simple enough that your own lawyer at home was comfortable with it. Today there is an amendment, eleven pages. A sheet of questions you wrote in your own language last night is folded in your bag.

You are shown into a conference room with a long glass table and a plate of pastries. Four people from the publisher: a business affairs manager, a catalog director, someone from royalties, and a woman introduced as "our interpreter, who has been with us for years and knows the business." She is kind. She is also on the publisher's payroll, and you notice it, and you decide not to make it awkward. You ask your first question: does the amendment change who controls the administration of your older works, and for how long? The business affairs manager nods. "Right. So, Administration Rights in Section 4 are defined by reference to the Controlled Catalog in Schedule A, which as amended in 2.3 includes, subject to 7.1, Works Delivered or Deemed Delivered." Every word is English. You cannot hold the sentence. The interpreter says something in your language that is shorter, and warmer, and not quite what you heard. You decide the trouble is your English.

"For how long?" you ask. The catalog director says: "Term is as set out in Term. Term is the Initial Term plus any Extended Term, which is defined in the Term section." You look at the page. It says what he said. Your face is getting hot. A third try would sound like you were not listening to the second.

You ask whether "Deemed Delivered" means works you have already finished. "That's a good question," says the royalties manager. "It would depend on the Administrative Period." You wait for the rest. There is no rest. You write the two words on your sheet with a question mark, and you cannot tell whether you asked something foolish or something sharp.

Then the afternoon takes over. Four officials answer four different clauses, the interpreter renders each answer after it, and the pages turn. Forty minutes in you have not received a single answer to the question you came with. The pastries are gone, there is a flight in the evening, and you would agree to nearly anything that made the room stop.

"I know this is a lot," the business affairs manager says gently. "Honestly, most of this is housekeeping. If you'd like to take it away and think about it, of course, but we are hoping to get it into the new system this quarter, and some of the processing depends on timing." You came to ask what it means. Now you are being asked whether you would like to take it away, and whether you are the delay.

"I want my lawyer to read it," you say.

"Absolutely. Of course. Is that the lawyer from home? That might be difficult with the translation, though. Our interpreter can walk you through any section you like."

You pick the section about term. She walks you through it for ten minutes, and when she finishes you understood every word and cannot repeat a single sentence.

You go back to your sheet. "The question I asked first. For how long?" The royalties manager smiles. "I wouldn't focus on that. Have you seen the portal?" A laptop appears, and someone demonstrates a dashboard with colorful bars while the interpreter explains what the colors mean. You watch the bars. You nod.

You sign nothing that day. On the plane you unfold the sheet, and you cannot say exactly what the top line was asking. You remember only that you were told, many times and very kindly, not to worry about it. At home you tell your lawyer it was "mostly housekeeping." You used to read a contract to its last line, and you have not opened this one. In the grocery store you say less than you could, in case it comes out wrong.

The sheet is still in your bag, folded the way you folded it.

## The Name

That's **The Confusion Protocol**.

## Definition

The Confusion Protocol is the sequence where a document or conversation is made so tangled, circular and crowded that the person with the question stops asking it, using Word Salad, Circular Logic, Strategic Ambiguity, Cognitive Overload, Moving Goalposts and Topic Switching to convert limited capacity for sustained focus under overload into the loss of the original concern ever being addressed. In music publishing it concentrates in amendments built from cross-referenced definitions, schedules and portals, where an ordinary person must read three sections to understand one sentence. It is hard to detect because every sentence can be defended as accurate, and because real contracts are genuinely dense. What the target can lose is the answer to the question they came with. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Word Salad** — "Administration Rights in Section 4 are defined by reference to the Controlled Catalog in Schedule A." This functions by burying a simple question in nested terms until the listener cannot hold the sentence. Concern rises when plain-language questions repeatedly receive defined-term answers.

2. **Circular Logic** — "Term is the Initial Term plus any Extended Term, which is defined in the Term section." This functions by sending the reader around a loop so that a question is "answered" without information arriving. Concern rises when an answer points only to the thing being asked about.

3. **Strategic Ambiguity** — "Deemed Delivered." "It would depend on the Administrative Period." This functions by leaving a key phrase open enough to be read later in whichever way suits the drafter. Concern rises when a defined term has no stated scope and the explanation declines to give one.

4. **Cognitive Overload** — Eleven pages, four officials, a portal demonstration and an interpreter, all in one afternoon. This functions by exceeding the listener's processing capacity so that agreement becomes the easiest exit. Concern rises when volume and time pressure arrive together and a break is treated as inconvenient.

5. **Moving Goalposts** — The question changes from "what does this mean?" to "do you want to take it away?" to "can your lawyer even read this?" This functions by raising the bar for asking, one small step at a time. Concern rises when each reasonable request is met with a new condition.

6. **Topic Switching** — "I wouldn't focus on that. Have you seen the portal?" This functions by redirecting attention to a pleasant subject at the moment of an unanswered question. Concern rises when the pivot always lands just after the hard question.

## Why This Order

**Word Salad** has to come first because it establishes that the document is beyond casual reading. Once the first answer cannot be followed, the listener quietly accepts that they are the problem. It has to come first, because 'Term is as set out in Term' only stops you once the first long answer has convinced you the trouble is your English; start with the loop instead and you would point out that it answers nothing.

> Picture a stranger giving you directions: "left at the second light after the old pharmacy, unless it's closed, then follow the other road." You nod, walk off, and blame yourself for not keeping up. Flip it: if the stranger answered "it's this way because it's this way" before any long directions, you would see it answers nothing and ask someone else.

**Circular Logic** is what prevents recovery. A second question about the first answer returns the same sentence, and the listener learns that there is no ground floor to this building. It has to come before 'Deemed Delivered' is left open, because 'it would depend' only goes unchallenged once you have learned that asking twice returns the same sentence; the other way round, you would ask for a written definition.

> You ask a clerk why a form was rejected and hear "because it was not accepted." You ask again and hear it again. Flip it: if the clerk said forms fail "in some cases" before any looping answer, you would ask him to say which case, in writing.

**Strategic Ambiguity** is the payload. The loop is not an accident of style; it protects a few key terms, such as "Deemed Delivered," whose meaning will matter later and whose breadth the drafter would prefer to leave unspecified. It has to come before the four officials and the turning pages, because the tiredness only matters while a key term like 'Deemed Delivered' is still unclear; the other way round, you would rest and ask about that one term.

> A shop sign says "refunds in most cases." Nobody can say which case is yours until the day you need one. Flip it: if the shop buried you in forms before the "most cases" sign came up, you would rest first and then ask which case is yours.

**Cognitive Overload** weakens independent evaluation. People, especially those working in a second language, make worse decisions when tired, and signing becomes a way to make the room stop. It has to come before 'take it away and think about it', because being cast as the delay only stings after forty minutes with no answer; the other way round, you would simply take the pages to your lawyer.

> After a long day of forms at a counter, you say "fine, whatever" to the last one without reading it. You only wanted to go home. Flip it: if asking to read a form felt like an insult first thing in the morning, before a long day at the counter, you would read it anyway.

**Moving Goalposts** raises the exit cost. The request for a lawyer, a break or a translation of one's own becomes a complication, and the interpreter employed by the other side becomes the gate through which the document passes. It has to come before the portal demonstration, because colorful bars only distract you once asking for your own lawyer has started to feel like a complication; the other way round, you would close the laptop and repeat 'For how long?'

> You ask a repair shop for a second opinion on the bill, and the question becomes "so you don't trust us?" Asking for something fair has turned into something you now have to apologize for. Flip it: if the repair shop showed you its new waiting room before you asked about the bill, you would still ask for a second opinion.

**Topic Switching** closes the loop and provides deniability. Nobody refused to answer; the conversation simply moved on to something exciting. It has to come last, because the dashboard only replaces your question once you are tired and doubting your English; earlier, in the first minutes, you would have said 'that is not an answer' and waited.

> You ask your landlord when the repair will happen, and he says, "Have you seen the new lobby paint?" You leave admiring the paint and without a date. Flip it: if the landlord had praised the lobby paint before any question of trust came up, you would have seen it for what it is and asked for a date.

The conversion point is when the question is no longer remembered. The sequence is weakened by written questions sent in advance, an independent interpreter, and a plain-language summary signed off by the drafter.

## Individual Level

The person may leave a meeting unable to say what was explained. They may blame their language skills, and apologize for slowness that was in the document and not in them. Over time they may stop asking questions in meetings and rely on the person with the clearest English, even if that person is employed by the other side.

They may sign things they only partly follow to end the discomfort. They may also feel shame, and avoid telling colleagues at home how hard the day was.

## Institutional Echo

Publishing amendments often rely on defined terms, schedules and cross-references, which are a normal drafting technique. The same technique, applied to a reader with limited English and no counsel in the room, can function as an obstacle even when no obstacle was intended. Where the interpreter is a staff member, the explanation of a document passes through the party that drafted it. Where migration to a new system is used as a reason for urgency, the pace may be driven by administration rather than by the artist's needs.

Many such meetings are simply poorly planned, with genuine efficiency motives and a sincere interpreter. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same document is explained differently to counsel, or whether the broad terms were ever narrowed on request.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that complexity is sophistication, and that anyone who cannot follow the paperwork is unprepared for the business. A related belief is that a contract is simply "standard," so reading it too closely is a sign of mistrust.

The value exploited is courtesy: the willingness of a guest in a country to avoid making a fuss. The language is polite: "housekeeping," "streamline," "align with our systems." The public story is a respected composer joining a respected house. The private story is a long table and a question that never got an answer.

## Observable Indicators

1. A plain question about duration, scope or control is answered with defined terms or a section number.
2. An explanation points back to the term being asked about.
3. Key phrases such as "deemed" or "as amended" have no stated scope.
4. The amendment is long, cross-referenced, and delivered shortly before a meeting.
5. The interpreter is employed by the counterparty.
6. Several people speak in turn, and a break is discouraged.
7. A pleasant topic is introduced at the moment of a hard question.
8. Timing pressure is attached to an administrative deadline.
9. Requests to consult one's own lawyer are met with practical objections.
10. No plain-language summary is offered and none is agreed in writing.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The amendment as received, with the email that delivered it and the date and time.
- Your written list of questions, in your own language, and any translation of it.
- Names and roles of everyone present, including whether the interpreter is staff or independent.
- Notes of each answer given, written the same day, and which section was cited.
- Any plain-language or translated summary provided, with who prepared it.
- The portal screenshots or demonstrations shown, with dates.
- Emails about timing, the "new system" deadline and any processing dependence.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Dense drafting is common, and many business affairs staff are sincerely trying to explain. An interpreter who works for a publisher may be a conscientious professional. A deadline linked to a system migration may be genuine.

The discriminator is whether a plain written answer is available when asked. Honest complexity yields to a request such as "please put in one sentence what this does to my older works" and returns a short, stable answer. This pattern returns another definition.

Concern strengthens when: the same plain question receives different answers in different meetings; a request for independent translation or counsel is treated as a delay; and a key term remains undefined after you ask what it covers.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Loop Test:**
Ask: "Can you tell me this without using a defined term?"
Concern rises if: the answer sends you to the term you asked about.

**SPOT IT — The Scope Test:**
Ask: "What exactly does 'deemed' include, and what does it leave out?"
Concern rises if: no one will state the scope in writing.

**SPOT IT — The Interpreter Test:**
Ask: "May I bring my own interpreter, or have this translated independently?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that the staff interpreter is enough.

**SPOT IT — The Pivot Test:**
Ask: "Did we move to a new topic right after my hard question?"
Concern rises if: the pivot happens more than once and the question is never revisited.

## Chain Breakers

- **Send your questions in writing beforehand**, in simple English, and ask for written answers.
- **Ask for a one-page plain-language summary** of what the amendment changes about term, scope and control, and ask the drafter to confirm it is accurate.
- **Bring or hire an independent interpreter**, and ask for the document to be translated for your own adviser.
- **Do not sign in the room.** "I'll take this to my adviser and return with questions."
- **Ask what changes if you do not sign** and when, in writing.
- **Request a redline** against the earlier agreement, so only changes need reading.
- **Seek independent advice** from an entertainment attorney with no tie to the publisher, ideally with experience of cross-border works.
- **Take a break** when you need one. It is not rude.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A plain question about term or scope goes unanswered; an interpreter is staff; an amendment is long and cross-referenced and you have been given little time.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** You are pressured to sign without advice; written answers conflict with what was said in the room; the amendment may change control of existing works; or the other party objects to your lawyer. Consider an entertainment attorney, a collective management or authors' society if you are a member, a musicians' union or composers' guild, or an interpreter and translation service with no tie to the publisher.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation about your immigration or work status, withheld travel documents or payments used as leverage, or any danger. Contact emergency services or a relevant crisis or legal-aid service, tell a trusted person, and do not sign or negotiate alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you tell me in one plain sentence what this changes about the term of my older works?"
- "I'd like to send my questions in writing and have your written answers."
- "I'd like my own interpreter to attend as well."
- "Could you send a redline against the previous agreement?"
- "I'm not able to sign today. I'll return with questions after my adviser has read it."
- "What does 'deemed delivered' include and exclude? Please put it in writing."
- "Could we return to my first question? I don't think it has been answered."
