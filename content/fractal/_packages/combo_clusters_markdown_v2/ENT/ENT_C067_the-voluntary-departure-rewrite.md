# C067: The Voluntary Departure Rewrite — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're an artist-development director at a label, forty-three, and for eleven years you have been the person new artists are sent to when they are scared. You built Open Door: before the first advance, every newly signed artist sits down with someone who explains recoupment in plain language and suggests they take the contract to an outside lawyer. It has a binder, a reading list and a waiting list. What you want is to run the autumn intake. The thank-you notes from past artists are pinned above your monitor.

The weather changed with a reorganization. A new head of business affairs arrived, and the word "alignment" began appearing in your calendar. At your last review you were told, with real warmth, that your "values don't fully align with the company's growth trajectory." You asked what that meant. "Fit," you were told. Your numbers were the best on the floor and your artists renewed. You go home looking for what you could change, and there is nothing to hold.

Then the invitation: the third-floor human resources room, the round table, the box of tissues no one uses. The head of the label sits in, which she has never done, beside an HR partner with a folder. "We want to talk about what's next for you," she says. She calls you one of the architects of the company's culture and says she wants your departure, "whenever you decide it is right," to honor that. You say you had not planned to depart.

There are two documents. The first is a performance improvement plan, a thick one, with objectives that mention no artist who has ever thanked you. It begins next Monday and runs ninety days. The second is a separation agreement. "Option one," the HR partner says, gently, "is the plan. Option two is this. We think option two honors your work." You look from one stack to the other. Both end with you gone. Staying is not on the table.

The agreement offers a payment, continued health coverage for a period, and a reference letter attached as Exhibit A, signed, dated and already written. It calls you "an exceptional leader" and "a rare talent." You read it and your throat tightens, because it is the kindest thing anyone has put in writing about you in months. You have rent, and a career still to carry that letter into.

The HR partner turns to page two. "This is standard language," she says. "You are leaving to pursue other opportunities, and you and the company mutually agree that this transition is voluntary." She says it pleasantly, like reading a menu. You ask whether the announcement will say you were let go. The head of the label says it will say you are "moving on to new adventures," and that this is better for everyone.

She slides the draft across. It thanks you warmly for eleven years and goes to every artist and the whole floor on Friday. You read it for the sentence that says what happened. It is not there. You picture an artist on the waiting list reading it, nodding, and having nothing to ask.

You ask what happens to Open Door. Its future, she says, is "under review," and your successor will "bring fresh energy." Nobody says the program will end. Nobody says it will not. Then the HR partner passes you the new-artist onboarding checklist your successor will use, for your handover notes. Contract, advance, photo shoot, release plan. There is no line for the sit-down, and no mention of Open Door. Your thumb rests on the gap where it used to be.

You ask for time. They say of course, a week. Back in your office you look at the binder, the thank-you notes and the schedule of new artists starting in the autumn. You are going to leave. That has been decided somewhere without you.

You have started eating lunch in your car. You let two artists' messages sit unanswered because you do not know what you are allowed to say. At night you rehearse sentences for your mother that begin with "I decided," and stop at the verb. Last week you could not have told anyone the last time you doubted your own work. Now you check the numbers again, the best on the floor, as if they might have changed.

Exhibit A is in the folder under your arm. You reach up, unpin the oldest thank-you note and hold it for a long while.

## The Name

That's **The Voluntary Departure Rewrite**.

## Definition

The Voluntary Departure Rewrite is the sequence where a person's success is recast as a poor fit, every option on offer is an exit, and the exit is narrated as their own free decision, using Values Misalignment Manufacturing, Binary Choice Construction, Positive Reference Leverage, Voluntary Narrative Installation, Community Confusion Exploitation and Legacy Erasure to convert a desire to leave with dignity into the loss of public credit and the continuity of the work. It can function as a quiet removal that leaves no visible removal. It is hard to detect because each step is gracious and the most important choice, whether to leave at all, is taken before the meeting. What the person may lose is public credit for what they built and the survival of the programs they led. Many departures really are voluntary and mutual; intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Values Misalignment Manufacturing** — "Your values don't fully align with our growth trajectory." This functions by redefining the person's strengths, here advocacy for artists, as a deviation from the company's direction, so that success itself becomes evidence of poor fit. Concern rises when strong results are met with abstract criticism that no metric supports.

2. **Binary Choice Construction** — Option one, the plan; option two, the agreement. This functions by presenting two departures as the only choices and omitting the option of staying. Concern rises when every path leads out and the possibility of mediation, appeal or reassignment is not mentioned.

3. **Positive Reference Leverage** — A glowing letter already written, attached as an exhibit. This functions by turning a reference, which the person's work already earned, into a prize to be exchanged for agreement. Concern rises when the reference is offered only on condition of signing, or withdrawn if a performance plan is chosen.

4. **Voluntary Narrative Installation** — "You mutually agree that this transition is voluntary," "moving on to new adventures." This functions by writing the story of the departure in advance, in a document the person signs. Concern rises when the agreed account differs from what the person experienced and cannot be corrected.

5. **Community Confusion Exploitation** — The artists and colleagues receive a gracious announcement and cannot tell what happened. This functions by leaving the people who benefited from the person's work without a clear reason to organize or ask. Concern rises when announcements are vague, when colleagues are asked not to discuss the departure, and when people who ask are given the same gentle summary.

6. **Legacy Erasure** — The program is "under review" and the successor will bring "fresh energy." This functions by dismantling the work once its champion is gone, along with the credit. Concern rises when the program's name, binder or practices disappear soon after, and the leader's name drops from related materials.

## Why This Order

**Misalignment** has to come first because the departure needs a reason that is not the real one. If the person were asked to go for the reason they are actually valued, the room would object. By converting a strength into a mismatch, the organization makes the later exit sound like the natural conclusion of a fit problem, and gives itself a reason that cannot be measured. It has to come first, because two exits with no option to stay need a reason behind them, and "fit" is that reason; start with the two documents instead and you would point to the best numbers on the floor and ask why staying is not offered.

> A friend's group stops inviting you and says, "You're just not quite our vibe." You are the one who always organised the trips. There is nothing to disprove, because "vibe" can't be checked. Flip it: if the group told you to leave quietly or be dropped before any talk of vibe, you would remind them you organised the trips and ask why staying isn't an option.

The **binary choice** comes next because it makes the exit a decision. Once both paths leave, the person is bargaining over how to depart, not whether to. It has to come before Exhibit A, because the kind letter only tips the choice once both paths already end with you gone; offered first, it would read as praise for a director who stays, and you could ask why it meant leaving.

> A landlord says, "You can leave next month, or we can raise the rent so high you will leave anyway." Neither answer lets you stay, and you start comparing them. Flip it: if the landlord offered you a glowing reference before saying you must leave either way, you would ask why a good tenant cannot stay.

The **reference letter** is what makes one path more attractive than the other. It is a real gift in a real career, and a person trying to leave with dignity will often pay for it with a narrative. It has to come before page two's "mutually agree" line, because the reference is what you are weighing when the pleasant words are read out; the other way round, you would hear "voluntary" first and could say you had not planned to depart.

> Picture a former boss offering a warm, glowing note for your next application, the one you badly need, with one small thing to agree to in return. It is hard to refuse something kind that you need. Flip it: if your former boss asked you to say you chose to leave before offering the warm note, you would say you had not planned to go.

**Voluntary Narrative Installation** is where the price is collected. The signature turns an imposed exit into a free one, and the announcement carries that story to everyone else. It has to come before Friday's farewell to every artist and the whole floor, because the announcement only repeats a story you have already signed; the other way round, you could ask for the sentence about what happened to be put in the draft.

> A friend says, "Let's just tell everyone we both decided to stop the weekly games." You nod, to keep the peace. Now that is the story everyone has, in your own words. Flip it: if everyone heard you had both decided to stop the games before your friend asked you, you would tell them what really happened.

**Community Confusion** then does quiet work: colleagues and artists hear a gracious version, cannot reconcile it with what they saw, and do not know what to ask, so no one organizes. It has to come before Open Door is put "under review", because artists on the waiting list who read "new adventures" do not know to ask about the sit-down; the other way round, they would notice the missing program first and ask you about it.

> Your whole book club gets a kind message that you are "stepping back." They saw how the last meeting went, and the message doesn't fit. Nobody knows what to ask, so nobody does. Flip it: if the club dropped the reading list you started before the "stepping back" message went out, you would notice and ask why.

**Legacy Erasure** is the last step because it needs the champion gone and the story fixed. With the person out and the departure called voluntary, nobody is positioned to ask why the program that mattered most is the first thing to be reviewed. It has to come last, because the gap in the onboarding checklist goes unquestioned only after you have agreed to leave and the farewell has gone out; earlier, you could point to the autumn waiting list and ask in writing what happens to Open Door.

> After the longtime caretaker retires, the community garden she built is "being looked at." Newcomers have never heard her name, so no one remembers whose it was. Flip it: if the garden was "being looked at" while everyone still knew it was hers, you would see it for what it is and say whose it was.

What weakens the sequence: independent advice on the agreement and the plan, a request for the real reasons in writing, and a calm record of the programs' results. The key conversion point is the phrase "mutually agree."

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt whether they misread eleven years of success. Over time, they may find themselves rehearsing the "moving on" line to friends, and noticing that it sounds like someone else's sentence. They may feel grief for the program and shame for not fighting, and may be unable to say which of those is louder.

They may hesitate to speak to colleagues for fear of jeopardizing the reference or the payment. They may be proud of the letter and embarrassed by what it cost. They may find, a year later, that nobody can say what the program was called.

## Institutional Echo

Labels reorganize frequently, and a new executive's priorities often redefine "fit" for roles that sit between artists and the commercial side. Performance improvement plans and separation agreements are common instruments, and the sequence of offering one alongside the other is a recognizable pattern in many workplaces. Reference letters, non-disparagement language and "voluntary resignation" wording can have consequences for benefits, claims and later employment that depend on the document and the place.

Programs built around one person's relationships often have no written charter or budget line, which makes them easy to put "under review". Whether a specific plan or departure was pretextual is a question for qualified review.

The honest reading is that roles change, strategies shift and a real mismatch can exist. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the stated misalignment preceded the program's results and whether the program was actually dissolved.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that leaving gracefully is the mark of a professional, and that making a fuss damages everyone, including yourself. "Don't burn bridges," "the industry is small" and "there's a time to move on" make quiet exits sound mature. The older story is that a leader serves the company, and that those who build things for others are replaceable.

The value being exploited is dignity: the wish to be remembered well. The public story is a smooth transition and a thank-you post. Both may be sincere. Neither says what happened to the Open Door binder.

## Observable Indicators

1. Strong results are paired with abstract criticism about "fit" or "alignment."
2. A new executive or reorganization precedes a change in how the person is described.
3. A performance plan and a separation agreement are presented in the same meeting.
4. Both options end employment, and staying is not offered.
5. A reference letter is attached to the agreement or offered only if the agreement is signed.
6. The agreement states the departure is voluntary or mutual.
7. The announcement language is vague and was drafted before the person agreed.
8. Colleagues and artists are asked not to discuss the departure.
9. The person's program is "under review" soon after the exit.
10. Mentions of the person's name are removed from internal decks, web pages or artist materials.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Reviews, results and metrics for the previous years, showing performance before the "alignment" comment.
- The performance plan, the separation agreement, the reference letter and any announcement draft, with the dates you received them.
- Calendar invites and notes for the meeting, including who attended and what was said.
- Written requests for the reasons behind the "fit" assessment and any answers.
- Program documents you lawfully hold, such as the Open Door curriculum, participant feedback and attendance records.
- Thank-you notes and messages from artists and colleagues about the program's value.
- Records of changes to the program or web pages after your exit, with screenshots and dates.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

People leave for good reasons, and a mutual departure with a fair package and an honest reference can be the best outcome for all. A restructuring that eliminates a role for stated business reasons is not this pattern.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **Real strategic change.** The company decided to stop a type of program and said so.
- **Genuine mutual decision.** The person wanted to leave and asked for a package.
- **A reference given without conditions.** Offered whether or not you sign.
- **A fair successor handover.** The program continues under new leadership with credit retained.

Concern strengthens when: a reference is offered only on signing; the voluntary label contradicts what you were told; the program is dismantled soon after your exit.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Strength Test:**
Ask: "Is the 'misalignment' the same thing my results were praised for last year?"
Concern rises if: the strength has become the problem.

**SPOT IT — The Third Door Test:**
Ask: "Is staying, mediation or reassignment on the table?"
Concern rises if: both options lead out.

**SPOT IT — The Letter Test:**
Ask: "Would I receive this reference if I did not sign?"
Concern rises if: the answer is no, or no one will say.

**SPOT IT — The Word Test:**
Ask: "Does the paper say 'voluntary' and 'mutual' about a decision I did not make?"
Concern rises if: the text cannot be changed to match what actually happened.

**SPOT IT — The Binder Test:**
Ask: "Who is responsible for what I built, and what happens to it in the first ninety days?"
Concern rises if: it is "under review" with no owner.

## Chain Breakers

- **Do not decide in the room.** Ask for the documents and a few days to review them.
- **Seek independent advice.** An employment attorney with no tie to the company, or a union or guild representative, before you respond.
- **Ask for the real reasons in writing.** "Could you send me the specific objectives I am not meeting, and the criteria used?"
- **Ask whether the reference is unconditional.** Request that it be provided regardless of which option you choose.
- **Ask to change the wording.** Propose "ended by the company" or "agreed separation" if the voluntary language does not match what occurred.
- **Document your work.** Keep a list of program results and artist outcomes from files you lawfully hold; do not copy confidential material you are not entitled to take.
- **Ask about the program's future.** Request a handover plan and credit, in writing.
- **Check non-disparagement and confidentiality terms** before you sign anything.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A new "alignment" criticism appears despite strong results; two exit options are presented together; the reference is tied to signing.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A plan is used to build a file while an agreement is offered; the voluntary wording is unavoidable; non-disparagement terms limit your ability to correct the record; the program is closed soon after. Relevant outside support may include an independent employment attorney, a union or guild representative, an internal ombuds if truly independent, a labor or employment authority, and a career adviser, with availability and reach that vary by place.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation or retaliation toward you or artists who defend you; coercion to sign under pressure; health or financial crisis; any thought of self-harm. Prioritize safety, emergency services and crisis lines. Do not confront anyone.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'd like the specific objectives and the criteria behind the 'alignment' feedback, in writing."
- "May I have a week and a copy of both documents to review with an adviser?"
- "Would the reference letter be provided whether or not I sign?"
- "The word 'voluntary' doesn't match my experience. Can we describe it as an agreed separation?"
- "What is the plan for the Open Door program, and how will its origins be credited?"
- "Could the announcement be shared with me before it goes out?"
- "Is staying in a different role, or mediation, an option?"

## Reflection

If you sign a sentence saying you chose to go, who is the next person who will be told that this is how leaving looks?
