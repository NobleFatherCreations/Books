# C026: Obedience Training — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a twenty-three-year-old alt-pop artist, three months into your first deal with a mid-major label, and at eleven at night you are cross-legged on your bed with a laptop open to a half-finished vocal chain when your phone lights up. A&R. You were asleep ten minutes ago, or almost. You pick up on the second ring. What you want is to keep this deal and make the record you hear in your head, and, tonight, to get back to the vocal chain.

In the first weeks there is a calendar invite for a nine o'clock call about "alignment." It lasts eleven minutes, and nothing is decided. Then a request that you re-send a lyric sheet in a different font. Then a reminder to tag the label in every story. None of it is outrageous. All of it arrives at once, in a tone of cheerful urgency, and you comply with everything, because refusing any one thing would cost more than doing it.

There are preferences about when you answer, too. In your first week you let one call go to voicemail, and the next morning's text said, "Everything okay? Just checking you're still all in :)." Now you pick up on the second ring. A note from the brand team says they would prefer you stop replying to fan comments from your personal account until they have reviewed the tone. Someone mentions that rest days are "better kept flexible." You open the contract on your phone, to the clause you have read four times, under a heading about availability. It says you will provide "reasonable promotional cooperation" as requested by the label from time to time. Your lawyer, a friend of a friend who read it for an hour, said it was common language and that it would probably be fine. You have decided that it is.

In the second month you forget to tag the label in a single story. "No worries at all!" the A&R says. For three days his replies are two words long. A vocal you finished and he loved comes back for a re-edit, though nothing about it has changed. A new rule arrives: every story goes to the brand team before it goes up. That night you send your first one for approval, and your thumb hangs over the arrow, wanting to explain.

Then the Tuesday. A radio visit is moved to six in the morning. You say that you have vocal rest the day before, you have a doctor's note, you could do ten. He is not angry. He is quiet. "Totally understand. I'll let them know you're not able to do it." The silence stays for four days. Messages that used to take minutes take a day. You ask for one hour of sleep after a late shoot, and the answer comes the next afternoon. You stop mentioning the doctor's note.

On Friday there is a call. Unexpectedly warm. Plans for the single, shared spreadsheets, a new idea for a shoot. You are so relieved you almost laugh, and you hear yourself say you will take the next morning visit. "You've been really great about being flexible lately," he says. The only thing that has changed is that you said yes to something. Your shoulders come down from around your ears.

Only now do you scroll up in the roster chat, the lively thing full of memes and gratitude, and see that it has not tagged you since Tuesday. A teammate you barely know sends a gentle message: "Hey, maybe just do the morning thing next time? People notice." You read it twice. Nobody has said anything to your face, and now nobody has to. You write back, "Totally, thanks!" and add a heart.

So you say yes to the six a.m. You say yes to the late edits. You answer the eleven o'clock calls with the brightness of a person who was waiting by the phone. The vocal chain has sat half-finished on your laptop for weeks. You no longer reply to fans, though a few of them ask if you are okay. You sleep with the phone on the pillow and flinch when it buzzes at noon. When your mother asks how it is going, you say "amazing" and ask about her garden.

Tonight the call lasts forty minutes. He is lovely about it. He says you have the best instincts on the roster, in the voice people use for puppies and promising investments. The order of tracks six and seven, already decided twice, stays as it was. When it ends, you look at the phone in your hand and cannot remember deciding to pick it up.

## The Name

That's **Obedience Training**.

## Definition

Obedience Training is the sequence where small, mostly meaningless demands are enforced with disproportionate consequences until a performer answers by reflex, using Enforcing Trivial Demands, Behavior Control, Overcorrection Demands, Punishment of Boundaries, Negative Reinforcement Traps, and Group Consolidation to convert a new artist's wish to avoid being labeled difficult into a habit of unquestioning availability. In the label and management economy it concentrates in late-night calls, promotional schedules, social media tone approvals and group chats, because an unclear availability clause lets responsiveness stand in for commitment. It is hard to detect because every demand is small and every individual request can be defended as business. What can be lost is the capacity for independent judgment about which requests matter. Intent would require additional evidence, since eager labels can be disorganized, anxious and demanding without designing anything.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Enforcing Trivial Demands** — The lyric sheet in a different font, the story tags, the nine o'clock alignment call about nothing. This functions by making compliance with small requests the daily proof of commitment, so that the content of the request stops mattering and the response is the point. Concern rises when requests are numerous, urgent in tone and low in consequence, and when they cluster after a decline.

2. **Behavior Control** — Preferences about when the artist answers, how she replies to fans, what she posts, and when she may rest. This functions by moving everyday decisions into the label's approval, so that the artist's sense of what she may decide shrinks. Concern rises when the approvals extend beyond released work into personal channels and routines, and when they are described as friendly suggestions that carry consequences.

3. **Overcorrection Demands** — A single declined radio visit answered with days of coolness, a re-edit of work that was fine, a new rule after a small slip. This functions by making the response to a minor deviation far larger than the deviation, so the artist learns that even small refusals are expensive. Concern rises when the response is out of proportion to the event, and when the correction is applied only to the artist.

4. **Punishment of Boundaries** — Vocal rest, a doctor's note, a request for an hour of sleep, followed by silence and slower replies. This functions by attaching cost to ordinary limits, so that the artist concludes the limit itself is the offense. Concern rises when a medically or practically reasonable limit is met with withdrawal rather than negotiation.

5. **Negative Reinforcement Traps** — The warm call on Friday, the "you've been so flexible lately," relief that comes only after saying yes. This functions by letting the artist escape the chill by complying, so that compliance feels like safety. Concern rises when warmth returns exactly after accommodation and the limit is never discussed.

6. **Group Consolidation** — The roster chat that stops tagging her, the teammate's "people notice." This functions by turning peers into carriers of the norm, so that enforcement no longer needs to come from the label. Concern rises when peers warn rather than inform, and when the group's praise is reserved for those who comply.

## Why This Order

**Enforcing Trivial Demands** comes first because it costs nothing to ask. A request for an odd font or an extra tag has no great stakes, and a refusal would look absurd. That is why it works: it builds a habit of answering without evaluating, and it asks for nothing the artist can reasonably defend. It has to come first, because checking with the brand team before replying to fans only feels normal once you have already re-sent a lyric sheet in a new font; start with the fan rule instead and you would ask why.

> A new flatmate asks you to hang your towel on the left hook and to text before using the hallway light. It seems silly to argue, so you just do it. Plenty of houses have odd little rules; what counts is where they lead. Flip it: if the flatmate told you who you may invite home before any rule about towels or the hallway light, you would ask why and say no.

**Behavior Control** extends the habit into territory that matters. Once the artist is used to checking, it becomes natural to check about replies to fans, about when to rest, about what to say. The vague availability clause quietly supports this by letting "reasonable" be defined in the moment by the party who asks. It has to come before the two-word replies, because one missed tag only feels serious once every story already goes for approval; the other way round, you would shrug off the silence and finish the vocal chain.

> Picture a gym membership that says "reasonable use of the facilities." The manager decides what reasonable means each time you walk in, so you start asking before you go. Flip it: if the gym punished you heavily for one tiny slip before any talk of reasonable use, you would ask for the rules in writing and maybe leave.

**Overcorrection Demands** tests the habit. A small deviation is answered with a large reaction, and the artist learns the exchange rate. The point is not any single punishment. It is the lesson about proportion. It has to come before the six a.m. radio visit, because his quiet reply only frightens you once a missed tag has cost three cold days; the other way round, you would hold up the doctor's note and offer ten.

> You are five minutes late to dinner, and your friend is silent all evening and makes you redo the booking. Next time you are twenty minutes early. Flip it: if your friend went silent for a week after you said no to a plan, before any late dinner, you would notice it and keep saying no when needed.

**Punishment of Boundaries** makes the lesson specific. When the limit is medical, practical or simply human, the response teaches that limits are not recognized in this relationship. The artist's boundaries are then renegotiated as favors. It has to come before Friday's warm call, because his praise for being flexible only feels like forgiveness after four silent days; the other way round, you would enjoy the shoot plans and still keep your vocal rest.

> You tell your manager you can't work this weekend because you're ill. He says, "Sure, fine," and then stops speaking to you for a week. Next time, being ill feels like a favor you have to ask for. Flip it: if your manager grew warm only once you agreed to work while ill, before any silent week, you would notice it and still stay home ill.

**Negative Reinforcement Traps** provide the exit, and the exit is compliance. The warmth that returns after a yes is experienced as being forgiven. It has to come before the teammate's 'people notice', because that gentle nudge only feels like the rule once warmth has already come back after your yes; the other way round, you would answer that you had a doctor's note.

> Your partner is cold for days, then cheerful again the minute you say, "You were right." The relief feels like love. Flip it: if friends told you to just say "you were right" before your partner had ever gone cold, you would ask them why and trust your own view.

**Group Consolidation** then closes the loop by making the norm social. Once peers are doing the enforcing, the label never has to be the one who says no. It has to come last, because the roster chat's silence only carries weight once you already say yes to end the A&R's chill; earlier, you would have asked the teammate who exactly had noticed and checked with your lawyer.

> A classmate whispers, "Maybe just do what the teacher says. People notice." The teacher has said nothing, and the whole class now does it for them. Flip it: if the whisper about people noticing came before the teacher had said anything to you, you would ask who noticed and do what you think is right.

What weakens the sequence: asking early what reasonable promotional cooperation means in practice; a written schedule that includes rest and rest of voice; and a trusted outside advisor who can say whether the pattern is normal. The key conversion point is the first time the artist answers because she is afraid not to, rather than because the request is reasonable.

## Individual Level

The artist may begin to answer reflexively, keeping the phone within reach at night and feeling a small drop in the stomach when it lights up. Over time, she may find it harder to tell which requests are important and which are habit, and may stop weighing them at all.

She may become wary of rest, treat sleep as a risk, and feel guilty for limits that are medically sensible. She may begin to repeat the label's language to herself: flexible, easy, grateful. She may also start to resent the very things she wanted, and be unable to explain why.

## Institutional Echo

Open-ended availability language, such as a promise of reasonable cooperation as requested from time to time, lets the party who asks define what is reasonable. Where there is no published schedule, a call can come at any hour and silence can be read as lack of commitment. Group chats and rosters can carry informal norms about responsiveness that are never written. An artist's legal status and the rules about working time, rest and contractor classification vary by jurisdiction and deal.

The honest reading is often that labels are fast-moving, that a release window can compress everything, and that an A&R who calls at eleven may simply keep strange hours. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether declines were consistently followed by withdrawal, and whether others on the roster describe the same.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that a real artist is always available, that gratitude means yes, and that setting limits shows you do not want it enough. "Everyone does this" and "do you know how many people would love to be you" sound like perspective and work as a leash.

The value exploited is a young artist's genuine commitment and her reasonable fear of a small industry. The public story is a new signing living the dream. It does not show a bedroom at eleven at night.

## Observable Indicators

1. Calls and messages arrive routinely outside agreed working hours, with an expectation of immediate reply.
2. Requests are numerous, small and urgent in tone.
3. A decline is followed by delayed replies, cooler tone or missing invitations.
4. The availability clause is vague, and no one will say what reasonable means in practice.
5. Approvals extend to personal channels, such as replies to fans, beyond released work.
6. Warmth returns after the artist says yes.
7. Medical, vocal or rest limits are treated as unprofessional.
8. Peers advise compliance rather than clarity.
9. The roster chat reacts differently to those who comply and those who do not.
10. The artist begins answering instantly without having decided to.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- A dated log of calls, texts and invitations, with times and what was asked.
- The availability and promotional cooperation clauses, with the draft history.
- Written records of any decline or limit you stated, with the reply and its timing.
- Medical or vocal rest notes and any accommodation requests.
- Roster or team group chat messages that set expectations on responsiveness.
- Approval requests for social media content, and the label's responses.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Labels move fast, release windows compress, and an A&R who calls late may be keen, disorganized or in another time zone. Most requests are ordinary business.

The discriminator is **proportion and consequence**. A busy label asks for a lot but accepts a reasonable no. The pattern appears when ordinary limits are answered with withdrawal and warmth returns only after compliance.

- **Genuine release crunch.** Short windows create real urgency.
- **Time-zone and travel differences.** Late calls can be innocent.
- **Reasonable brand guidance.** Review of social content can be sincere risk management.
- **Enthusiastic A&R.** Some people are simply excited and sleepless.

Concern strengthens when: declines reliably produce silence; the same demands cluster after a limit is set; peers repeat the label's pressure.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I don't answer or say no?"
Concern rises if: coldness, delay or lost opportunity follows, and warmth returns only after you give in.

**SPOT IT — The Proportion Test:**
Ask: "Is the response as big as what I actually did?"
Concern rises if: a small decline produces days of consequence.

**SPOT IT — The Reasonable Test:**
Ask: "What does reasonable promotional cooperation mean in hours, days and notice?"
Concern rises if: nobody will put it in writing, and the answer is that you will know it.

**SPOT IT — The Peer Test:**
Ask: "Are my peers helping me understand the rules, or warning me not to break them?"
Concern rises if: the advice is only to comply.

## Chain Breakers

- **Set and send your hours.** "I'm available for calls between nine and eight; anything urgent outside that I'll address first thing." Say it once, kindly, in writing.
- **Ask what reasonable means.** Request a simple promotional schedule with notice periods, rest days and voice care. A good partner can answer that.
- **Put limits in medical or practical terms** and in writing. A vocal rest note is not a favor you are asking.
- **Take the clause to independent counsel** with no tie to the label before the next signature or amendment.
- **Keep a log** of late calls and consequences so a pattern is on paper.
- **Do not make approval of your personal channels a precedent.** Ask what is being reviewed and why, and what the process is.
- **Keep one friend outside the roster** who can tell you if this is normal.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Calls outside agreed hours that you feel you must answer; coolness following a decline; a vague availability clause with no schedule; warmth returning only after you agree.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Documented withdrawal of opportunities after medical or rest limits; pressure to ignore doctor guidance; approval demands over personal channels; the clause being used to extend obligations beyond the agreement. Routes may include an independent entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, and an artist advocacy or legal-aid group.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to your career or safety, pressure to work through illness or injury, signs of self-harm or crisis, or any threat connected to refusing. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'm available for calls between nine and eight. If something is urgent outside that, I'll respond first thing."
- "Could you send me the promotional schedule with notice periods, so I can plan around it?"
- "I have a vocal rest day before that, and my doctor has asked for it. I can do ten o'clock."
- "I'd like to understand what reasonable promotional cooperation means for this release. Could we write it down?"
- "Thanks for the heads-up on the social review. What exactly is being checked, and how long does it take?"
- "I'd like my lawyer to read this before I answer. I'll come back tomorrow."

## Reflection

When the phone rings at eleven, who decided you would pick up?
