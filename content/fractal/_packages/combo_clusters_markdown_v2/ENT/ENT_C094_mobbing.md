# C094: Mobbing — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a choreographer, thirty-six years old, and the rehearsal studio on the fourth floor is the only place in the world where you have never once felt lost. Mirrors on three walls, a sprung floor you could find blindfolded, a speaker with a crack in the grille that you refuse to replace because it has a sound you know. You have been hired to stage a six-week run of a touring show, with twenty-two dancers and a cast of principals. In week two the production office told you the second act had to come down by four minutes to meet a venue curfew. You cut it. Eleven dancers lost a section they had worked on. You explained why, to everyone, in the room, and said, truthfully, that you didn't like it either.

Then the week changes shape. On Monday a dancer you've worked with before pulls you aside and says, with a nervous laugh, that people have been "saying things in the warm-up." You ask what. She shrugs. "Just that you're intense. Maybe too intense." You say intensity is the job. "Right. That's what I said." That same afternoon the remarks come from three directions at once: a principal says your counts are "a lot," a swing asks if you could "soften the notes," and the dance captain says the room "feels tense lately." On Wednesday, in the green room, a small group says your name, and then you hear the silence of people who have heard you.

On Tuesday the stage manager is a little formal with you. On Wednesday two assistants who used to ask you questions during breaks start eating lunch in the corridor. On Thursday you walk into the studio and a conversation ends in the way conversations end only when they were about someone.

The following Monday the production office holds a meeting. You sit at a long table with the producer, the stage manager and the company manager. Your name is said once, and then no one says a word, neither for you nor against. The radiator ticks. You go back through your six weeks for the thing you did, because a silence like that must be answering something.

"We have noticed a pattern," the producer says. You ask what the pattern is. "Multiple voices." The word that has followed you all week is in the room now: intense, several people have said, too intense. You say intensity is the job, and the producer writes something down.

In the corridor afterwards the dancer who first warned you stands with the two assistants. They have a way of standing now. You say good morning, and she looks at them before she looks at you. It would take more than a smile to cross that space.

You ask whether the cut to the second act might be part of what people are reacting to. The producer says that decision was made by "the creative team," which is to say, you. The curfew and the schedule, which nobody has mentioned all week, have somehow become your personality.

It began on the Friday before, you remember. Three separate complaints reached production within the same two-hour window; the company manager, a person you trust, told you in the corridor over the weekend and then regretted it. One was about tone. One was about a rehearsal running over by seven minutes. One was about a note you gave a dancer in front of others, a note that began, you are certain, with a compliment. An email had come that afternoon, copying the company manager and a liaison from the presenting venue: *We have received several concerns regarding the working environment in rehearsal. We would like to schedule a conversation early next week.* Not an accusation, and not an invitation. The sentence had been made as smooth as a stone. You asked which concerns, and were told that for confidentiality details could not be shared yet.

Nobody has said one thing so large it can be answered. They have said a dozen things so small that answering them would look like defensiveness. You give notes quietly now, one dancer at a time, and start each with a compliment you have to think about. You check the clock against seven minutes. You sleep in pieces and go through every rehearsal like a film you cannot stop. You have not played the cracked speaker since.

The next morning, on the whiteboard where you post the call times, someone has written in marker: *please be kind.* You do not know who. It could be about you. It could be to you.

## The Name

That's **Mobbing**.

## Definition

Mobbing is the sequence where a person is targeted by many contributors whose individual actions each seem minor, using Pile-Ons, Ostracism, Silent Coalitions, Smear Campaigns, Group Consolidation, Scapegoating, and Coordinated Reporting to convert the need for safety in numbers and the fear of standing alone into the loss of individual judgment and of safety from coordinated group pressure. In rehearsal, touring and production settings, where a leader works in close quarters with dozens of people under time pressure, discomfort is common and grievances are real, which makes the pattern hard to separate from legitimate concern. It is hard to detect because each complaint may be sincere, because the cumulative effect is greater than any single piece, and because institutions can use the volume as evidence of fault. Whether the activity was coordinated would require additional evidence. What can be lost is professional standing, a sense of fair process, and the ability to think clearly under pressure.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Pile-Ons** — Comments in warm-ups, remarks in the green room, small criticisms from several directions at once. This functions by multiplying minor grievances until their number feels like a verdict. Concern rises when the comments arrive together, from different directions, and each is too small to answer.

2. **Ostracism** — Lunch moved to the corridor, assistants no longer asking questions, a conversation that ends when you enter. This functions by cooling the social temperature around the person, so that isolation precedes any formal process. Concern rises when the withdrawal is broad, sudden and unexplained.

3. **Silent Coalitions** — No one says a word at the Monday meeting, neither for nor against. This functions by letting institutional silence grant permission, since no one is seen to disagree with the pile. Concern rises when people with influence say nothing and decline to confirm or dispute what they know.

4. **Smear Campaigns** — "Intense," "too intense," "the pattern." This functions by recasting professional decisions as personal failings, such as a curfew-driven cut becoming a temperament. Concern rises when characterizations of the person replace descriptions of specific events.

5. **Group Consolidation** — Those who joined the concern become a bloc, and returning to neutral becomes harder. This functions by bonding people through a shared target and making the group's cohesion depend on the target's fault. Concern rises when people who were privately sympathetic stop speaking up because it would cost them their place.

6. **Scapegoating** — The cut to the second act, the curfew and the schedule, all institutional decisions, become the choreographer's character. This functions by moving the blame for a system's pressures onto an individual who can absorb it. Concern rises when decisions made by several parties are described as one person's behavior.

7. **Coordinated Reporting** — Three formal complaints reach production within two hours, copied to higher offices. This functions by overwhelming the capacity of a formal process and by making a cluster appear to be independent corroboration. Concern rises when timing, wording or routing suggest shared origin, and when the process treats count as proof.

## Why This Order

**Pile-Ons** come first because they are the lowest-risk move for any single participant. A remark in a warm-up costs nothing, commits to nothing, and cannot be refuted. But many of them, arriving together, create the atmosphere that everything else depends on. It has to come first, because the assistants eating lunch in the corridor only looks reasonable once "too intense" is already in the warm-up talk; start with the empty lunch table instead and you would ask them plainly what had changed.

> At work, one person jokes about your lunch, another sighs at your idea, a third rolls their eyes. Each thing is tiny, and you could never complain about any one of them. Flip it: if your usual table at work filled up before anyone joked about your lunch, you would ask people plainly why.

**Ostracism** converts atmosphere into isolation. Before anyone files anything, the target is already alone in the room, and the loneliness itself weakens judgment. It has to come before the Monday meeting where nobody speaks, because that silence only feels like a verdict once you are already eating alone; the other way round, you would ask the stage manager for his view and expect an answer.

> You walk into the break room and the chat stops. The next day your usual table has somehow filled up. Nobody has said a word to you, and alone, every small doubt gets louder. Flip it: if nobody answered you in a team meeting while friends still sat with you at break, you would ask one of them what they thought.

**Silent Coalitions** then supply permission. When people with authority say nothing, the pile does not feel like an act; it feels like the way things are. It has to come before "intense" becomes the theme in the producer's mouth, because one word only sticks once nobody with authority has spoken for you; the other way round, you would ask the producer to name a specific note.

> You raise a problem in the team meeting and nobody answers, either way. You go home wondering what you did, because a silence like that has to mean something. Flip it: if people called you "a bit much" before any meeting went silent, you would ask someone to name one thing you did.

**Smear Campaigns** move from mood to story. A set of complaints needs a theme, and "intense" supplies it. The theme turns professional decisions into character, which is the step that makes Scapegoating possible later, and which means that every note the choreographer ever gave can be re-read as evidence. It has to come before the dancer and two assistants start standing together, because a group only holds firm once it has a story to defend; the other way round, you would talk to each alone about the cut.

> Once people call you "a bit much," a loud laugh, a long email and a firm opinion all become the same thing: you being a bit much. Even your kindest moments get read that way. Flip it: if a group stood together against you before anyone called you a bit much, you would talk to each person alone.

**Group Consolidation** hardens the arrangement. People who joined early now have a stake in being right. It has to come before the curfew cut becomes your personality, because the venue's pressure only lands on you once a group needs to be right; the other way round, you would point to the curfew and the four-minute cut.

> Three friends said you were "being weird" at the barbecue. Now they have a story to protect, and backing down would mean admitting they were wrong. Flip it: if you were blamed for something at the barbecue before your friends had any story to protect, you would point to what really went wrong.

The **Scapegoating** step then discharges something real: the show is behind, the schedule is cut, the venue's rules are inflexible. Those pressures are uncomfortable to hold and easy to place on one person. It has to come before the three complaints in one Friday window, because separate forms only aim at one person once the schedule has already become your fault; the other way round, you would ask production to look at the curfew.

> The family holiday goes wrong because a train was cancelled, and somehow it becomes "the trip Dad ruined by booking it." Nobody mentions the train again. Flip it: if three relatives complained about Dad's booking on the same afternoon before the trip, you would ask each of them what the worry was.

**Coordinated Reporting** is the closing move and the key conversion point. Once formal complaints arrive at the same time, the institution cannot ignore them, and cannot easily tell whether they are many independent concerns or one concern repeated. It has to come last, because three same-afternoon complaints only look like a pattern once the silence, the label and the blame are in place; earlier, you would ask production to show you each one separately.

> Three people complain about you on the same afternoon. To whoever reads them it looks like three separate worries. Nobody can tell whether it is one worry said three times. Flip it: if the three same-afternoon complaints had come before anyone was blamed for anything, you would have seen it for what it is and asked to read each one.

What would weaken the sequence: the right to see the specifics; a process that investigates each complaint on its own merits; a single colleague who says, in the open, "that is not what I saw."

## Individual Level

The person may begin to doubt their own judgment, replaying rehearsals for evidence against themselves. Over time, they may soften their work to avoid conflict, withdraw from the room, or become so careful that the artistic voice that earned the job disappears.

They may lose sleep, feel dread on the way to work, and become hypervigilant to tone. Sincere feedback and group pressure can blur together, and the person may struggle to tell which concerns deserve a thoughtful response and which are momentum.

## Institutional Echo

Production environments combine tight schedules, rotating casts, and layered reporting lines, so that discomfort has no obvious outlet and informal complaint tends to travel. Where complaint processes count the number of reports and not their independence, a cluster can carry more weight than its content warrants.

Where a presenting venue, a production office and a creative team share responsibility for a schedule, the pressure created by a decision may attach to whoever is most visible. Confidentiality around complaints protects participants and may also prevent the target from responding to specifics.

The honest reading is that real concerns about conduct in rehearsal exist, that some complaints in a cluster may be legitimate, and that a fair process must take them seriously. The pattern lies in the combination of convergent timing, personalization of institutional decisions, and a process that cannot distinguish them. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that when many people say the same thing, it must be true. It sits beside the creative-industry saying that great work requires a tough leader, and the newer belief that any discomfort is harm. Each belief can be used to explain whatever outcome the group has already reached.

The value being exploited is our wish to belong to the many: it is safer to stand with the crowd than beside the accused. The public story is a workplace that listened. The private loss is a person who was never given the specifics, and who will wonder for years which of those voices was real.

## Observable Indicators

1. Several complaints arrive within hours of each other, with similar wording or routing.
2. Complaints are about tone or personality, not specific events, dates or witnesses.
3. Group social contact with the person cools abruptly across multiple people.
4. Those with authority decline to state a position or confirm facts they know.
5. Decisions made by production, a venue or management are described as the person's behavior.
6. The person is told details cannot be shared, but the conclusion is already described as a "pattern."
7. Colleagues report being asked to "add their voice" or "share their experience."
8. Ordinary feedback is later cited as part of a pattern.
9. Complaints are copied to additional offices at once.
10. Support from colleagues is offered privately but not in the room.
11. The meeting to respond is scheduled quickly, with limited time to prepare.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Rehearsal schedules, call sheets and run times, showing who set the curfew and the cuts.
- Written notes of the production decision that reduced the second act, with who approved it.
- Dated copies of any emails from the production office about "concerns," with recipients.
- A private log of when each change in colleagues' behavior began.
- Your own rehearsal notes and feedback given, in their original form.
- Names of colleagues who witnessed specific rehearsals and are willing to describe them.
- Any request you made for details of complaints, and the answer you received.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Rehearsal rooms are intense and a number of people may sincerely feel a leader's approach is difficult. Complaints at the same time can reflect a shared experience of one event, such as a hard note or a cut. A formal process that receives several complaints is doing its job.

Benign lookalikes include:

- **A genuine shared grievance.** Several people reacting to the same event is not necessarily coordination.
- **A legitimate conduct concern.** Some complaints are valid and require a serious, fair response.
- **Honest creative friction.** Disagreement about staging is part of the work.
- **A new reporting channel.** Several people may use it in the same week because it has just been opened.

Concern strengthens when: complaint wording or timing suggests a shared script; the person is denied the specifics; and institutional decisions are recast as personal conduct.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Specifics Test:**
Ask: "Can anyone name a date, a rehearsal and an event?"
Concern rises if: the concerns are all about tone and personality, and no event can be named.

**SPOT IT — The Same Window Test:**
Ask: "Did the complaints arrive at about the same time, and by what route?"
Concern rises if: they cluster within hours, with similar wording.

**SPOT IT — The Whose Decision Test:**
Ask: "Who actually made the decision people are upset about?"
Concern rises if: a schedule or venue decision is now described as my temperament.

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "Who in authority knows what happened and will not say?"
Concern rises if: several people with knowledge decline to speak either way.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for specifics in writing.** "Could you share the dates and events behind these concerns, so I can respond to each?"
- **Request that each complaint be considered on its own.** Ask how the process distinguishes independent concerns from repetition.
- **Document the decision trail.** Write down who set the curfew, who approved the cut, and when.
- **Bring a support person or representative to any meeting,** such as a union or guild representative or a trusted colleague.
- **Do not respond to individuals in the group.** Direct replies to rumor can look like retaliation.
- **Respond to substance, not tone.** If a concern is valid, acknowledge it plainly and describe what you will change.
- **Keep your work consistent.** Maintain your normal standard in rehearsal, without overcorrecting.
- **Find one outside voice.** A mentor or peer from another production can offer perspective.
- **Take care of yourself.** Sleep, support and medical help if stress is affecting you.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A cluster of complaints in a short window; cooling of social contact; decisions of the production office attributed to you.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A formal process that refuses to share specifics, or treats number of complaints as proof; loss of work or assignments tied to the cluster; evidence that people were asked to submit complaints. Relevant routes may include a union or guild representative, an employment or entertainment attorney, a mediator, and a labor or workplace authority. Which routes apply is jurisdiction-specific.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats or intimidation; harassment that moves beyond the workplace; blackmail or demands for money or silence; any risk to your physical safety; thoughts of self-harm. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "I take these concerns seriously. Could you share the specific events, so I can respond to each?"
- "I'd like to understand how the process considers separate complaints on their own merits."
- "The second-act cut was made to meet the venue curfew. Here is the email trail."
- "May I bring a representative to our meeting?"
- "If a note of mine landed badly, I'd like to hear about it and talk about it directly."
- "I'm open to feedback. I'd like it in writing, so that I can respond carefully."

## Reflection

If every voice against you were true, what would you change? And if only some were, who decided which ones counted?
