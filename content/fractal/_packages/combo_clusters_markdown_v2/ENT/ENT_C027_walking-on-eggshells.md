# C027: Walking on Eggshells — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a road manager, forty-two, running logistics for a headliner and a crew of thirty, and what you want is to get all of them to the last date with every show intact and your name still the first one the headliner says at breakfast. The run-of-show sits in a binder on your lap, in the seat by the bus door: load-in, soundcheck, meet-and-greet, doors, set, load-out. In the margins, in pencil, are marks only you can read. A circle is a good day. A square is be careful. A triangle is do not bring up the merch numbers.

Morning, a hotel corridor. The headliner passes you without slowing and says, to the air, "Some people just don't listen," and you cannot tell whether they mean the bus driver or you. In the lobby: "Who booked this place?" You did, from a list approved three weeks ago. At breakfast the sunglasses stay on indoors. The table goes quiet, the monitor engineer turns a spoon over, and the stage manager studies the floor. Nothing has happened that you could report. You draw a square beside the day, and your shoulders have crept up toward your ears.

Evening, a venue production office with a flickering fluorescent tube and a whiteboard of curfews. The headliner arrives early and says, kindly, "You're the only one who gets it, you know that?" For twenty minutes you are the most important person in the building. Gratitude stings behind your eyes. The cold breakfast feels like something that happened to someone else, and you would carry a great deal for that sentence again.

Then comes the task. Find a vegan caterer at four o'clock, for a show that starts at eight. You find one, with a menu and a delivery slot. "Did you even check it was vegan?" "It says so on the menu," you say, which is true. "So now you're arguing with me." The tone has gone flat, and it is public, with the monitor engineer and the stage manager standing right there. "I asked for something simple. This is what I mean about everyone." Your face goes hot. Nobody moves. Nobody looks at anyone.

You wait until the corridor is empty, and you say it quietly, because you have rehearsed it: could the calls stop after midnight, and could you look at Tuesday's settlement together, since you are over your hours this week. The headliner nods slowly. "Of course. Whatever you need. Clearly I'm too much." The chill is immediate, a door closing without a sound. At six the production call happens without you. You hear from the stage manager that soundcheck has been moved, decided "upstairs." You delete the settlement question from your notes.

At seven, a text: "ur the best. sorry. long day. dinner? my treat." The relief is physical, a fist opening in your chest. You say yes. At the restaurant the headliner orders for you, asks after your mother, laughs with their whole face. Over dessert: "I don't even have to say it. You always know what I need before I do. Don't ever change." It is the warmest thing anyone has said to you all week. On the bus you open the binder and build tomorrow around a face you have not seen yet: the meet-and-greet pushed back twenty minutes in case, soundcheck shortened, a square beside the morning.

The next afternoon the label sends two guests. The headliner is easy and generous with them, a hand on your arm: "I'd be lost without this one." Ten minutes later on the bus, door pulled shut, the same voice: "Is this really the best you could do with the schedule?" That night you say to the tour accountant, carefully, that the bus has been hard. She frowns. "Really? They're so lovely to me." "Yes," you say. "They are." You have not told your partner what happens on the bus, because you cannot find a way to say it that does not sound small.

You wake at four now, phone in your hand, composing replies to a person who is asleep. You rehearse their possible reactions the way other people rehearse prayers. You laugh at jokes that are a little too sharp. Your calls home have shrunk to "fine, busy." You have driven a tour through a blizzard, and you cannot say the venue's load-in plan out loud until you know what the headliner will say about it. An unmarked page in the binder now makes your stomach drop.

Tonight tomorrow's page is blank. You sit with the pencil over it, and you do not know which mark to draw.

## The Name

That's **Walking on Eggshells**.

## Definition

Walking on Eggshells is the sequence where an unpredictable atmosphere trains the people around it to monitor and manage one person's moods, using Ambient Abuse, Intermittent Reinforcement, Baiting, Punishment of Boundaries, Push-Pull, and Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde to convert a professional's need to keep a show and a team safe into constant vigilance that leaves little attention for self-advocacy or exit. On tour it concentrates around headliners, producers and label heads whose tolerance for dissent is low and whose control over work is high, and it falls on road managers, assistants, crew and band. It is hard to detect because the unpredictability is the mechanism, and because the same person can be generous and exacting in the same day. What can be lost is the attention and energy that would otherwise go into asking for clearer terms, support or an exit. Intent would require additional evidence; stress, exhaustion and a demanding craft can produce similar behavior.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Ambient Abuse** — The sharp remark in the corridor, the flat public tone, the pointed question about a booking, the silence that makes a room study the floor. This functions by setting a baseline of tension that is never quite an incident, so there is nothing specific to report and everything to manage. Concern rises when the atmosphere is routine, when bystanders have learned to look away, and when it falls most on those who cannot easily leave.

2. **Intermittent Reinforcement** — "You're the only one who gets it," a text that says dinner is on me, a warm morning followed by a cold afternoon. This functions by making approval unpredictable enough that the person keeps trying to earn it. Concern rises when praise is lavish and unconnected to specific work, and when its arrival cannot be predicted from anything the person did.

3. **Baiting** — A simple task, such as finding a caterer in four hours, set up so that any result may be wrong; a question that invites a defensible answer and then punishes it. This functions by creating situations where the staff member cannot succeed, so that the reaction can be treated as their fault. Concern rises when tasks have no criteria, when the standard changes after delivery, and when the reaction is public.

4. **Punishment of Boundaries** — A request not to be called during the night, a limit on hours, a question about a settlement, followed by chill, public correction or a quiet removal from decisions. This functions by attaching a cost to ordinary limits so they stop being stated. Concern rises when stating a limit is followed by consequences that look unrelated.

5. **Push-Pull** — "Dinner? my treat" after the public correction; closeness right after distance. This functions by alternating warmth and chill so the person works to restore the warmth. Concern rises when the pull comes just after the push and asks the person to move past it.

6. **Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde** — Two reliably different presentations: the generous confidant and the exacting critic, without a clear trigger. This functions by making it impossible to know which one to prepare for, so vigilance becomes permanent. Concern rises when the person comes to feel they must predict which presentation will appear. Naming the pattern describes behavior, not character.

## Why This Order

**Ambient Abuse** has to come first because it sets the climate. Before the first obvious incident there is a baseline of tension, small and deniable, and people adapt to it the way they adapt to a loud room. By the time something is clearly wrong, adaptation has already happened. It has to come first, because 'You're the only one who gets it' only lands so hard after a morning of sunglasses and silence; start with the praise instead and you would hear it as a kind word and nothing more.

> Picture a shared house where one flatmate sighs every time you walk into the kitchen. There is nothing you could report. Within a month you are tiptoeing, and you have stopped noticing that you do. Flip it: if the flatmate gave you rare, warm praise before any sighing in the kitchen, you would take it as a kind word and walk in freely.

**Intermittent Reinforcement** then makes approval valuable. A person who is occasionally told they are the only one who gets it will work for that sentence. The inconsistency is what matters: if praise were steady, it would be a wage. It has to come before the vegan caterer task, because the public scolding only feels like your failure once you badly want that rare praise back; the other way round, you would point to the menu and let it go.

> Think of a coach who says "nice one" about once every five practices. You would play harder for that single nod than for a daily "good job." Flip it: if the coach gave you vague orders and scolded the result before any "nice one", you would ask plainly what he wanted.

**Baiting** creates the occasions. A task with no criteria produces a failure on demand, and the reaction can be described afterwards as a response to the failure. It has to come before your quiet corridor request, because asking about midnight calls only feels risky once you have been scolded in front of the crew; the other way round, you would ask about Tuesday's settlement in plain words.

> Someone tells you to "just tidy up a bit" and will not say what counts as tidy. Whatever you do is wrong, and the moment you snap is the part that gets remembered. Flip it: if they went cool for a week over a small limit you set before any vague "tidy up a bit", you would notice it and keep the limit.

**Punishment of Boundaries** removes the exits. When asking for a night off or a clearer process is followed by chill, the person learns that safety lies in not asking. It has to come before the 'ur the best' text, because dinner only feels like relief after the production call happens without you; the other way round, you would accept dinner and still bring the settlement question.

> You tell a friend you can't take calls after ten. They go cool for a week and stop inviting you out. You never say it again. Flip it: if your friend went cold and then oddly sweet before you ever mentioned no calls after ten, you would ask what was going on and still set your limit.

**Push-Pull** is what keeps them there. The warm text after the public correction does not repair anything. It restores the relationship on the other person's terms, and the rewarded behavior is moving on. It has to come before the two faces in front of the label guests, because the bus remarks only seem normal once you have learned to move on; the other way round, you would tell the accountant plainly what happens.

> After a long, cold silence in the car, your partner hands you a coffee and a grin as if nothing happened. You are so relieved that you never mention the silence. Flip it: if your partner was charming to everyone and short with you before any cold silence in the car, you would tell a friend plainly what happens.

**Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde** is the result: the two presentations are now expected, so the person lives in the gap between them and cannot say which one is real. It has to come last, because the gap between the two faces only traps you once every earlier pressure has taught you to read moods; earlier, you would have described the bus to your partner and trusted your own account.

> Imagine a neighbour who is the life of every street party and short with you over the fence. When you describe the fence version to anyone, they say, "But he's lovely." Flip it: if the neighbour had been short over the fence before any friendly street party, you would trust what you saw and say so plainly.

What weakens it: criteria agreed in writing before a task, a defined channel for concerns that does not run through the person being managed, a second adult in the room for difficult conversations, and a private log that shows the pattern on paper. The key conversion point is the moment the road manager begins to plan the day around the mood rather than the show.

## Individual Level

The person may become hyper-alert to small cues, checking tone, timing and posture, and be unable to switch it off at home. Over time, they may find their working day organized around forecasting, with little energy left for asking about pay, hours or support.

They may rehearse conversations that have not happened, apologize for things that were not wrong, and keep annotations only they understand. They may feel loyal and exhausted at once, and unable to explain either feeling to people outside the tour. They may begin to doubt their own memory of what was said. This is an ordinary response to an unpredictable environment, not a flaw.

## Institutional Echo

Touring structures can make this pattern hard to challenge. Where a headliner or head of a production controls hiring, scheduling and rehire, concerns about conduct have no independent route. Where crew and staff are hired on short engagements, the cost of speaking up is the next job. Where run-of-shows and daily documents carry informal notes about moods, management of one person's temperament becomes an operational task. Workplace conduct protections and who is covered on tour are jurisdiction-specific.

The honest reading is often that touring is exhausting, that high-pressure artists can be difficult and generous, and that managing a performer's state before a show is a legitimate part of a road manager's job. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the difficult behavior is directed at those with less power and whether it stops when there are witnesses of equal status.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is that genius is difficult, that the show must go on, and that the person who absorbs the storm is the real professional. "That's just how they are" turns a pattern of behavior into weather, and weather cannot be held to account.

The value exploited is a professional's pride in keeping things running. The public story is a seamless show and a grateful headliner. It does not show the margins of the run-of-show.

## Observable Indicators

1. Staff adjust schedules and communications in anticipation of one person's mood.
2. Informal mood notes or codes appear in operational documents.
3. Tasks are assigned without criteria and judged after delivery.
4. Criticism is delivered publicly, and bystanders go quiet.
5. Praise is lavish, intermittent and unconnected to specific work.
6. Warm gestures follow public corrections without any repair of the issue.
7. Stating a limit or asking a question is followed by chill, exclusion or a new demand.
8. The same person is described by different staff as both kind and frightening.
9. Staff avoid raising concerns to anyone outside the immediate circle.
10. Staff report exhaustion, rehearsing conversations, or difficulty switching off.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- A dated log of incidents: what was said or done, where, who was present, and what followed.
- Annotated run-of-shows and daily documents, preserved as you lawfully hold them.
- Messages that alternate public correction with private warmth, with dates.
- Records of requests for hours, rest, or process clarity and the responses that followed.
- Names of bystanders and anyone who raised similar concerns.
- Any written complaint route in your agreement, and your use of it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Touring is exhausting, and performers under pressure can be short, exacting, then generous. A road manager's craft includes reading a performer's state before a show. Not every bad day is a pattern.

The discriminator is **direction, predictability and consequence**: does the behavior fall mainly on people with less power, does it follow no pattern anyone can learn, and do ordinary limits produce chill?

- **Show-day stress.** Pre-show nerves can produce curt behavior that is apologized for and does not recur.
- **Genuine perfectionism.** Exacting standards with clear criteria are demanding, not baiting.
- **Warmth that is just warmth.** A kind text after a hard moment can be sincere repair.
- **Mutual friction.** Two stressed professionals can snap at each other.

Concern strengthens when: the behavior is routine and falls on those who cannot leave; limits produce chill; repair never addresses the issue.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Forecast Test:**
Ask: "How much of my day is spent predicting one person's mood?"
Concern rises if: forecasting has become a main part of the job and shapes how you schedule and speak.

**SPOT IT — The Criteria Test:**
Ask: "Did I know what a good result looked like before I started?"
Concern rises if: the standard appeared after the delivery and changed.

**SPOT IT — The Repair Test:**
Ask: "After a public correction, did anything actually get fixed?"
Concern rises if: warmth returns but the issue and the setting are never addressed.

**SPOT IT — The Question Test:**
Ask: "What happens if I raise a concern or state a limit?"
Concern rises if: the consequence is chill, exclusion or a new demand.

## Chain Breakers

- **Agree criteria in writing before tasks.** "For the caterer, can you confirm the dietary list and budget by two?" Concrete specifications are harder to move.
- **Keep a private incident log.** Date, what happened, who saw it. It turns a feeling into a sequence.
- **Move difficult conversations to a calm setting** with another person present when possible, and follow up with a brief written summary.
- **Use a channel outside the person.** A production company contact, an agent, a union or guild representative, or a trusted peer, not a rumor.
- **Stop annotating moods in operational documents.** Keep the run-of-show about the show.
- **Protect rest.** Define hours, say them once, and treat the first test of the limit as information.
- **Talk to someone outside the tour** who can say whether this is normal for the work.
- **Plan an exit you could use,** so the job is never the only door.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Public corrections that recur; tasks judged without criteria; limits followed by chill; you organizing your day around moods.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A pattern directed at less powerful staff; consequences for raising a concern; other crew describing the same; concern routes that run through the person involved. Routes may include an independent attorney, a union or guild representative, a production company's compliance contact, or a creators' legal-aid group.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, physical danger, stalking, retaliation involving your safety or livelihood, or signs of crisis in you or a colleague. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Before I book it, can you confirm the dietary list and budget by two?"
- "I'd like to talk about this later, when it's quieter. Can we find ten minutes?"
- "I'll send a quick summary of what we agreed, so we all have the same version."
- "I'm not available for calls overnight unless it's a safety issue. I'll reply first thing."
- "When I'm corrected in front of the team, it's hard for me to work. Could we raise things privately?"
- "I'd like to check how concerns about the tour are handled. Who is the right contact?"
