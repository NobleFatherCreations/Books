# C085: The Public Shaming Ritual — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a label intern, twenty-one, six weeks into the summer you have wanted since you were fifteen. You carry coffee, scan receipts, transcribe call notes, and sit near the window of the marketing floor listening to people argue about singles. Every Monday at ten there is an all-hands in the glass conference room: forty people, a screen on the wall, bagels nobody finishes. The interns sit on the low chairs along the back wall.

Last Thursday you were asked to deliver a set of clearance forms to a licensing contact by five. They were in a folder a colleague had left in your care. At four-thirty you found two missing a signature, spent an hour chasing the signer, who was in transit, and sent the pack at six-fifteen with a polite note. Nobody told you it was a problem. Nobody told you anything at all.

On Monday, after the numbers and the upcoming releases, the slide called "Delivery" changes at ten-twenty. The performance board, a grid of names and dates with green, yellow and red dots, now has your name on it in red. Beside it: "Clearance pack: LATE. Missed deadline. Impact on partner relationship." The screen is four feet high. The room turns, politely, as one. Your hands go flat on your knees.

"I want to be clear," the department head says, cheerful, "this isn't about being harsh. This is about respect. When we miss a delivery we're telling a partner they don't matter." He is smiling. He is not shouting. That is somehow worse. You have the facts: the missing signatures, the folder handed over late. Said aloud now, they would sound like excuses, so you keep them behind your teeth.

"Can we all take a second and think about what we'd want to say to someone who treated our work like that?" Someone says, lightly, "It's a good lesson." Someone else laughs, not unkindly. A senior coordinator you like says, "We've all been there," in a tone that means the opposite. Your face burns, and you hear yourself say "I'm sorry, it won't happen again" to forty people and a screen.

"Thank you, everyone," he says as the laughter fades. "That was healthy. Let's keep doing this." Nobody objects. You find that you are nodding with the rest, because the room has said yes and you are sitting in it.

On Tuesday you send the missing forms. The contact writes back, "Thanks, no problem at all, got it in time." You tell nobody. On Wednesday your name is still red. On the following Monday the board is refreshed with new names and yours is gone, not marked green, simply gone. Nobody says it was resolved. Nobody says it was understood.

What stays is a habit. You check the clock when you collect a package. You photograph every folder you hand over. You say "I'll get that to you right now" when you mean "I'll try." A few Mondays on, a quiet young man from the second floor looks at the floor when his name arrives, and you do not feel sorry for him. You feel relieved it is not you. You feel a flicker of agreement with the room.

You used to say what you thought of a single, quietly, to whoever sat beside you. You have not since. You read each email twice before sending it, and on Sunday nights you wake before the alarm with the board already glowing behind your eyes. When your mother asks how the summer is going, you say "great" and get off the phone.

The department head catches your eye as you leave and gives you a small nod, the kind a teacher gives a student who has finally understood. You nod back before you decide to. At five a colleague hands you a folder, and before you say thank you, you take out your phone.

## The Name

That's **The Public Shaming Ritual**.

## Definition

The Public Shaming Ritual is the sequence where an ordinary failure is displayed before a group, reframed as a character flaw, and used to teach everyone watching what will happen to them — using Public Identification, Moral Framing, Audience Activation, Social License Generation, Correction Invisibility, and Internalized Surveillance to convert the natural fear of failing in front of colleagues into permanent self-monitoring. It benefits whoever wants compliance without having to negotiate it. It is hard to detect because it is presented as transparency, accountability and mentorship. In music and entertainment, where interns, junior staff and assistants depend on references, introductions and access, what can be lost is the safety to make an ordinary mistake, the privacy around it, and the sense that a record of a failure will be matched by a record of its repair.

## Verbal Breakdown

The component tactics, in order:

1. **Public Identification** — A person's name and failure appear on a board, slide or announcement in front of colleagues. This functions by making an error visible and attached to an identity, so the cost is social rather than practical. Concern rises when the display is not needed for any work purpose, and when the name is shown without context.

2. **Moral Framing** — A late file or missed deadline is described as disrespect, laziness or lack of care. This functions by changing the subject from a process problem to a character problem. Concern rises when the language is about what the person is, rather than what happened, and when causes are described as excuses.

3. **Audience Activation** — The room is invited to react, comment or reflect on how they feel about the person. This functions by making colleagues participants, so that watching becomes joining, and objecting becomes risky. Concern rises when laughter, moralizing comments or "lessons" are encouraged.

4. **Social License Generation** — The reaction of the room is taken as permission to do it again. This functions by making the practice feel normal, popular and deserved. Concern rises when people say "it builds character" and the display becomes a recurring slot.

5. **Correction Invisibility** — The fix is not shown, while the failure remains visible or remembered. This functions by creating an asymmetry in which the error has a public audience and the repair does not. Concern rises when the name disappears without a note that the problem was resolved, or when later facts never reach the same room.

6. **Internalized Surveillance** — The person, and the watchers, begin policing themselves. This functions by making outside shaming unnecessary, because the person now supervises their own behavior. Concern rises when people photograph every handoff, apologize in advance, or feel relief when someone else is named.

## Why This Order

**Public Identification** comes first because without a visible name the rest has nothing to attach to. The board makes a private slip into a public object. It also quietly reverses the usual order: instead of asking what went wrong, it asks who. It has to come first, because the word "respect" needs your red name on the four-foot screen to land on; start with the talk of respect instead and it would be a general point, and you could explain the missing signatures to him privately later.

> Picture a class list pinned in the corridor with one name circled in red. Before anyone asks what happened, everyone is looking at you. Flip it: if a teacher spoke about respect to the whole class before any name was circled in red, you would take it as a general point and carry on.

**Moral Framing** then upgrades the meaning. A late pack is a logistical event; "disrespect" is a verdict. Once the failure is a moral one, explanations become excuses, and the person who has the relevant facts is the person least able to use them. It has to come before "what we'd want to say to someone", because the room's remarks only feel fair once a late pack has been called disrespect; the other way round, you would hear people's reactions first and could explain that the signer was in transit.

> You turn up ten minutes late to a friend's dinner because the bus broke down. "It shows how little you care," she says. Now the bus does not matter. Flip it: if the other guests started saying what they would have done before your friend called it not caring, you would explain the bus broke down.

**Audience Activation** is the conversion point. The room is not neutral. By inviting reactions, the ritual makes forty people complicit and teaches them what to do if it happens to them: nod, laugh, be relieved. A single person shamed is a story; forty people trained to participate is a culture. It has to come before "that was healthy, let's keep doing this", because a practice seems agreed once forty people have laughed and nodded; the other way round, you would hear the plan announced to a quiet room and could ask for it to be handled privately.

> At a family table someone says, "Go on, tell everyone what you did." Soon each cousin is saying what they would have done, and nobody is just listening any more. Flip it: if someone said "let's do this at every dinner" before any cousin joined in, you would ask for it to be kept private instead.

**Social License Generation** turns the event into a rule. When the room responds, the response becomes the permission to repeat it. This is also what gives the practice plausible deniability: "everyone was fine with it." It has to come before your name simply disappears from the board, because a quiet removal seems normal once the room accepted the loud one; the other way round, you could ask that the board show the contact's "got it in time" email.

> You laugh along once at a joke about you at the pub. Next week it is back, a little sharper, because last week's laugh counted as a yes. Flip it: if your side of the story was quietly dropped before any joke at the pub, you would ask for it to be said out loud.

**Correction Invisibility** ensures the cost accumulates. The repair is quiet, the failure was loud, and the record of the one is far larger than the record of the other. It has to come before you start photographing every folder, because watching yourself grows from a red name that was never marked green; the other way round, with the correction shown on the board, you would have less reason to check the clock at every package.

> A neighbour tells the whole street you left your bins out all week. You quietly put them away, and nobody tells the street. Years on, you are still the bins house. Flip it: if you were checking your bins every hour before any neighbour said a word, you would notice and ease off, because nobody had told the street anything.

**Internalized Surveillance** closes the loop: after enough rounds, the board is no longer needed, because each person carries one. It has to come last, because the board inside your head forms only after the red name, the laughter and the silent removal; earlier, you could have told your mother about the Monday meeting and asked someone you trust what to do.

> After being shushed once for laughing in a library, you check the room before you laugh anywhere. The librarian went home long ago. Flip it: if you had started watching yourself before anyone in the library shushed you, you would have seen it for what it is and laughed freely.

What would weaken the sequence: a process that reviews problems privately and fixes the system; a colleague who says "I think we have the context on that"; and a rule that corrections are as visible as the errors.

## Individual Level

The target may begin to experience a spike in dread on Mondays, and to rehearse an apology before any mistake has occurred. Over time, the person may become hypervigilant about small tasks, over-document everything, and avoid taking on work with any risk of failure.

They may stop asking questions, because questions look like unreadiness. They may feel quiet shame that outlasts the slide, and they may start to judge others the way they were judged. And they may carry the lesson into later jobs, where it can look like perfectionism but began as fear.

## Institutional Echo

Entertainment workplaces often run on weekly meetings, scorecards and informal rankings, and some use displays of who is on time and who is late as a motivator. Where interns and junior staff are unpaid or low-paid and depend on references, the cost of objecting is high. Where performance boards, red dots and verbal call-outs are the only record, the repair is rarely documented. Whether such displays are lawful or appropriate depends on the jurisdiction and on the status of the worker.

The honest reading is often that managers want to be transparent, that deadlines with licensing partners can matter, and that a clumsy leader may believe visibility motivates. Intent would require additional evidence: whether the board is applied equally, whether causes are ever heard, and whether repairs are acknowledged.

## Civilizational Echo

The myth is "pay your dues": the belief that humiliation is how a young person learns the industry, and that the ones who succeed are the ones who took it. Sitting beside it is the idea that transparency is always virtuous, and that anyone uncomfortable with being measured has something to hide.

There is something real underneath. Accountability matters, deadlines have consequences and learning does involve discomfort. The pattern borrows those truths to justify a display that teaches fear more than skill.

The public story is a candid, high-performing team. The private loss is a twenty-one-year-old who now apologizes before she is asked.

## Observable Indicators

1. A board or slide shows individual names beside red, yellow or green status in a group setting.
2. The display includes junior staff, interns or assistants more than senior staff.
3. Failures are described using moral words such as respect, care or attitude.
4. Causes and context are not shown beside the status.
5. The room is invited to react, reflect or comment on the person.
6. Laughter, "lessons" and "we've all been there" comments accompany the display.
7. Resolved problems are not marked as resolved.
8. The same practice recurs weekly or monthly.
9. People begin over-documenting handoffs or apologizing in advance.
10. Colleagues express relief when someone else is named.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Photographs or exports of the board, slide or message as shown, with date.
- Your own dated note of what was said and who was present.
- Emails, receipts and messages showing when a task was assigned, handed over and delivered.
- The confirmation from the partner or recipient that the delivery was received or acceptable.
- Any written feedback, review or reference that mentions the incident.
- Records showing whether similar delays by others were displayed.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

A late delivery is a real problem, and a manager who discusses it directly is not necessarily shaming anyone. Some teams share a delivery tracker because the work is interdependent, and honest feedback can feel uncomfortable without being a ritual. Consumer-style criticism of an artist or product is a different matter and falls outside this combo.

The discriminator is whether the cost is proportionate, private where it can be, and balanced by visible repair. A good-faith review asks what happened, fixes the process and treats the person with ordinary dignity.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **A team tracker.** A neutral status board that shows tasks, not blame.
- **Direct feedback.** A private conversation, however firm, is not public shaming.
- **A genuine service failure.** A partner who was actually harmed may justify a firm response.

Concern strengthens when: the display is aimed at juniors while seniors are omitted; the room is invited to comment; and repairs never appear.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Name Test:**
Ask: "Does the work need this person's name on a screen?"
Concern rises if: a process fix would not need it.

**SPOT IT — The Word Test:**
Ask: "Is the language about what happened or about what kind of person did it?"
Concern rises if: words like respect, attitude and character appear.

**SPOT IT — The Repair Test:**
Ask: "Will the correction be shown as widely as the failure?"
Concern rises if: the name quietly disappears.

**SPOT IT — The Relief Test:**
Ask: "Do I feel relieved when someone else is named?"
Concern rises if: yes, because that means the ritual is working on me.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write down what happened, in neutral terms, the same day.** Dates, times, who held the folder and when it moved.
- **Ask for a private conversation about the incident.** "Could I walk you through the timeline and understand what was expected?"
- **Ask how the board is intended to work.** "How are the red items resolved, and how will mine be updated?"
- **Request that resolved items be marked.** "The pack was received on Tuesday. Could the board show that?"
- **Ask a trusted mentor outside the chain** how this is handled elsewhere in the industry.
- **If you are a bystander, say something small and factual.** "I think we have the context on that, can we move on?"
- **Do not retaliate with a public counter-display.** It escalates and rarely repairs.
- **Talk to a counselor or trusted adult if the shame is lingering.** Ordinary mistakes should not leave a mark that lasts.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** You are named publicly for a process failure with no context; resolved items remain; colleagues begin to avoid you.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The shaming recurs, is used against references or future placements, or is applied unevenly to certain staff. Appropriate outside support may include a human resources contact, a school or program coordinator if you are an intern placed through one, a union or guild representative, and an employment attorney or labor office.

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, harassment tied to the shaming, discriminatory targeting, signs of crisis or self-harm, or a minor involved. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, do not confront anyone, and tell a trusted person.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we set up ten minutes to go through what happened with the clearance pack? I'd like to understand what was expected."
- "The timeline was as follows. I'd welcome the chance to fix the process."
- "Could the board reflect that the pack was received on Tuesday?"
- "I'd rather feedback like this be given to me directly, so I can improve."
- "Is there a shared tracker where everyone's status appears in the same way?"
- "Who would be a good person to ask about how deliveries are handled?"

## Reflection

When you check your own work for the fifth time, who exactly are you afraid of, and when did they last need to be in the room?
