# C178: The Big Ask — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a game-audio sound designer, thirty-three, and a studio has asked you to build the whole soundtrack of their next title: the score, the interface sounds, the footsteps, the creature voices, everything you can hear. It is the biggest job of your career, and the call is friendly. The business affairs contact at the game's publisher has a warm voice, a tidy background and a way of saying "we really admire your work" that sounds like she means it.

You are reasonable. People who know you say so, and you say so yourself. You are the kind of person who would rather meet someone halfway than win. That is the trait the next three weeks are designed around, although nobody in this story would put it that way.

The first draft arrives after the video call. It is eleven pages. You read it twice at your kitchen counter with a cup going cold. The first number is a fee for a full buyout: every sound, every cue, every stem, every layer, all future versions, ports, sequels and spin-offs, in all media, forever. Eight deliverable packages. Exclusivity for the length of production. Your name in the credits "at the publisher's discretion". The fee looks like a lot of money until you divide it by the hours.

You do not sign. You email back a short, careful note: this is a very large grant of rights, and you would want to retain ownership of your tools and library and be credited. The reply comes in a day. It is gracious. "Totally hear you. We went in high because that's how our template starts. Let's see what we can do."

The next draft is a compromise. You can keep your tools and your library. You will grant a license rather than a buyout, but the license is perpetual, worldwide and exclusive, and covers sequels. Credit is now "customary credit". The fee is up by a little. The email says the publisher has "moved a long way" and that this is their "most flexible position". You look at the two documents side by side and, honestly, the second one feels fair. It feels fair because you have just spent a day with the first.

You say yes to the shape. Just the shape, in a quick reply: "That's close to workable, I'll review the details." Within the hour a project coordinator writes to thank you for the "agreement in principle" and asks you to start on pre-production, a first pass at the main theme and the interface sounds, so the team can test them against the build. The contract, they say, is a formality. You send the theme on Tuesday. You send the interface pack on Thursday. You have not slept properly in a week.

Then the deadline email arrives. It is brief and polite. The publisher's legal team must close the studio's packages by Friday. The slot is allocated. The studio has told its team that you are in. If the paperwork is not returned by the end of Friday, they will need to "reluctantly move to another provider", and it would be a shame, given everything you have already delivered. The email does not mention that you have been paid for none of it.

It also says, gently, that your emails over the past two weeks "indicate your acceptance of the core terms" and that the studio is "counting on the collaboration you have confirmed."

You open the second draft again. Nothing in it is outrageous. That is the point. You close the lid, you open it again, and you try to remember the moment you agreed to something, and you can't find it.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's the Big Ask.

## Definition

The Big Ask is the sequence where an outsized opening request makes a still-heavy real request look reasonable, using Door-in-the-Face, Anchoring, Commitment Traps / Foot-in-the-Door, Ultimatums and Obligation Enforcement to convert the wish to be fair and reasonable into terms the person would not have chosen on a fresh read. It benefits the side with the contract template and the schedule. It exploits the ordinary human habit of treating a concession as a gift to be matched. It is hard to detect because each step looks like negotiation, and negotiation is normal. What the target can lose is rights, income and, importantly, a clear memory of what was actually agreed. Hard but honest bargaining is common and legitimate; intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Door-in-the-Face** — A deliberately outsized first request: a full buyout, eight packages, all media, in perpetuity. This functions by making the second request feel like a favor you can now return. Concern rises when the first draft is described as "just our template" and the real terms only appear after you object.

2. **Anchoring** — The first number, and the first scope, become the reference every later figure is measured against. This functions by making a modest improvement look generous even when the end terms are still heavy. Concern rises when no independent benchmark is offered and the other side frames the second draft as their "most flexible position".

3. **Commitment Traps / Foot-in-the-Door** — A small yes, "that's close to workable", is converted into an agreement in principle, then into delivered work. This functions by letting your own actions become evidence that you have already agreed. Concern rises when work is requested before the contract is signed and the paper is described as a formality.

4. **Ultimatums** — A deadline and a loss: sign by Friday or the slot goes elsewhere. This functions by compressing the time in which independent advice could be sought. Concern rises when the deadline arrives after you have delivered work, and when it was not mentioned earlier.

5. **Obligation Enforcement** — Your emails and deliveries are cited as acceptance, and the other party's commitments to its team are cited as your responsibility. This functions by turning informal exchanges into a debt. Concern rises when casual messages are quoted back as consent and the unpaid work is treated as proof of commitment.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] The extreme ask has to come first because it creates the contrast. Without it, the second draft would be read as a heavy deal. With it, the second draft is read as relief. The other side is not selling terms; they are selling distance from the first draft.

Anchoring has to follow immediately because the first number and the first scope are doing the real work, not the conversation. Once the buyout sits in your mind, every later figure feels like a gain. The question "is this fair?" quietly becomes "is this better?", and those are very different questions.

Commitment then turns a feeling into a fact. A casual "close to workable" is easy to say and hard to unsay. Once pre-production is delivered, the work is a visible investment on both sides, and the person who delivered it now has something to lose by walking away. That is what makes the next step possible.

The ultimatum works because it arrives after the work. Had it come first, you might have asked for a week and an attorney. It comes when you have already given something, which is why refusal now feels like waste.

Obligation enforcement closes the loop and supplies plausible deniability. Nobody forced anything. You said things, you delivered things, and the record shows an agreement. The other side can say, truthfully, that they relied on what you sent.

The conversion point is the quick reply, "I'll review the details", read as acceptance. What weakens the sequence is a written statement of what is not yet agreed, a refusal to deliver before signing, a request for the time and the advisor, and asking what a fair deal would look like without reference to their first draft.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may find that their sense of what they agreed to has become unclear, and may start rereading messages looking for the yes. Over time, the person may judge each new draft against the first, rather than against their own needs, and may feel unreasonable for objecting to a deal that is merely heavy.

They may deliver work to be fair and then feel they cannot withdraw it. They may blame themselves for answering quickly. They may also feel real gratitude to the other side for their flexibility, which makes it harder to say the sentence that matters.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Business affairs departments commonly open with template-based drafts that favor the publisher, and negotiation is expected to move them [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Broad rights grants, buyouts and perpetual licenses are widely discussed in creative contracts, though their terms vary and what is "standard" is contested [REQUIRES SUBJECT-MATTER REVIEW]. Early-work requests framed as "pre-production" or "testing" before a signed agreement can place unpaid labor inside a negotiation [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Production schedules and milestone slots can create deadlines that are genuinely external to the person drafting the email [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN].

The honest reading is often plain. A high first draft is a recognized opening position, concessions can be real, and a studio under schedule pressure may need an answer. Intent would require additional evidence: whether the first draft was ever intended to be signed, whether the deadline was introduced only after work was delivered, and whether other contractors describe the same sequence.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that a reasonable person meets others halfway, and that the midpoint between two positions is, by definition, fair. It is an admirable instinct and a very easy one to use. Beside it sits the creative-industry story that exposure, credit and a big title are payment enough, and that anyone who pauses over a clause does not love the work.

The language that hides it is cooperative: "we moved a long way," "this is our best offer," "the team is counting on you." The public story is a collaboration between artist and studio. The private loss, when the pattern is present, is the ownership of years of work, handed over by a person who thought they were simply being fair.

## Observable Indicators

1. The first draft requests rights far wider than the work described.
2. The first draft is described afterward as a template or a starting point.
3. The second draft is presented as a major concession without independent comparison.
4. Each concession is paired with a request for something in return.
5. A quick informal "yes" is described as an agreement in principle.
6. Work is requested before a signed agreement exists.
7. A deadline appears after work has been delivered.
8. The consequence of missing the deadline is framed as harm to others.
9. Informal messages are quoted back as acceptance of core terms.
10. Requests for time or review are treated as reluctance or a lack of professionalism.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every draft in the sequence, including the first, with the dates and the sender's cover notes.
- Your emails and call notes showing what you said was and was not agreed, including the "close to workable" reply.
- A dated list of everything you delivered, with file names, timestamps and who requested it.
- The deadline email and any earlier message about timing, so the order can be shown.
- Any statement that early work is a formality, a test or covered later.
- Written summaries you send after calls, and the replies or silence that follow.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Hard negotiation is normal. A high opening position, a counter, a deadline and a request to begin work are all part of ordinary deals, and a publisher can honestly be bound by a studio's schedule.

The discriminator is whether the process is transparent. In a genuine negotiation the real priorities emerge, the other side explains the reasoning for each ask, work is paid or clearly scoped, and a request for time is respected. This pattern produces a changing anchor, early unpaid work, and a deadline that arrives after the commitment.

Concern strengthens when: the opening ask is never defended on its merits; unpaid work is requested before signature; the deadline appears only after delivery.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Distance Test:**
Ask: "Is this draft fair on its own terms, or only fair compared with the first one?"
Concern rises if: the second draft feels good mainly because of the first.

**SPOT IT — The First-Yes Test:**
Ask: "Did I say anything that could be quoted back as agreement, and was that my intention?"
Concern rises if: a casual reply is now described as acceptance in principle.

**SPOT IT — The Work-Before-Paper Test:**
Ask: "What have I delivered that is not covered by a signed agreement?"
Concern rises if: the list is growing and the contract is a formality.

**SPOT IT — The Clock Test:**
Ask: "When was this deadline first mentioned, relative to my first delivery?"
Concern rises if: it arrived only after work was in their hands.

## Chain Breakers

- **Stop delivering until terms are signed.** "I'll share work once the agreement is signed or a paid scope is in writing."
- **Write a one-paragraph status after each call.** "To confirm, we have not agreed X, Y or Z. I am still reviewing the rights grant."
- **Name the anchor.** "I'd like to evaluate the license on its own terms, not relative to the first draft."
- **Get independent advice before replying to any deadline.** An entertainment attorney or an adviser with experience in game-audio contracts can say what a grant of this scope means. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]
- **Separate the three things being bundled.** Rights, fee and credit can each be answered on their own.
- **Offer a paid, scoped first phase.** A small, paid, written deliverable shows good faith without conceding the whole grant.
- **Ask what happens if you decline.** A calm, specific answer is informative.
- **Do not sign today.** A genuine deadline survives a request for another day.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A first draft far wider than the described work; a concession offered in exchange for another request; a request to begin work before signature.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A deadline following delivered unpaid work; your informal replies quoted as acceptance; a threat to replace you as leverage for terms you have not agreed; rights grants that include tools, library or future projects. Relevant routes may include an independent entertainment attorney, a composers' or game-audio professional association or union, and a mediator if the contract names one. **HIGH → URGENT:** Threats toward your safety, reputation attacks, harassment, or coercion beyond business pressure; thoughts of self-harm from the strain. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not negotiate alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "I've delivered the theme as a courtesy. I need the agreement signed before I send more."
- "To confirm, we have not yet agreed on the rights grant, only that I'm reviewing it."
- "Could you explain why the license needs to cover sequels and spin-offs?"
- "I'd like to evaluate this offer on its own terms, not as compared with the first draft."
- "I need until Wednesday to have the draft reviewed. If that doesn't work, please tell me why."
- "My earlier email was not an acceptance of terms. I'm happy to clarify in writing."
- "I'd be glad to do a paid pre-production phase with a defined scope."

## Reflection

If the second offer only looks fair next to the first, what would it look like alone?
