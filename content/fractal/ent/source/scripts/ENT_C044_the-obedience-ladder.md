# C044: The Obedience Ladder — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a twenty-five-year-old radio promotion coordinator, three jobs deep into a career you used to dream about from a bedroom with a clock radio. You work in the promotions department of a label, on a floor of gray carpet and good coffee and a whiteboard that says WEEK IN SPINS in your boss's blocky hand. You are the person who makes the weekly spin report.

The report is a spreadsheet. It has a tab for each single, a row for each station, a column for plays. You fill it from the data feeds and from the emails the field promoters send, and then you send it up. It is a small, dull, essential document. A lot of people look at it. A lot of decisions lean on it.

The first favor is almost nothing. Your boss, the head of promotion, who learned the business in the days of cassettes and has the voice to prove it, leans on your desk on a Thursday. "Hey. Could you round that count? Fourteen-point-six, that's a fifteen, come on. Nobody's going to know the difference." It feels, honestly, like being asked to be reasonable. You round it. You feel a small warm click of being trusted.

Two weeks later: "That station in the Midwest. We're pretty sure they played it Sunday, the plugger swears. Put it in. We'll get the confirmation." You hesitate. The word "pretty" sits in your throat. But the plugger is experienced, and your boss says, "That's how we do it," and you put it in. The confirmation never arrives. Nobody mentions it.

A month later, a number goes up before the numbers are in. "Just get ahead of it," he says. "The report goes to the A&R meeting at nine." You enter the figure you think is likely, and the actual figures arrive and turn out lower, and you quietly change the numbers in the cell to match the projection, because the report has already been circulated and reconciling it would make everyone look careless.

By spring there is a ladder, and you cannot see where it started. Each step is about the height of the last. Nobody has asked you to do anything terrible. The head of promotion has never raised his voice. In the break room you hear him told, laughing, that he is the reason the department is the department. The VP of marketing says he does not need to see the detail; "just give me the headline."

The week of the big single, he drops an email on your desk. "Please update the tab per our discussion." Per our discussion. You open the sheet. The tab contains a column of plays against a list of stations, and several of them are not yours. They are not confirmed. They are not even, as far as you can tell, real.

You ask the question quietly. "Are these confirmed?" He smiles, patient. "Everyone upstairs has seen the numbers. They're signed off. You're not the one deciding this, you're just the one who types." A colleague nods from the next desk. "It's the same on every team."

And the thing that frightens you, standing in the gray light with the mouse under your hand, is not the number. It is that you cannot find the step where you stopped being the person who would refuse. If someone had shown you this tab on your first day, you would have said no without thinking. Now you are thinking about it, carefully, in the voice of someone who has been agreeing for a year.

You look at the cell. The cursor blinks. Behind you, the whiteboard says WEEK IN SPINS, and it feels like a title for something else.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Obedience Ladder**.

## Definition

The Obedience Ladder is the sequence where a small, easy request is followed by slightly larger ones, each justified by authority and shared among many hands, until the target is doing what they would have refused at the start, using Foot-in-the-Door, Authority Worship, Graduated Compliance, Diffusion of Responsibility, Moral Disengagement, Normalization, and "Just Following Orders" to convert trust that small compromises will not compound into a large one into a position of personal exposure. It benefits whoever wants a result that others will not support in full view. It is hard to detect because each step is nearly identical to the one before, the requests come from someone admired, and the target helped build the ladder with their own hands. What may be lost is the person's integrity as they understand it, their professional reputation, and their trust in their own judgment. This script describes a pattern of pressure on junior staff; it makes no assertion about what any real person did or about legal responsibility, and intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Foot-in-the-Door** — "Round that count." This functions by starting with a request so small and reasonable that agreeing seems like good sense, which makes the next, slightly larger request feel consistent with who you already are. Concern rises when a minor favor touches the accuracy of a record and is framed as obvious.

2. **Authority Worship** — The boss who "learned the business in the days of cassettes," the VP who does not need to see detail. This functions by treating the senior person's judgment as beyond question, so deference replaces evaluation. Concern rises when seniority is cited as the reason and the question "is this accurate?" is treated as disrespect.

3. **Graduated Compliance** — From rounding, to an unconfirmed play, to a projection entered as fact, to a column of unverified stations. This functions by making each step only slightly larger than the last, so it feels small beside what has already been done. Concern rises when requests creep in one direction and you are asked to do something you would have refused on day one.

4. **Diffusion of Responsibility** — "Everyone upstairs has seen the numbers." "It's the same on every team." This functions by spreading ownership across so many people that no one feels fully accountable. Concern rises when everyone is said to have approved but no one is named, and you are the one who types.

5. **Moral Disengagement** — "You're not the one deciding this, you're just the one who types." This functions by relabeling the task as clerical, not ethical, so the question of right and wrong seems to belong to someone else. Concern rises when your role is described as mechanical precisely when the task touches accuracy.

6. **Normalization** — "That's how we do it." "Nobody's going to know." This functions by presenting the practice as ordinary, so doubt feels naive. Concern rises when you cannot tell whether other teams actually do the same, and the claim cannot be checked.

7. **"Just Following Orders"** — "Per our discussion." This functions by creating a written instruction that appears to move responsibility upward while leaving the target's hands on the work. Concern rises when the instruction is vague, undated, arrives only by email after the fact, or when the deference you were encouraged to show is later described as your own decision.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Foot-in-the-Door has to be first because the whole ladder depends on the first yes. A request to enter unconfirmed numbers on day one would produce a refusal or a question. A request to round a decimal produces neither. And once the first yes is given, the person has a story about themselves: I am someone who is flexible, who helps, who is trusted.

Authority Worship prepares the second step by supplying a reason that is not a reason: because he is senior, because he knows the business. It makes the target less likely to check, and more likely to read doubt as youth. Graduated Compliance then does the real work, because every step is anchored on the last; the only available refusal is a refusal of an increment that is nearly identical to one already accepted, which is psychologically very hard.

Diffusion of Responsibility is what weakens independent evaluation: if the numbers have been seen by everyone, the target's doubt has no obvious owner. Moral Disengagement raises exit cost by recasting the target as a clerk, and refusal as a refusal of the job. Normalization supplies plausible deniability, because a practice that is "just how we do it" cannot be anyone's decision. "Just Following Orders" closes the loop with a document, usually an email, which looks like protection for the junior person and is often the opposite: it records that they did the work.

The conversion point is the first time the person adjusts a number they cannot verify. What would weaken the sequence: a written standard for what counts as a confirmed play, a rule that data changes need a named approver, a peer who also asks "are these confirmed?", and a refusal of the very first increment.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may start to feel complicit and cannot say when it began. Over time, they may rehearse justifications in the elevator, and may develop a flat, careful voice when they speak about their work. They may stop checking whether they would have agreed to the first request on day one. They may be afraid to ask a colleague for fear of what the answer would show. They may feel loyal to the person who mentored them while also feeling afraid of them. They may become anxious about the report itself, or about what could happen if it were examined. And they may confuse the pressure to comply with being trusted, which is the very feeling the ladder is built on.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Radio promotion and reporting involve intermediaries, projected figures, field promoters and tight weekly cycles, and reports often travel up through several levels before a decision [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. In departments where numbers are revised under deadline pressure, the line between a projection, an estimate and a confirmed figure can blur without anyone intending it [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Rules on how airplay and promotional activity may be reported, and on gifts and incentives to stations, vary by place [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC] and should be checked with qualified sources [VERIFIED SOURCE NEEDED]. ## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that loyalty and deference to experienced people is how you learn, and that you earn your place by being useful. "Pay your dues," "don't burn bridges" and "be a team player" are the cultural voice of the ladder. The value being exploited is the genuine wish to be trusted, and the respect young people rightly have for people who know the work. The public story is a department that hits its numbers and a young coordinator who is going places. The private cost, when the pattern is present, is a person who held the pen on something they would not have signed on day one.

## Observable Indicators

1. A small request to adjust a figure is framed as obvious or trivial.
2. Requests grow in size or ambiguity over weeks.
3. Unconfirmed entries are requested "with confirmation to follow" and it never arrives.
4. Projected figures are asked to be entered as actuals.
5. Approval is described as given by "everyone upstairs" with no names.
6. The task is described as clerical or "just typing."
7. Instructions arrive by short email after a verbal ask, or not at all.
8. Questions about accuracy are described as lack of trust.
9. Seniority is cited as the reason an entry is right.
10. Colleagues describe the same practice as universal, but cannot show it.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Promotion teams round, estimate and revise constantly, and a manager who asks for a quick figure before a meeting, clearly labeled as a projection, is doing ordinary work. Honest requests are labeled as estimates, are corrected when actuals arrive, come with a source, and are comfortable being questioned. A manager who says "I need that by nine, mark it as projected, and we'll update it" is not building a ladder.

Concern strengthens when: (1) estimates are entered as confirmed and never corrected; (2) each request is slightly more questionable than the last; (3) questions about accuracy are treated as disloyalty; (4) approval is claimed from people who cannot be named.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The First-Day Test:**
Ask: "If this request had been my first one, would I have said yes?"
Concern rises if: "No, but each step felt like the one before."

**SPOT IT — The Source Test:**
Ask: "Can I point to the data behind every figure I enter?"
Concern rises if: "No, and I'm told confirmation will follow."

**SPOT IT — The Owner Test:**
Ask: "Who, by name, approved this?"
Concern rises if: "Everyone, supposedly, but nobody I can name."

**SPOT IT — The Typist Test:**
Ask: "Am I being told that accuracy is not my question?"
Concern rises if: "Yes, I'm 'just the one who types.'"

## Chain Breakers

- **Run the first-day test.** Write down the first request and the current one and ask whether you would have accepted the second at the start.
- **Label everything.** Keep estimates marked as estimates, and actuals as actuals. A label is a small, quiet refusal.
- **Ask for the standard.** "What counts as a confirmed play for the report, and who approves changes?" Ask once, politely, in writing.
- **Confirm verbal asks.** "Per our call, you'd like X entered as Y. Is that right?" This documents the request without accusing anyone.
- **Keep your own records.** Save versions and sources lawfully, within what you are authorized to access. Do not copy files you are not entitled to.
- **Do not alter existing records to hide a change.** Keep the history intact.
- **Seek advice outside the department.** A trusted mentor in another company, a union or guild representative, or an employment attorney with no tie to the label can help you see what is ordinary.
- **Route concerns to the right body.** If your company has a compliance function, ombuds or HR, ask what the procedure is, and consider independent advice first.
- **Slow the timeline.** "I'll update it as soon as the confirmation comes in."

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** You have been asked to enter something you cannot verify more than once; the requests are growing; or you notice you are rehearsing reasons for them.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** You are asked to enter figures as confirmed that you believe are not, to backdate or remove history, or told that refusing will affect your standing; peers describe the same requests; or the report is used for external statements. Relevant routes may include an employment attorney, a union or guild representative, an internal compliance or ombuds channel, and a relevant regulator or industry body, with the question of obligations and protections for people raising concerns being [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]. Whether any practice breaches a rule is for qualified professionals to assess, not for this script.

**HIGH → URGENT:** You are threatened, pressured with retaliation or blackmail, isolated, or fearful for your safety or livelihood; or you feel unable to cope. Prioritize safety: contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and seek legal and medical support. Do not confront anyone and do not sign or delete anything while under threat.

## Safer Response Language

- "Is this figure confirmed or projected? I'd like to label it correctly in the report."
- "Could you send me what you'd like entered in an email, so I have it exactly right?"
- "I can mark that as estimated and update it when the confirmation comes. Does that work?"
- "Who signed off on these numbers? I'd like to note the approver."
- "I'm not comfortable entering something I can't verify. What would you like me to do instead?"
- "What's our standard for what counts as a confirmed play?"
- "I'd like to check with someone outside the team about what's customary before I change this."
