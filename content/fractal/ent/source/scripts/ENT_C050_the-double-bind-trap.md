# C050: The Double Bind Trap — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a singer-songwriter, twenty-nine years old, booked for the second stage of a summer festival, and you have never been so grateful to be offered something in your life. It is the biggest crowd you have ever been asked to play to. You have driven four hours to a field full of trailers, and you are sitting on a folding chair in the production tent while a coordinator named in your email as "your artist liaison" sorts through lanyards. Everyone is courteous. Everyone is rushed.

Three weeks ago you signed the booking contract. You read it twice. You are not naïve; you have signed a few of these. You also did not have a lawyer, because the fee was small and the offer was not the sort you can say no to twice.

Two clauses matter. Clause 7 says the artist agrees to "actively promote the festival engagement across all owned channels, in accordance with the festival's content guidelines, in the eight weeks prior to the event." Clause 11 says that during the same period the artist shall "refrain from announcing, advertising or promoting any other performance within the exclusivity region, or any activity reasonably capable of being understood as competing for the festival audience." You noticed the tension. You asked, in a polite email, which would prevail if you were promoting a festival date and also mentioning a local show. The reply said, "No worries, we'll be reasonable!"

Now, in the tent, the coordinator holds up her phone. "Hey, we saw the post from last night. The one with the poster from your hometown gig in August." You had posted it to thank a small venue that took a chance on you. The caption was two lines long.

"We're so sorry to say this," she says, "but that might be a problem with exclusivity. It's the kind of thing that can reflect on the whole bill."

"But clause seven says I'm supposed to promote," you say. "And I did. Four posts. Two videos."

"Of course, and we love those. They just weren't quite what we were looking for. The festival has really specific content guidelines." She pulls up a document you have not seen, a one-page "tone sheet." It says posts should be "energetic but not salesy" and "should not reference other appearances." "If you want to post more, that would be great. But we'd ask you to be careful."

"So if I post more I risk breaching clause eleven. And if I post less I risk breaching clause seven."

She smiles, kindly, and her face seems to concede that this is hard. "I think you may be overthinking it. Most of our artists just get the tone."

You look at your own phone and feel your thoughts flatten. You realize you have been asked to do something for which the instructions cancel each other, and that you are being told that anyone sensible would manage.

Later, in the hospitality tent, the programming director stops at your table. She is quiet and gracious. "It's a shame, really. We were so excited about you. I'd hate for the family to feel like you weren't all in." She does not say anything about the poster. She does not have to.

On stage you are good. The crowd is kind. Afterward, backstage, you are handed a card that reads "Thank you for being part of the family!" and you feel that you have been given something you may have to give back.

In the car you sit with your hands on the wheel and realize that, in twelve days of correspondence, nobody has said you did anything wrong. And yet you cannot think of a single message you could have sent that would not have been wrong.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Double Bind Trap**.

## Definition

The Double Bind Trap is the sequence where two instructions that contradict each other are placed in the same document, so that any action can be framed as a failure, using Double Binds, Moving Goalposts, Gaslighting, Shame Installation, DARVO and Conditional Love to convert a person's desire to find the correct or safe response into the loss of any workable choice and of the belief that a right answer exists. In festival booking it concentrates in promotion obligations paired with exclusivity and radius terms, and in tone sheets that are issued after signing. It is hard to detect because each clause alone is ordinary, and because the conflict appears only when both are read together. What the target can lose is leverage, standing and confidence. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Double Binds** — "Promote the festival" and "do not promote anything else, or anything reasonably capable of being understood as competing." This functions by making every available action a possible breach. Concern rises when no compliant path is identified by the party who drafted the clauses.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — A tone sheet appearing after signing, with "specific content guidelines." This functions by changing the standard after the performer has acted on the old one. Concern rises when new criteria arrive only after a post they dislike.

3. **Gaslighting** — "You may be overthinking it. Most of our artists just get the tone." This functions by converting a real contradiction into a personal failing. Concern rises when a concrete question about conflict is answered with a comment on the asker's perception.

4. **Shame Installation** — "I'd hate for the family to feel like you weren't all in." This functions by attaching the problem to the performer's character rather than the document. Concern rises when feelings about loyalty replace references to terms.

5. **DARVO** — The shift from "your clauses conflict" to "we're sorry to have to raise this with you," with the festival as the troubled party. This functions by reversing the roles so that the performer is defending conduct that complied with the contract. Concern rises when a question about the document is answered as if it were an accusation.

6. **Conditional Love** — "Thank you for being part of the family!" delivered after the warning. This functions by making warmth depend on compliance with unwritten expectations. Concern rises when welcome and affection cool in proportion to questions asked.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Double Binds come first because the structure must exist in the contract before it is used. The clauses are signed in good faith, long before anyone has a reason to test them.

Moving Goalposts activates the trap. Once the tone sheet arrives, the standard can shift whenever a post displeases the festival, and the performer can never be certain they have met it.

Gaslighting weakens independent evaluation. When the performer points to the contradiction and is told they are overthinking, they start to doubt the plain reading of the paper.

Shame Installation raises the exit cost. The performer is not told they breached a clause; they are told they let people down. The cost of continuing to ask is social, and in a small scene the social cost is real.

DARVO supplies the reversal. Once the performer has said "these two clauses conflict," the festival can present itself as the reasonable party that was put in an awkward position.

Conditional Love closes the loop and provides deniability: the festival never penalized anything. It simply made warmth conditional. The conversion point is when the performer stops looking for the right answer and starts looking for the way to avoid blame. The sequence is weakened by naming the conflict in writing before the engagement, asking which clause prevails, and getting an independent reading.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may begin to censor their own posts, and to draft and delete messages. Over time they may become anxious about any public mention of their own work. They may apologize preemptively, and feel uncertain about ordinary decisions.

They may begin to doubt that the contract can be read at all. They may avoid asking questions, fearing being labeled difficult, and carry the shame of being in a position for which no correct answer exists.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that a good artist is a grateful artist, and that gratitude means silence. The industry loves the performer who "just gets it," and quietly treats the one who reads clauses as unprofessional.

The value exploited is belonging: the wish to be invited back, to be part of the family. The language is affectionate: "we'll be reasonable," "the family," "the vibe." The public story is a festival that lifts new voices. The private story is a performer who was told she should have known which clause to break.

## Observable Indicators

1. A contract requires promotion and restricts other announcements in overlapping terms.
2. A question about the conflict is answered with reassurance instead of a ruling.
3. Content guidelines appear after signing.
4. Criteria for acceptable posts are described as "tone," "vibe," or "what we're looking for."
5. A warning is delivered informally, in person, not in writing.
6. Feedback is framed as feelings about loyalty or "the family."
7. The festival refers to your conduct but not to a clause.
8. Different staff give different answers about which clause prevails.
9. Warmth cools after a question about terms.
10. No written statement of which instruction takes priority exists.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The booking contract as signed, with the exact wording of the promotion and exclusivity clauses and any rider or schedule.
- Your question about the conflict and every reply, including "we'll be reasonable."
- The tone sheet or guidelines, with the date you first received them.
- Screenshots of your posts and captions, with dates, plus the festival's posts about you.
- Notes of in-person comments, written the same day, with names and times.
- Any list of what counts as "competing" or the region covered by the exclusivity term.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Promotion and exclusivity clauses are both normal, and festivals have real commercial reasons to protect announced lineups. Staff may sincerely believe that "being reasonable" will resolve the conflict, and may simply lack authority to say so in writing.

The discriminator is whether a written ruling can be obtained. Honest drafting yields to the request "please confirm which clause governs a thank-you post for a local venue." This pattern responds with reassurance, then a warning.

Concern strengthens when: the guidelines arrive after the post they disapprove; different staff give different answers; and questions about the contract are treated as a loyalty problem.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Both-Clauses Test:**
Ask: "Is there any action that satisfies both clauses at once?"
Concern rises if: nobody can name one.

**SPOT IT — The Ruling Test:**
Ask: "Will you confirm in writing which clause governs?"
Concern rises if: the answer is "we'll be reasonable."

**SPOT IT — The Tone Test:**
Ask: "Were the guidelines available before I signed?"
Concern rises if: they appeared after a post they disliked.

**SPOT IT — The Family Test:**
Ask: "When I asked about a clause, did the talk turn to loyalty?"
Concern rises if: warmth now depends on silence.

## Chain Breakers

- **Name the conflict in writing, early.** "Clause 7 asks me to promote and clause 11 restricts announcements. Please confirm which prevails when a post thanks a local venue."
- **Ask for an amendment**, not a promise. "Could we add a sentence permitting posts thanking existing venues?"
- **Keep your posts within your own reading** of the strictest clause while the question is open, and record that you did so.
- **Ask for the guidelines in writing**, with the date they take effect.
- **Do not argue the matter in the tent.** "I'd like to deal with this by email so we both have the same record."
- **Seek independent advice** from an entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or an artists' advocacy service about the clauses and a sensible written question [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].
- **Keep a short log** of what you were told, by whom and when.
- **Ask who has authority** to resolve the contradiction.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A contradiction between clauses; a question answered with reassurance; guidelines issued after signing; an informal warning.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A written or implied threat of withheld fees, removal from the bill or breach; different staff giving conflicting rulings; the festival invoking a clause only after you asked about it; or pressure to accept new terms. Consider an entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild, an artists' advocacy or legal-aid clinic, or the dispute channel named in the contract. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, harassment, or pressure that raises concern for your safety or mental health. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not negotiate alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Clause 7 asks me to promote and clause 11 restricts announcements. Could you confirm which governs?"
- "Could you send the content guidelines in writing, with the date they apply from?"
- "I'd like to follow whichever instruction you tell me takes priority. Please put it in an email."
- "Could we add a sentence about thank-you posts for existing venues?"
- "I'd rather settle this in writing so we both have the same record."
- "Who has authority to decide this?"
- "I'm happy to be part of the lineup. I'd just like the terms to be clear."
