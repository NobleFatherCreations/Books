# C090: Divide and Conquer — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're an orchestra personnel manager, forty-seven years old, and your whole career has been standing in the gap between two groups who rarely eat in the same room. On one side are the players: seventy-some musicians who have spent twenty years on one instrument and can tell you in a heartbeat who sat second chair in 1998. On the other are the stagehands, loaders and technicians who build the stage around them, set the chairs, run the lights and strike everything at midnight. You know both by name. You know which violist's mother is ill and which rigger brings the good coffee. That is the job. The job is knowing everybody.

This year the venue is renegotiating its arrangements. New scheduling rules, a revised overtime structure, a proposed change to how a "service" is counted. Management's office sits above the loading dock, with a window onto the stage, and the managing director has been warm in the way of someone who needs something. She asks you to help "make sure everyone has what they need to be informed."

You are given two packets.

The players' packet is on cream paper, handsomely bound. It describes the proposed changes in terms of artistic flexibility, protected rehearsal time, and a new commitment to guest conductors. It has a page about the crew, which is careful: the crew's overtime provisions are, it says, "under review for efficiency," and there is a paragraph noting that some crew have raised "concerns about scheduling that may affect concert quality."

The crew's packet is stapled, photocopied, and printed on plain white. It describes the same changes in terms of hours, call times and turnaround. It has a page about the players, and the page is also careful: the players, it says, "have asked for a number of protections that may reduce flexibility for support staff," and there is a line suggesting the orchestra is "focused on its own priorities."

You read them side by side on your kitchen table on Sunday night. They do not contradict each other. Each is accurate. Each is a version of the truth that has been cut so that the other group looks like the obstacle.

You are asked to deliver them. Not to explain them, just to hand them over and answer questions.

At the union hall on Tuesday, a cellist, someone you have known for fifteen years, takes the cream packet and says, not unkindly, "I hear the crew's being difficult about hours." You say you haven't heard that. She says, "That's what Dana in the office said. She said they were really digging in." Across town a rigger takes the white packet and says, "The players are going to throw us under the bus, aren't they? They always do." You say nobody said that. He says, "That's what I was told, they've got their own deal in mind."

You realize something else, and your hands go cold. Two weeks ago, the managing director had separate private conversations with the principal players and the head carpenter. She told each that the other group was "a little hard to reach right now." And she asked you, in the most casual way possible, to hold off on the joint meeting. "Let's let people absorb first."

The joint meeting has been postponed twice. Each group's view of the other has become a story it tells itself at lunch.

You are the only person in the building who has read both packets.

You can see the one thing nobody else can: the cellist and the rigger want almost the same things. Predictable hours, honest numbers, a say in the schedule. They have never been in a room together to find that out.

You look at the two packets, one cream and one white, sitting in your bag. Tomorrow you are supposed to deliver them. And you are aware, with an exactness that surprises you, that this is the moment your quiet usefulness has been converted into somebody else's leverage.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **Divide and Conquer**.

## Definition

Divide and Conquer is the sequence where natural allies are kept apart by a party that controls the information flowing between them, using Triangulation, Smear Campaigns, Information Control, Splitting, Priming, Comparative Devaluation, and Selective Information Sharing to convert each group's trust that the other sees the same full picture into the loss of any shared, complete view of the situation. In performing arts organizations, where players, crew, staff and management are tightly interdependent but organized in separate groups with separate channels, this is especially easy to achieve, since each group's ordinary habits supply half the work. It is hard to detect because every document is individually accurate, because each group's suspicion of the other feels like its own conclusion, and because the person who could compare both views is often an intermediary asked to deliver rather than explain. Whether the design was deliberate would require additional evidence. What can be lost is leverage, solidarity and the chance to bargain from a shared set of facts.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Triangulation** — The managing director speaks privately to each group and positions herself between them, the reasonable center relaying what the "other side" supposedly thinks. This functions by making a third party the necessary channel, so direct contact appears unnecessary. Concern rises when messages between groups only travel through one person and never directly.

2. **Smear Campaigns** — "The crew's being difficult," "the players always do that." This functions by seeding a negative impression of the other group, usually as a general trait, without an event anyone can verify. Concern rises when each group hears an unflattering characterization of the other from the same source.

3. **Information Control** — A joint meeting postponed twice, packets handed over without explanation, questions routed to the office. This functions by deciding who learns what and when, and keeping the groups from comparing notes. Concern rises when requests for joint sessions are met with "let people absorb first" and the delay has no stated end.

4. **Splitting** — Either the players are reasonable and the crew are difficult, or the reverse; there is no shared interest. This functions by encouraging binary thinking so that sympathy for one group means distrust of the other. Concern rises when conversations frame the situation as choosing a side rather than solving a scheduling problem.

5. **Priming** — The cellist and the rigger arrive at the union hall already expecting the other to be uncooperative. This functions by setting the emotional frame before any meeting, so that neutral statements are heard as confirmation. Concern rises when each group reports the other's "attitude" before they have met.

6. **Comparative Devaluation** — Each group is validated at the expense of the other: players are told their artistry deserves protection, the crew told their work is the practical backbone, each with a quiet implication that the other lacks it. This functions by flattering one group while lowering the other, making agreement feel like a favor. Concern rises when praise for one group is routinely paired with a remark about the other's shortcomings.

7. **Selective Information Sharing** — Different packets, different emphasis, different pages about the other group. This functions by ensuring that the two data sets never produce the same picture, so no one can see the overlap. Concern rises when comparable figures, dates or proposals appear in one packet and not the other, or in different framing.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Triangulation comes first because everything else needs a hub. Once all messages between two groups pass through one person, that person controls the ground on which every later step is fought.

The Smear Campaign then fills the channel with something to carry. Information Control keeps the channel exclusive: a joint meeting would let the cellist and the rigger hear each other's actual words, which would dissolve the smear instantly, so the meeting must be delayed. Splitting makes the fragmentation feel like a conclusion each group reached on its own, which is more durable than anything imposed.

Priming prepares every future contact. When the groups finally meet, they will bring the expectation with them, and a neutral sentence will land as a hostile one. Comparative Devaluation raises the cost of reconciling, because each group has been quietly told that its own side is the honorable one, and joining the other would feel like a downgrade.

Selective Information Sharing is the last and the structural step, because it is what keeps the whole arrangement stable if suspicion fades. Even if the groups talk, the numbers will not match, and the mismatch will look like a lie by the other side instead of two edits by the same author. That is also the source of plausible deniability: every document is accurate.

The key conversion point is the postponed joint meeting. What would weaken the sequence: sharing both packets; a meeting with a neutral chair and a common agenda; and one person from each group willing to say, "Let's compare."

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The intermediary may begin to feel complicit and resentful at the same time, aware of the mismatch and uncertain whether it is their place to name it. Over time, the person may become the keeper of unspoken knowledge, carrying a picture neither group can see.

Players and crew may start to attribute ordinary delays to the other group's bad faith. Those who hold doubts may stay silent, because raising them can sound disloyal.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Performing-arts organizations are structured in separate bargaining and communication groups: musicians, stagehands, administrative staff and management often have different representatives, schedules and documents [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Where the information given to each group differs in emphasis, the differences can function as a divide without any single false statement [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE].

Where negotiations proceed in parallel tracks, with limited joint sessions, each group may see only the part of the proposal that affects it [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Whether this reflects design or the practical complexity of running several negotiations at once would require additional evidence.

Tailored briefings are ordinary; the concern lies in distortion of the other group, delayed joint conversation, and persistence across negotiations.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that groups with different jobs have different interests, and that artists and technicians belong in separate worlds. It is reinforced by the language of professional respect: stay in your lane, let the experts handle it.

The value being exploited is trust in the people who tell us how others feel. Most of us accept secondhand accounts of groups we rarely see. The public story is that management kept everyone informed. The private loss is that two groups who shared the same hall never discovered how much they shared.

## Observable Indicators

1. Two groups with related interests receive different documents about the same decision.
2. Each document describes the other group in cautious but unflattering terms.
3. A joint meeting is postponed more than once with no firm date.
4. Questions about the other group are answered by one office, not by the group itself.
5. Both groups report the same hub person as their main source.
6. Each group expects hostility before any direct contact has occurred.
7. Figures or dates differ between the two sets of materials without explanation.
8. Praise for one group is routinely paired with criticism of the other.
9. Requests to share documents are met with confidentiality or "not ready" responses.
10. Participants describe the other group's "attitude" using the same phrases.
11. Meetings are scheduled separately, back to back, with no overlap in attendance.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Both packets, scanned or photographed side by side, with the dates received and from whom.
- A comparison table of what each packet says about the same item: hours, services, overtime, call times.
- The history of postponed joint meetings: invitations, cancellations and stated reasons.
- Messages in which a hub person characterizes the other group.
- Notes of private briefings, with date, who attended and what was said about the other group.
- Any union or guild correspondence about which documents each group was entitled to receive.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Different audiences genuinely need different documents. Players need rehearsal terms; crew need call times and turnarounds. Parallel negotiations can happen for legal or structural reasons, and a postponed meeting may reflect scheduling, not strategy.

Benign lookalikes include:

- **Tailored detail.** Differences in what each group is told may be about relevance, not distortion.
- **Separate bargaining units.** Parallel tracks are common and can be required by agreement.
- **Genuine disagreement.** Players and crew may have competing priorities, and an honest conflict is not manipulation.
- **A busy calendar.** Postponements can be just that.

Concern strengthens when: the two sets of materials describe the same proposal inconsistently; each group is described unfavorably in the other's document; and joint conversation is repeatedly delayed.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Two-Packet Test:**
Ask: "If I laid both groups' documents next to each other, would they describe the same proposal the same way?"
Concern rises if: figures, dates or framing differ, and nobody has explained why.

**SPOT IT — The Hub Test:**
Ask: "Do messages between us only travel through one person?"
Concern rises if: attempts to talk directly are discouraged or postponed.

**SPOT IT — The Pre-Judgment Test:**
Ask: "What did I expect the other group to say before I met them, and where did that expectation come from?"
Concern rises if: it came from a single source that benefits from our separation.

**SPOT IT — The Compliment Test:**
Ask: "When someone praises my group, do they also say something about the other?"
Concern rises if: the praise always arrives with a comparison.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask to see the other group's version.** "Could I have a copy of what the crew received?" A transparent process has no reason to refuse.
- **Request a joint session with a neutral chair and a shared agenda.** Put the request in writing and propose dates.
- **Compare packets with a counterpart from the other group.** One trusted conversation across the divide can dissolve the whole picture.
- **Check any secondhand claim directly.** "I heard the crew is digging in. Is that how you see it?"
- **Resist taking sides quickly.** Say, "I'd like to hear from both groups before forming a view."
- **If you are the intermediary, say what you have seen.** Describe the difference between the packets in neutral terms to both groups, without attributing intent.
- **Involve your union or guild representatives early.** Ask what each group is entitled to see. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]
- **Share only what you are authorized to share,** and ask permission for more.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Different documents about the same decision; a postponed joint meeting; secondhand statements about the other group.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Figures that conflict across the two sets of materials in ways that affect pay or conditions; repeated refusal of joint sessions; evidence that statements about one group were made to the other in order to prevent contact. Relevant routes may include a union or guild representative, an entertainment or labor attorney, a mediator or a labor relations authority. Which routes apply is jurisdiction-specific. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats against anyone for comparing documents or speaking across groups; retaliation that endangers someone's safety or livelihood; coercion to sign under pressure; any physical danger on site. Contact emergency services or the proper safety authority, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could I see what the other group received, so we're all working from the same facts?"
- "I'd like to propose a joint session, with one agenda, a neutral chair, and both groups present."
- "Before I form a view, I'd like to hear it from them directly."
- "I noticed that the two packets describe the overtime change differently. Can the office explain the difference?"
- "I'm not saying anyone is acting in bad faith. I'm saying we need the same numbers."
- "I heard something about your group. Is that accurate?"
