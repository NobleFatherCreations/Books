# C031: The Rigged Game — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're an A&R coordinator, twenty-eight, and you work on a label floor where the carpet is dark gray and the walls are covered with framed records you did not help make. Your job is to find artists before anyone else does. You go to small rooms on weeknights, you listen to demo links in the elevator, you keep a notes app full of names, and you are good at it. You know, with the quiet certainty of someone who has been right before, that one of the acts you have been tracking will matter.

Every quarter there is a scorecard. It is a one-page document with your name at the top, a grid of categories down the left side, and a column of ratings on the right. Categories include "signings sourced," "pipeline quality," "cross-team alignment," and one called "strategic judgment." The scale runs from one to five, and nobody has ever told you what a four looks like.

In the first quarter you bring in a young songwriter from a local scene. You do the work: the introduction, the showcase, the three weeks of follow-up. Your boss takes the meeting where the offer is made. When the label announces the signing, the internal email says, "Great pickup by the team, led by" and then his name. The scorecard gives you a three for signings sourced. "You were involved," he says. "But you weren't the lead."

In the second quarter you stop bringing in acts without his name attached, because it seems to count for more. He says the pipeline is too thin. You bring in four acts. He says the pipeline is too crowded and lacks focus.

In the third quarter you bring in two, carefully chosen, and one of them breaks on a streaming playlist. The room is happy. Your boss says in the Monday meeting that "we're seeing the benefit of the strategy we set." The scorecard, when it arrives, says that "strategic judgment" needs improvement. Under comments: "Be bolder, but also more disciplined. Be more independent, and check in more."

You read the last line three times.

Then an act you flagged six months ago signs elsewhere, and you are asked, kindly, to explain the miss. You point to the email in which you recommended a meeting, which he had not had time for. "I don't remember it that way," he says. And, perhaps, he does not. He has a hundred things on his plate.

You look at the quarterly review form and see that it asks you to evaluate yourself first. You write an honest paragraph. He comments, with a smile, that you "seem to be deflecting." When you redo it with harsher self-criticism, he says he is glad you are finally being realistic.

A friend from another label says over a drink: "Honestly, that sounds normal." You nod, and in the nod there is a small collapse. Because the scorecard is not the thing you are afraid of. It is that you have started to agree with it. You have begun to wonder whether you simply do not have the ear you once thought you had. In the bathroom you turn off the light and stand there in the dark for thirty seconds before going back to your desk.

The next quarter's scorecard has already been created. It is waiting in the shared folder, with your name at the top and a column of blank ratings.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Rigged Game**.

## Definition

[SPEAK] The Rigged Game is the sequence where a system that claims to be fair is calibrated so that losses belong to the target and wins belong to someone else, using Impossible Metrics, Moving Goalposts, Double Binds, Scapegoating, Credit Theft, Shame Installation, and Malicious Compliance to convert a professional's belief in fair evaluation into self-blame and eventual disengagement. In the label world it concentrates in A&R and junior staff scoring, split-sheet and credit decisions, and delivery targets, where contribution is real but attribution is controlled by the person above. It is hard to detect because the process looks fair from outside, with scorecards, ratings and reviews, and each judgment can be defended in isolation. What can be lost is trust in evaluation and the willingness to keep trying. Intent would require additional evidence, since managers can be forgetful, overloaded and sincerely convinced of their own role.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Impossible Metrics** — A one-to-five scale with no definition of a four, categories like "strategic judgment" that cannot be measured. This functions by setting criteria that cannot be verified, so that the evaluator's judgment is the only metric. Concern rises when no one can describe what each rating means and the same result gets different scores in different quarters.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — The pipeline is too thin, then too crowded and unfocused. This functions by redefining success after the work is done. Concern rises when the standard changes in response to the effort and never to the outcome.

3. **Double Binds** — "Be bolder, but also more disciplined. Be more independent, and check in more." This functions by combining contradictory demands so that any approach violates one. Concern rises when contradictory instructions appear together and failure is attributed to the person.

4. **Scapegoating** — The explanation of the miss, the flagged act that signed elsewhere, assigned to the coordinator though the meeting was never held. This functions by locating collective or managerial failure in one person. Concern rises when losses are assigned to the lowest-ranking person involved and the decision chain is not examined.

5. **Credit Theft** — "Great pickup by the team, led by" the boss; the signing that is described as his. This functions by moving the benefit of the work to someone with more power, so that effort strengthens the evaluator rather than the person who did it. Concern rises when internal announcements and scorecards credit senior staff for work done by junior staff.

6. **Shame Installation** — "You seem to be deflecting," "finally being realistic." This functions by making the person feel responsible for the system's failures. Concern rises when self-criticism is rewarded and defense is read as evidence.

7. **Malicious Compliance** — The review form that asks for self-evaluation, the technically correct follow-through that defeats the purpose, the "I did what you asked" response to a request for clarity. This functions by letting a party comply with the letter of a process while ensuring the outcome is the same. Concern rises when procedures are followed exactly and nothing changes, and when the form itself is used against the person.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Impossible Metrics have to come first because without them the game can be audited. A scorecard with defined measures can be checked. One where a four is whatever the evaluator says it is cannot.

Moving Goalposts then keep the target from being hit. If the work meets the standard, the standard changes. The coordinator can run faster and faster and find the finish line still moving. Double Binds close the remaining routes: when two contradictory demands exist, nothing the coordinator does can be fully right.

Scapegoating provides an explanation for losses. When something fails, a person is needed to hold it, and the lowest-ranking person involved is cheapest. Credit Theft completes the asymmetry: wins go upward, losses go downward. This is the heart of the game.

Shame Installation is the conversion. After enough rounds, the coordinator begins to explain the outcomes by looking inward. The system no longer needs to tell her she is the problem; she says it. Malicious Compliance keeps the process looking fair: forms are filled in, meetings held, ratings given, and the paperwork becomes the evidence that the game was played properly.

What weakens it: defined criteria agreed in advance; records of who did what, written at the time; a second reviewer; outside benchmarks from other labels; and a split of credit that is documented before the announcement. The key conversion point is the first time the coordinator accepts a loss that was not hers.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may begin to doubt their ear, hold back recommendations, and attach a senior name to their own ideas. Over time, they may find it difficult to say what they actually contributed.

They may stop trusting reviews, feel ashamed of results they did not control, and lose enthusiasm for the work that drew them to it. They may tell friends that it sounds normal. They may keep trying harder in a game they cannot win, or leave and take the doubt with them.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Label scorecards often rely on subjective categories whose scale is not defined [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Where internal announcements credit a team lead for a signing, junior contributions can be invisible [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Where quarterly reviews ask for self-evaluation first, honest answers can become evidence in a review [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. In wider credit questions, such as split sheets, writer credits and metadata, records created late can favor the more senior party [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. The honest reading is often that signings are collaborative, that a senior executive may truly be the one who closes the deal, and that managers can be forgetful or overloaded. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether credit consistently flowed upward across several staff and quarters.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that talent rises on its own, that the process is meritocratic, and that those who do not get credit did not deserve it. "Pay your dues" and "the industry is small" make unequal credit look like apprenticeship.

The value exploited is the belief that fairness is real, which is among the most valuable things a young professional can hold. The public story is a label that discovers artists. It does not show who discovered them.

## Observable Indicators

1. Evaluation categories have no definitions, or the scale has no description of each level.
2. The same result receives different ratings in different periods.
3. Feedback contains contradictory demands.
4. Internal announcements name senior staff as leads for work done by others.
5. Losses are assigned to junior staff although decisions were made above them.
6. Self-evaluations are used against the person.
7. Requests for clarity are answered with procedure but no change.
8. The coordinator cannot get a record of who did what.
9. Junior staff begin attaching senior names to their ideas.
10. The coordinator describes herself as the problem.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- Every quarterly scorecard, review form and the comments, with dates.
- Emails and notes showing your recommendations, introductions and follow-up, with timestamps.
- Internal announcements and press language describing who led a signing.
- Notes of meetings in which you were present, and who attended.
- Any documented targets, scales or definitions you were given.
- Records of flagged acts, including the recommendation and the response.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Signings are collaborative, executives do close deals, and subjective evaluation is common. A low rating is not proof of a rigged game.

The discriminator is **whether criteria exist, whether credit and blame follow the actual decisions, and whether the same standard is applied across people and quarters**.

- **Genuine team credit.** A senior executive may truly have led the closing.
- **Honest subjectivity.** A scorecard can be imprecise and still sincere.
- **Forgetfulness.** A manager may honestly misremember.
- **Developmental feedback.** A reasonable review may include real criticism.

Concern strengthens when: credit consistently flows upward and losses downward; criteria are undefined and change; documents recording contributions are not accessible.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Definition Test:**
Ask: "What exactly does a four look like?"
Concern rises if: no one can say in writing.

**SPOT IT — The Credit Test:**
Ask: "Who is named as lead on the work I did?"
Concern rises if: a more senior person is named and the record of your contribution is hard to find.

**SPOT IT — The Direction Test:**
Ask: "When it goes well, who gets credit, and when it goes badly, who gets blame?"
Concern rises if: wins go up and losses go down.

**SPOT IT — The Both-Ways Test:**
Ask: "Can I do what this review asks without breaking another part of it?"
Concern rises if: bold and cautious are demanded together.

## Chain Breakers

- **Create your own contemporaneous record.** After a meeting or recommendation, send a short email: "Following up on our conversation about the songwriter; summarizing my steps so far."
- **Ask for definitions in writing.** "Could we agree on what each rating means for signings sourced?"
- **Propose credit language before announcements.** "Can we list who sourced, introduced and closed this signing?"
- **Ask for a second reviewer** or a skip-level conversation about the criteria, not the person.
- **Respond to the scorecard in writing,** factually, with dated examples.
- **Benchmark outside the building:** a mentor at another company, an industry peer group.
- **Seek advice from an independent employment adviser** about your review and credit rights [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].
- **Do not give up your ear.** Keep your notes app, with dates, as your own record.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Undefined scale; your work credited upward; contradictory feedback; self-evaluations turned against you.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Consistent credit transfer across quarters; blame for outcomes you did not control; reviews linked to pay, promotion or termination; other staff reporting the same. Routes may include human resources or an ombuds, an independent employment attorney, a union or guild representative, and a creators' or workers' legal-aid group [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation, retaliation affecting your livelihood or safety, harassment, or signs of crisis or self-harm. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

Tier definitions and sector bodies are in the Sector Standards.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we agree on what each rating means, so I know what to aim for?"
- "Following up on our conversation, here's my summary of my steps on the songwriter so far."
- "For the announcement, could we list who sourced, introduced and closed the signing?"
- "I see two instructions that pull in different directions. Could you tell me which takes priority?"
- "I'd like to add a short written response to this scorecard with some dated examples."
- "Could a second reviewer join the next review, so the criteria are applied consistently?"

## Reflection

If someone else were judged by this scorecard, would you call it fair, and why has it taken you so long to ask?

## Credibility Audit

1. **Claims needing verification:** No named label, A&R, artist, executive or person appears; the coordinator, boss and scorecard are a hypothetical composite, and no figures beyond the scale shape are used. Tagged: undefined subjective scales [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN], announcements crediting a lead [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE], self-evaluation used in reviews [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE], late-created credit records [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE], employment and credit entitlements [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].
2. **Overstatement risks:** "Wins go upward, losses go downward" is stated as the heart of the game; softer: "in this composite, credit and blame may flow in opposite directions." "Malicious Compliance" implies intent that would need additional evidence.
3. **Overapplication risks:** Collaborative signings, honest forgetfulness and developmental criticism are normal. A low rating or an unnamed contribution is not evidence of a rigged system. Readers should look for a consistent pattern across quarters and people.
4. **Sector accuracy check:** An insider should verify how label scouting is scored; whether A&R coordinators are rated on signings sourced; how internal signing announcements are worded; how the self-review step works; and whether a one-to-five scale with comments is typical.
