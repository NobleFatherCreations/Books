# C085: The Public Shaming Ritual — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a label intern, twenty-one, six weeks into the summer you have wanted since you were fifteen. You carry coffee, you scan receipts, you transcribe call notes, you sit near the window of the marketing floor and listen to people argue about singles. Every Monday at ten there is an all-hands in the glass conference room. Forty people, a screen on the wall, bagels nobody finishes. The interns sit along the back wall, on the low chairs.

The meeting has a ritual no one writes down. After the numbers and the upcoming releases there is a slide called "Delivery," and on it is the performance board: a grid of names, projects and dates, with a green dot, a yellow dot or a red one. It is presented as transparency. "We like to keep each other honest," the head of the department says, cheerful. People call it the board, the way you might say the weather.

Last Thursday you were asked to deliver a set of clearance forms to a licensing contact by five. The forms were in a folder a colleague had left in your care. You found out at four-thirty that two were missing a signature, and you spent the next hour chasing the signer, who was in transit. You sent the pack at six-fifteen, with a polite note. Nobody told you it was a problem. Nobody told you anything at all.

On Monday, at ten-twenty, the slide changes. Your name is on it, in red. Beside it: "Clearance pack: LATE. Missed deadline. Impact on partner relationship." The screen is four feet high. The room turns, politely, as one.

"I want to be clear," the department head says, "this isn't about being harsh. This is about respect. When we miss a delivery we're telling a partner they don't matter. We want our interns to learn what that means." He is smiling. He is not shouting. That is somehow worse. "Can we all take a second and think about what we'd want to say to someone who treated our work like that?"

Someone says, lightly, "It's a good lesson." Someone else laughs, not unkindly. A senior coordinator you like says, "We've all been there," in a tone that means the opposite. By the end of the slide you feel your face burning and you hear yourself say "I'm sorry, it won't happen again," to forty people and a screen.

You do not say that the forms had a missing signature. You do not say the folder was handed over late. Those facts feel like excuses, and the word "excuse" has been placed carefully in the room.

On Tuesday you send the missing forms to the contact, who writes back, "Thanks, no problem at all, got it in time." You tell nobody. On Wednesday your name is still red. On the next Monday the board is refreshed with new names, and yours is gone, not marked green, simply gone. Nobody says it was resolved. Nobody says it was understood.

What stays is a habit. You start checking the clock when you collect a package. You photograph every folder you hand over. You find yourself saying "I'll get that to you right now" when you mean "I'll try." On the next Monday you watch a different intern, a quiet young man from the second floor, look at the floor when his name arrives, and you notice that you do not feel sorry for him. You feel relieved it is not you. You feel, God help you, a flicker of agreement with the room.

The department head catches your eye as you leave and gives you a small nod, the kind a teacher gives a student who has finally understood. And you realize that no one has ever needed to tell you to watch yourself again. You do it already.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Public Shaming Ritual**.

## Definition

The Public Shaming Ritual is the sequence where an ordinary failure is displayed before a group, reframed as a character flaw, and used to teach everyone watching what will happen to them — using Public Identification, Moral Framing, Audience Activation, Social License Generation, Correction Invisibility, and Internalized Surveillance to convert the natural fear of failing in front of colleagues into permanent self-monitoring. It benefits whoever wants compliance without having to negotiate it. It is hard to detect because it is presented as transparency, accountability and mentorship. In music and entertainment, where interns, junior staff and assistants depend on references, introductions and access, what can be lost is the safety to make an ordinary mistake, the privacy around it, and the sense that a record of a failure will be matched by a record of its repair.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Public Identification** — A person's name and failure appear on a board, slide or announcement in front of colleagues. This functions by making an error visible and attached to an identity, so the cost is social rather than practical. Concern rises when the display is not needed for any work purpose, and when the name is shown without context.

2. **Moral Framing** — A late file or missed deadline is described as disrespect, laziness or lack of care. This functions by changing the subject from a process problem to a character problem. Concern rises when the language is about what the person is, rather than what happened, and when causes are described as excuses.

3. **Audience Activation** — The room is invited to react, comment or reflect on how they feel about the person. This functions by making colleagues participants, so that watching becomes joining, and objecting becomes risky. Concern rises when laughter, moralizing comments or "lessons" are encouraged.

4. **Social License Generation** — The reaction of the room is taken as permission to do it again. This functions by making the practice feel normal, popular and deserved. Concern rises when people say "it builds character" and the display becomes a recurring slot.

5. **Correction Invisibility** — The fix is not shown, while the failure remains visible or remembered. This functions by creating an asymmetry in which the error has a public audience and the repair does not. Concern rises when the name disappears without a note that the problem was resolved, or when later facts never reach the same room.

6. **Internalized Surveillance** — The person, and the watchers, begin policing themselves. This functions by making outside shaming unnecessary, because the person now supervises their own behavior. Concern rises when people photograph every handoff, apologize in advance, or feel relief when someone else is named.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Public Identification comes first because without a visible name the rest has nothing to attach to. The board makes a private slip into a public object. It also quietly reverses the usual order: instead of asking what went wrong, it asks who.

Moral Framing then upgrades the meaning. A late pack is a logistical event; "disrespect" is a verdict. Once the failure is a moral one, explanations become excuses, and the person who has the relevant facts is the person least able to use them.

Audience Activation is the conversion point. The room is not neutral. By inviting reactions, the ritual makes forty people complicit and teaches them what to do if it happens to them: nod, laugh, be relieved. A single person shamed is a story; forty people trained to participate is a culture.

Social License Generation turns the event into a rule. When the room responds, the response becomes the permission to repeat it. This is also what gives the practice plausible deniability: "everyone was fine with it."

Correction Invisibility ensures the cost accumulates. The repair is quiet, the failure was loud, and the record of the one is far larger than the record of the other. Internalized Surveillance closes the loop: after enough rounds, the board is no longer needed, because each person carries one.

What would weaken the sequence: a process that reviews problems privately and fixes the system; a colleague who says "I think we have the context on that"; and a rule that corrections are as visible as the errors.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The target may begin to experience a spike in dread on Mondays, and to rehearse an apology before any mistake has occurred. Over time, the person may become hypervigilant about small tasks, over-document everything, and avoid taking on work with any risk of failure.

They may stop asking questions, because questions look like unreadiness. They may feel quiet shame that outlasts the slide, and they may start to judge others the way they were judged. And they may carry the lesson into later jobs, where it can look like perfectionism but began as fear.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is "pay your dues": the belief that humiliation is how a young person learns the industry, and that the ones who succeed are the ones who took it. Sitting beside it is the idea that transparency is always virtuous, and that anyone uncomfortable with being measured has something to hide.

There is something real underneath. Accountability matters, deadlines have consequences and learning does involve discomfort. The pattern borrows those truths to justify a display that teaches fear more than skill.

The public story is a candid, high-performing team. The private loss is a twenty-one-year-old who now apologizes before she is asked.

## Observable Indicators

1. A board or slide shows individual names beside red, yellow or green status in a group setting.
2. The display includes junior staff, interns or assistants more than senior staff.
3. Failures are described using moral words such as respect, care or attitude.
4. Causes and context are not shown beside the status.
5. The room is invited to react, reflect or comment on the person.
6. Laughter, "lessons" and "we've all been there" comments accompany the display.
7. Resolved problems are not marked as resolved.
8. The same practice recurs weekly or monthly.
9. People begin over-documenting handoffs or apologizing in advance.
10. Colleagues express relief when someone else is named.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

A late delivery is a real problem, and a manager who discusses it directly is not necessarily shaming anyone. Some teams share a delivery tracker because the work is interdependent, and honest feedback can feel uncomfortable without being a ritual. Consumer-style criticism of an artist or product is a different matter and falls outside this combo.

The discriminator is whether the cost is proportionate, private where it can be, and balanced by visible repair. A good-faith review asks what happened, fixes the process and treats the person with ordinary dignity.

Other benign lookalikes:

- **A team tracker.** A neutral status board that shows tasks, not blame.
- **Direct feedback.** A private conversation, however firm, is not public shaming.
- **A genuine service failure.** A partner who was actually harmed may justify a firm response.

Concern strengthens when: the display is aimed at juniors while seniors are omitted; the room is invited to comment; and repairs never appear.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Name Test:**
Ask: "Does the work need this person's name on a screen?"
Concern rises if: a process fix would not need it.

**SPOT IT — The Word Test:**
Ask: "Is the language about what happened or about what kind of person did it?"
Concern rises if: words like respect, attitude and character appear.

**SPOT IT — The Repair Test:**
Ask: "Will the correction be shown as widely as the failure?"
Concern rises if: the name quietly disappears.

**SPOT IT — The Relief Test:**
Ask: "Do I feel relieved when someone else is named?"
Concern rises if: yes, because that means the ritual is working on me.

## Chain Breakers

- **Write down what happened, in neutral terms, the same day.** Dates, times, who held the folder and when it moved.
- **Ask for a private conversation about the incident.** "Could I walk you through the timeline and understand what was expected?"
- **Ask how the board is intended to work.** "How are the red items resolved, and how will mine be updated?"
- **Request that resolved items be marked.** "The pack was received on Tuesday. Could the board show that?"
- **Ask a trusted mentor outside the chain** how this is handled elsewhere in the industry.
- **If you are a bystander, say something small and factual.** "I think we have the context on that, can we move on?"
- **Do not retaliate with a public counter-display.** It escalates and rarely repairs.
- **Talk to a counselor or trusted adult if the shame is lingering.** Ordinary mistakes should not leave a mark that lasts.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** You are named publicly for a process failure with no context; resolved items remain; colleagues begin to avoid you.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** The shaming recurs, is used against references or future placements, or is applied unevenly to certain staff. Appropriate outside support may include a human resources contact, a school or program coordinator if you are an intern placed through one, a union or guild representative, and an employment attorney or labor office. **HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, harassment tied to the shaming, discriminatory targeting, signs of crisis or self-harm, or a minor involved. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, do not confront anyone, and tell a trusted person.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could we set up ten minutes to go through what happened with the clearance pack? I'd like to understand what was expected."
- "The timeline was as follows. I'd welcome the chance to fix the process."
- "Could the board reflect that the pack was received on Tuesday?"
- "I'd rather feedback like this be given to me directly, so I can improve."
- "Is there a shared tracker where everyone's status appears in the same way?"
- "Who would be a good person to ask about how deliveries are handled?"
