# CAT08: Digital & Technological Composites — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Category Introduction

[SPEAK] The career now lives on a screen. Streaming dashboards, distributor accounts, playlist pitch forms, social handles, content calendars, shared drives, a team group chat that never sleeps. Much of this is genuinely useful, and for an independent artist it is the whole business. But every one of those systems is also a window, and sometimes a lever, and the person who built the account, holds the password or reads the data usually has more say than the artist realizes. This category runs quietly beneath all the others, because it works through infrastructure, not through any single conversation.

Look at how it feels. A post is due every day, and the numbers decide what happens next. The algorithm rewards one kind of song, so the next song is shaped to match. The pitch tool is a black box. A distributor changes a policy and a catalog disappears for a weekend. The team asks for shared access to your accounts so they can help, and then helps. A parent, a manager or an assistant has your location, your messages, your calendar, framed as keeping you safe on the road. Your handle, your followers and your release history sit in an account that belongs to someone else. And a rumor, a clip, a manufactured pile of comments, can travel around the platform before you have finished your coffee. The gamified rhythm of streaks, counts and milestones starts to feel like the job itself, an unpaid shift that never ends.

None of this is automatically sinister. Platforms change rules for everyone. Teams need access. Safety monitoring around a young performer is part of real duty of care. Content expectations during a release cycle are normal. The concern grows where monitoring expands beyond its stated reason, where consent terms are too long to read and too important to skip, and where the artist cannot see or take their own data.

Protect the basics. Own the account that holds your name. Know who has the logins and what each person can see. Keep a separate device or backup for the things that matter. Read the terms that touch your catalog, your payouts and your likeness. Export your dashboards while you still can. Ask what is collected, who can see it, and what happens to it when you leave.
