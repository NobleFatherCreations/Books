# C196: The Afterlife — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Safety Note (read first)

This script touches on tracking, control after separation and legal pressure. If you believe you are being followed, monitored or threatened, or you fear for your safety or a child's, contact local emergency services first. A specialist domestic-abuse or victim-support service in your area can help you plan safely. Do not confront the person, and do not remove or disable a tracking setting or device before getting specialist advice, because a sudden change can raise the risk. Use a phone, email and computer the other person has never had access to when you seek help. Nothing here is a reason to access anyone's accounts or devices. Threats, stalking and self-harm statements go to emergency and crisis services, not to negotiation.

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a violist, thirty-five, and for eleven years the quartet was the one home you never had to explain. Four chairs, one van, one name on the poster. Your husband founded it and managed it, and for most of those years the arrangement looked like love with a spreadsheet. He found the bookings, held the accounts, and knew which hall had the good acoustic and which promoter paid in thirty days. You played. You were told, warmly and often, that this was the deal that let you just play.

Three weeks ago you left. Not the quartet, him. You told yourself the two could be separated, because the other three players were your friends too, and the music was yours as much as his. You took two suitcases, your viola, and the good bow, and you signed a six-month lease on a one-bedroom with bare walls and a radiator that knocks.

The first week was quiet in a way that felt like winning.

The second week, the quiet grew edges. In a message about a rehearsal date he mentioned, lightly, that he'd heard your new building had a nice courtyard. You had not told him the building. You stood very still in the kitchen and remembered that years ago, for the van insurance, you had both set up a location-sharing setting on your phones, and he had done the setup. You did not remember agreeing it should outlive the marriage. You did not remember it ever being turned off.

So on Saturday you went to the mobile carrier store, a kiosk between a phone-case stand and a bakery. The assistant was kind and young. She looked at the account and said, gently, that the line sat on a family plan, that the plan holder was your husband, and that she could not change the device list or the sharing settings without him or without a letter from a court. You asked about a new number. She said you could, but the number you used for the quartet, the one on every contract, every promoter's list and every rider, was on his plan. Keep it and he keeps it. Change it and the people who book you will keep calling the old one.

That evening you tried to log in to the distributor account to see whether the spring recording had gone live. A polite page said the account email had been updated. Not to yours. You looked up the quartet's website address, the one printed on all four of your business cards. The registration was in his name, due to renew in nine days with his card on file, and the contact form now forwarded to an address you did not recognize.

On Tuesday your lawyer's office called. A letter had arrived from his counsel, and you drove over. You sit in the waiting room under a framed print of a lighthouse, reading it while the receptionist pours water you didn't ask for. It is a demand letter about "quartet assets": the instruments bought through the ensemble's account, the recordings, the name, the mailing list, the sheet-music library. It asks you to stop "representing yourself as a member of the ensemble" pending resolution, to return "all ensemble property" within fourteen days, and to preserve all communications. It is courteous. It is long. It is signed.

Stapled behind it are three attachments. The first is the website registration record, in his name. The second is the distributor account summary, in his name. The third is a list of upcoming bookings, in which your line reads "TBC (viola)."

What you cannot make anyone understand, not your sister, not the cellist, not the receptionist, is that no single piece of this is cruelty. A plan holder is a plan holder. A registrant is a registrant. A lawyer sends letters. Each item, alone, passes for administration. Together they draw a map of where your life still lives in his name.

You get back to your car and sit with the viola case on the passenger seat. Your phone, face down in the cup holder, lights the ceiling for a second with a message from a promoter you have worked with for six years: "Heard you've moved on from the quartet. Best of luck." You never said that to anyone.

For the first time you wonder whether the safest thing to do about your phone is nothing at all, until someone who understands this tells you how.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Afterlife**.

## Definition

The Afterlife is the sequence where separation does not end the hold: after a person leaves a partner, manager or team, they may be monitored, their contact points and accounts may stay in the other side's hands, legal demands may be used to keep them occupied and frightened, control may continue through shared business assets, and the story of the exit may travel ahead of them into new work. It uses Location Tracking, Account Control, Legal Abuse, Post-Separation Abuse and Legacy Contamination to convert the hope that leaving ends the danger into lost safety, privacy, money and a clean start. In music it is hard to detect because ensembles often run on one founder's name, one set of logins and one phone plan, so each piece looks like administration. Intent would require additional evidence, and where safety is in question, specialist support comes before any analysis.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Location Tracking** — Awareness of where she lives and goes that she did not knowingly share: an inherited location-sharing setting, a shared plan's device list, remarks that imply knowledge. This functions by keeping the person visible after she has left, so every move carries the possibility of being observed. Concern rises when the other side refers to places or times it was never told about, and the setting cannot be changed without that person.

2. **Account Control** — The phone number, distributor account, website registration, mailing list and social handles remain in his name or under his logins. This functions by holding the channels through which income, bookings and fans reach her, so she must ask permission to be reachable. Concern rises when credentials or contact details change without notice and requests to transfer or share access meet delay.

3. **Legal Abuse** — A demand letter that lists everything, restricts how she may describe herself and sets a short return deadline. This functions by turning a business and personal separation into an expensive, frightening process, where responding costs money and silence costs standing. Concern rises when demands are broad, deadlines are short, and the claimed assets include things she made or paid for.

4. **Post-Separation Abuse** — Control by other means once the relationship has formally ended: messages about her new life, scheduling decisions, pressure through shared colleagues. This functions by keeping her attention, time and nerves tied to the former partner. Concern rises when business messages carry personal knowledge, and ordinary quartet logistics become the only channel.

5. **Legacy Contamination** — The exit narrative reaches new promoters, venues and collaborators ahead of her ("moved on from the quartet"). This functions by shaping how new opportunities read her before she can speak for herself. Concern rises when people she has never told repeat a version she did not give, and a consistent framing appears across sources.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Tracking comes first because everything else works better when the other side knows where she is, what she is doing and which door she will try next. Even the possibility of observation changes behavior: people pause before moving, and a person who pauses is easier to manage.

Account control comes second because it turns visibility into dependency. A phone number on his plan, a distributor in his name and a website under his registration mean her professional existence passes through him. She cannot simply start again, because the people who book her still look for the old address.

Legal abuse then lands on a person already unsettled and already short of access. A demand letter is much more effective when she cannot check the recordings, the bookings or the account history it describes. The paper looks reasonable. The process is draining.

Post-separation abuse keeps the pressure personal. Business logistics become a channel for contact, comment and quiet reminders, so the end of the relationship never quite arrives.

Legacy contamination closes the loop. By the time she reaches a new collaborator, a version of the story is already there, and any later account from her reads as a rebuttal. The key conversion point is the moment she begins to act on assumption: she stops mentioning plans, stops using her own name, stops trusting her own devices. The sequence weakens when she gets specialist advice before acting, moves her essential channels onto accounts nobody else holds, and has a lawyer answer paper so she does not have to.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may begin to check the courtyard, the car park, the rear-view mirror. Over time she may stop talking about plans on her phone, hold back from rehearsing or publishing under her name, and feel surveilled even where there is no surveillance. She may feel foolish for not having foreseen it, and may hesitate to tell friends, because each piece sounds small when said aloud. She may blame herself for not having kept her own copies of anything.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Small ensembles and independent acts are often run through one founder's accounts, which means registrations, distribution, mailing lists and plan contracts can sit in one name after a split [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Where "ensemble property" is loosely defined, a demand letter can sweep in work that several people made [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Booking networks are small and informal, so an exit story can travel faster than a correction [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. ## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that the band is a family and a family does not divide property. A second myth says the founder built the thing and everyone else is a guest. Under both runs the older story that a woman who leaves should leave quietly, taking nothing but her instrument. The value being exploited is loyalty to the work. The public story is a respectful parting of ways, and the private cost is a person who cannot stop being found.

## Observable Indicators

1. The other side refers to places, times or addresses it was not told.
2. A location-sharing setting or shared device list remains active and cannot be changed without the other party.
3. The phone number, distributor, website registration or mailing list stays in one person's name after separation.
4. Account contact details change without notice.
5. Requests to transfer or share access meet delay or a demand.
6. A legal demand lists broad "ensemble assets" and carries a short return deadline.
7. Bookings list the person as "TBC" or omit her without explanation.
8. Business messages carry personal knowledge or comment.
9. New collaborators repeat an exit story the person did not tell.
10. Third parties are asked to relay messages.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The dated list of which accounts, numbers, registrations and platforms sit in whose name, and who paid for each.
- Screenshots and notes of any location-sharing settings or device lists you can see lawfully on your own phone, kept before any change, and discussed with a specialist first.
- The demand letter, envelope or email header, deadlines and every attachment.
- Records of who bought each instrument, recording and library item, and from what account.
- Any message that shows knowledge of your whereabouts, with date and time.
- Booking changes and notices to promoters, with the original calendar entry.
- Messages from third parties repeating an exit story, and their approximate dates.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Separations in small ensembles are messy and mostly administrative. A founder may genuinely hold the domain, the plan and the distributor account because he set them up. Counsel may genuinely write a broad letter to protect a client's position. A former partner may mention a courtyard because the building was in a listing, or because mutual friends talk. A shared location setting may simply have been forgotten. Promoters may hear of a departure through ordinary gossip.

The discriminators are control, knowledge and consistency. Benign handovers are prompt and clear. This pattern shows knowledge no one gave, access that cannot be changed, and demands that follow the exit.

Concern strengthens when: the other side shows knowledge of places it was not told; access to shared accounts is withheld or altered after separation; demands grow after each boundary she sets; an exit story reaches third parties in the same framing.

Apply the Universal Benign Explanations and concern tests in the Sector Standards.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Knowledge Test:**
Ask: "How does he know where I am, and who told him?"
Concern rises if: no one did, and a sharing setting or device list may explain it.

**SPOT IT — The Name-on-the-Account Test:**
Ask: "Whose name is on my number, my distributor, my website and my mailing list?"
Concern rises if: the answer is his, and moving any of them needs his consent.

**SPOT IT — The Deadline Test:**
Ask: "Did the demand arrive with a short deadline and a long list?"
Concern rises if: the list includes things I made or paid for, and the deadline leaves no time for advice.

**SPOT IT — The Story Test:**
Ask: "Who told this promoter I had moved on, and what else do they now believe?"
Concern rises if: the framing is the same across several sources and none came from me.

## Chain Breakers

- Start with safety, not strategy. Contact a specialist support service and, if you feel in danger, emergency services. Ask how to handle the location-sharing question before touching it.
- Use a device and account he has never had access to for planning, searching and reaching helpers.
- Do not log in to accounts that are not yours, and do not guess or reuse his credentials, even to retrieve your own work. Ask your lawyer to request access, copies or transfer in writing.
- Move your essentials onto accounts you hold alone: a new email, new plan, new mailing list, with notice to the people who book you.
- Let your lawyer answer the demand letter. Keep a dated list of what you made, paid for and own, with receipts.
- Send one brief, neutral note to key promoters with your new contact details. Do not rebut gossip, and do not argue the case in public.
- Keep a dated timeline of messages and incidents, with screenshots of what you lawfully receive.
- Tell one trusted person where you are and what is happening.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Account names, plan holder or registrations remain his after separation and requests to change them are ignored; a demand letter arrives; an exit story reaches booking contacts.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** A demand claims things you made or paid for, with a tight deadline; access to your own recordings, bookings or mailing list is blocked; you cannot be reached by the people who book you. Consider an independent entertainment attorney, a family-law adviser, a musicians' union or legal-aid service, the distributor's formal dispute channel, and a domestic-abuse advocate who can help with legal and safety planning [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

**HIGH → URGENT:** Evidence that you are being followed or located without consent, appearances at your home or work, threats, messages that show knowledge of your movements, any threat, including a threat of self-harm, or fear for your safety. Contact emergency services and a specialist support service. Do not confront, negotiate alone, or disable devices without advice.

## Safer Response Language

- "Please send any requests about the ensemble's assets through my attorney."
- "I'd like the account details and registration records for the website and distributor in writing."
- "My new contact details are below. Please use them for bookings."
- "I'm not able to discuss my location or schedule."
- "I'll respond to the letter through counsel within the time that gives me for review."
- (To a promoter:) "I'm taking some time to organize my own bookings. I'll send details when they're ready."
- "I'd like to keep this to business matters, in writing."

## Reflection

If leaving was meant to end it, what is still reaching you, and who has had to carry that for you?

## Credibility Audit

1. **Claims needing verification:** No named ensemble, platform, carrier, registrar or person appears; the scenario is a [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Single-founder account control is tagged [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Enforceability of any demand is tagged [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]. Nothing here states that any act is unlawful.
2. **Overstatement risks:** "Together they draw a map" implies design; softer: "together they can read as a map." Tracking is only inferred from remarks and shared settings; neither proves it. Intent would require additional evidence.
3. **Overapplication risks:** Messy splits, forgotten settings and broad lawyer letters are common and often benign. The pattern is knowledge no one gave, blocked access, escalating demands and a shared story, not a single lawyer letter. Do not read it onto every founder who holds a domain.
4. **Sector accuracy check:** An insider should verify: single-name control of plans, registrations and distributor accounts in small ensembles; how "ensemble property" is usually defined; how quickly exit news travels among promoters; how distributors handle disputes over account ownership; and whether family-law and entertainment advice are normally separated.
