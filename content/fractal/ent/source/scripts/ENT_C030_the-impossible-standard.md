# C030: The Impossible Standard — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a library-music composer, thirty-four, and you work from a spare room with acoustic foam on one wall and a window you keep meaning to clean. You deliver cues to a sync agency: sixty-second pieces with names like "Hopeful Dawn" and "Corporate Momentum," built to sit under a car commercial or an explainer video. It is a craft, and you are proud of it. You have a brief, a deadline and a good ear for what a brief means.

The agency's notes arrive by email, and you have learned to read them like weather reports. The first batch is generous: "Love the energy! Could the intro be a touch more cinematic?" You make it more cinematic. Two days later: "We're feeling it's a bit too cinematic. Can it be more organic and intimate, but still big and commercial?"

You answer with a version that is organic, intimate, big and commercial. You are quite pleased with it.

The reply comes in the evening, from a different person on the agency's team, cc'ing the first. "Thanks! Love the direction. Our client wants it more distinctive but also more familiar. Can it feel fresh without straying from the reference?" The reference, attached, is a track that has nothing in common with the brief.

You open the email chain, now forty-one messages long, and read from the bottom up. Somewhere in there, the same cue is both too slow and too rushed, too quiet and not breathing enough, not unique enough and too weird. There is a line near the middle that says, plainly, "Please keep the arrangement exactly as is, and make it feel completely different." You laugh. Then you do not laugh.

The delivery commitment in your agreement says you will supply tracks "to the agency's reasonable satisfaction." It is two lines in a longer document and you signed it with the quiet confidence of someone who thinks reasonable is a fixed point.

You accept a call. The head of music is warm. "This one's so close. It's just... not there yet. You know what I mean? I know you can do it." You do not know what he means. You say yes anyway, because the alternative is to say you do not know, which would be the very thing they have been hinting at.

The next round, the approval comes: "Great! Perfect." You exhale. The next morning, a note: "We've decided to go another way on the brief. Can you adapt it to the new one by Friday?" The cue you delivered has become a draft for a different request, and the praise has become a sort of fiction.

At midnight you find yourself writing to a colleague. "I think I'm just not good at this anymore." You delete it, because you don't want to be a person who says that. But you do think it. You have thought it for weeks. It has not occurred to you, yet, to ask who wrote the standard you are failing, or whether they could pass it themselves.

You open the forty-second message. It is waiting for you. It says: "A few small tweaks."

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Impossible Standard**.

## Definition

[SPEAK] The Impossible Standard is the sequence where contradictory demands coexist, success is redefined after it is achieved, and failure is framed as personal deficiency rather than the system's design, using Double Binds, Moving Goalposts, Impossible Metrics, Shame Installation, Overcorrection Demands, and Conditional Love to convert a creator's legitimate wish to meet professional expectations into a belief that effort can never be enough. In library and sync work it concentrates in revision cycles, delivery commitments with undefined standards and feedback chains passed through several people. It is hard to detect because every note sounds reasonable and many are, and because the worker is rewarded for responsiveness. What can be lost is unpaid time, income and the sense that any effort can be sufficient. Intent would require additional evidence, since clients genuinely change their minds and feedback chains genuinely contradict themselves.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Double Binds** — "Keep the arrangement exactly as is, and make it feel completely different." "Fresh, but not straying from the reference." This functions by presenting two demands that cannot both be met, so that any response violates one. Concern rises when the contradictory instructions appear in the same message and when failure is attributed to the composer rather than the brief.

2. **Moving Goalposts** — Approved on Tuesday, "another way" on Wednesday. This functions by redefining success after it has been achieved, so that completion never arrives. Concern rises when approval does not close a stage and when the standard changes without the brief changing.

3. **Impossible Metrics** — "Reasonable satisfaction" with no stated criteria; "distinctive but familiar"; "just not there yet." This functions by setting a standard that cannot be measured or reached, so that satisfaction stays in the evaluator's hands. Concern rises when no objective criteria exist and when the evaluator cannot say what would be accepted.

4. **Shame Installation** — "I know you can do it," "it's just not there yet." This functions by locating the problem in the composer's ability rather than the process, so that the person begins to say it about themselves. Concern rises when feedback shifts from the work to the worker, and when the worker begins to apologize for the standard.

5. **Overcorrection Demands** — Round after round of revisions, a new brief on Friday, a "few small tweaks" with large consequences. This functions by requiring effort far beyond the issue raised, so that revision itself becomes the punishment. Concern rises when the volume of revisions is out of proportion to the notes and no one tallies the unpaid hours.

6. **Conditional Love** — The warm call, the "love the direction," the "so close." This functions by tying warmth and future work to an unattainable standard, so that approval feels like a prize. Concern rises when affection and the promise of more work rise and fall with each round.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Double Binds come first because they establish the impossibility. A single contradictory note could be an honest mistake. But a pattern of contradictions teaches the composer that the problem cannot be solved by skill, and, crucially, makes the composer search for the solution inside themselves.

Moving Goalposts then prevent resolution. If the first approval were final, the contradictions would end. Redefining success after it is achieved means the target is always a step beyond. Impossible Metrics supply the cover: if criteria were stated, the composer could point to them. A "reasonable satisfaction" clause lets the evaluator remain the only judge.

Shame Installation is the conversion. When the process cannot be blamed, the person is. Phrases like "I know you can do it" sound like faith and work like a quiet accusation. The composer begins to internalize the failure. Overcorrection Demands then monetize it: the more the composer feels at fault, the more unpaid revisions they accept.

Conditional Love closes the loop. Warmth and future work arrive just after an acceptable round and leave just after a failed one. The composer learns that being valued is possible but must be earned again each time.

What weakens it: written, objective criteria before work begins; a defined number of revision rounds; a single person who consolidates feedback; and outside benchmarks from other composers. The key conversion point is when the composer starts to ask what is wrong with them, rather than what is wrong with the brief.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The composer may begin to doubt their ear, second-guess work that was fine, and apologize for revisions that were not their error. Over time, they may find it hard to tell good work from accepted work.

They may stop trusting their own sense of completion, write more cautiously, and work late for the next round in the hope that this one is the one. They may feel ashamed and avoid telling anyone. They may start to dread opening email. This is a reasonable reaction to a standard that moves.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Sync and library arrangements can involve delivery standards expressed as satisfaction rather than specification [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. Where several people in an agency pass notes from a client, contradictions can arise and no one owns them [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Where revisions are unlimited and unpaid, the cost of changing course falls on the composer [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Whether a delivery clause based on reasonable satisfaction can be relied on, and how disputes are handled, is jurisdiction-specific [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

The honest reading is often that clients truly are unsure, that briefs shift, and that agency staff pass notes along in good faith. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same pattern affected other composers and whether approval was ever treated as final.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that great work is never finished, that the client is always right, and that a real professional absorbs revisions without complaint. "Make it pop" and "just a few tweaks" turn an open-ended standard into a virtue.

The value exploited is the composer's pride in craft and wish to deliver. The public story is a seamless soundtrack under a polished advertisement. It does not show the forty-second message.

## Observable Indicators

1. Feedback contains contradictory instructions in the same message or chain.
2. Approval is followed by a request to redo or rethink the same work.
3. Delivery standards are described only as satisfaction or similar terms with no stated criteria.
4. Notes arrive from multiple people with no consolidation.
5. Feedback addresses the composer's ability rather than the work.
6. Revisions are numerous and unpaid, with no cap.
7. A brief changes while the deadline stays.
8. Praise precedes new demands.
9. The composer cannot say what would count as finished.
10. The composer begins apologizing for the standard.

- A log of hours spent on each round.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Sync work is subjective, clients do change their minds, and feedback chains truly contradict themselves without anyone intending it. Many revision requests are exactly that.

The discriminator is **whether the contradictions are acknowledged and resolved, and whether approval is allowed to close a stage**.

- **A genuinely changing brief.** Clients revise their needs midway.
- **Honest ambiguity.** Creative language is imprecise.
- **Competent but crowded notes.** Several reviewers can contradict one another.
- **Fair revision norms.** A reasonable number of rounds is standard practice [REQUIRES SUBJECT-MATTER REVIEW].

Concern strengthens when: contradictory notes are never reconciled; approvals are repeatedly reopened; the composer is blamed for the brief.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Contradiction Test:**
Ask: "Can I satisfy every note in this email at the same time?"
Concern rises if: no version could.

**SPOT IT — The Finish Test:**
Ask: "What would count as finished, in writing?"
Concern rises if: no one will say.

**SPOT IT — The Approval Test:**
Ask: "Has an approval ever stayed an approval?"
Concern rises if: approved work keeps returning.

**SPOT IT — The Blame Test:**
Ask: "Is the feedback about the work, or about me?"
Concern rises if: it is about my ability while the brief is never questioned.

## Chain Breakers

- **Ask for consolidated notes.** "To avoid conflicting changes, could you send one set of notes from a single contact?"
- **Name the contradiction, calmly, in writing.** "Note 2 asks to keep the arrangement and note 4 asks to change it. Which should I follow?"
- **Set a revision cap and price extras.** "This covers two rounds. Further rounds will be billed at my day rate."
- **Get criteria first.** "Could we agree on two or three reference points and success criteria before I begin?"
- **Treat approval as a milestone.** Reply: "Thanks for approving. I'll treat this cue as delivered; further changes would be a new request."
- **Keep your hours log,** so unpaid time becomes visible.
- **Compare with outside benchmarks:** other composers, a mentor, an independent adviser. Ask what is normal in your corner of the market.
- **Have the satisfaction clause reviewed** by independent counsel before the next agreement [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Contradictory notes in one chain; approval reopened; criteria undefined; unpaid revisions rising.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Payment withheld pending unreachable satisfaction; the clause used to refuse payment for delivered work; repeated reopening of approved cues; other composers reporting the same. Routes may include an independent attorney, a composers' or writers' guild or association, an agency's formal dispute channel, or a creators' legal-aid group [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats connected to payment or future work, harassment, signs of crisis or self-harm, or intimidation related to a dispute. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "To avoid conflicting changes, could you send one consolidated set of notes?"
- "Note 2 and note 4 seem to pull in opposite directions. Which should I prioritize?"
- "Could we agree on success criteria and a number of revision rounds before I start the next version?"
- "Thanks for approving this. I'll treat it as delivered; further changes would be a new request."
- "I've spent additional hours on revisions beyond our agreed rounds. Could we discuss how to cover them?"
- "Could you share what 'not there yet' would look like, ideally with a reference?"
