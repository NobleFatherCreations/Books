# Sector Standards — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Universal Evidence To Preserve

In this sector the paper that matters is often spread across inboxes, DMs, shared drives, dashboards and a manager's phone, and much of it is created in a hurry around sessions, showcases and signings. Keep what you lawfully hold, in its original form where possible, with dates visible.

- Agreements and their history: signed and unsigned drafts, redlines, term sheets, offer emails and any "final" version that differs from the one discussed; recording, publishing, co-publishing, administration, management, producer, booking, merchandise and brand or likeness agreements; option and delivery clauses; NDAs and non-disparagement clauses; minor performer contracts and guardian arrangements.
- Creative and credit records: split sheets and writer credit confirmations (with the date they were signed relative to the session), work-for-hire and session releases, beat licenses, sample clearance records, voice memos and demos with timestamps, session logs, studio invoices and booking confirmations.
- Money records: advance and draw payments, recoupment statements, royalty statements and accounting summaries, distribution and streaming dashboard exports, tour budgets, settlement sheets, merch settlement records, payment records for showcases or program fees, commission invoices, and bank records showing what was actually paid and received.
- Registration and metadata: ISRC and work registrations, metadata screenshots, platform and distributor account records, policy notices and playlist pitch records.
- Scheduling and obligation records: call sheets, itineraries, calendar invites, content calendars and posting requirements, riders and technical riders, delivery notices and default letters, work permits and guardian consents where minors are involved, and medical or wellness accommodation requests.
- Communications: emails, DMs and texts with labels, managers, producers, agents, publicists and assistants, especially around session and signing dates; written summaries of verbal promises ("per our call, my understanding is..."); audit requests and responses; union or guild correspondence; second legal opinion notes.
- People: names of those present at signings, sessions and meetings, and who recommended which lawyer, accountant or manager.

> Preserve records lawfully. Do not secretly record where it is illegal.
> Do not access systems, files, accounts, or documents you are not
> authorized to access.

## Universal Benign Explanations

Most friction in music and entertainment has an ordinary explanation. Before reading a pattern into any single event, rule these out, and remember that intent would require additional evidence beyond the pattern.

- Genuine mentorship, enthusiastic A&R interest, or a real industry supporter who is simply excited about the work.
- Standard recoupment, advance and cross-collateralization structures that were clearly explained and agreed with independent counsel. [REQUIRES SUBJECT-MATTER REVIEW]
- Real release windows, embargoes, festival lineup deadlines and limited offer periods that create honest urgency.
- Legitimate tour cost constraints, venue commitments, and settlement timing that delays payment without hiding it.
- Good-faith guidance on image, styling and brand fit, and reasonable approval rights over released work.
- Normal commission or fees for services genuinely delivered.
- Confidentiality around unreleased projects, unannounced collaborations and other parties' terms.
- Collaboration in which splits were fairly discussed and agreed, and honest creative disagreement in sessions.
- Administrative delay in royalty processing, distributor and publisher reconciliation lag, and platform or technology failures.
- Metadata and credit errors that are simple mistakes, corrected when raised.
- Small teams in which one person legitimately wears several hats, and genuine family involvement in a minor's career.
- Platform policy changes that apply neutrally to everyone, and reasonable content expectations during a promotion cycle.
- Regional or jurisdictional differences in contract practice. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]
- Overloaded assistants, disorganized management, crisis conditions on the road, and clumsy but good-faith communication.
- Normal networking events and the ordinary reputation concerns of a small industry.

## Concern Strengthens When

- The same pattern repeats across deals, projects, tours or releases rather than appearing once.
- Questions about royalties, splits, ownership, settlement or contract terms are met with delay, deflection or a change of subject.
- Independent advice is discouraged ("you don't need a lawyer," "only difficult people ask for lawyers") or the only lawyer offered is one recommended by the counterparty.
- Written clarity is avoided: terms stay verbal, split sheets are "sorted out later," and requests for a written summary are treated as a lack of trust.
- Raising a concern is followed by consequences such as being shelved, dropped from a bill, left off a call sheet, or labeled "difficult."
- Explanations shift between conversations, or verbal promises ("your name on the track," "we'll take care of the business side") contradict what the documents say.
- Rules are applied unequally between artists, between crew and headliners, or between those who ask questions and those who do not.
- Leaving a deal, a team or a crew is punished, or made costly through option clauses, unreturned masters, exclusivity or reputation threats.
- Other artists, writers, players or crew describe similar experiences with the same party.
- Deadlines are created or tightened at the moment of signing, especially after a viral moment, showcase or tour announcement.
- Dependency increases: housing, income, advances, per-diems or health support increasingly routed through the same person or entity.
- Confidentiality terms are used to stop a person from seeking counsel or talking to peers, rather than to protect unreleased work.
- Statements, dashboards or contract versions are withheld, or access to the data used to make decisions about the artist's career is blocked.

## Concern Weakens When

- Time to review is welcomed, and the other side encourages the artist to take the draft to independent counsel.
- Terms, splits, commissions, options and delivery standards are put in clear written form, in plain language, before work or signing.
- Mistakes in credit, metadata or statements are acknowledged and corrected promptly.
- Independent advice, a second opinion or an audit is encouraged rather than resisted.
- Policies, approval rights and settlement practices are applied consistently across artists and crew.
- Saying "no," asking for changes or passing on an opportunity carries no punishment or loss of standing.
- Exit is clean: notice provisions, reversion or termination paths and handover of masters or files are clear and honored.
- The party accepts accountability, answers questions about statements, and names who is responsible for each decision.
- Both benefits and obligations are specific, including what the artist receives, what is recouped and what happens at the end of the term.
- Oversight is transparent: royalty statements are readable, dashboards are shared, and audit requests are answered.
- Roles are disclosed, and conflicts of interest, such as a manager who also acts as lender or label contact, are named and managed.

## Escalation Tiers For This Sector

**LOW — clarify and document.** Ask for the term, split or figure in writing. Send a short written summary of a call. Keep drafts, dates and statements. Set a calm boundary on timing. Note who was present. Ask for the royalty statement, delivery standard or settlement sheet you are entitled to see under your own agreement.

**MODERATE — slow down and get independent help.** Pause the signing or the next delivery. Ask for time to review. Seek independent advice from an entertainment attorney who has no tie to the counterparty, or from a trusted peer or mentor outside the deal. Compare alternatives, get terms in writing, bring a support person to meetings, request a second opinion on a draft, and keep a dated timeline.

**HIGH — appropriate outside support.** Consider consulting qualified, independent professionals and bodies such as an entertainment attorney, a royalty auditor or forensic music accountant, a musicians' union or guild representative, an artist advocacy or legal-aid clinic for creators, a collective management or performing rights body where an administration or registration question is involved, the platform's or distributor's formal dispute or policy channel, a state or national labor or consumer-protection office, or an arbitration or mediation service named in the contract. For minor performers, a guardian should also look to child-performer or work-permit authorities and an independent trustee or advisor. Which bodies exist, and what they can do, is jurisdiction-specific. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]

**URGENT — safety first.** Threats, stalking, blackmail, extortion, physical danger, sexual or other exploitation, any concern for a child or teen performer's safety, self-harm threats, illegal confinement, withheld travel documents or passports, severe retaliation, fraud, or abuse of a dependent person. Prioritize safety and emergency support: contact emergency services or local crisis lines, and for child-welfare concerns the appropriate child-protection authority. Do not confront the person. Do not sign anything, delete anything, or negotiate alone. Tell a trusted person where you are and what is happening. Substance, relapse and mental-health crises on the road go to medical and crisis professionals, not to the team that benefits from the artist staying on schedule.
