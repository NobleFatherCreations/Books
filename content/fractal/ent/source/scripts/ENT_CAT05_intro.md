# CAT05: Accountability Evasion Composites — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Category Introduction

[SPEAK] Something is wrong with the statement. A credit is missing, a payment never arrived, a settlement sheet does not add up, something was said in a session that should not have been. So you ask. And here is where this category begins, because the question is the trigger. The label points to the publisher. The publisher points to the distributor. The distributor says it is a metadata matter and opens a ticket. The accounting department says that is a question for the manager, and the manager says it is a question for business affairs, who are, regrettably, in a meeting. Nobody refuses. Nobody decides.

This is the stage that arrives after a complaint, and it can follow any other pattern in the book. In an industry with fragmented rights, that fragmentation is often real: income genuinely does pass through several hands, and delay is often only delay. What this category is about is the point where the fragmentation stops being a fact about the business and starts behaving like a shield. A review is announced, and it examines the person who raised the concern more than the concern. An apology arrives that acknowledges feelings and nothing else. A policy is cited, but not shown. A confidentiality clause is mentioned in the first reply. The window to object quietly closes while the process is still politely underway. An exit package appears, with an agreement to sign and a request not to speak.

The sector's language helps. It's a complicated situation. Let's keep this in the family. We don't want to make this public. The industry is small, don't burn bridges. A complaint becomes a risk to your reputation before it becomes a risk to anyone else's. And many of the people who might give an honest opinion, the lawyer, the accountant, the senior colleague, depend on the same relationships.

So prepare before you file. Read the actual agreement and whatever process governs it. Ask for the statement, the audit rights, the dispute channel, and put every request in writing so that dates and names accumulate. Use more than one channel. Get advice from someone who is paid by you, not by the party you are asking. Notice whether delay is ever accompanied by an explanation, a deadline and a named person. Good-faith slowness can tell you where it stands. Evasion cannot.
