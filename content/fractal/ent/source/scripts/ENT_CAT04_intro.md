# CAT04: Reality Distortion & Perception Control Composites — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Category Introduction

[SPEAK] Here is a conversation that happens in studios, tour buses and conference calls. You remember being told your name would be on the track. You remember the room, the coffee, the nod. Now someone with a calm voice says that was never discussed, that you must be thinking of another project, that you've been under a lot of stress. And you stop. Because they were there too. Because the split sheet, which would settle it, was never signed on the day. Because in this business most of the important things are said out loud, fast, in the middle of something else.

That is the soil this category grows in. The music industry runs on memory and relationships: a handshake at the session, a call from the car, a promise made at the after-party with the engineer packing up cables. The official record is whatever ends up in the file, and the file is often assembled later, by someone else. Credits, metadata, royalty statements, the version of the agreement everyone supposedly saw. When the record and the memory disagree, the person with the institution behind them has the advantage of sounding like the calm one. The artist, who has a feeling and no document, sounds emotional. Over time the explanation for the same event shifts a little each time it is told, and the artist is the one who learns to doubt their own recall.

The sector has its own phrases for it. That's not how it works. That's standard. You're misremembering. Don't make this about the money. You're being difficult. Some of them are honest: memories of informal conversations do differ, and administrative errors are often only errors. The question is whether the person who made the mistake is willing to look at the paper. Pattern, not a single moment, is what matters.

The defense is deliberately unglamorous. After every call, send the short written summary: per our call, my understanding is. Ask for it to be corrected if it is wrong. Keep the date a split sheet was signed relative to the session. Save the versions. Find a witness who was in the room, or a colleague who will listen without trying to settle it for you. You are not trying to win an argument about the past. You are trying to keep your own account of it intact, in a form that someone else can read.
