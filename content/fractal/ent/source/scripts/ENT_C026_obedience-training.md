# C026: Obedience Training — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

You're a twenty-three-year-old alt-pop artist, three months into your first deal with a mid-major label, and at eleven at night you are cross-legged on your bed with a laptop open to a half-finished vocal chain when your phone lights up. A&R. You were asleep ten minutes ago, or almost. You pick up on the second ring, because in your first week you let one go to voicemail and the next morning's text said, "Everything okay? Just checking you're still all in :)."

He wants to talk about the tracklist. Specifically, the order of tracks six and seven, which has already been decided twice. You talk for forty minutes. He is lovely about it. He says you have the best instincts on the roster. He says it with the voice people use for puppies and promising investments.

The next morning there is a calendar invite for a nine o'clock call about "alignment." Then a request that you re-send a lyric sheet in a different font. Then a note that the label's brand team would prefer you stop replying to fan comments from your personal account until they have reviewed the tone. Then a reminder to tag the label in every story. None of it is outrageous. All of it arrives at once, in a tone of cheerful urgency, and you comply with everything, because refusing any one thing feels like it would cost more than doing it.

You open the contract on your phone the way you sometimes do, scrolling to the clause you have read four times. It is in the artist's obligations section, under a heading about availability, and it says you will provide "reasonable promotional cooperation" as requested by the label from time to time. You have asked your lawyer, a friend of a friend who read it for an hour, what reasonable means. He said it was common language and that it would probably be fine. You have decided that it is.

In the third month there is the Tuesday. A radio visit moved to six in the morning. You say that you have vocal rest the day before, you have a doctor's note, you could do ten. The A&R is not angry. He is quiet. He says, "Totally understand. I'll let them know you're not able to do it." The silence stays for four days. Messages that used to take minutes take a day. The group chat for the roster, which is a lively thing full of memes and gratitude, stops tagging you. A teammate you barely know sends you a gentle message: "Hey, maybe just do the morning thing next time? People notice."

On Friday there is a call. Unexpectedly warm. Plans for the single, shared spreadsheets, a new idea for a shoot. You are so relieved you almost laugh. You are told you have been "really great about being flexible lately," although the only thing that has changed is that you said yes to something.

So you do. You say yes to the six a.m. You say yes to the late edits. You answer the eleven o'clock calls with the brightness of a person who was waiting by the phone. You tell yourself it is the price of the career you wanted, and sometimes you almost believe that you chose it.

At eleven-fifteen tonight, the phone rings again. You are already reaching for it. It is only afterwards, with the call ended and the tracklist unchanged, that you notice you did not decide to pick up.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **Obedience Training**.

## Definition

[SPEAK] Obedience Training is the sequence where small, mostly meaningless demands are enforced with disproportionate consequences until a performer answers by reflex, using Enforcing Trivial Demands, Behavior Control, Overcorrection Demands, Punishment of Boundaries, Negative Reinforcement Traps, and Group Consolidation to convert a new artist's wish to avoid being labeled difficult into a habit of unquestioning availability. In the label and management economy it concentrates in late-night calls, promotional schedules, social media tone approvals and group chats, because an unclear availability clause lets responsiveness stand in for commitment. It is hard to detect because every demand is small and every individual request can be defended as business. What can be lost is the capacity for independent judgment about which requests matter. Intent would require additional evidence, since eager labels can be disorganized, anxious and demanding without designing anything.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Enforcing Trivial Demands** — The lyric sheet in a different font, the story tags, the nine o'clock alignment call about nothing. This functions by making compliance with small requests the daily proof of commitment, so that the content of the request stops mattering and the response is the point. Concern rises when requests are numerous, urgent in tone and low in consequence, and when they cluster after a decline.

2. **Behavior Control** — Preferences about when the artist answers, how she replies to fans, what she posts, and when she may rest. This functions by moving everyday decisions into the label's approval, so that the artist's sense of what she may decide shrinks. Concern rises when the approvals extend beyond released work into personal channels and routines, and when they are described as friendly suggestions that carry consequences.

3. **Overcorrection Demands** — A single declined radio visit answered with days of coolness, a re-edit of work that was fine, a new rule after a small slip. This functions by making the response to a minor deviation far larger than the deviation, so the artist learns that even small refusals are expensive. Concern rises when the response is out of proportion to the event, and when the correction is applied only to the artist.

4. **Punishment of Boundaries** — Vocal rest, a doctor's note, a request for an hour of sleep, followed by silence and slower replies. This functions by attaching cost to ordinary limits, so that the artist concludes the limit itself is the offense. Concern rises when a medically or practically reasonable limit is met with withdrawal rather than negotiation.

5. **Negative Reinforcement Traps** — The warm call on Friday, the "you've been so flexible lately," relief that comes only after saying yes. This functions by letting the artist escape the chill by complying, so that compliance feels like safety. Concern rises when warmth returns exactly after accommodation and the limit is never discussed.

6. **Group Consolidation** — The roster chat that stops tagging her, the teammate's "people notice." This functions by turning peers into carriers of the norm, so that enforcement no longer needs to come from the label. Concern rises when peers warn rather than inform, and when the group's praise is reserved for those who comply.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Enforcing Trivial Demands comes first because it costs nothing to ask. A request for an odd font or an extra tag has no great stakes, and a refusal would look absurd. That is why it works: it builds a habit of answering without evaluating, and it asks for nothing the artist can reasonably defend.

Behavior Control extends the habit into territory that matters. Once the artist is used to checking, it becomes natural to check about replies to fans, about when to rest, about what to say. The vague availability clause quietly supports this by letting "reasonable" be defined in the moment by the party who asks.

Overcorrection Demands tests the habit. A small deviation is answered with a large reaction, and the artist learns the exchange rate. The point is not any single punishment. It is the lesson about proportion.

Punishment of Boundaries makes the lesson specific. When the limit is medical, practical or simply human, the response teaches that limits are not recognized in this relationship. The artist's boundaries are then renegotiated as favors.

Negative Reinforcement Traps provide the exit, and the exit is compliance. The warmth that returns after a yes is experienced as being forgiven. Group Consolidation then closes the loop by making the norm social. Once peers are doing the enforcing, the label never has to be the one who says no.

What weakens the sequence: asking early what reasonable promotional cooperation means in practice; a written schedule that includes rest and rest of voice; and a trusted outside advisor who can say whether the pattern is normal. The key conversion point is the first time the artist answers because she is afraid not to, rather than because the request is reasonable.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The artist may begin to answer reflexively, keeping the phone within reach at night and feeling a small drop in the stomach when it lights up. Over time, she may find it harder to tell which requests are important and which are habit, and may stop weighing them at all.

She may become wary of rest, treat sleep as a risk, and feel guilty for limits that are medically sensible. She may begin to repeat the label's language to herself: flexible, easy, grateful. She may also start to resent the very things she wanted, and be unable to explain why.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Open-ended availability language, such as a promise of reasonable cooperation as requested from time to time, lets the party who asks define what is reasonable [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. ## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that a real artist is always available, that gratitude means yes, and that setting limits shows you do not want it enough. "Everyone does this" and "do you know how many people would love to be you" sound like perspective and work as a leash.

The value exploited is a young artist's genuine commitment and her reasonable fear of a small industry. The public story is a new signing living the dream. It does not show a bedroom at eleven at night.

## Observable Indicators

1. Calls and messages arrive routinely outside agreed working hours, with an expectation of immediate reply.
2. Requests are numerous, small and urgent in tone.
3. A decline is followed by delayed replies, cooler tone or missing invitations.
4. The availability clause is vague, and no one will say what reasonable means in practice.
5. Approvals extend to personal channels, such as replies to fans, beyond released work.
6. Warmth returns after the artist says yes.
7. Medical, vocal or rest limits are treated as unprofessional.
8. Peers advise compliance rather than clarity.
9. The roster chat reacts differently to those who comply and those who do not.
10. The artist begins answering instantly without having decided to.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Labels move fast, release windows compress, and an A&R who calls late may be keen, disorganized or in another time zone. Most requests are ordinary business.

The discriminator is **proportion and consequence**. A busy label asks for a lot but accepts a reasonable no. The pattern appears when ordinary limits are answered with withdrawal and warmth returns only after compliance.

- **Genuine release crunch.** Short windows create real urgency.
- **Time-zone and travel differences.** Late calls can be innocent.
- **Reasonable brand guidance.** Review of social content can be sincere risk management.
- **Enthusiastic A&R.** Some people are simply excited and sleepless.

Concern strengthens when: declines reliably produce silence; the same demands cluster after a limit is set; peers repeat the label's pressure.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Silence Test:**
Ask: "What happens when I don't answer or say no?"
Concern rises if: coldness, delay or lost opportunity follows, and warmth returns only after you give in.

**SPOT IT — The Proportion Test:**
Ask: "Is the response as big as what I actually did?"
Concern rises if: a small decline produces days of consequence.

**SPOT IT — The Reasonable Test:**
Ask: "What does reasonable promotional cooperation mean in hours, days and notice?"
Concern rises if: nobody will put it in writing, and the answer is that you will know it.

**SPOT IT — The Peer Test:**
Ask: "Are my peers helping me understand the rules, or warning me not to break them?"
Concern rises if: the advice is only to comply.

## Chain Breakers

- **Set and send your hours.** "I'm available for calls between nine and eight; anything urgent outside that I'll address first thing." Say it once, kindly, in writing.
- **Ask what reasonable means.** Request a simple promotional schedule with notice periods, rest days and voice care. A good partner can answer that.
- **Put limits in medical or practical terms** and in writing. A vocal rest note is not a favor you are asking.
- **Take the clause to independent counsel** with no tie to the label before the next signature or amendment [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].
- **Keep a log** of late calls and consequences so a pattern is on paper.
- **Do not make approval of your personal channels a precedent.** Ask what is being reviewed and why, and what the process is.
- **Keep one friend outside the roster** who can tell you if this is normal.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** Calls outside agreed hours that you feel you must answer; coolness following a decline; a vague availability clause with no schedule; warmth returning only after you agree.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** Documented withdrawal of opportunities after medical or rest limits; pressure to ignore doctor guidance; approval demands over personal channels; the clause being used to extend obligations beyond the agreement. Routes may include an independent entertainment attorney, a musicians' union or guild representative, and an artist advocacy or legal-aid group [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats to your career or safety, pressure to work through illness or injury, signs of self-harm or crisis, or any threat connected to refusing. Contact emergency services or a crisis line, tell a trusted person, and do not confront anyone alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "I'm available for calls between nine and eight. If something is urgent outside that, I'll respond first thing."
- "Could you send me the promotional schedule with notice periods, so I can plan around it?"
- "I have a vocal rest day before that, and my doctor has asked for it. I can do ten o'clock."
- "I'd like to understand what reasonable promotional cooperation means for this release. Could we write it down?"
- "Thanks for the heads-up on the social review. What exactly is being checked, and how long does it take?"
- "I'd like my lawyer to read this before I answer. I'll come back tomorrow."
