# CAT01: Capture & Attachment Composites — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Category Introduction

[SPEAK] Every career in music begins with a door, and this category lives at the door. It covers the first stretch of the lifecycle: the cold DM that arrives after a song gets traction, the producer who says come by the studio tonight, the manager who has been in the back of every one of your shows for a month, the showcase invitation that lands the week after a clip goes viral. Nothing here has been signed yet, or it has only just been signed. The terms are still a rumor. What exists is a feeling, and the feeling is the product.

That is what makes this stage so hard to see. In this industry, being noticed is the thing almost everyone has been waiting for, sometimes for years. The praise is specific. The access is fast. Someone knows your lyrics. Someone says your name in a room you have never been allowed into. When the longing is that old, the first real attention feels like a rescue, and a rescue does not invite you to read the fine print. The people most open to it are the ones with the least armor: the unsigned artist on a first deal, the young songwriter, the beatmaker whose work has never been paid for, the parent standing behind a teenager and trying to do right by them.

The sector has beautiful words for all of it. Discovery. A shot. Development. We're a family. We'll take care of the business side, you just focus on the music. Each phrase can be true, and many are, which is exactly why they work as cover. Real enthusiasm and a staged one can look the same from the outside for weeks. What separates them tends to appear in the paperwork and the timeline: whether the terms arrive in writing before the work does, whether a lawyer of your own is welcomed or quietly discouraged, whether the deadline was there when the interest began or appeared the moment you hesitated. Watch the speed. Watch who benefits from the speed.

So the work of this category is the work of the threshold. Slow the timeline. Ask for the draft and the date by which an answer is needed, in writing. Find advice that is not connected to the person making the offer. Write down what was promised in the room, and send it back as a short message so it can be corrected. None of this assumes bad faith. A good partner will welcome all of it, and a hard bargain can survive daylight. The pattern worth learning to recognize is the one that cannot.
