# C047: The Confusion Protocol — Entertainment & Music Industry

## Cold Open

[SPEAK] You're a composer, forty-one years old, with a catalog of film and concert music that is respected in your own country and almost unknown in this one. Your English is good enough for a hotel and a grocery store, and not yet good enough for a sentence with six clauses and a cross-reference. You came to this city because a publisher here wanted to represent your work, and the first agreement, a year ago, was simple enough that your own lawyer at home was comfortable with it.

Today there is an amendment. Eleven pages. The email said it would "streamline administration and align the catalog with our current systems." You have been shown into a conference room with a long glass table, bottled water, a plate of pastries and four people from the publisher: a business affairs manager, a catalog director, someone from royalties, and a woman introduced as "our interpreter, who has been with us for years and knows the business."

She is kind. She is also on the publisher's payroll, and you notice it, and you decide not to make it awkward.

You ask the first question you wrote down in your own language last night: the amendment seems to change who controls the administration of your older works, and for how long. The business affairs manager nods. "Right. So, Administration Rights in Section 4 are defined by reference to the Controlled Catalog in Schedule A, which as amended in 2.3 includes, subject to 7.1, Works Delivered or Deemed Delivered." The interpreter says something in your language that is shorter, and warmer, and not quite what you heard.

You ask again. "For how long?" The catalog director says: "Term is as set out in Term. Term is the Initial Term plus any Extended Term, which is defined in the Term section." You look at the page. It says what he said. You are going in a circle and you can feel your face getting hot.

You ask whether "Deemed Delivered" means works you have already finished. "That's a good question," says the royalties manager. "It would depend on the Administrative Period. But I wouldn't focus too much on that. The bigger thing is the new portal, which is going to be a really exciting change for your statements. Have you seen the portal?" You have not. A laptop appears. Someone demonstrates a dashboard with colorful bars while the interpreter explains what the colors mean.

Forty minutes later you realize that you have not received a single answer to the question you came with. You also realize you are very tired, and the pastries are gone, and the afternoon is nearly over. There is a flight in the evening. The business affairs manager says gently, "I know this is a lot. Honestly, most of this is housekeeping. If you'd like to take it away and think about it, of course, but we are hoping to get it into the new system this quarter, and some of the processing depends on timing."

"I want my lawyer to read it," you say.

"Absolutely. Of course. Is that the lawyer from home? That might be difficult with the translation, though. Our interpreter can walk you through any section you like."

You pick the section about term. She walks you through it. The explanation takes ten minutes, and when she finishes, you realize you understood the meaning of every word she said and cannot repeat a single sentence.

You sign nothing that day. But on the plane you find that the question you started with has gone soft in your hands. You can no longer say exactly what it was. You remember only that you were told, many times and very kindly, not to worry about it.

## The Name

[HARD CUT] That's **The Confusion Protocol**.

## Definition

The Confusion Protocol is the sequence where a document or conversation is made so tangled, circular and crowded that the person with the question stops asking it, using Word Salad, Circular Logic, Strategic Ambiguity, Cognitive Overload, Moving Goalposts and Topic Switching to convert limited capacity for sustained focus under overload into the loss of the original concern ever being addressed. In music publishing it concentrates in amendments built from cross-referenced definitions, schedules and portals, where an ordinary person must read three sections to understand one sentence. It is hard to detect because every sentence can be defended as accurate, and because real contracts are genuinely dense. What the target can lose is the answer to the question they came with. Intent would require additional evidence.

## Verbal Breakdown

[SPEAK] The component tactics, in order:

1. **Word Salad** — "Administration Rights in Section 4 are defined by reference to the Controlled Catalog in Schedule A." This functions by burying a simple question in nested terms until the listener cannot hold the sentence. Concern rises when plain-language questions repeatedly receive defined-term answers.

2. **Circular Logic** — "Term is the Initial Term plus any Extended Term, which is defined in the Term section." This functions by sending the reader around a loop so that a question is "answered" without information arriving. Concern rises when an answer points only to the thing being asked about.

3. **Strategic Ambiguity** — "Deemed Delivered." "It would depend on the Administrative Period." This functions by leaving a key phrase open enough to be read later in whichever way suits the drafter. Concern rises when a defined term has no stated scope and the explanation declines to give one.

4. **Cognitive Overload** — Eleven pages, four officials, a portal demonstration and an interpreter, all in one afternoon. This functions by exceeding the listener's processing capacity so that agreement becomes the easiest exit. Concern rises when volume and time pressure arrive together and a break is treated as inconvenient.

5. **Moving Goalposts** — The question changes from "what does this mean?" to "do you want to take it away?" to "can your lawyer even read this?" This functions by raising the bar for asking, one small step at a time. Concern rises when each reasonable request is met with a new condition.

6. **Topic Switching** — "I wouldn't focus on that. Have you seen the portal?" This functions by redirecting attention to a pleasant subject at the moment of an unanswered question. Concern rises when the pivot always lands just after the hard question.

## Why This Order

[SPEAK] Word Salad has to come first because it establishes that the document is beyond casual reading. Once the first answer cannot be followed, the listener quietly accepts that they are the problem.

Circular Logic is what prevents recovery. A second question about the first answer returns the same sentence, and the listener learns that there is no ground floor to this building.

Strategic Ambiguity is the payload. The loop is not an accident of style; it protects a few key terms, such as "Deemed Delivered," whose meaning will matter later and whose breadth the drafter would prefer to leave unspecified.

Cognitive Overload weakens independent evaluation. People, especially those working in a second language, make worse decisions when tired, and signing becomes a way to make the room stop.

Moving Goalposts raises the exit cost. The request for a lawyer, a break or a translation of one's own becomes a complication, and the interpreter employed by the other side becomes the gate through which the document passes.

Topic Switching closes the loop and provides deniability. Nobody refused to answer; the conversation simply moved on to something exciting. The conversion point is when the question is no longer remembered. The sequence is weakened by written questions sent in advance, an independent interpreter, and a plain-language summary signed off by the drafter.

## Individual Level

[SPEAK] The person may leave a meeting unable to say what was explained. They may blame their language skills, and apologize for slowness that was in the document and not in them. Over time they may stop asking questions in meetings and rely on the person with the clearest English, even if that person is employed by the other side.

They may sign things they only partly follow to end the discomfort. They may also feel shame, and avoid telling colleagues at home how hard the day was.

## Institutional Echo

[SPEAK] Publishing amendments often rely on defined terms, schedules and cross-references, which are a normal drafting technique [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN]. The same technique, applied to a reader with limited English and no counsel in the room, can function as an obstacle even when no obstacle was intended [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Where the interpreter is a staff member, the explanation of a document passes through the party that drafted it [HYPOTHETICAL COMPOSITE]. Where migration to a new system is used as a reason for urgency, the pace may be driven by administration rather than by the artist's needs [COMMON INDUSTRY PATTERN].

Many such meetings are simply poorly planned, with genuine efficiency motives and a sincere interpreter. Intent would require additional evidence, such as whether the same document is explained differently to counsel, or whether the broad terms were ever narrowed on request.

## Civilizational Echo

[SPEAK] The myth is that complexity is sophistication, and that anyone who cannot follow the paperwork is unprepared for the business. A related belief is that a contract is simply "standard," so reading it too closely is a sign of mistrust.

The value exploited is courtesy: the willingness of a guest in a country to avoid making a fuss. The language is polite: "housekeeping," "streamline," "align with our systems." The public story is a respected composer joining a respected house. The private story is a long table and a question that never got an answer.

## Observable Indicators

1. A plain question about duration, scope or control is answered with defined terms or a section number.
2. An explanation points back to the term being asked about.
3. Key phrases such as "deemed" or "as amended" have no stated scope.
4. The amendment is long, cross-referenced, and delivered shortly before a meeting.
5. The interpreter is employed by the counterparty.
6. Several people speak in turn, and a break is discouraged.
7. A pleasant topic is introduced at the moment of a hard question.
8. Timing pressure is attached to an administrative deadline.
9. Requests to consult one's own lawyer are met with practical objections.
10. No plain-language summary is offered and none is agreed in writing.

## Evidence To Preserve — Additions

- The amendment as received, with the email that delivered it and the date and time.
- Your written list of questions, in your own language, and any translation of it.
- Names and roles of everyone present, including whether the interpreter is staff or independent.
- Notes of each answer given, written the same day, and which section was cited.
- Any plain-language or translated summary provided, with who prepared it.
- The portal screenshots or demonstrations shown, with dates.
- Emails about timing, the "new system" deadline and any processing dependence.

## False-Positive Check — Specific

Dense drafting is common, and many business affairs staff are sincerely trying to explain. An interpreter who works for a publisher may be a conscientious professional. A deadline linked to a system migration may be genuine.

The discriminator is whether a plain written answer is available when asked. Honest complexity yields to a request such as "please put in one sentence what this does to my older works" and returns a short, stable answer. This pattern returns another definition.

Concern strengthens when: the same plain question receives different answers in different meetings; a request for independent translation or counsel is treated as a delay; and a key term remains undefined after you ask what it covers.

## Recognition Cue Cards

**SPOT IT — The Loop Test:**
Ask: "Can you tell me this without using a defined term?"
Concern rises if: the answer sends you to the term you asked about.

**SPOT IT — The Scope Test:**
Ask: "What exactly does 'deemed' include, and what does it leave out?"
Concern rises if: no one will state the scope in writing.

**SPOT IT — The Interpreter Test:**
Ask: "May I bring my own interpreter, or have this translated independently?"
Concern rises if: the answer is that the staff interpreter is enough.

**SPOT IT — The Pivot Test:**
Ask: "Did we move to a new topic right after my hard question?"
Concern rises if: the pivot happens more than once and the question is never revisited.

## Chain Breakers

- **Send your questions in writing beforehand**, in simple English, and ask for written answers.
- **Ask for a one-page plain-language summary** of what the amendment changes about term, scope and control, and ask the drafter to confirm it is accurate.
- **Bring or hire an independent interpreter**, and ask for the document to be translated for your own adviser.
- **Do not sign in the room.** "I'll take this to my adviser and return with questions."
- **Ask what changes if you do not sign** and when, in writing.
- **Request a redline** against the earlier agreement, so only changes need reading.
- **Seek independent advice** from an entertainment attorney with no tie to the publisher, ideally with experience of cross-border works [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC].
- **Take a break** when you need one. It is not rude.

## Escalation Triggers — Specific

**LOW → MODERATE:** A plain question about term or scope goes unanswered; an interpreter is staff; an amendment is long and cross-referenced and you have been given little time.

**MODERATE → HIGH:** You are pressured to sign without advice; written answers conflict with what was said in the room; the amendment may change control of existing works; or the other party objects to your lawyer. Consider an entertainment attorney, a collective management or authors' society if you are a member, a musicians' union or composers' guild, or an interpreter and translation service with no tie to the publisher. [LEGAL CLAIM — JURISDICTION-SPECIFIC]

**HIGH → URGENT:** Threats, intimidation about your immigration or work status, withheld travel documents or payments used as leverage, or any danger. Contact emergency services or a relevant crisis or legal-aid service, tell a trusted person, and do not sign or negotiate alone.

## Safer Response Language

- "Could you tell me in one plain sentence what this changes about the term of my older works?"
- "I'd like to send my questions in writing and have your written answers."
- "I'd like my own interpreter to attend as well."
- "Could you send a redline against the previous agreement?"
- "I'm not able to sign today. I'll return with questions after my adviser has read it."
- "What does 'deemed delivered' include and exclude? Please put it in writing."
- "Could we return to my first question? I don't think it has been answered."
