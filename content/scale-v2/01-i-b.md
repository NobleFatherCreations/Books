=== the-gradient
title: The gradient
question: Was that rude, or was it abuse? How do I tell the difference?
seo: Rude or abusive? The seven-step gradient between them
short: Most hurtful behaviour is neither fine nor abuse. The gradient has seven positions: rude, thoughtless, inconsiderate, unkind, harmful, controlling, abusive. Three things turn a behaviour to the right: whether your freedom shrinks, whether it repeats, and what happens when you name it. Place it accurately and you can answer it without burning the word abuse.
---
Most over-detection is a vocabulary failure. Your eyes work fine. Your dictionary has two words in it.

The two words are *fine* and *abuse*. If those are the only containers you own, you'll sort the whole of human behaviour into one or the other. Very little human behaviour is fine. So a great deal of it lands in the second container, including the person who ate your leftovers and the person who reads your messages at 2am. Those two people should not share a word.

A two-word vocabulary is a light switch. What you need is a dial.

## What is the gradient?

The gradient is a seven-position scale for hurtful behaviour: rude, thoughtless, inconsiderate, unkind, harmful, controlling, abusive. It replaces the two words most people grab under stress, fine and abuse. You place a behaviour by asking three things: did your freedom shrink, does it keep happening, and what happened when you named it?

Here's the dial, left to right.

::: gradient
- rude
- thoughtless
- inconsiderate
- unkind
- harmful
- controlling
- abusive
:::

Each click to the right is a different thing. It deserves a different response. Collapsing the seven into two is the most common calibration failure there is.

::: handle
Seven positions, not two.
:::

## Rude or abusive? The difference

Rude behaviour hurts your feelings and leaves your life the same size. Abusive behaviour shrinks your life: where you can go, who you can see, what you can say, what you can spend, what you're allowed to remember. Between them sit five more positions, and most of the bad behaviour you'll ever meet lives in those five.

The internet doesn't believe in the middle five. Post that your partner left a pan in the sink overnight and within an hour a stranger with a sunset avatar has diagnosed them, ranked their attachment style and advised you to go no-contact. That's a light switch with a comments section.

## Place these eight before you read on

Turn the dial for each one. Commit to a notch before you tap.

::: sort
- He forgot your birthday. || Thoughtless. Forgetting isn't a plan. If he forgot, heard that it hurt, and forgot again next year with a shrug, it has clicked right to inconsiderate.
- She rolled her eyes while you talked about your job. || Rude, once. Every time, in front of friends, with a little commentary, and it's unkind with a Decoder page of its own: [contempt](/playbook/contempt).
- He booked a weekend away with his mates on your anniversary without asking. || Inconsiderate. Your plans didn't cross his mind. Worth saying out loud. Not worth a dossier.
- She called you "getting chunky" at dinner and said it was a joke. || Unkind. If "it was a joke" is the answer every single time you raise it, it's still unkind, now with [minimization](/playbook/minimization) on the side.
- He told his friends something you trusted him with. || Harmful. Real damage to your life and your trust. Harm doesn't need intent to count.
- She says you're free to see your sister, then sulks for two days every time you do. || Controlling. Nobody banned anything. But you now see your sister less, and you know why. A normal choice has a price, and the price is set by her.
- He checks your phone every night and asks who each name is. || Controlling, and it has a name in the Decoder: [monitoring](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance). If refusing costs you, and it sits inside a wider pattern, it's further right still.
- She says if you go to the party you'll regret it, and last time you went she smashed your phone. || Abusive. A threat with a history behind it. This one isn't a dial question at all. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

Notice what did the moving. It was never how upset you felt. It was the three dials below.

## What turns a behaviour to the right

Intent rises as you move right too, from absent (rude, thoughtless) through indifferent (inconsiderate, unkind) to purposeful (controlling, abusive). The trouble is that intent lives inside someone else's head, where you can't see it. [Intent, impact and the mind-reading trap](ch:intent-impact-and-the-mind-reading-trap) is about why guessing it goes wrong. So weigh the three things you can see.

**Freedom.** This is the hinge. Rudeness hurts. Control *reduces the number of moves available to you*. Ask: after this, can I still do what I could do before, without paying for it? If the answer is no, you've crossed into the territory where the vocabulary of this library starts to apply, and the Decoder calls the general shape [behaviour control](/playbook/behavior-control).

**Repetition.** A moment, then a habit, then a system with maintenance. One forgotten birthday is a moment. Forgetting every plan you make is a habit. Forgetting only the plans you make with your friends, every time, is starting to look maintained. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) gives you the four questions that measure this.

**Response.** What happened when you named it? People on the left of the dial mostly adjust when told. They grumble, they get it wrong a few times, they improve. Behaviour that survives being named clearly, more than once, drifts right whatever it started as.

::: pull
A rude comment is not abuse. A selfish decision is not coercive control. Saying so is accuracy, and accuracy is what keeps the word working for the day you need it.
:::

## Am I making this bigger than it is?

Maybe, and it's worth checking, because inflating has a real price. If you're asking because someone keeps telling you you're overreacting, that's a separate question with its own chapter, [When people say you're overreacting](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting). Here's the price of inflating on your own.

**It burns the word.** If everything is abuse, then when you meet the real thing you have nothing left to say. The people you'll need to hear you, a friend, a doctor, a court, have already discounted your scale.

**It throws away the fix that would have worked.** Rudeness has a remedy: say something. Thoughtlessness has a remedy: ask for what you need. Both are cheap and they usually work. File the same behaviour as abuse and it gets no remedy at all. It gets a verdict, and verdicts don't fix Tuesdays.

**It costs you the relationship you wanted.** Most people who hurt you this week are on the left half of the dial. Most of them would have adjusted if told.

## The other direction, because it cuts both ways

Deflating is the same error facing backwards, and this library exists because it's so common.

"He's just blunt." "That's her sense of humour." "They had a hard childhood." Each of those sentences turns something several clicks left. "He's just blunt" in particular is doing a lot of unpaid work in a lot of kitchens. Keep saying it and controlling behaviour gets described in the vocabulary of rudeness forever.

Refusing to inflate doesn't mean minimising. *Only unkind* is still worth raising. *Only inconsiderate*, on repeat, for years, after being named, has quietly become something else.

::: warn The dial is yours to turn
If something on your list involves a threat, violence or fear for your safety, skip the dial and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). And if someone hands you this chapter to argue their behaviour down a notch, that's the move described in [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). The gradient is for placing what happened to you. Nobody else gets to turn it for you.
:::

## The dial in a real week

::: scene
*LABEL*

At 1am Tomás is on his fourth article about covert narcissists. His girlfriend, Lena, made a crack at a dinner party about his cooking: "Tomás makes a lasagne you could use as a doorstop." Everyone laughed. He went quiet. His group chat has already ruled. One friend wrote "red flag," another wrote "she's humiliating you," and a third sent a gif of a man walking out of an explosion.

He tries the dial instead. Freedom: nothing shrank. He can still cook, go out, see anyone. Repetition: she has teased him about the lasagne before, never about anything that matters. Response: he has never told her it stings.

So he puts it at rude, maybe unkind. That's a sentence he can say out loud. He says it the next morning: "The lasagne joke in front of everyone got to me."

Lena winces. "God, sorry. I thought it was our thing. I'll drop it."

She drops it. The group chat is quietly disappointed.
:::

=== red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker
title: Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker
question: Is this a red flag, or just a flaw I'd be silly to worry about?
seo: Red flag or just a flaw? Red flags vs dealbreakers
short: A flaw costs you patience and predicts nothing worse. A red flag predicts harm, usually to your freedom, honesty or safety, because of what it tends to grow into. A dealbreaker is anything you won't live with, for your own reasons. Red flags are about them. Dealbreakers are about you. Flaws annoy you. Red flags predict.
---
Picture three bins by the door. Every new person you date, befriend or work beside will hand you things to sort, and you have to put each one somewhere.

The first bin says **flaw**. The second says **red flag**. The third says **dealbreaker**. Most people have one bin, labelled "red flag," and it's overflowing.

Some of that is the internet's doing. According to the short-video economy, red flags include being close to his mum, not being close to his mum, liking pineapple on pizza, owning a guitar, and having opinions about films. Run that list against any human and they fail. So does the person posting it.

## The three bins in one line each

- **Flaw:** something about them that costs you some patience and predicts nothing worse. They have several. So do you, and the people who love you have a list.
- **Red flag:** a behaviour that predicts harm, usually to your freedom, your sense of what's real or your safety, because of what it tends to grow into.
- **Dealbreaker:** something you won't live with, for your own reasons, whether or not it predicts harm.

::: handle
Flaws annoy you. Red flags predict.
:::

## Red flag or just a flaw?

Ask what it turns into if it gets worse. A flaw grows into a bigger version of the same annoyance: messier, later, louder at the cinema. A red flag grows into something that takes from you, such as your time, your friends, your money or your confidence in your own memory. Same irritation today, very different direction.

That question links straight to [the gradient](ch:the-gradient). Flaws live at the left end of the dial. Red flags are the early, small, deniable versions of things at the right end.

## Red flag vs dealbreaker

A red flag is a prediction about them. A dealbreaker is a decision about you. Someone who doesn't want children isn't showing a red flag, and if you want three, it's still a dealbreaker. You don't need a red flag to leave, and you don't need to justify a dealbreaker to anyone, including the person it breaks the deal with.

The mix-up runs both ways. People inflate their dealbreakers into red flags so they'll feel allowed to leave ("he doesn't like dogs, which honestly says a lot about him"). You were already allowed. And people hand real red flags a pass because the person ticks every other box. A red flag in a beautiful package is still a red flag.

## What is considered a red flag?

The things that reliably predict trouble tend to fall into a few families:

- **How they treat your first no.** A small no, about a drink, a Saturday or a topic, tells you how they'll handle the big ones.
- **Pace.** Big feelings, big promises and big plans before they know your middle name. The Decoder calls the extreme version [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing), and [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) tells it apart from ordinary enthusiasm.
- **Every story where they're never the problem.** All exes crazy, every boss jealous, every friend a traitor.
- **Reaching into your life.** Your phone, your clothes, your friends, your money, early and "as a joke."
- **Honesty about checkable things.** Small lies about things that don't matter predict bigger lies about things that do.
- **How they treat people who can't do anything for them.** Waiters, cleaners, their own younger siblings.

None of these is proof on its own. Each one is a reason to watch the next few weeks closely.

## Fifteen things to sort

Pick a bin for each before you tap. Then read the answer, because the bins move with context.

::: sort
- Twenty minutes late to the first date, with an apology. || Flaw. Late every time, and annoyed that you mind? Now it's about how they treat things you've said matter, and it's edging toward the flag bin.
- Calls every single ex "crazy." || Red flag, light. Not proof, but in every story they're the reasonable one. Watch how they talk about the next person they fall out with.
- Chews loudly. || Flaw. If it makes you want to move to another postcode, it's a dealbreaker, and that's allowed.
- Says "I love you" in week two and is talking about moving in by month two. || Red flag. Pace is information. The real test is what happens when you slow it down.
- Doesn't want children, and you do. || Dealbreaker. It predicts nothing bad about them. It predicts a sad conversation in five years if you ignore it.
- Rude to the waiter. || Red flag. How someone treats people who can't do anything for them predicts how they'll treat you once you can't either. One bad day you heard about afterwards? Ask before you sort.
- Goes quiet for a day after an argument, then comes back to talk it through. || Flaw, or just a style. Quiet that ends in a conversation is cooling off. Quiet that ends only when you apologise is [the silent treatment](/playbook/withholding-silent-treatment).
- Picks up your phone on date three "as a joke" and scrolls. || Red flag. That's [monitoring](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance) in its baby teeth.
- Smokes, and you hate smoking. || Dealbreaker, if you say so. Purely yours.
- Sulks when you say you can't see them on Saturday. || Depends. A disappointed "aw, okay" is human. Guilt, ten messages, or turning up anyway is a flag, and the Decoder calls it [punishment of boundaries](/playbook/punishment-of-boundaries).
- Has no friends and says everyone is fake. || Ask more. New in town, or shy, is a flaw at most. "Everyone's fake" plus nudging you away from your own friends is a flag.
- Terrible at texting back. || Flaw. If you need quick replies to feel okay, it might be a dealbreaker on fit. It isn't a flag. [Reading a text message](ch:reading-a-text-message) explains why reply speed predicts so little.
- Keeps pushing after you say no to going back to theirs. || Red flag, and a serious one. Someone who argues with your first no is showing you exactly how they treat your consent.
- Different politics or religion from yours. || Dealbreaker or nothing, your call. Contempt for yours is the red flag.
- Makes "jokes" about your weight or your job, then says you're too sensitive. || Red flag. That's [negging](/playbook/negging) with [minimization](/playbook/minimization) as a chaser. One clumsy joke, apologised for when you winced, is a flaw.
:::

Look at how many answers start with "depends." That's the point. A bin is a first guess. The next few weeks of behaviour, especially after you say something, are what move it.

## Red flags move, and so do flaws

A flag that changes when you name it was probably a flaw in a bad outfit. A flaw that turns nasty when you name it has just moved bins. This is the response dial from [the gradient](ch:the-gradient) again: what someone does after you say "that doesn't work for me" is better evidence than anything they did before.

::: warn A flaw is not a pass
If someone tells you their controlling behaviour is "just a flaw" and you should be more accepting, sort it yourself. If what's happening has frightened you, it isn't a bin question at all: go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). And if anyone quotes this chapter to talk you out of a flag you've already seen, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).
:::

Six weeks into dating Callum, Ama's best friend asks for "the full list." Her friends treat red flags as a team sport, and they want a report.

Ama sorts it properly instead. He's always ten minutes late, owns one pan, and narrates football to the television. Flaws, all three. He wants to live in a big city and she's staying put. Possible dealbreaker, which means a conversation, not a crisis.

Then one flag. Last week she said she couldn't make Friday. He sent six messages in an hour, the last one "guess I know where I rank." That's a reaction to her first real no.

Callum goes red. "Yeah. That was pathetic. Sorry."

Two weeks later she cancels again. He replies, "No worries, next week?" and nothing else.

The flag goes back in the flaw bin, labelled "gets a bit much when keen." She keeps an eye on it.

=== conflict-or-control
title: Conflict or control
question: Is this just normal fighting, or is it controlling or abusive?
seo: Toxic relationship or coercive control? How to tell
short: Ordinary conflict is weather: storms about specific things that come and go, with both people able to argue back and both still free. Coercive control is climate: one person adapting all the time to avoid the other's reaction while their freedom shrinks. Ask who is afraid, who adapts, and what you've stopped doing. Storms pass. Climate stays.
---
Every relationship has weather. People who share a bathroom, a bank account and a set of opinions about how to load a dishwasher will argue, sometimes with the intensity of a constitutional crisis. That's weather. It's loud, it passes, and both people are still standing in the same place afterwards.

Coercive control is climate. It's the steady condition of the place, and it can be very quiet. That's why the couple next door who shout about money get the gossip, and the couple who never raise their voices, because one of them has learned not to, get missed.

So you're holding two weather maps. One shows storms. The other shows the climate.

::: handle
Storms pass. Climate stays.
:::

## Is it a toxic relationship or coercive control?

"Toxic" is too blunt a word to answer that, because it now covers everything from someone who leaves milk out of the fridge to someone who reads your messages at 2am. The useful question is whether the trouble is about specific issues and comes and goes, or whether one person's freedom is steadily shrinking around the other person's moods and demands.

The first is conflict. It can be painful, and plenty of conflict is worth fixing or leaving. The second is control, and it's a different kind of problem with a different kind of danger.

## What researchers found when they split the two

For years, studies of "domestic violence" seemed to contradict each other. Large surveys of ordinary households found occasional outbursts of violence by either partner. Women's shelters found something else: mostly one partner terrorising the other, systematically. The sociologist Michael P. Johnson argued in 1995 that both were right, because they were looking at two different things. He argued that the difference was control: whether the violence was part of a wider effort by one partner to control the other.

In his 2008 book, Johnson called the first **situational couple violence**: conflicts that escalate into violence, by either partner, without being part of a wider effort to control. He called the second **intimate terrorism**: violence as one tactic inside a general pattern of control. A later paper with Joan Kelly called it **coercive controlling violence**.

He also described a third type, **violent resistance**, which is violence used against an intimate terrorist. His typology is a model, widely used and argued over, and it's useful here for one reason. It puts control, not the size of the row, at the centre.

::: warn Violence is never a storm to wait out
Johnson's model sorts kinds of violence. It doesn't make any of them safe, and it isn't a tool for downgrading what happened to you. If there has been violence of any kind, or a threat of it, or you're afraid of what happens if you do something your partner doesn't like, stop weighing and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## What is coercive control?

Coercive control is a pattern in which one partner takes over the other's everyday life through intimidation, isolation, monitoring and rules, until the other person's freedom has shrunk. The sociologist Evan Stark, in his 2007 book *Coercive Control*, called it a "liberty crime," because the main harm is to the victim's freedom. He described the micro-regulation of daily life, and argued that this control can do its damage with little or no physical violence.

That last point is why the climate map matters. If you only look for storms, the loud events, you'll miss a relationship where nothing dramatic ever happens because one person has already given up everything that would cause a scene. The Decoder shows how the pieces combine in [coercive control](/playbook/route-coercive-control).

## Normal fighting or abuse?

Normal fighting is about something, it goes both ways, and it ends. Each person can say no, argue back and walk away for an hour, and afterwards someone tries to repair it. Abuse is about who decides. One person is afraid of the other's reaction, does most of the adapting, and finds that saying no costs them every time.

Here are the two maps side by side.

::: compare The two weather maps
| Storms (conflict) | Climate (control) |
|---|---|
| About something: the money, the in-laws, the dishes. | About who decides, and the topic keeps changing. |
| Nobody is afraid of the other person. Both are angry, or hurt, for a while. | One person is afraid of the other's reaction, routinely. |
| Both adapt sometimes. | One person adapts constantly, and in advance. |
| Either can say no, and both survive it. | One person's no always costs them. |
| It ends. Someone repairs, even clumsily. | It goes underground. Afterwards there's a new rule. |
| Your freedom is about the same size as before. | Your friends, money, phone, clothes and time are shrinking. |
| Friends see two people being themselves. | Friends see one person getting smaller. |
| Over months, it goes up and down. | Over months, it goes one way. |
:::

Read the left column and you'll recognise most couples you know. The healthy ones fight too. [The reference sample](ch:the-reference-sample) shows what actually separates them. How often they argue isn't on the list. If your life matches the right column, the volume of the arguments barely matters.

## The freedom test

Storms are easy to see. Climate is easier to feel than to see. So measure it with three questions.

1. **What have you stopped doing, seeing, saying or wearing since this relationship began?**
2. **For each one, did you choose to stop, or did you learn to?** Choosing means you weighed it up and preferred the change. Learning means you found out what happens when you don't.
3. **Do you already know how they'd react if you started again?** If you can describe the reaction in detail, the sulk, the questions, the three days of cold, you've been trained.

A short list full of "chose" is ordinary life. People do give things up for each other. A long list full of "learned," which is still growing, is the climate. The Decoder has a page on how that feels from the inside: [walking on eggshells](/playbook/route-walking-on-eggshells).

You don't have to test the list by doing the things again. If doing one could put you at risk, don't. Read [Before you test: is testing safe?](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe) first. What happens when you do refuse something small, in a situation where that's safe, is [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no).

## What about when we both shout?

Then you'll need a sharper question than who shouted. In relationships where one person controls the other, the controlled person sometimes snaps, and the controlling person points to that as proof of "mutual" abuse. Ask who is afraid of whom, and who adapts. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) covers reactive abuse in full.

## A quiet house

::: scene
*LABEL*

Elif's sister says the relationship is toxic. "You two never even argue. It's weird."

Elif laughs it off, then tries the two maps that night.

Storms? There are hardly any. Rob doesn't shout. Who's afraid? She is, of his mood when she gets in late. Who adapts? She does. She texts when she leaves work. She stopped Thursday climbing. She doesn't wear the green dress.

She writes the freedom list on her work computer. It runs to eleven lines. "Chose" goes next to two of them.

She remembers the one time she went climbing anyway. He didn't shout. He didn't speak for three days. That week she found her phone had been moved, unlocked, on his side of the bed.

There were no storms because she had removed everything that might cause one.

Yes, this was coercive control. Elif doesn't confront Rob. From her desk at work she contacts the domestic abuse service where she lives and starts talking it through with someone who has no stake in the answer.

=== a-boundary-or-a-rule
title: A boundary, or a rule for someone else
question: Is my partner setting boundaries, or are they controlling me?
seo: Boundaries vs control: is it a boundary or a rule?
short: A boundary governs your own behaviour: what you'll do, accept or leave. A rule governs someone else's: who they see, what they wear, where they go. A quick test is grammar: a boundary's subject is "I," a rule's subject is "you." The real test is whose behaviour it governs. A boundary can hurt and still be a boundary.
---
Take out a red pen. This chapter is a grammar lesson, and like every grammar lesson it has one rule and a page of exceptions.

The rule: **a boundary has "I" as its subject. A rule has "you."**

"I'm leaving at ten." Boundary. "You're not going out tonight." Rule. Circle the subject of the sentence and you've done most of the weighing.

The word "boundary" went to therapy, came back, and is now being used to win arguments. "I have a boundary that you don't follow women on Instagram" is a rule in a yoga outfit. "My boundary is that you share your location with me at all times" is a rule that has learned to say please. The Decoder has a page on the general move: [weaponized therapy-speak](/playbook/weaponized-therapy-speak).

## Boundaries vs control

A boundary is a decision about your own life: what you'll do, what you'll accept, what you'll walk away from. Control is a decision about someone else's life, enforced by what it costs them to ignore it. A boundary changes what you do. A rule changes what they're allowed to do. One protects you. The other manages them.

## Is my partner setting boundaries, or controlling me?

Ask whose behaviour their sentence governs. If it ends with you doing less, seeing fewer people, changing your clothes, handing over your phone, it's a rule, whatever it's called. If it ends with them doing something, leaving the room, not lending money, going home early, it's a boundary, even if you hate it.

::: handle
Whose behaviour does it govern?
:::

## Circle the subject: twelve sentences

Mark each one boundary, rule or request before you tap. The answers include a rewrite where one helps.

::: sort
- "You're not going out with your friends this weekend." || Rule. Rewrite as a request: "I'd love us to have Saturday together. Can we?" A request can be turned down without a penalty. That's what makes it a request.
- "I'm not lending money anymore." || Boundary. It governs your wallet. Nobody else's.
- "My boundary is that you don't talk to your ex." || A rule in a boundary's clothes. The honest version is a feeling and a request: "It bothers me how much you two talk. Can we talk about it?" If nothing changes, the boundary is about you: "I can't stay in a relationship where that's happening." Then the choice to stay is yours, and so is the choice to leave.
- "If you shout at me, I'll leave the room." || Boundary. You're describing what you'll do. They still get to choose their volume.
- "Give me your passcode if you've got nothing to hide." || Rule, and it has a Decoder name: [monitoring](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance).
- "I don't discuss my weight." || Boundary.
- "Don't wear that, it's embarrassing." || Rule. Their body, their clothes.
- "I need you to text me when you get there." || Depends. Two people who both do it for reassurance, and can skip it without a row, have a habit. Required, checked, and punished when missed, it's a rule.
- "I won't stay at your mum's if she keeps commenting on my parenting." || Boundary. You're saying where you'll be.
- "I need space tonight, so I'm sleeping in the spare room." || Boundary. Not a punishment, as long as it isn't used as one every time you disagree.
- "If you go to that party, we're done." || Usually a rule with a threat attached, and the Decoder files the habit under [ultimatums](/playbook/ultimatums). The exception is a line about what you'll stay for, after a real breach, said once and meant. Ask yourself whether it protects you or manages them.
- "I'm not answering work messages after seven." || Boundary. Your inbox will survive.
:::

## The page of exceptions

Grammar gets you most of the way. These are the places it trips.

**Control can say "I."** "I'll leave if you see your friends" has "I" as its subject and still governs their friendships. Your presence is being used as leverage over something that's theirs. So after you circle the subject, ask the handle question. What does this sentence reach into? Your body, time, money, space and attention are yours to govern. Their friends, family, clothes, phone and body are theirs.

**A boundary can say "you."** "Don't call me that" is a request about how someone treats you, and it's completely fair. Requests about how people treat *you* are fine. Rules about how they live *their* life are the problem.

**A boundary can hurt.** "I'm not coming for Christmas this year" can wreck someone's December. It's still a boundary. Disliking a boundary doesn't make it control. Being affected by someone's choice is the price of them having choices.

**A boundary can be used as a weapon.** Leaving the room once, when shouting starts, is a boundary. Leaving the room every time you're asked a question you don't like, so no subject can ever be raised, is [stonewalling](/playbook/stonewalling) with good posture.

## Before you go on

Say the tests without looking back.

☐ First, circle the subject: "I" or "you"?
☐ Then ask: whose behaviour does it govern?
☐ A request can be turned down without a penalty. A rule can't.
☐ A boundary can hurt someone and still be a boundary.
☐ A rule can start with "I" and still be a rule.
:::

## Saying a real boundary

The shape is short. *When this happens, I will do this.* You describe your own action and stop. You don't need a speech, a diagnosis of them, or their agreement.

::: script
**You:** "When the conversation turns into shouting, I'm going to step out and come back in twenty minutes."

**If they say:** "So now you're controlling me?"

**You:** "No. I'm telling you what I'll do. What you do is up to you."

**If they say:** "That's not a boundary, you're just punishing me."

**You:** "I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm telling you where I'll be."

**If they say:** "Fine. Then my boundary is that you stop seeing your sister."

**You:** "That's a rule about my life. I'm not agreeing to it."
:::

How someone reacts to a real boundary is evidence in itself. People who respect you grumble and adjust. People who need control treat your boundary as a fight to win, and the Decoder calls what follows [punishment of boundaries](/playbook/punishment-of-boundaries) or, when they keep nudging the line to see if it holds, [boundary testing](/playbook/boundary-testing). Tracking that price over time is [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no).

::: warn Before you use this script
If saying no to this person could cost your safety, don't use the script to find out. Read [Before you test: is testing safe?](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe), and if you're already afraid, go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). And if someone hands you this chapter to prove your boundary is really control, circle the subject yourself, then reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).
:::

## When the red pen points at you

::: scene
*LABEL*

Kofi reads this chapter to prove a point about his girlfriend, Ines. She has started calling things boundaries, and he's sure half of them are rules.

He circles her sentences first. "I'm not going to your work drinks every month." Subject: I. Governs: her Fridays. Boundary. Annoying. Hers.

Then he gets to the sort and stops at "My boundary is that you don't talk to your ex." Two weeks ago he said almost exactly that to Ines about her friend Marco. Not even an ex. Just a man who makes her laugh.

He circles the subject. "You."

That evening he tries the rewrite. "I said that thing about Marco like it was a boundary. It wasn't. I get jealous when you two message. That's mine to sort out. I'm not asking you to stop."

Ines looks at him for a long second. "Thank you. That one had been sitting on me."
:::

If the sort turned up your sentences, the way it did for Kofi, that's useful information about a habit you can change. [Am I the one doing it?](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it) is the honest look in the mirror, and [The gradient](ch:the-gradient) will tell you how far right a habit has drifted.

::: try Try it in two minutes
Write one boundary you need, in the shape *"When ___, I will ___."* Read it back and circle the subject of the second half. If it says "you," rewrite it until it says "I." Then decide whether to say it this week, and to whom.
:::
