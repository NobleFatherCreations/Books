question: Is there a quick way to work out whether what's happening is manipulation?
seo: Is it manipulation? The 60-second weigh, step by step
short: The whole method on one screen. First check for danger, and if it's there, stop and act. Then name the room, place the behaviour on the gradient, write three explanations, ask the four pattern questions, run one safe test, and write two separate sentences: what you believe and what you'll do.
---
The whole book, folded small enough to fit in a pocket. Run it in order. Each step has a chapter behind it for when you have more than a minute.

## 0. The floor: is anyone in danger?

Violence or a threat of it, a child at risk, being cut off from help, leaving made hard or dangerous, or fear that lives in your body: stop here. You are not weighing. Get safe, then think. [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops) has what to do, and [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands) has the three rules that outrank every step below.

If it's 2am and you're exhausted, also stop here, for a different reason. Write it down and run the rest in daylight ([No verdicts at 2am](ch:the-state-youre-judging-from)).

## 1. Name the room

Is this the wide world, where late replies and bad moods are nearly always boring? Or a local room with a history of punishment, watching or cutting off, where the odds start higher? Say which out loud. [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days)

## 2. Place it on the gradient

Rude, thoughtless, inconsiderate, unkind, harmful, controlling, abusive. Pick a notch. Three things turn it right: your freedom shrank, it keeps happening, and naming it made things worse. [The gradient](ch:the-gradient)

## 3. Write three explanations

One hostile, one neutral, one about you or the situation. Then the line that matters: what could you see or find out that would tell them apart? Check the boring causes first. [The discipline of three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations), [The boring explanations](ch:the-boring-explanations)

## 4. Ask the four pattern questions

How often, in what window? Over how long? Getting worse, stable or easing? What changed when you named it? Some things need no pattern at all: violence, sexual coercion, harm to a child, a deception that changed the shape of your life. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern)

## 5. Run one safe test

Only if [Before you test](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe) comes back clear. Then pick one: say the act and the effect and stop talking ([The naming test](ch:the-naming-test)), or refuse something small and watch the price ([The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no)). If you can't bring yourself to run it, that's a finding too.

## 6. Two sentences, kept apart

*"What I currently believe is happening is ___, and I'm about ___% sure."*

*"What I'm going to do is ___, because ___."*

The second sentence never has to wait for the first to reach 100. "I don't want this" is a complete reason. [Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision)

Then write the call down with a date, so future you can mark it. [Your track record sheet](ch:track-record-sheet) has the slip, and [The weighing sheet](ch:weighing-sheet) prints this whole page as a form.

**What healthy actually looks like.** Regular conflict, disappointment, unresolved things and spells of distance are all normal. What sets healthy apart: things can be named, repair happens, "no" is survivable, and your freedom isn't shrinking.

**The safety gate.** Any violence or threats? What happened the last time you refused? What do they control: home, money, job, visa, children, community? **The cost of "no."** Watch temperature, access and framing, not for a row. Track the pattern, not one reaction.

**The fear test.** Who is afraid of whom? Who adapts in advance? Who can say no? Who tells the story afterwards?

Months later, a fourth: what actually happened. After eight or ten entries your tilt appears, and it corrects every judgement you'll make after that.

## Is it me?

**Am I overreacting?** Measure the event and your reaction separately. Place the event on the gradient, from rude to abusive. Then measure your reaction by what you said and did, not by how strongly you felt. If the two roughly match, you aren't overreacting, whoever says so. If your reaction is much bigger, check whether it's carrying a pattern or your past. [Read more](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting)

**Why do people say "you're overreacting"?** Usually because your reaction surprised them, made them uncomfortable, or honestly looked too big. Sometimes because it was. And sometimes because it works: it moves the conversation from what they did to how you responded. The test is whether the original thing ever gets discussed. If the topic is always your reaction, it's a tactic. [Read more](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting)

**Am I the toxic one?** Maybe in places, and you can find out. Judge yourself on the last six months the way you'd judge anyone: specific acts, how often, and what happened when someone raised them. Most honest people find one or two real habits, under one kind of pressure, with one or two people. That's a habit you can fix. [Read more](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it)

**Am I the toxic one or the victim?** Possibly some of both, and one doesn't cancel the other. Run the fear test honestly. If you're the one who is afraid, adapting and paying for every no, a reaction you're ashamed of doesn't make you the abuser. If they're afraid of you and adapt around you, being provoked doesn't make you the victim. [Read more](ch:when-you-both-say-victim)

**Is it me, or am I being manipulated?** That question offers two answers, and the truth is often a third. Write three explanations for the specific thing that happened: hostile, neutral, and one about you or the situation. Then ask what would tell them apart, and go and look. If the hostile one keeps fitting best after that, you have your answer, with evidence instead of dread. [Read more](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations)

**Should I trust my gut about someone?** Yes, as a first reading. Your gut is fast pattern-matching on small signals you never consciously noticed. But the same machinery fires on resemblance to old harm, and the two feel identical. Give the feeling ten minutes and a piece of paper. If it unpacks into things a camera would have caught, weigh them. If not, it's an alarm. [Read more](ch:the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm)

**Is it my gut feeling or anxiety?** Watch what happens when you get information. A gut feeling points at something specific outside you, and it calms down or sharpens when you learn more. Anxiety is vaguer, circles back to you, and treats each answer as the reason for the next question. If reassurance buys you twenty minutes before the feeling returns, it's probably anxiety. [Read more](ch:gut-feeling-or-anxiety)

**Is it anxiety or manipulation?** It can be both, and each can feed the other. Anxiety reads threat into ordinary things. Manipulation makes ordinary-looking things threatening on purpose, and living with it makes most people anxious. So skip the question of which one you are. Ask what the other person did, in specifics, and whether it keeps happening. Behaviour can settle it. Your nerves can't. [Read more](ch:gut-feeling-or-anxiety)

**Why do I see manipulation everywhere now?** Because you learned the names, and the names fit nearly everything. Manipulation tactics are built out of ordinary human behaviour, so every tired partner, slow reply and clumsy apology resembles something on the list. Resemblance is free and proves very little. The skill you need next is weighing whether the tactic is the best explanation for what happened. [Read more](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh)

**How do I stop seeing red flags everywhere after a toxic relationship?** Sort them instead of trying to stop them. Write each one down, name exactly what set it off, and ask whether it's a resemblance or a behaviour. Then extend small trusts and record how people respond. Over weeks, the resemblance alarms quieten while the real ones keep ringing. That's recalibration. [Read more](ch:weighing-after-youve-left)

## Normal, flaw, or something worse?

**What's the difference between rude and abusive?** Rude behaviour hurts your feelings and leaves your life the same size. Abusive behaviour shrinks your life: where you can go, who you can see, what you can say, what you can spend, what you're allowed to remember. Between them sit five more positions, and most of the bad behaviour you'll ever meet lives in those five. [Read more](ch:the-gradient)

**Is it a red flag or just a flaw?** Ask what it turns into if it gets worse. A flaw grows into a bigger version of the same annoyance: messier, later, louder at the cinema. A red flag grows into something that takes from you, such as your time, your friends, your money or your confidence in your own memory. Same irritation today, very different direction. [Read more](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker)

**What's the difference between a red flag and a dealbreaker?** A red flag is a prediction about them. A dealbreaker is a decision about you. Someone who doesn't want children isn't showing a red flag, and if you want three, it's still a dealbreaker. You don't need a red flag to leave, and you don't need to justify a dealbreaker to anyone. [Read more](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker)

**Is it normal fighting or abuse?** Normal fighting is about something, it goes both ways, and it ends. Each person can say no, argue back and walk away for an hour, and afterwards someone tries to repair it. Abuse is about who decides. One person is afraid of the other's reaction, does most of the adapting, and finds that saying no costs them every time. [Read more](ch:conflict-or-control)

**What is coercive control?** A pattern in which one partner takes over the other's everyday life through intimidation, isolation, monitoring and rules, until the other person's freedom has shrunk. The sociologist Evan Stark called it a "liberty crime," because the main harm is to the victim's freedom. It can happen without any physical violence at all. [Read more](ch:conflict-or-control)

**Is my partner setting boundaries or controlling me?** Ask whose behaviour their sentence governs. If it ends with you doing less, seeing fewer people, changing your clothes or handing over your phone, it's a rule, whatever it's called. If it ends with them doing something, such as leaving the room or going home early, it's a boundary, even if you hate it. [Read more](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule)

**Is it love bombing or genuine interest?** Both are fast and both feel wonderful, so speed alone won't tell you. Slow things down a notch and watch. Genuine interest is disappointed, then adjusts to your pace and is still there next week. Love bombing argues with your pace, sulks, or speeds up. The test is what the attention does when you ask for less of it. [Read more](ch:weighing-someone-new)

**Is my parent controlling or just difficult?** Difficult parents criticise, nag, sulk and repeat themselves, but your choices stay yours. Controlling parents need to approve them. Watch for three things: your no gets punished, they reach into your adult life (money, keys, partner, children), and other relatives get sent to deliver the message. Disapproval is allowed. Punishment is the line. [Read more](ch:weighing-family)

**Is my boss bad or toxic?** Score them. If your manager is weak on clarity, consistency or credit but your nos, your concerns and your exit are safe, they're a bad manager: annoying, possibly worth leaving. If the trouble is what happens when you refuse, speak up or try to leave, that's control, and it calls for records and advice. [Read more](ch:weighing-a-boss)

**Is my friend using me?** Maybe, or maybe they're in a hard season. Look at more than now. In a hard season the giving runs one way for a while, there's a cause you can name, and small returns still happen. Using runs one way in good times too. Ask for something small back, and watch what happens. [Read more](ch:weighing-a-friend)

**What does a healthy relationship actually look like?** Messier than most people expect. Healthy couples argue, disappoint each other, get defensive when tired, and carry problems they never solve. What sets them apart is what happens next: either person can raise a problem, someone repairs it, saying no doesn't cost you your standing, and nobody's world is getting smaller. [Read more](ch:the-reference-sample)

## Lies, texts and memory

**How can you tell if someone is lying?** Mostly, not from their face. Across 206 studies, people judged lies and truths correctly about 54% of the time, barely better than a coin. Eye contact, fidgeting and eye direction tell you almost nothing. What works is slower: specific, checkable detail, core facts that hold or move between tellings, the record, and time. [Read more](ch:can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying)

**Can you tell if someone is lying over text?** Not from tone, punctuation or reply speed. Look at content instead: vague answers to easy questions, answers to a different question, and core facts that change between messages. A text thread is a record, so scroll up and compare. Then check the facts you can. [Read more](ch:reading-a-text-message)

**What does this text mean?** Usually less than you think. A text carries words and almost nothing else, so tone, mood and intent get supplied by the reader, and an anxious reader supplies the worst. Before you decide, list three readings that fit the words. Then ask what you'd need to know: how they text everyone, what's happening for them, and whether this is new. [Read more](ch:reading-a-text-message)

**Is it gaslighting or do we just remember it differently?** Honest disagreement happens now and then, goes both ways, and stays about the memory: the day, the words, the order. Gaslighting is a pattern. Its corrections always land in the other person's favour, it attacks your mind instead of your memory ("you make things up"), and it treats any record you keep as the problem. [Read more](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way)

**How do I know if it's a pattern?** Count four things. How many times it has happened in a set window. Over how many weeks or months. Whether it's getting more frequent, more severe, or reaching further into your life. And what happened after you raised it. A behaviour that continues, worsens or gets punished after being clearly named is a pattern you can act on. [Read more](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern)

**Am I being paranoid about my partner?** Ask whether your file has a second column. If every single thing they do, warm or cold, counts against them, your folder is probably running itself. If you have specific, dated things they did, and the kind things you noticed haven't cancelled them, you may simply be right. Count both, then check what happened when you named it. [Read more](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself)

## Bringing it up

**How do I bring something up and tell if they're manipulating me?** Describe what they did and its effect on you in one plain sentence, with no diagnosis and no guess at motive. Then stop talking and watch. A decent person engages with the subject, even badly. A controlling person moves the subject onto you. Only do this if it's safe, and check again weeks later. [Read more](ch:the-naming-test)

**Is getting defensive a sign of guilt?** No. Defensiveness is what most people do when they hear they've hurt someone, guilty or not, so it tells you about their nerves. The signal is what comes after: whether they come back to the subject, even an hour later and grumpily. Defensive and engaged is normal. Calm and never engaged is the one to watch. [Read more](ch:the-naming-test)

**How do I know if an apology is real?** Hold it up to the light and look for four things. It names the specific act. It comes without conditions or counter-charges. The behaviour changes and stays changed over the following weeks. And nobody sends you a bill for having apologised. Real apologies are often clumsy. Specifics count for far more than fluency. [Read more](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit)

**What happens when you say no to a controlling person?** Usually not a scene. More often a price paid later, and deniably: a cold few days, help that stops arriving, your no retold as proof you're selfish, the request coming back bigger. One bad reaction means little. When every small no gets charged for, refusing is being made expensive, and that is control. [Read more](ch:the-cost-of-no)

**Can a manipulative person change?** Some can, and some do, but rarely quickly and never because they said so. Real change shows up as specific behaviour that holds for months, keeps holding when it's inconvenient, and doesn't need an audience. The surest sign is that your no gets cheaper. Time is the evidence. [Read more](ch:can-people-change)

## Deciding, and after

**Do I need proof to leave a relationship?** No. Proof is what you'd need to convince someone else, and leaving doesn't require convincing anyone. "I don't want this" is a complete reason to end a relationship, a friendship or a job. Weighing what's happening is still worth doing, because it helps you choose well. It was never a permission slip. [Read more](ch:assessment-is-not-decision)

**What should I do if I'm scared of my partner?** Treat the fear as enough. Get somewhere safe, tell one person outside the relationship, and contact the domestic abuse service where you live. If you're in danger now, call your local emergency number. You don't need to prove anything, name what's happening, or decide anything about the relationship before you reach out. [Read more](ch:when-weighing-stops)

**How do I stop second-guessing myself?** Move the argument out of your head and onto paper. Write each call, how sure you are and what would prove you wrong, then check back in a few months. After ten entries you'll know whether your doubts are earned. Second-guessing feeds on a record nobody kept. A kept one gives the doubt something real to argue with. [Read more](ch:scoring-your-own-past-calls)

**Is it paranoia if you're right?** Being right once doesn't make the method sound. Paranoia is a method in which everything confirms and nothing could ever disconfirm. You can be right about one person while running a method that will be wrong about the next ten. So check how you reached the conclusion, and whether anything could have changed it. [Read more](ch:certainty-is-the-tell)

**How do you get closure when you'll never know the truth?** Stop trying to settle the question, and settle your position instead. Say "I don't know, and here's how I'm going to live anyway," then write down what that means in behaviour: what you'll stop checking, who you'll stop asking, which explanation you'll act on. Closure is something you do. [Read more](ch:when-you-cant-ever-know)

**How do I trust again after being manipulated?** Start small and go up in steps. Rely on someone for something minor, share something low-stakes, say a small no, and watch what happens each time. Climb when they handle it well, step back when they don't. Expect to be wrong sometimes. The point is to be wrong at a height you can survive. [Read more](ch:trust-as-a-skill-you-rebuild)

## A to F

**Assessment and decision.** Two separate questions: what you believe is happening, and what you're going to do. The first needs evidence; the second needs only what you want, what you can bear and what it costs. [Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision)

**Attribution error.** Explaining someone else's behaviour by their character when the situation explains it, while excusing your own by the situation. "He's late because he's selfish. I'm late because of traffic." [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone)

**Audience test.** Comparing how someone behaves with you, with others, in public and in private. Worse with everyone is personality, worse with everyone close is intimacy, and worse only with you, only unwatched, is information. [Is it you, or is it everyone?](ch:is-it-you-or-is-it-everyone)

**Base rate.** How often something is true before you know anything about the particular case. Your evidence has to drag a suspicion up from that starting number. [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days)

**BITE model.** Steven Hassan's model of how high-demand groups control members, sorted into Behaviour, Information, Thought and Emotional control. A checklist of questions, not a test that proves anything. [Weighing a group](ch:weighing-a-group) · Decoder: [Behavior control](/playbook/behavior-control)

**Boring explanations.** The ordinary causes behind most bad moments: thoughtlessness, exhaustion, distraction, mismatched expectations, logistics, the body. Check them before a plan, then watch what a boring fix does. [The boring explanations](ch:the-boring-explanations)

**Boundary.** A sentence about what you will do, with "I" as its subject: "If you shout, I'll leave the room." It governs your own behaviour, even when the other person hates it. [A boundary or a rule](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule)

**Calibration.** Your confidence matching your track record. A calibrated person who says "90% sure" is right about nine times in ten: loud for fire, quiet for toast, honest when they can't tell. [You learned to see](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh)

**Coercive control.** A pattern in which one partner takes over the other's everyday life through intimidation, isolation, monitoring and rules, until their freedom has shrunk. Evan Stark called it a liberty crime. [Conflict or control](ch:conflict-or-control) · Decoder: [Coercive control](/playbook/route-coercive-control)

**Concept creep.** The psychologist Nick Haslam's term for words like abuse, bullying and trauma widening over time to cover milder experiences than they first described. Some of the widening caught real harm; some of it blunted the words. [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon)

**Confirmation bias.** The habit of looking for, noticing and interpreting evidence in ways that favour what you already believe. Certainty rises with every entry; accuracy doesn't move. [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself) · Decoder: [Confirmation bias exploitation](/playbook/confirmation-bias-exploitation)

**Cost of no.** What happens to you after you refuse someone something reasonable. Small and brief in a healthy relationship; in a controlling one, a price paid later in temperature, access and framing. [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no) · Decoder: [Punishment of boundaries](/playbook/punishment-of-boundaries)

**DARVO.** Deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender: the person who was named ends up cast as the one who was wronged. Not every denial is DARVO, because some denials are true. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) · Decoder: [DARVO](/playbook/darvo)

**Dealbreaker.** Anything you won't live with, for your own reasons. A red flag is a prediction about them; a dealbreaker is a decision about you, and it needs no justification. [Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker)

**Evidence ladder.** Four rungs, from strongest to weakest: the record, the observation, the inference, the feeling. All are real, but only the top rungs can carry a verdict. [What is evidence](ch:what-is-evidence)

**False negative.** Missing something real: manipulation explained away as a hard week, one more time. Its cost is paid in years. [The two errors](ch:the-two-errors-and-what-they-actually-cost)

**False positive.** Seeing something sinister where there's ordinary failure: a bad Tuesday filed as a tactic. Its cost is paid in people. [The two errors](ch:the-two-errors-and-what-they-actually-cost)

**Fear test.** Four questions for when both people say they're the victim: who is afraid of whom, who adapts in advance, who can say no, and who tells the story afterwards. Answer each about both of you. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim)

**Flaw.** Something about a person that costs you patience and predicts nothing worse. Everyone has several, including you. [Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker)

**Freedom test.** Listing what you've stopped doing, seeing, saying or wearing, then asking whether you chose to stop or learned to. A long, growing list of "learned" is the climate of control. [Conflict or control](ch:conflict-or-control) · Decoder: [Walking on eggshells](/playbook/route-walking-on-eggshells)

## G to N

**Gaslighting.** A pattern of making someone doubt their memory and their sanity over time, with corrections that always favour the gaslighter and records treated as the problem. One disagreement about a memory is two memories, not gaslighting. [I don't remember it that way](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way) · Decoder: [Gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting)

**The gradient.** A seven-position scale for hurtful behaviour: rude, thoughtless, inconsiderate, unkind, harmful, controlling, abusive. Three dials turn a behaviour right: shrinking freedom, repetition, and the response when you name it. [The gradient](ch:the-gradient)

**Group polarisation.** People who already lean one way end up further that way after discussing it together. It's why certainty about your partner can rise in a group chat with no new facts at all. [Who benefits from your verdict?](ch:who-benefits-from-your-verdict)

**Halo effect.** Letting one strong good trait colour everything else about a person, so a charming partner's cruelty gets read as banter. Its mirror image, the horns effect, does the same with one bad trait. [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone) · Decoder: [Halo effect exploitation](/playbook/halo-effect-exploitation)

**Hindsight bias.** Outcomes looking obvious once you know them. It edits old calls to fit what happened and then blames you for not seeing it sooner. [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone)

**Hostile attribution bias.** Reading hostility into accidents and ambiguous acts. "She didn't hold the door. She wanted it to hit me." [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone)

**Hypervigilance.** A threat alarm with the threshold turned down and the speed turned up, usually built where danger was real and hard to predict. Excellent at detection, prone to false alarms, and often right. [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history)

**Intent and impact.** Intent is what someone meant; impact is what their action did to you. You can know the impact for certain and rarely the intent, so a request can rest on impact alone. [Intent, impact and the mind-reading trap](ch:intent-impact-and-the-mind-reading-trap)

**Local base rate.** How common something is in this particular room, as opposed to people in general. Inside a controlling home, group or workplace, manipulation really is more common, so the odds start higher. [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days)

**Love bombing.** Fast, intense attention that argues with your pace, sulks or speeds up when you ask for less. Defined by what it does to your freedom, not by how nice it feels. [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) · Decoder: [Love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing)

**Motivated reasoning.** Reaching the conclusion you need and finding reasons afterwards. It often pushes toward excuses, protecting a relationship you've invested years in. [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone)

**Mutual abuse.** Both people controlling and frightening each other. It exists, but the label often counts incidents and hides a one-sided pattern, which the fear test is built to see. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim)

**Naming test.** Describing what someone did and its effect on you in one plain sentence, with no diagnosis or motive, then stopping and watching. A decent person engages with the subject; a controlling one moves it onto you. [The naming test](ch:the-naming-test)

**Negativity bias.** Weighting one bad moment over many good ones. "Nine good weeks and one bad night, and the night is all I think about." [The biases in everyone](ch:the-biases-in-everyone)

## R to W

**Reactive abuse.** A survivors' and support workers' term, not a diagnosis. Someone controlled or provoked for a long time finally snaps, and the controlling person holds up the reaction as proof the victim is the abuser. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) · Decoder: [Baiting](/playbook/baiting)

**Red flag.** Behaviour that predicts harm, usually to your freedom, honesty or safety, because of what it tends to grow into. A flag that changes when you name it was probably a flaw. [Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker)

**Reference sample.** Your working picture of ordinary, decent relationships, which you weigh every relationship against. Built in a controlling home or relationship, it misreads friction and danger both. [The reference sample](ch:the-reference-sample)

**Repair.** What someone does after hurting you to make the relationship safe again: an apology that is specific, unconditional and sends no bill, followed by changed behaviour over weeks. The weeks are the part you check. [Repair, and apology's counterfeit](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit) · Decoder: [Non-apology apologies](/playbook/non-apology-apologies)

**Rule.** A sentence about what someone else must do, with "you" as its subject, whatever it's called. "You're not going out tonight" is a rule, and so is "my boundary is that you don't talk to your ex." [A boundary or a rule](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule)

**Secondhand account.** Anything you know about a person because someone else told you. You're weighing two things at once, what happened and the teller. [Secondhand accounts](ch:secondhand-accounts) · Decoder: [Flying monkeys](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment)

**Shape check.** Five signs that a belief is a funnel rather than an explanation: it explains everything, nothing could prove it wrong, doubt costs you, the world splits clean, and certainty climbs faster than evidence. [Certainty is the tell](ch:certainty-is-the-tell)

**Smoke alarm.** A gut reading that fired on resemblance to old harm rather than on anything this person did. It arrives as certainty but won't unpack into specifics a camera would have caught. [The gut: sensor or smoke alarm](ch:the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm)

**Three explanations.** Before settling on why someone did something, write one hostile, one neutral and one about you or the situation, then ask what would tell them apart. Sometimes it confirms the hostile one. [The discipline of three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations)

**Tilt.** The direction your judgement leans, shown by a track record: where you run hot, where you run soft. Once you know it, you can correct for it. [Scoring your own past calls](ch:scoring-your-own-past-calls)

**Track record.** A written list of your calls about people: what you concluded, how sure you were, what would have proved you wrong, and what actually happened. It stops memory editing old calls after the fact. [Scoring your own past calls](ch:scoring-your-own-past-calls)

**Weaponised therapy-speak.** Using psychological terms to win, end or dodge a conversation instead of describing what happened. If it can't be said in plain words, it's a label, not a finding. [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon) · Decoder: [Weaponized therapy-speak](/playbook/weaponized-therapy-speak)

**Windows and walls.** Windows are reversible decisions you can open on a lean and shut again. Walls stay knocked through, so they deserve more evidence, more preparation, or both. [Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision)

## How to read your tilt

Read down the "what actually happened" lines, not across them. One entry tells you about one call. Ten tell you about you.

- **Ran hot** means you saw more than was there: the curt manager who turned out to be curt with everyone.
- **Ran soft** means you saw less: the charming one who sulked at the first no.
- **Look for clusters.** Hot on people who remind you of someone? Soft on charm? Good at work, shaky with family? Right about the behaviour and wrong about the reason?

Then use it. If you know you run twenty points hot on one kind of person, knock twenty points off the next time your gut says 85%. The wrong entries are the valuable ones, because they're the only ones carrying new information.

If you can't fill in *what would show me wrong*, you haven't made a call. You've adopted a position. Look hard, too, at any entry marked 95 or 100: [Certainty is the tell](ch:certainty-is-the-tell) explains why.

The logbook is for calls, not for danger. You never need a good track record to be allowed to protect yourself, and if someone points at your wrong entries to say you're wrong about them, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). If you're frightened of someone, don't write a percentage. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

=== limits
title: The limits of this instrument
question: What can't this book tell me, and where could it be wrong?
seo: The Weighing's limits: what it can't do, where it fails
short: What this book cannot do, where it fails, and what would change its mind. It can't tell you whether a specific person is manipulating you, make you safe, or diagnose anyone. It's most likely to fail someone inside a coercive situation, and it names the findings that would prove it wrong.
---
A book that demands evidentiary standards and publishes none for itself fails its own test. So here is the honest accounting.

## What this book cannot do

**It cannot tell you whether a specific person is manipulating you.** It has never met them, and no framework applied at a distance can substitute for evidence you gather yourself. Anyone offering that certainty in a book is selling something.

**It cannot make you safe.** Calibration is a thinking skill. Safety is a logistics problem: money, housing, documents, distance, and people whose job this is. [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops) points at them and stops, because those answers are local and this book is not.

**It cannot diagnose anyone, including you.** Nothing here is clinical. The vocabulary this library uses is descriptive, not diagnostic, and [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon) is about what happens when that line is crossed.

**It cannot resolve the unresolvable.** Some questions never close. [When you can't ever know](ch:when-you-cant-ever-know) is an admission, not a technique.

## Where it is most likely to fail

**On someone currently inside a coercive situation.** This is the book's central risk and it is real. Every argument here (consider alternatives, check your instrument, wait for a pattern) is available for misuse against exactly the reader who most needs to act. The safeguards in [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands), [Before you test](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe), [Helping someone else weigh](ch:helping-someone-else-weigh) and [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops) exist because of this, and they may not be enough for every reader. If these chapters are making you slower and more apologetic rather than clearer, something has gone wrong and you should stop.

**On grief.** [Grief in the costume of analysis](ch:grief-in-the-costume-of-analysis) names the problem and cannot solve it. Analysis holding up a loss will not be dislodged by better analysis.

**Across cultures it does not know.** The norms in [Difference is not malice](ch:difference-is-not-malice) are illustrative, not exhaustive, and the book's own assumptions about directness, apology, and emotional expression are not neutral.

**On institutions, at depth.** [Institutions weigh differently](ch:institutions-weigh-differently) is an introduction. The real instrument for that is The Sacred Divide and its records toolkit.

## What would change this book's mind

Stated plainly, so it can be held to them:

- If readers of the calibration material reliably became *slower to leave harmful situations* than readers of the recognition material alone, the central premise would be wrong and the book should not exist in this form.
- If the naming test turned out not to discriminate (if healthy and coercive responses to plain description were indistinguishable in practice), movement four would need rebuilding.
- If the four pattern dimensions failed to predict outcomes better than a single strong instance, [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) would be overstating its case.
- If the "three explanations" exercise were shown to function mainly as a rationalisation device rather than a discrimination device, [The discipline of three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations) would need to go.
- If research found a face or body cue that ordinary people could use to spot lies well above chance, [Can you tell when someone is lying?](ch:can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying) would be wrong. It tells you to stop reading faces because, in the studies, people did little better than a coin.
- If people read tone in texts from those close to them far better than the email studies found, [Reading a text message](ch:reading-a-text-message) would be too cautious. You could trust your first reading more than it says.
- If fear, adapting and the price of a no were spread as evenly in controlling relationships as in mutual ones, the fear test would not tell them apart. [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) would need rebuilding. Its term "reactive abuse" is a survivors' and support workers' term, not a diagnosis, and the chapter says so.
- If real and performed change looked the same at twelve months, the audit in [Can people change?](ch:can-people-change) would be useless. If programmes for people who abuse partners ended everyday control as well as they cut physical violence, the chapter would be too cautious.

## What this book is confident about

Two things only. That recognition without calibration produces isolation, and that the aftermath of a harm (whether it can be named, whether repair happens, what a refusal costs) carries more information than the harm itself.

Everything else here is an instrument offered for testing, including by you.
