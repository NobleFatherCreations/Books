short: Detection asks whether something resembles a tactic. Weighing asks whether that tactic is the best explanation for what actually happened. You've just finished learning to see.

Maybe it was a list of tactics, or a long book, or the Decoder putting a name on something that had been shapeless for years. However you got here, you can now name things that used to be fog. Most people go their whole lives without those words.

Something else changed too, and nobody warned you.

Your detector is on now, and it has no off switch. It runs during arguments. It runs while you read a work email. It runs when your mother pauses half a second too long before answering. It has started finding things everywhere, because finding things is the only job a detector has.

A smoke alarm can't tell a house fire from toast. It smells smoke and screams. That's what it's for, and in a kitchen with a toaster, it is exhausting to live with.

This book is about telling the toast from the fire.

::: pull
**Detection asks: does this resemble a tactic?**

**Weighing asks: is that the best explanation for what actually happened?**
:::

Those two questions feel like one question. They aren't, and the gap between them is where most of the damage gets done, in both directions.

## Why do I see manipulation everywhere now?

Because you learned the names, and the names fit nearly everything. Manipulation tactics are built out of ordinary human behaviour. Once you know the list, every tired partner, slow reply and clumsy apology resembles something on it. Nothing is wrong with your detector except that it's new and loud, and nobody has taught it smoke from fire yet.

Withdrawal is a tactic, and also a thing tired people do. Charm is a tactic, and also a thing warm people do. Explaining, apologising, going quiet, asking questions, remembering details, giving gifts: every one of them appears on the tactic lists. Every one also appears in a decent person having a normal week.

That overlap is what makes tactics work, and it's what makes them hard to prove. It's also why a freshly trained detector behaves like a friend who has just binged a true-crime podcast. Give it a fortnight and it will catch your dentist [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing) you.

## The same evening, twice

Here's one ordinary evening. Then we'll run it through two different machines.

::: scene
*LABEL*

Maya's partner, Sam, gets home at seven. Maya says hi and asks how the day went. Sam says "Fine," drops the bag by the door and starts scrolling. At dinner Sam answers in three words at a time. Maya asks if something's wrong. Sam says, "Nothing, I'm just tired." At half nine Sam goes to bed without saying goodnight.

Maya sits on the sofa with her phone. She has read about [stonewalling](/playbook/stonewalling). She has read about [the silent treatment](/playbook/withholding-silent-treatment). Both pages describe exactly what she just watched.

So does a third description she hasn't read anywhere: a person who had a terrible day and has nothing left to perform with.
:::

Now split the screen.

::: compare The split screen
| Through the detector | Through the weigher |
|---|---|
| "Fine" and three-word answers? That's stonewalling. | Short answers fit stonewalling. They also fit an empty tank. Which fits better? |
| "Nothing, I'm just tired" is a brush-off. | Is "tired" plausible today? Sam had a deadline and slept badly. Yes. |
| No goodnight is punishment. Maya replays the week, hunting for her crime. | Has Sam done this before when nothing was wrong? Was it ever punishment? What happened the last time Maya named it? |
| Verdict by ten o'clock: silent treatment. Maya lies awake drafting the confrontation. | Verdict: not enough yet. It's one evening. Maya asks in the morning, after they've both slept. |
| Cost: nothing. It was free, instant, and it felt like clarity. | Cost: some patience, some evidence, and being willing to be wrong. |
:::

The detector asked one question and answered it in four seconds. The weigher asked six, and had to wait for the morning to answer them.

In the morning, Sam apologises for being a ghost, says the deadline flattened them, and asks about Maya's day. Toast.

Suppose the morning had gone differently. A cold "you know what you did." A week of quiet that only lifts when Maya apologises for something nobody will name.

Then it's smoke, and the weigher sees it too, with far more confidence than the detector ever had. The tools for that kind of week are in [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) and [The naming test](ch:the-naming-test). If the apology that ends it is the kind that blames Maya for being upset, the Decoder has a page on [the non-apology](/playbook/non-apology-apologies).

::: handle
Resemblance is cheap. Explanations cost.
:::

Resemblance is available for free, instantly, about anyone. An explanation costs something: evidence, alternatives, time, and the willingness to be wrong. A read that cost you nothing is worth about what you paid for it.

## What is calibration?

Calibration means your confidence matches your track record. A calibrated person who says "I'm 90% sure" turns out right about nine times in ten. With people, it means your alarm goes off about as often as there's actually something there: loud for fire, quiet for toast, and honest when it can't tell yet.

That one word is the whole book. Turning the alarm down is how people stay in bad places for years, so the goal is an accurate alarm, which is harder and better. You'll learn to grade your own over time in [Scoring your own past calls](ch:scoring-your-own-past-calls).

## Is it bad to be suspicious of everyone?

It is when it runs unchecked, because it costs you the people worth keeping. The usual worry about learning these tactics is that someone reads about manipulation and stays too long. That happens. The more common failure is the person who reads about it and can't get close to anyone again.

They start pre-screening. They score a first date against a checklist. They read a friend's slow reply as a power move.

They hear a partner say *I don't remember it that way*, which two honest people say to each other constantly, and feel the floor tilt. ([I don't remember it that way](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way) sorts honest memory from gaslighting.) Eventually the only relationships that survive are the ones with no friction, which means the ones with no depth.

They call it safety. What they've built is a smaller cage with better vocabulary.

## What this book will do, and what it won't

It gives you the second half of the skill. You'll learn what counts as evidence about a person and what only feels like it.

You'll learn four tests that separate manipulation from ordinary human failure, and when running them isn't safe. You'll learn to check your own instrument, since the instrument is you. And you'll learn to be wrong well, because you will be.

It won't talk you out of what you know. If you've concluded that something is wrong, this book doesn't exist to soften that. Sometimes the hostile explanation is simply the correct one. The point of learning to weigh is being able to say so with confidence instead of anxiety.

It isn't neutral about danger either. Everything here assumes you're safe enough to think. If you're not, skip the weighing and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

## Why does this chapter come second?

Because this book's argument sounds like an abuser's. Stripped to its bones it says: *maybe you're seeing something that isn't there. Maybe there's another explanation. Maybe you're reacting to your history and not to this person.* A book on calibration and a campaign of [gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting) make the same noise from a distance.

It is the entire script. *You're overreacting. You're reading into it. That's your past talking. You always do this.*

So the floor goes in before the arguments. Calibration, defined in [the first chapter](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh), means matching how sure you are to how often you're right. You do it to your own judgment. Nobody gets to do it to you.

## What if someone uses this book against me?

Then they have shown you something. Rule two says it plainly: if the person you named hands you these chapters to prove you misread them, that act is itself evidence. You don't have to argue with them about base rates or patterns. You can close the book, leave the room, and talk to someone with no stake in the answer.

Here are lines a person might use, borrowing this book's language, and the rule that answers each one.

::: compare The line, and the rule that answers it
| What they say | What answers it |
|---|---|
| "You can't leave until you can prove what I did." | Rule one. You don't need proof to protect yourself. |
| "Read the chapter on base rates. Most people aren't manipulators. So why would I be?" | Rule two. Handing you this book to undo your accusation is itself evidence. |

Being told your reaction is too big has a name in the Decoder: [minimization](/playbook/minimization). Being made afraid has one: [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation). Being cut off from the people who would help has one: [isolation](/playbook/isolation). Borrowing this book's vocabulary doesn't change what any of them are.

## Do I need proof before I leave?

No. You can leave a relationship, a job or a conversation because it is bad for you. You don't need proof of intent, a name for the tactic, or anyone else agreeing. Proof can matter later, in court. It is never the price of your safety. Rule one covers this, and nothing later in the book overrides it.

## Why the floor has to come this early

Every honest book about judgment gets read by two people at once. One is over-detecting and needs to loosen. The other is under-detecting and needs to hold on. The same paragraph helps one and endangers the other.

There is no way to write it so that only the right reader takes it. What can be done is this. Make the protective rules unconditional. Put them before the arguments, not after. Repeat them at the head of every movement, so that wherever you open the book, the floor is within reach.

::: pull
A book that asks you to doubt yourself has to first prove it is on your side. This is that proof, and it is the only chapter here that is not up for discussion.
:::

## False positive and false negative in relationships

The terms come from testing. A medical test can say you have something when you don't, or miss it when you do. Judging a person works the same way. You can't get either error to zero, and pushing one down pushes the other up. The skill is deciding which one you can afford to make here.

## What is a false negative?

A false negative is when something real is happening and you miss it. In a relationship, it means real manipulation explained away. You give the benefit of the doubt one more time, decide it was a hard week, and stay. The test said "nothing here." The test was wrong, and you find out later, usually all at once.

## What is a false positive?

A false positive is when nothing sinister is happening and you see it anyway. In a relationship, it means ordinary human failure read as strategy. Someone forgets, snaps or handles something badly, and you file it as a tactic. The test said "manipulation." What actually happened was a bad Tuesday and a person who handled it badly.

## The two receipts

An accusation is the cheapest thing in the shop at the till. So is a second chance. Both bills come later, itemised.

::: compare Two receipts
| False negative: you missed it | False positive: you invented it |
|---|---|
| Paid in years. | Paid in people. |
| The slow erosion of your read on reality. | A marriage ended over a pattern that was actually depression. |
| The friendships that quietly thinned out. | A friend of fifteen years dropped for a clumsy sentence. |
| The version of you that existed before, and hasn't been seen since. | A parent cut off on evidence that wouldn't survive an afternoon of scrutiny. |
| At its worst: your safety, and sometimes a life. | At work: you become the person who reads intent into every email, until you're the difficult one and don't know why. |
| Who notices: you, eventually, from inside the bill. | Who notices: everyone except you. |
:::

People who have lived through a false negative rarely need convincing that the error is real. They live inside its bill.

The false positive gets underrated, because it looks like caution. Nobody notices the friendship you ended for nothing, least of all you. It shows up years later as a smaller life and a vague sense that people keep letting you down. Your colleagues notice sooner. They start forwarding your emails to each other with "see below" and no other comment.

There's one cost aimed at you in particular. A false positive removes the people who could help you recover. Someone rebuilding after real harm needs ordinary, imperfect, non-strategic relationships more than almost anything. A detector running hot takes apart exactly those.

::: warn Before you use the second receipt
If someone shows you the false-positive column to prove you're being paranoid about them, stop and reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). These receipts are for pricing your own decisions. Nobody else gets to hand them to you as evidence.
:::

## Which is worse: missing it or imagining it?

Neither, across the board. The costs flip depending on where you're standing. In a relationship with a history of harm that's getting worse, missing it is catastrophic and over-calling is survivable. In a friendship, family or job with no history of harm, over-calling does the damage. Find where you're standing, then move your threshold to match.

Bodies run on the same trade. The psychiatrist Randolph Nesse calls it the smoke detector principle. When a false alarm is cheap and a missed threat could be deadly, a well-designed defence fires far more often than there's real danger. That's why your nervous system jumps at shadows.

A detector trained by a bad relationship is priced for the house you used to live in, and [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history) is about repricing it.

::: tree Where are you right now?
- If there has been violence or a threat, a child is at risk, you're being cut off from help, leaving is being made hard, or you're physically afraid → Stop pricing. This isn't a weighing situation. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
- If it's a partner, or anyone with power over you, with a history of harm that's getting worse → Missing it is the expensive error. Weigh heavy toward protection and act on less certainty. If you're wrong about leaving, you're single and embarrassed. If you're wrong about staying, the cost has no ceiling.
- If it's a friend, relative or colleague with no history of harm → Inventing it is the expensive error. It ends things that were fine, in exchange for a risk that was small. Ask for more before you act; [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) shows what "more" means.
- If it's someone new → Both errors are cheap so far, because you've invested little. Watch before you rule. [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) covers the first months.
- If you can't tell which of these you're in → Start with [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days), which helps you name the room.
:::

::: pull
The threshold isn't fixed. Knowing which situation you're standing in, and moving your threshold to match, **is** the skill this book teaches.

## Pricing it for real

::: scene
*LABEL*

Priya has been with Mark for six years. Over the last two he has taken over the money. Her wages go into the joint account. He holds the card. She asks before buying shoes. Lately he checks the receipts.

Priya isn't sure it's control. Mark is careful with money, and they did have debts. So she prices both mistakes.

If she's wrong that it's manipulation and she acts anyway, what does that cost? She opens a small account in her own name and moves part of her wages there. Cost: an awkward conversation, maybe a row.

If she's wrong that it's innocent and does nothing, what does that cost? More years with no money of her own, and no way out if she ever needs one.

The second number is far bigger, so she doesn't need certainty first. She opens the account and tells Mark calmly.

Mark doesn't ask why. He demands the login. When she says no, he rings her mother to say Priya is "hiding money," and he won't speak to her for a week unless she apologises.

A careful spender would have been annoyed. Mark punished her for having money he couldn't see. Yes, it was [financial control](/playbook/financial-control). Pricing the errors let her move before she was sure.
:::

What happened after the account is a test in its own right, the cost of a small no, covered in [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no). If acting the way Priya did could put you at risk, read [Before you test: is testing safe?](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe) first.

## How do I decide when I can't be sure?

You decide how sure you need to be, then act at that level. Pricing the errors usually gives you a lopsided pair of costs, not an answer. That's correct. Most real decisions about people are made under uncertainty. The honest move is to decide how to act while you still lack confidence, instead of manufacturing confidence you don't have.

::: try Price it tonight
Take the situation you're stuck on and answer two questions out loud, with real consequences instead of feelings.

1. *If I'm wrong that this is manipulation, what does that cost me?*
2. *If I'm wrong that this is innocent, what does that cost me?*

Then let the bigger number move your threshold, not your conclusion. This won't tell you what's true. It tells you how much certainty you need before acting, which is a different and more useful thing.
:::

Pricing the errors tells you how sure to be. The next question is how likely the bad explanation was in the first place, and that's [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days).

=== most-people-most-days
title: Most people, most days
question: How do I know if what's happening is normal, or if it's just this relationship?
seo: What is a base rate? Judging people without paranoia
short: A base rate is how often something is true of people before you know anything about this person. Most bad moments have boring causes, so a suspicion has to beat those first. But base rates are local. Inside a controlling home, group or workplace, manipulation really is more common. Name the room first.
---
Here's a question you almost certainly didn't ask before your last conclusion about someone: *how often is this actually true of people?*

That number has a name. Skipping it is the single most efficient way to convict the world.

## What is a base rate?

A base rate is how often something is true before you know anything about the particular case. If, say, one late reply in a hundred were a power move, any late reply would start at one in a hundred. Your evidence then has to drag it up from there. Skip that starting number and every late reply looks guilty.

Your brain doesn't like base rates. In a classic 1973 study, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave people a short personality sketch of a graduate student called Tom W. People judged which subject he studied by how well he matched a stereotype. They mostly ignored how many students were actually in each field. A good story beat the numbers.

A tactic list is a stack of very good stories.

## The two rooms

Picture two rooms.

The **wide room** is the world: strangers, coworkers, new dates, the barista, your cousin's new partner. In the wide room, manipulation is common enough that this library needed writing. It is not so common that every difficult moment is an instance of it. Most people aren't manipulators. Most people who *have* manipulated someone aren't doing it in every interaction, or even most.

Most bad moments in the wide room, by a long way, have no strategy in them at all. Stress. Incompetence. Mismatched expectations. Fear. A phone on 2%. A bad week, a bad night, a bad decade.

The **local room** is one specific place with one specific history: this house, this family, this church, this team, this person who has done it before.

Base rates are population facts, and you might not be living in the population. If you're inside a high-control environment, such as a coercive relationship, a controlling family, a closed religious community or an abusive workplace, your local base rate really is higher. Manipulation really is more common in that room than in the world outside it.

So your detector firing often in there may be accurate reporting, not a fault.

::: handle
Name the room first.
:::

## Why can't I trust anyone after a toxic relationship?

Because your detector was set in a room where suspicion was correct. In that room the local base rate was high, and your alarm was right to fire often. Then you walked into the wide room and it kept firing at the old room's rate. The reading is honest. It belongs to a different room, and it can be reset.

Where that setting came from is the subject of [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history). Resetting it slowly, without switching it off, is [Weighing after you've left](ch:weighing-after-youve-left).

## What a detector does without base rates

It matches. Endlessly and successfully, because matching is easy.

Give a trained mind a list of three hundred tactics and any human being to watch, and it will find hits within an hour. By Sunday it has a case file on half your contacts.

The person isn't necessarily manipulating anyone. The tactic list is a list of *human behaviours*, and the person is human. A detector with no sense of how common things are reports a signal in every direction with equal confidence, which is the same as reporting nothing.

::: pull
Ask **how often is this true of people?** before you ask **does this fit a tactic?** The order matters. Reversed, the tactic list becomes a machine for producing suspects.
:::

None of this means your read is wrong. It means your read has to beat a large and boring field of alternatives before it earns confidence. That field gets its own countdown in [The boring explanations](ch:the-boring-explanations).

## Two numbers, not one

So carry two numbers.

- **The wide rate**: how common is this in people generally? Use it for strangers, colleagues, new people, and anyone outside the place that hurt you.
- **The local rate**: how common is this *here*, in this room, with these people, given what has already happened? Use it inside the place you're actually assessing.

Most calibration failures are one of these numbers used where the other belongs. Use the local rate on the whole world, and a survivor ends up unable to trust a barista. Use the wide rate on a dangerous house, and someone talks themselves into staying another year.

::: warn When the wide rate doesn't apply
This chapter is not permission for anyone to talk you out of an accurate read of an unusual room. Knowing that base rates are local is what lets you say *things here are not normal* and mean something precise by it. If someone quotes "most people aren't manipulators" at you to dismiss what you've lived through, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). If you're in danger, base rates don't apply at all: go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## The room in practice

::: scene
*LABEL*

Jordan left a controlling relationship eight months ago. Three weeks into a new job, a colleague called Ade says in a meeting, "Let's park Jordan's idea and come back to it."

Jordan's chest goes tight. In the old house, "let's park that" meant "never." It meant being shut out and then told you were imagining it. The detector fires at full volume.

Jordan uses the one move from this chapter. *Which room am I in?*

This is the wide room. Ade is new, with no history of anything. In offices everywhere, "let's park it" mostly means the meeting is running over. The alarm is reporting the old room's number.

So Jordan doesn't decide anything yet. After the meeting, Jordan asks, "When do you want to pick my idea back up?" Ade says, "Thursday. I've put it on the agenda." On Thursday, it's on the agenda.

The alarm was right about the old house and wrong about this office. Jordan doesn't switch it off. Jordan just notes which room it was built in.
:::

## How do I know if this is normal?

Ask what you'd expect from people in general, then ask whether this place has a history that changes the odds. Late replies, forgetfulness and bad moods are normal in the wide room. The same things from someone who has already punished, watched or cut you off belong to a local room, and they start from higher odds.

Ten moments. Before you tap, decide which room's base rate applies.

::: sort
- A new coworker doesn't say good morning. || Wide room. New person, no history. Most people who skip hellos are shy, busy or wearing headphones.
- A first date talks about themselves for forty minutes. || Wide room. Nerves make people narrate. Annoying and common. Whether it's a flaw or a flag is in [Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker).
- The barista gets your order wrong twice. || Wide room. Nobody at the coffee shop is running a long game on your oat milk.
- A friend replies "k." || Wide room, unless this friend has a history of punishing you with silence. Mostly "k." is someone typing with one thumb.
- Your manager forgets to copy you on one email. || Wide room. Inboxes are chaos.
- Your manager, who has left you out of three meetings since you raised a complaint, forgets to copy you again. || Local room. Same act as the last one. The history changes the starting odds.
- Your father, who used silence as punishment for years, goes quiet at dinner after you disagree. || Local room. He has done this before, on purpose.
- A partner who has already pulled you away from your sister now says your best friend is "a bad influence." || Local room. A history of [isolation](/playbook/isolation) raises the odds on the next instance.
- Your group's leader says doubts are a sign of spiritual weakness. || Local room. In a high-demand group this is common. See [Weighing a group](ch:weighing-a-group).
- Your ex, who read your messages for years, asks who you were on the phone with. || Local room. History makes the innocent version less likely, not impossible.
:::

Look at the two manager items. Same behaviour, different room, different answer. The room does half the weighing before you've looked at the behaviour at all.

::: try Name it out loud
Next time a detection fires, name the room before you do anything else. Say it out loud if you can: *this is the wide world*, or *this is that house.* The same reading means different things in each, and you'll usually know which room you're in the moment you ask.
:::

Once you know the room, the next question is how bad the behaviour is. Rude and abusive are two ends of a line with five stops between them, and that line is [The gradient](ch:the-gradient).
