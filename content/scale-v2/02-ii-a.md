=== your-detector-has-a-history
title: Your detector has a history
question: Why do I react so strongly to things that remind me of someone who hurt me?
seo: Is it trauma or a red flag? Where your alarm was tuned
short: Your alarm for danger was tuned in a specific room, by a specific person, when reading them right kept you safe. It still fires on anything that resembles them, so it is accurate about the past and unproven about the present. Ask whose voice the alarm sounds like, which era it belongs to, and whether the size matches.
---
Every alarm was installed by somebody. Yours went in during a stretch of your life when reading one particular person correctly kept you safe. A parent, a partner, sometimes a whole house. You learned that a certain tone came before a bad night, and you learned it fast, because slow learners had worse nights.

It is still tuned to that room.

[The first chapter](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh) defined calibration as an alarm that goes off about as often as there's something there. Every alarm is calibrated somewhere. This chapter digs up where yours was.

Nothing in you broke. The system did exactly what it was built to do. A person who learns, at nine or nineteen or thirty-four, that a particular silence comes before shouting grows a fast, pre-conscious, very reliable alarm for that silence. The alarm saved something. It may have saved everything.

The trouble is that it doesn't know the war ended, and it doesn't check ID.

## What is hypervigilance?

Hypervigilance is a threat alarm with the threshold turned down and the speed turned up. You scan rooms, faces and messages for danger, often without deciding to. It tends to grow wherever danger was real and hard to predict. Clinicians list it among the symptoms of PTSD, but having it doesn't mean you have PTSD.

It fires on *resemblance* rather than evidence, because in the place that built it there was no time to gather evidence. By the time you had proof, it was too late to use it. So the system learned to act on the first two notes of the song.

That means it now fires on anyone who happens to hit those two notes. A build. A cadence. A particular flavour of silence. Someone who pauses before answering the way your father paused before answering.

The alarm is real, and it's reporting something true. What it reports is *this resembles the thing that hurt me.* That's information about your history. It isn't yet information about the person in front of you.

::: pull
Your alarm is accurate about the past and unproven about the present. Both halves of that sentence matter, and most people only ever believe one of them.
:::

## The dig

Treat the alarm like an archaeologist treats a find. Before you decide what it means, you work out which layer it came out of. There are usually three, and in every one of them you ask the same question.

::: handle
Whose voice does this alarm sound like?
:::

Ask it literally. Most people can answer within seconds, and the answer is rarely the person currently in the room.

### Layer one: the room you grew up in

This is the deepest layer and the oldest tuning. Here you learned what anger sounded like before it arrived, what a closed door meant, and which questions were safe to ask. These alarms often land in the body before they become a thought: a drop in the stomach, a sudden urge to be very helpful.

What sets them off now is tone, volume, a sigh in the next room, or a parent-shaped disappointment from a partner or boss who has no idea they're doing an impression.

If the voice is your mother's and you're forty-one, in a meeting about quarterly targets, the alarm is filing its report from 1994.

### Layer two: the worst relationship

The middle layer is tuned to one person, very precisely. You learned their warning signs the way a sailor learns the sky. That knowledge was hard won, and a lot of it is still good.

This layer holds a second setting people don't expect, which is relief. If someone hurt you and then came back warm, your system learned that their return felt like safety. That's the hook in [hoovering](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait): the person who set the alarm off turns up offering to switch it off. The relief is real. It still isn't evidence that anything changed.

If the voice is your ex's and the person in front of you is a new date who checked his phone once, the alarm is reading from the old file.

### Layer three: the internet

This is the newest layer, and the only one you installed yourself, at 1am, one video at a time. Four hundred clips about covert narcissists and your alarm is tuned to a stranger's worst ex, as narrated by someone with a ring light and a discount code.

Now it fires on "phrases narcissists use" that turn out to be phrases everyone uses, like "I'm tired" and "Can we talk about this later?"

The internet gave a lot of people the words for what happened to them. It also hands out other people's alarms for free. If the honest answer to "whose voice?" is an account with two million followers that has never met your husband, that's a layer-three alarm. It's the easiest one to turn down, because you can stop feeding it.

## Is it my trauma or a real red flag?

Often both, and the feeling alone can't tell you, because both arrive with the same certainty. Work out which layer the alarm came from, then look at the present. What did this person actually do, and does it keep happening? A trauma-tuned alarm points at resemblance. A red flag shows up in behaviour you could describe to a stranger.

[Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker) sorts the behaviour once you have it. The next chapter gives you a ten-minute test for whether there's anything present-tense to sort.

## Two more questions for the dig

Once you have the voice, ask two more.

1. *What era does this belong to?* If the feeling comes with a decade attached, a kitchen, a song, a smell, that's information.
2. *Am I responding to the size of this event, or to the size of the thing it reminds me of?* This is the diagnostic. A mismatch between how big your reaction is and how big the event was is the clearest sign you're reading history rather than the present. [When people say you're overreacting](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting) has the full size check, including what to do when "too big" is somebody else's verdict.

## What this chapter isn't saying

It isn't saying your alarm is wrong. Saying that would be the same move the people who hurt you made, in kinder language.

Hypervigilance is often *right*. People who grew up reading a dangerous adult often become the most accurate observers in any room. They notice what others structurally can't, because they had to. A very sensitive instrument is excellent at detection and prone to false alarms, which is exactly how a sensitive instrument should behave.

So the work is to treat its output as a first reading. Let it tell you *look here*. Don't let it tell you *you already know*.

::: warn If someone tells you it's just your past
Your history is yours to dig through, privately, by choice. If the person you're worried about tells you your alarm is only your trauma talking, that is the move [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands) warns about, and you can close the conversation.
:::

## The one place it stays in charge

Safety. If the alarm says *get out of this room*, get out of the room, and do the dig afterward from somewhere else. You can always come back and revise. This is rule three from [Chapter two](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands), and learning to calibrate doesn't weaken it. If you're in danger now, go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

## A dig on a second date

::: scene
*LABEL*

Bea is on a second date with Owen. He's funny, and he's nice to the waiter. Then she mentions she might quit her job, and he goes quiet for three seconds, looks down at the table, and says, "Right."

Her chest locks. She wants to leave. She goes to the loo and digs instead.

Whose voice? Her stepdad's. That exact "Right," with the look at the table, meant he'd decided she was being stupid and would say so later, in front of people.

What era? She was fourteen. The kitchen with the orange tiles.

What size? Owen said one word. Her body is reacting to four years.

She goes back and asks. "You went quiet when I said I might quit."

Owen winces. "Sorry. I got made redundant in March. Job talk makes me go weird. I think it's brave, honestly."

Layer one. Bea makes a note to keep watching, which is different from a verdict. The third date is on Thursday.
:::

=== the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm
title: The gut: sensor or smoke alarm
question: Should I trust my gut feeling about someone?
seo: Should you trust your gut about someone? A 10-minute test
short: Trust your gut as a first reading, never as a verdict. A sensor reading can be unpacked into specifics a camera would have caught. A smoke-alarm reading can't, because it fired on resemblance to something old. Give the feeling ten minutes and a piece of paper. Either way, you never need evidence to protect yourself.
---
"I just had a feeling about him."

It's the most commonly offered evidence in any conversation about people, and the least examined. Your gut is a pattern detector with a grudge. Sometimes the grudge is against exactly the right person.

It can't be dismissed, either, because dismissing it is how people get talked back into houses they had already correctly decided to leave.

So take it seriously first, then sort it.

## Should you trust your gut about someone?

Yes, as a first reading. Your gut is fast pattern-matching on hundreds of small signals you never consciously noticed, and it often gets somewhere before your reasoning does. But the same machinery fires on resemblance to old harm, and the two feel identical. Trust it enough to look closer. Test it before you conclude anything about a person.

## Why the gut deserves respect

Intuition isn't mysticism. It's aggregation running below conscious access: timing, small mismatches between what was said and how, the half-beat too long before an answer. The processing is real and fast, and it often reaches conclusions your reasoning takes weeks to catch up with.

People who override that signal to be fair, or polite, or not paranoid, sometimes pay enormously for the courtesy.

## Why the gut can't be the whole case

The same machinery produces the alarm from [the last chapter](ch:your-detector-has-a-history). Present-tense signals and old resemblance feel *identical from the inside*. Both arrive as certainty, before language, feeling like knowing rather than guessing.

The feeling of certainty is a sensation, and it tells you nothing about the world on its own. It's generated the same way whether the match is about this person or about someone twenty years gone.

## What is a smoke-alarm reading?

A smoke-alarm reading is a gut feeling that fired on resemblance rather than on anything this person did. It arrives as certainty but won't unpack into specifics. You reach for details and find only atmosphere: "something about him," "bad energy," "I just knew." Like the one in your kitchen, it's loud, fast, and often set off by toast.

[Chapter one](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh) introduced the alarm that can't tell toast from fire. Resemblance was cheap there, and it's cheap here. A **sensor** reading is the other kind. With some effort it unpacks into present-tense specifics, things a camera would have caught.

## The ten-minute test

There is one test, and it isn't "trust it or don't."

::: pull
**Can you name what set it off?**

A **sensor** reading can be unpacked into present-tense specifics with effort. A **smoke alarm** reading cannot, because there are none. It fired on resemblance.
:::

Give the feeling ten minutes and a piece of paper. Write down only what a camera would have recorded. No mood words, no adjectives about energy.

**Sensor** sounds like this once you sit with it: *he answered a question I didn't ask. She looked at her phone when I mentioned the money. His story about Tuesday doesn't fit what he said on Sunday. Everyone at that table went quiet when he walked in.* Specifics. Present tense.

**Smoke alarm** resists unpacking. The camera test is brutal on vibes. "He had a dark energy" becomes "he wore a black jacket and didn't laugh at my joke," which describes every bass player alive. That still counts for something. It counts as a reading about resemblance, and you treat it as one.

::: handle
Name the smoke, or call it an alarm.
:::

::: tree What did ten minutes give you?
- If you're frightened for your safety, or there's been a threat → Skip the test. Leave, decline, get somewhere safe. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
- If you wrote down three or more camera facts → Sensor reading. You've got something to weigh. Take it to [What is evidence?](ch:what-is-evidence) and [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern).
- If you wrote down one or two → Partial reading. Keep the list and keep watching. One thing isn't a pattern yet.
- If you got only atmosphere → Smoke alarm. Worth noticing, worth some caution, not worth a conclusion. Ask whose voice it is.
- If you got a loop of "what if" that every answer feeds → That may be anxiety rather than your gut. [Gut feeling or anxiety](ch:gut-feeling-or-anxiety) has the table.
:::

## Sensor or smoke alarm? Sort ten real ones

Decide before you tap.

::: sort
- "He answered a question I didn't ask." || Sensor. Specific, present tense, and a camera would have caught it. One instance, so keep watching.
- "Something about her energy was off." || Smoke alarm, for now. Run the camera test. What did she actually do?
- "She looked at her phone every time I mentioned money." || Sensor. Specific, and it happened more than once.
- "He reminds me of my ex." || Smoke alarm, and an honest one. That's resemblance. Ask which layer it comes from.
- "His story about Tuesday doesn't match what he said on Sunday." || Sensor. It's checkable, so check it.
- "I just know he's cheating." || Smoke alarm, unless you can list what changed. A new passcode, new gym hours, a name that came up a lot and then stopped: those are sensor items.
- "Everyone at the table went quiet when he walked in." || Sensor. Other people's detectors are data too.
- "I felt sick the moment I met her." || Either. Spend the ten minutes. If nothing comes, it's an alarm, and you can still keep your distance.
- "He pushed back when I said I'd get a cab home on my own." || Sensor, and a serious one. How someone treats your first no is evidence.
- "The group felt weirdly perfect." || Partial. Ask what made it perfect. Everyone agreeing with the leader, or three people asking for your number in the first hour, are sensor items. [Weighing a group](ch:weighing-a-group) takes it from there.
:::

## Should I trust my gut about cheating?

Trust it enough to look at what changed, and not enough to convict. A gut picking up real change can usually name it: new passwords, new hours, a story with gaps in it. A gut that names nothing, and gets louder at 2am, is more likely an alarm or anxiety. Behaviour you can describe beats a feeling you can't.

Nobody has ever found peace by reading someone's "recently played" at 2am for evidence of a secret emotional life. Why 2am readings run hot is in [The state you're judging from](ch:the-state-youre-judging-from). Why a suspicion keeps finding support once you start looking is in [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself).

## When the gut was right

::: scene
*LABEL*

Five weeks in, Nadia's gut says something is off about Chris. Her friends love him. She can't say why, so she gives it ten minutes and the back of a receipt, camera facts only.

One: he has given her three different reasons why he's between flats. Two: when she asked what he does at work, he talked for five minutes about his boss and never said. Three: he has asked to borrow money twice, £300 for a deposit and then £150 "till Friday."

Three items. A sensor reading. She doesn't accuse him of anything. She says no to the loan and asks for his new landlord's name, so she can help with the move.

He goes cold. Then he says she obviously doesn't trust him after everything he's shared with her, and that his ex was exactly the same.

Two weeks later a mutual friend tells her Chris owes money to two other women he met this year. Both heard about the same deposit.

Yes, it was manipulation. The feeling flagged it, and the receipt proved it.
:::

The move he made when she said no has a page in the Decoder: [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping). Notice what Nadia didn't need. She didn't need to be sure before she kept her money. She only needed to be sure before she said anything about him.

## The exception that outranks the test

Unsurfaceable dread is still worth acting on **for safety**. If you can't name why you don't want to get in the car, don't get in the car.

::: warn This overrides everything above
You do not need to justify a decision that costs you an inconvenience and might cost you everything else.
:::

The distinction is between *protecting yourself*, which needs no evidence at all, and *concluding something about a person*, which does. You can decline the lift and hold the question open. Those aren't in conflict, and confusing them is why people either ignore their instincts or convict on them.

=== gut-feeling-or-anxiety
title: Gut feeling or anxiety
question: How do I know if it's my gut telling me something, or just my anxiety?
seo: Gut feeling or anxiety? How to tell intuition from fear
short: Intuition points at something specific outside you, and it settles or sharpens when you get information. Anxiety is vaguer, loops back to you, and wants another answer every time it gets one. OCD doubt, trauma alarms and attachment panic have their own tells. Having anxiety doesn't make you wrong. Intuition informs. Anxiety interrogates.
---
Intuition and anxiety ring from the same number. Both go off in your chest, both say "something's wrong," and neither leaves its name.

The difference is what each one does with an answer. Give intuition a fact and it either settles or sharpens into a better question.

Give anxiety a fact and it thanks you, files it, and asks for three more. Anxiety is the friend who watches you lock the door, asks if you're sure you locked it, asks you to film yourself locking it, then wonders whether the video might be from yesterday.

::: handle
Intuition informs. Anxiety interrogates.
:::

## Gut feeling or anxiety?

Watch what happens when you get information. A gut feeling points at something specific outside you, and it calms down or sharpens when you learn more. Anxiety is vaguer, circles back to you, and treats each answer as the reason for the next question. If reassurance buys you twenty minutes before the feeling returns, it's probably anxiety.

## Before the table

This chapter sorts how a feeling *behaves*. It can't diagnose you, and the five labels below describe feelings. They aren't conditions you have. Plenty of people meet all five in a single week.

If the feeling is running your days, your sleep or your relationships, take it to someone trained, such as your doctor or a therapist. The bridge near the end of this page helps you find one who understands this territory.

## The symptom table

Five different things can arrive sounding like "my gut says." Here they are by their tells: whether they're specific, what an answer does to them, and where they point.

::: compare Five feelings that all say "something's wrong"
| Feeling | Sounds like | What an answer does | Where it points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intuition | "He changed the subject when I asked about Friday." Specific: a camera could catch it. | Settles it, or sharpens it into one clear question. | Outward, at something they did. |
| Anxiety | "Something bad is going to happen. I don't know what." Vague, and the topic drifts. | Calms it briefly. Then it needs another. | Inward and forward, at "what if." |
| OCD-type doubt | "But do I *really* love him? How can I be sure?" Specific, about certainty itself. | Feeds it. Each answer needs checking again. | Inward, at your own feelings or whether things are "right." |
| Trauma alarm | "This is exactly like before." Specific about the past, vague about now. | Very little. It's matching an old pattern. | Backward, at someone who isn't here. |
| Attachment panic | "They haven't replied. They're pulling away." Specific about distance, vague about meaning. | Calms it when contact comes back, until the next gap. | At the gap between you. |
:::

Read across the "what an answer does" column. That's the fastest tell in the book. A real signal gets *more useful* when it meets a fact. The other four either ignore the fact or eat it and ask for seconds.

## Trusting your gut when you have anxiety

You can, with one adjustment. Anxiety makes your alarm fire more often, so a feeling alone counts for less and specifics count for more. Ask what you'd point to if a friend asked why. If you can name it, weigh it like anyone would. If you can't, let the feeling make you careful, and don't let it make you certain.

There's research on the trap. In a 1995 study, Arnoud Arntz and colleagues found that people with anxiety disorders inferred danger from their own anxious feelings as well as from the facts, while people without them went by the facts.

The researchers called it ex-consequentia reasoning: *I feel afraid, so there must be something to be afraid of.* Your fear is a reading about you. Treat it as one, and go and find the facts about him.

Having anxiety doesn't make you wrong. Anxious people get lied to, cheated on and controlled like everyone else, and a person who wants to control you will find your anxiety very convenient. "That's just your anxiety," used to close down a specific question, is something the Decoder calls [trash-can diagnosis](/playbook/accountability-evasion-via-diagnosis-trash-can-diagnosis).

## Is it my gut or my OCD?

OCD-type doubt is about certainty itself. It asks whether you *really* love them, *really* trust them, or are *really* sure, and no answer counts for long. Checking and reassurance feel like they should help, and instead the doubt comes back hungrier. A gut feeling settles when it gets a real answer. Only a clinician can tell you whether it's OCD.

Researchers have a name for the relationship version. Guy Doron and colleagues describe relationship obsessive-compulsive disorder (ROCD) as obsessive doubts and preoccupation centred on a partner or the relationship: whether you love them enough, whether they're right for you, whether this is the right relationship. The habits that come with it include monitoring your own feelings and seeking reassurance.

That matters for how you use this book. Most of it says *gather more information*. If what you've got is OCD-type doubt, more checking usually feeds it, which is why a therapist who knows OCD is a better next step than another chapter.

## Is it intuition or attachment anxiety?

Attachment panic fires on distance: a slow reply, a quiet evening, vague plans. It asks for contact, and contact calms it until the next gap. Intuition fires on behaviour, such as a story that changed or a promise that broke, and it stays put after they text back. If a reply fixes the feeling, the feeling was about the gap.

Attachment panic has done more forensic analysis of "haha" against "hahaha" than most police forces have done of anything. What reply speed and punctuation can and can't tell you is in [Reading a text message](ch:reading-a-text-message).

## Is it intuition or trauma?

A trauma alarm fires on resemblance. Something about this person matches someone who hurt you, and the feeling arrives fast, often in the body, with an era attached. Intuition fires on what this person did. Ask whose voice the feeling uses. If it's someone from your past, you've found a trauma alarm, and it still deserves respect.

The full dig, layer by layer, is in [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history).

## Is it anxiety or manipulation?

It can be both, and each can feed the other. Anxiety reads threat into ordinary things. Manipulation makes ordinary-looking things threatening on purpose, and living with it makes most people anxious. So skip the question of which one you are. Ask what the other person did, in specifics, and whether it keeps happening. Behaviour can settle it. Your nerves can't.

::: warn Anxiety doesn't cancel your evidence
If someone points at your anxiety, your diagnosis or your therapy to dismiss a specific thing you've raised, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). And if you're afraid for your safety, it doesn't matter which of the five feelings it is. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## The table at midnight

::: scene
*LABEL*

Farah's girlfriend, Jess, is at a work leaving do. It's 11.40pm. Jess's last message, at nine, said "home by 11 x." Farah's chest has been going for twenty minutes.

She runs the table instead of the phone. Specific or vague? Vague. Jess hasn't done anything except be late. Where does it point? At the gap. What would an answer do? She texts "you ok?"

Jess replies at 11.52: "yes!! train delayed, sorry, 20 mins."

The feeling drops for about four minutes. Then it starts on whether "yes!!" is suspiciously cheerful.

That's the tell. An answer arrived, and the feeling wanted another one. Attachment panic, with anxiety as backup. Farah puts the phone face down and does what she'd tell a friend to do, which is wait twenty minutes.

Jess gets in at 12.15 with chips.
:::

## Name the source

Six feelings. Name the source before you tap.

::: sort
- "I've asked him three times if he's still into me, and each yes helps for about an hour." || Anxiety or attachment panic. Reassurance that wears off fast is the tell.
- "She said she was at her sister's, but her sister posted from Spain that night." || Intuition, with a fact attached. Specific, outward, checkable.
- "What if I don't really love him and I'm only scared of being alone? I need to be sure." || OCD-type doubt. The question is about certainty itself. A therapist who knows OCD is the right person to take it to.
- "He raised one eyebrow exactly like my dad did before he exploded." || Trauma alarm. That's resemblance. Ask whose voice it is.
- "They didn't reply for four hours, so they're losing interest." || Attachment panic. It's about the gap, and a reply would fix it.
- "Something terrible is going to happen this weekend." || Anxiety. Vague, pointing forward, and no person attached.
:::

::: bridge longafter:finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this
If anxiety, doubt or old alarms are running more of your life than you'd like, [Finding a therapist who understands this](/longafter/finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this) in The Long After covers what to look for and what to ask before you book.
:::

::: try Try the one-answer test
Next time the feeling hits, write down the one fact that would settle it. Get it if you safely can, then set a timer for an hour. If it's still settled when the timer rings, it was a real question. If it's back with a new one, that was anxiety, and the answer to anxiety is rarely another answer.
:::

Sometimes the doubt doesn't start inside you at all. Somebody else hands it to you, in three words, and [When people say you're overreacting](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting) is how you check whether they're right.

=== when-people-say-youre-overreacting
title: When people say you’re overreacting
question: Am I overreacting, or are they making me think I am?
seo: Am I overreacting? How to tell, and when it's a tactic
short: Measure two things separately: how big the event was, and how big your reaction was in actions rather than feelings. If they match, you aren't overreacting. If your reaction is bigger, check whether it's carrying a pattern or your past. Then check who's measuring. "You're overreacting" from the person who did it, every time, is a tactic.
---
"You're overreacting" has never once calmed anybody down. It's a sentence that throws petrol on the fire and then complains about the smoke.

It's also true sometimes, which is what makes it such a good weapon. So you need a way to check it that doesn't depend on the person saying it.

Picture two rulers held side by side. One measures the event. The other measures your reaction. Then look at whose hand is holding them.

## Am I overreacting?

Measure the event and your reaction separately. Place the event on the gradient, from rude to abusive. Then measure your reaction by what you said and did, not by how strongly you felt. If the two roughly match, you aren't overreacting, whoever says so. If your reaction is much bigger, check whether it's carrying a pattern or your past.

## Ruler one: the event

Place it on [the gradient](ch:the-gradient), the seven-position dial from rude to abusive. A forgotten call sits near the left end. Reading your private messages sits well to the right. Be honest in both directions. The dial doesn't care how it felt. It cares what happened, whether it shrank your freedom, and whether it keeps happening.

## Ruler two: your reaction

Most arguments about overreacting fall apart once you split the reaction in two. Your feeling and your action get measured separately.

Feelings don't have to be proportionate. You can feel furious about a missed bin day. That's weather inside you, and it's allowed. Actions have to be sized: what you said, how loudly, what you did, and for how long. "Overreacting" is only a fair word for actions. Nobody gets to tell you your feeling was the wrong size.

Then add history. A big reaction to a small event can be the right size for the fortieth time it happened. If so, say the fortieth time out loud, because the other person can only see tonight's bin. And if the history is older than this relationship, [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history) helps you find which room it's from.

## Then check who's holding the rulers

The same measurement means different things depending on who takes it.

- **The person who did it.** They have a reason to want a short ruler for the event and a long one for your reaction. Their verdict counts, but it counts least.
- **Someone with no stake.** A friend who likes you both, a sibling who wasn't there. Their read is worth the most.
- **You, at 2am.** Every ruler is longer at 2am. [The state you're judging from](ch:the-state-youre-judging-from) explains why.
- **The group chat.** It will tell you you're *under*-reacting to a missed call. It's casting a drama, and your partner just got the villain's part.

::: handle
Match the size, then check who's measuring.
:::

## Three honest outcomes

::: tree Too big, too small, or being called too big?
- If you're frightened of them, or there's been a threat → Stop measuring. Fear isn't a reaction to be sized. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
- If your actions were much bigger than the event, and the event has no history → You may be overreacting. Check whose voice the alarm has and what state you're in. Then repair the action. The feeling doesn't need repairing.
- If your actions were bigger than this event but the right size for a pattern → You're reacting to the pattern. Name the pattern rather than the incident. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) helps you describe it.
- If your reaction was smaller than the event → You may be under-reacting. Imagine your closest friend describing it about her partner, and listen to what you'd say back.
- If the sizes match and you're still told you're overreacting, by the person who did it, every time you raise anything → The word is being used to close the subject. That's [minimization](/playbook/minimization). If it comes with "that never happened," it's heading toward [gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting).
:::

## The outcome nobody mentions: under-reacting

People who were told for years that they overreact start shrinking things before anyone else can. "It's fine, she only reads my messages when she's stressed." "He's just like that when he's had a few." By the time they describe it to anyone, the event has been pre-measured down to nothing.

If that's you, use the friend test from the tree. Your friend's partner reading her messages whenever she's stressed is not something you'd call fine.

## How do I know if I'm overreacting in a relationship?

Ask three things. Did my actions match where this event sits on the gradient? Am I reacting to this one time, or to a pattern I haven't named yet? Who is telling me I'm overreacting: someone with no stake, or the person whose behaviour I raised? If it's always them, about everything, the word is doing a job.

The same two rulers work at work and with family. [Weighing a boss](ch:weighing-a-boss) and [Weighing family](ch:weighing-family) apply them to those rooms.

## Why do people say "you're overreacting"?

Usually because your reaction surprised them, made them uncomfortable, or honestly looked too big. Sometimes because it was. And sometimes because it works: it moves the conversation from what they did to how you responded. The test is whether the original thing ever gets discussed. If the topic is always your reaction, it's a tactic.

When you're told you're overreacting, you can agree to look at your reaction and still keep the original topic on the table.

::: script
**If they say:** "You're overreacting."

**You:** "Maybe. Let's settle what happened first. Then you can tell me if my reaction was too big."

**If they say:** "You always do this."

**You:** "We can talk about my reactions another time. Right now I'm asking about Saturday."

**If they say:** "I'm not talking to you while you're like this."

**You:** "Okay. When can we talk about it?"
:::

Refusing to discuss what you said until your tone is perfect has a Decoder page: [tone policing](/playbook/tone-policing). So does being told that what you feel is wrong: [emotional invalidation](/playbook/emotional-invalidation).

::: warn If this chapter is used on you
If someone hands you this chapter, or the tree above, to prove you're overreacting about them, that is the move [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands) describes. The rulers are yours to hold. And you never need to win the size argument before you protect yourself.
:::

## When the rulers matched

::: scene
*LABEL*

Imani finds her journal in the wrong drawer, open at last week. She asks Joel about it. He says he flicked through it, "barely," and asks why she's being so dramatic.

She measures. The event: he read her private journal. That sits well right of the middle of the dial. Her reaction: she cried, said she felt violated, and slept at her friend Rachel's. Big, but the sizes match.

Who's measuring? Joel. The person who did it.

The next morning she comes back calm and says it in one sentence: "Reading my journal crossed a line, and I need it not to happen again."

Joel says she's hysterical. Then he says he never read it, she must have left it open herself, and maybe she should see someone about how paranoid she's getting.

That's three moves in two minutes. "Barely" became "never." Her reaction became the topic, and then her mind did.

She writes down both versions of his story, with the times, and rings Rachel. Yes, this was manipulation. It started as minimization and became gaslighting before breakfast.
:::

## When the rulers didn't

::: scene
*LABEL*

Dan's friend Wes forgets to ring him on his birthday. Dan unfollows him, leaves the five-a-side group chat, and drafts a long message about loyalty.

His sister says he's overreacting. He's furious with her too. Then he measures. The event: one forgotten call from a friend of twelve years, with no history of it. Thoughtless, far left on the dial. His actions: an unfollow, a group exit and a 600-word draft. Right-hand actions for a left-hand event.

Who's measuring? His sister, who likes them both and gains nothing either way.

He deletes the draft and texts Wes: "You forgot my birthday, you absolute muppet." Wes rings within a minute, mortified, and buys the first round on Friday.

Dan was overreacting. His feeling was allowed. His actions were sized for a betrayal that never happened.
:::

## Measure it again, without looking

::: sort
- What does the first ruler measure? || The event. Place it on the gradient.
- What does the second ruler measure, and what's the split? || Your reaction, in actions. Feelings don't have to be proportionate. Actions do.
- When can a big reaction to a small event still be the right size? || When it's the right size for a pattern. Then name the pattern.
- The third question? || Who's measuring, and what do they gain from a short ruler or a long one?
- When is "you're overreacting" a tactic? || When it comes from the person who did it, every time, and the conversation never gets back to what happened.
:::

Take the last time someone said you were overreacting. Write three lines. One: where the event sits on the dial. Two: what you actually did and said, as a list. Three: who said "overreacting," and what a smaller ruler would have saved them. Read the three lines back as if a friend had written them.
:::

Your brain judges people with shortcuts. They're fast, they're usually fine, and they go wrong in predictable directions. Psychologists have been cataloguing them for about fifty years, and the main finding is that you aren't special. Everyone has them, including everyone who has ever written a book about them. This one included.

So here's a field guide. Think of a bird-spotter's guide, except every bird lives in your head. Each card has the name, one sentence on what it does, how it looks in the wild, and one move that checks it.

::: handle

:::

## What are cognitive biases in relationships?

They're the predictable ways a normal brain misjudges people. You blame someone's character when the situation explains it, read hostility into accidents, let one great or awful trait colour everything, and weight one bad moment over ten good ones. They push both ways: toward suspicion of decent people and toward excuses for harmful ones.

The biggest bias of all, confirmation bias, has a chapter to itself: [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself). The eight below are the ones that do most of the rest of the damage.

## The field guide

### 1. The fundamental attribution error

**What it does:** explains other people's behaviour by their character, and your own by your circumstances.

**In the wild:** He's late because he's selfish. You're late because of the trains.

**Counter-move:** Before you decide what kind of person they are, name one situation that would make a decent person do the same thing.

The psychologist Lee Ross gave it its name in 1977. It's the reason [three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations) always includes a situational one.

### 2. Hostile attribution bias

**What it does:** reads an ambiguous act as a deliberate attack.

**In the wild:** He didn't hold the lift. He wanted you to miss it.

**Counter-move:** Ask what this would look like if it were an accident. If the answer is "exactly like this," you don't know yet.

In a 1980 study, Kenneth Dodge had a peer wreck a boy's building project in a way that was clearly hostile, clearly accidental, or ambiguous. When it was ambiguous, aggressive boys responded as if it had been hostile, and non-aggressive boys as if it had been an accident.

If your detector was tuned in a room where things often *were* on purpose, expect this one to be loud.

### 3. The halo and horns effect

**What it does:** lets one strong trait, good or bad, colour your read of everything else.

**In the wild:** He's charming and generous, so the way he talks to his sister must be banter. She was rude once at a party, so now even her new haircut seems aggressive.

**Counter-move:** Judge the behaviour as if a stranger had done it.

In 1977, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson showed students one of two videos of the same lecturer, acting either warm or cold. Students who saw him warm found his appearance, mannerisms and accent appealing. Students who saw him cold found the same things irritating. They didn't know their overall impression was doing it.

The halo is a controlling person's best friend: concerns about how they treat you get answered with how much everyone loves them, a move the Decoder calls [halo effect exploitation](/playbook/halo-effect-exploitation).

### 4. Negativity bias

**What it does:** makes a bad thing weigh more than a good thing of the same size.

**In the wild:** Ten kind evenings and one cutting remark, and the remark is what you replay in the shower.

**Counter-move:** Count, in writing. Then ask whether the bad thing is the pattern or the exception.

In a 2001 review, Roy Baumeister and colleagues concluded that "bad is stronger than good" across a broad range of psychological findings.

Correcting for it has one hard limit. A single threat or act of violence outweighs any number of good evenings, and weighing it that way is accurate. If that's what you're counting, stop counting and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

### 5. Hindsight bias

**What it does:** makes an outcome look obvious once you know it.

**In the wild:** "The signs were all there." Some were. Some only became signs after you knew.

**Counter-move:** Ask what you actually knew then, not what you know now.

In 1975, Baruch Fischhoff found that people who were told how events turned out judged that outcome as more predictable than people who weren't told, and were largely unaware their judgment had shifted.

It's also the engine of "I should have known," which is unfair to the version of you who didn't. [The retrospective rewrite](ch:the-retrospective-rewrite) shows how to revise a past memory by memory instead.

### 6. Motivated reasoning

**What it does:** reaches the conclusion you want, then finds reasons that sound fair.

**In the wild:** You want him to be the one, so the lie about his job was "a misunderstanding." Or you want out, so forgetting your mum's birthday was "contempt."

**Counter-move:** Ask what you'd conclude if you wanted the opposite. If the evidence would flip, it wasn't the evidence deciding.

Ziva Kunda's 1990 review found that people are more likely to arrive at the conclusions they want, limited mainly by whether they can build a reasonable-sounding case for them. Wanting to stay is a motive. So is the time already spent, which the Decoder calls [sunk cost exploitation](/playbook/sunk-cost-exploitation) when someone else points at it to keep you in.

### 7. Emotional reasoning

**What it does:** treats the strength of a feeling as proof of what's true.

**In the wild:** "I feel betrayed, so he betrayed me." "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong."

**Counter-move:** Name the feeling. Then, on a separate line, list what happened. Feelings are strong evidence about you and weak evidence about them.

Arnoud Arntz and colleagues found in 1995 that people with anxiety disorders inferred danger from their own anxiety as well as from the facts, which they called ex-consequentia reasoning. The guilt version is the one manipulative people farm, and it has a Decoder page: [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping). [Gut feeling or anxiety](ch:gut-feeling-or-anxiety) has more on telling a feeling from a fact.

### 8. Rejection sensitivity

**What it does:** expects rejection, spots it fast in ambiguous moments, and reacts hard.

**In the wild:** He seemed distracted at dinner. He's going off you.

**Counter-move:** List three other reasons someone might be distracted, then ask the boring question: "Long day?"

Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman, in 1996, described people high in rejection sensitivity as tending to "anxiously expect, readily perceive, and overreact to" rejection. In new relationships, they readily saw intentional rejection in a partner's insensitive behaviour.

## Can bias make me miss abuse?

Yes. People think of bias as the thing that makes you paranoid, but several of these push toward excuses. The halo turns a charming partner's cruelty into banter. Motivated reasoning protects a relationship you've invested years in. Hindsight bias then blames you for not seeing it sooner. Before you correct for a bias, check which direction it's pushing you.

::: warn "You're just biased" is not an answer
Everyone has all eight of these, including the person telling you you're biased. If someone uses this chapter to wave away something you've seen, ask them which bias and which counter-move, then run it yourself.

If the person saying it is the one you're worried about, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). None of this applies when you're in danger: go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## Three cards on a Tuesday

::: scene
*LABEL*

Theo's new manager, Ruth, sends emails like "Need this by 3." and "See me." No hello, no name. By week two Theo has decided she dislikes him, and he's drafting a message to an old colleague asking if her team is hiring.

He runs three cards first.

Hostile attribution: what would these emails look like if they were innocent? Exactly like this.

Fundamental attribution error: what situation would make a decent person write like that? Back-to-back meetings. Typing on a phone.

Negativity bias: he counts. Eleven curt emails. Also, she put his name on the project update to the director, and asked how his son's operation went.

He decides he doesn't know yet. On Thursday he mentions the emails. Ruth looks horrified. "God, I write them in the lift. I'll add a hello."

She never adds a hello. The emails stay terrible. She stays decent.
:::

## Name the bias

Eight lines, eight biases. Name each one before you tap.

::: sort
- "He's late because he's selfish. I'm late because of traffic." || The fundamental attribution error.
- "She didn't hold the door. She wanted it to hit me." || Hostile attribution bias.
- "He gives so much to charity. He can't be cruel at home." || The halo effect.
- "Nine good weeks and one bad night, and the night is all I think about." || Negativity bias.
- "Looking back, it was obvious from day one." || Hindsight bias.
- "I've decided I'm staying, so the lie must have been a misunderstanding." || Motivated reasoning.
- "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." || Emotional reasoning.
- "She took an hour to reply. She's going off me." || Rejection sensitivity.
:::

::: try Try it today
Pick one judgment you made about someone this week. Run it past two cards from the guide and write each counter-move's answer in a line. If your judgment survives both, keep it with more confidence. If it doesn't, you've just saved somebody a very strange conversation.
:::

Biases bend a judgment. Sometimes the whole investigation exists for a different reason, which is to keep a loss at arm's length, and that's [Grief in the costume of analysis](ch:grief-in-the-costume-of-analysis).
