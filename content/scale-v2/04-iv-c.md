=== weighing-someone-new
title: Weighing someone new
question: Is this new person just keen, or is this love bombing?
seo: Love bombing or genuine interest? Weighing someone new
short: In the first months you have almost no record, so don't try to weigh the whole person. Weigh the pace, and what happens to it when you slow things down. Genuine interest can be fast and still lets you set the speed. Love bombing is fast and needs you to keep up. Slow it one notch and watch.
---
Week three. They know your coffee order. They've met your dog on video. They've named the cottage you'll rent in March, and the dog is invited.

You're delighted. You're also reading this at 1am, which tells you something too.

Both are allowed. What you can know in the first ninety days is less than you'd like. What you can watch is more than you think.

## What you can't know yet

You can't know who they are. Not in a month. Everyone is on their best behaviour early on, including you. They haven't met your "I'm fine" yet, and you haven't met their opinions about parking. Most of us have said "I don't normally do this" on a date we were very much normally doing.

The internet disagrees. By its rules, every good-morning text is love bombing and every gift is a tactic. Follow that and you'll end up suspicious of a man for buying you a sandwich.

So the whole-person verdict is off the table. A pattern needs weeks to show itself, and you don't have the weeks yet ([One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) counts them). Most new people are also ordinary, which is less exciting than the threads suggest ([Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days) explains why that matters).

## What you can know: the dual-control car

Picture a driving-school car. Two sets of pedals. The learner drives, and the instructor has a brake on the passenger side.

A new relationship, or a new friendship, is that car with both of you in it. Either of you can drive fast. The thing to watch is whether your brake still works.

- **Enthusiasm** drives fast, and slows down when you press your brake. A bit disappointed, maybe. Still slows down.
- **Love bombing** drives fast, and treats your brake as a fault. It argues with the pedal, sulks about the pedal, or puts its foot down harder.

That's why pace on its own proves nothing. Lots of decent people are fast. What tells you something is the speed after you've pressed the brake.

::: ask
Ask yourself: have I actually pressed the brake yet, or only worried about it at 1am?
:::

## Love bombing or genuine interest?

Both are fast and both feel wonderful, so speed alone won't tell you. Slow things down a notch and watch. Genuine interest is disappointed, adjusts to your pace, and is still there next week. Love bombing argues with your pace, sulks, or speeds up. Watch what the attention does when you ask for less of it.

::: compare Enthusiasm or love bombing
| | Enthusiasm | Love bombing |
|---|---|---|
| What it's about | You, specifically. Your actual stories, your odd job, your sister's name | A script that would fit anyone: "soulmate", "never felt this way", "you're different" |
| The future | Next weekend, maybe a gig next month | Moving in, holidays, children, all before you've seen their kitchen |
| When you slow it down | "Fair enough." Still there next week | Hurt, a speech, ten messages, or bigger gestures |
| Gifts and favours | Easy to say no to, nothing owed | Arrive with a weight attached, and come up later |
| Your friends and plans | Wants to meet them. Fine when you're busy | Wants you to themselves, or charms your friends like it's a campaign |
| Month three | Warmth settles into something ordinary | Warmth turns conditional, on when you comply and off when you don't |
:::

"I've never felt this way about anyone" in week two is either true or a line, and both versions sound the same. The line version is identical on everyone it's used on, like a mail merge with your name typed in.

The Decoder files the pieces separately: [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing) itself, [future faking](/playbook/future-faking) for the cottage in March, [premature exclusivity](/playbook/premature-exclusivity) for "delete the apps, I already have", and [mirroring on demand](/playbook/mirroring-on-demand) for the person who, astonishingly, loves every one of your favourite bands. The last stage, where the warmth is withdrawn once you're attached, is the [idealise, devalue, discard cycle](/playbook/idealize-devalue-discard-cycle).

## The first ninety days

What's ordinary, what to watch and when to press the brake, by stage. None of this is a deadline. Some people move faster, some slower, and both are fine if both of you are choosing it.

**Week one.** Ordinary: lots of questions, some nerves, texting more than you will later, a slightly edited version of their life. Worth watching: how they talk about exes ([Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker) has that one), and how they treat the waiter. Press the brake if: they want a promise of exclusivity before a second date. If they push after you say no to anything physical, you're past braking: see the warning further down.

**Month one.** Ordinary: plans for the next few weeks, meeting a friend or two, your first small disagreement, a bit of excitement about where this might go. Worth watching: the first no, in either direction. Press the brake if: they're planning years ahead, asking you to drop friends or apps, or sharing something enormous on date two and then expecting your secrets in return. The Decoder calls that last one [premature vulnerability](/playbook/premature-vulnerability-trauma-dumping), and the debt it creates is the point.

**Month three.** Ordinary: less performance on both sides. You've seen them tired and a bit boring, and they've seen you the same. Worth watching: what happens to the warmth when you have a life of your own that week. Press the brake if: you've started adapting in advance, checking their mood before you mention plans. That's the climate from [Conflict or control](ch:conflict-or-control), arriving early.

## The same thing, with a new friend

Friendships get love bombed too, and nobody warns you, because we don't have songs about it.

The new friend who calls you their best friend after one weekend, wants every evening, tells you a huge secret in hour two, and gets oddly cold about your older friends is running the same pattern without the flowers.

The communication researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated how much time it takes to make a friend. Roughly 50 hours together takes you from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 to friend, and more than 200 to close friend. Those are averages, measured on friendship, and people vary. Still, someone calling you their best friend after a weekend has skipped about 190 hours of the course and is asking for the certificate.

## Is it love bombing, or am I just not used to being treated well?

Sometimes the second. If your last relationship was cold, ordinary kindness can feel suspicious, and your alarm calls it love bombing. What makes something love bombing is what it does to your freedom. How nice it feels doesn't come into it. Kindness that respects your pace is kindness. Kindness that needs you to keep up is pressure.

[Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history) covers alarms built in an old relationship. If you're dating after one that hurt you, so does the Long After chapter in the bridge below.

## Trusting your gut when dating

Use it as a pointer, then check where it points. If your gut can name specifics, such as a story that changed or a no that got argued with, that's a sensor reading worth acting on. If it can only say "too good to be true", check whether you're reacting to this person or to the last one.

[The gut: sensor or smoke alarm?](ch:the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm) has the ten-minute test, and [Gut feeling or anxiety?](ch:gut-feeling-or-anxiety) separates intuition from the panic that early dating can set off.

## Press the brake on purpose

You don't need to invent a test. Early dating hands you dozens of natural brakes: a night you're busy, a weekend you're not ready for, a topic you'd rather not discuss yet. Use a real one.

Say it once, warmly, without a speech. Then watch the next few days. [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no) has the price list (temperature, access, framing), and it works on someone you've known for a month as well as on someone you've known for ten years.

::: script Pressing the brake
**You:** "I'm really enjoying this. I'd like to keep it to weekends for a while."
**If they say:** "Oh. Okay. I get carried away, sorry."
**You:** "No harm done. Saturday?"
**If they say:** "I thought we had something special."
**You:** "We might. I'd like to find out at my speed."
**If they say:** anything about your past, your walls, or your trust issues.
**You:** "Maybe. Weekends, for now." Then watch what the week brings.
:::

::: warn Not a pace question
If someone pushes past a no about sex or your body, turns up where you didn't invite them, or frightens you, stop weighing pace. You don't need a second data point. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops), and find the domestic abuse service where you live through [Find help](/longafter/find-help).
:::

## Fast, and maybe fine

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Zara's last boyfriend sent flowers to her office on their second date, and a year later he was reading her emails. So when Diego sends a good-morning text every day for three weeks, her stomach goes cold.

In week four he says he thinks he's falling for her, and that his friend has a cottage by the sea they could borrow in March.

She doesn't diagnose him. She presses the brake. "I like you a lot. I want to keep it to once a week for now, and I'm not ready for a weekend away."

Diego goes pink. "Right. Sorry. My sister calls me a golden retriever." The good-morning texts carry on, a little less often. He asks how her pottery class went, and remembers the teacher's name. Saturday arrives as planned.

Two weeks later, over pasta, he mentions that he's asked his friend to keep March free for them. "Just in case. No pressure."

Zara puts her fork down. Her sister thinks he's adorable. Her stomach still thinks every keen man is her ex. The brake has worked once.

She says, "Let's talk about March in March," and watches his face.
:::

If anyone uses this chapter to make you doubt a no you've already said, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).

=== weighing-family
title: Weighing your own family
question: Am I overreacting about my parents, or are they actually controlling?
seo: Am I overreacting about my parents? Difficult or controlling
short: To tell if you're overreacting about your parents, look at what it costs. Difficult family costs you an evening: the comment, the sigh, the old argument, and then you go home still yourself. Controlling family costs you yourself: your money, partner, friends or plans need their approval, and saying no gets punished. Weigh each person separately.
---
Every family has a seating plan nobody wrote down, and everyone knows exactly where it is.

Picture one long table on a holiday. The good plates are out. You arrived forty minutes ago and you already feel about fourteen. You're thirty-eight, you manage a team of twelve, and you have just sulked in your childhood bedroom under a poster of a band you now deny liking.

Families are the hardest people to weigh, for three reasons. Your detector was built in this house, so the house reads as normal. Leaving costs more than leaving anyone else does. And every one of them can say the sentence that ends arguments: "We're your family."

So don't weigh "the family". Weigh the table one chair at a time.

## Am I overreacting about my parents?

Maybe. Check two things. First, the size: does your reaction match what they did today, or what they've done for twenty years? Second, the price: what happens when you say no to them? If a no costs a sulk, they're difficult. If it costs money, access, or your siblings' goodwill, it's control.

[When people say you're overreacting](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting) has the full size-match. With family the second ruler matters more, because "you're overreacting" is often the family's way of closing a subject that's been closed since 1997.

## Chair one: your dad

He asks whether you're "still doing the website thing" as if it were a phase. He has strong views on the gravy. He's told the same story about the time the car broke down since you were nine, and he's asleep by eight.

Weigh him with the three tests from [the gradient](ch:the-gradient). Freedom: does anything shrink? No. You do the website thing, he grumbles, and nothing happens. Repetition: every year. Response: you told him once, years ago, that the job comments got to you. He grumbled, then toned it down for a month or two.

**Difficult.** He costs you an evening and some patience. Your life stays your own.

## Chair two: your mum

She cooked for three days and she's lovely. She also asks if you've booked your flight for Easter, which is in four months. She has a key to your flat and uses it to leave food and, once, to tidy your post into piles. When you mentioned spending Christmas with your partner's family, she cried at the table and didn't touch her dessert.

Weigh it with [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no). Last year you did go to your partner's family. She didn't speak to you until February. Your aunt rang to ask why you'd "abandoned" her. When contact came back, it came back with a condition: next year, no excuses.

**Controlling.** The warmth is real, and it's also the currency. Love that's switched off when you choose differently is what the Decoder calls [conditional love](/playbook/conditional-love). "After everything I've done" is a [guilt legacy](/playbook/guilt-legacy), the aunt's phone call is [proxy recruitment](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment), and the key used without asking is your adult life with a door that doesn't lock.

That's the line between the first two chairs. Your dad disapproves of your choices. Your mum invoices you for them.

::: ask
Ask yourself: when I say no to them, do I get a sigh or a bill?
:::

## Chair three: your brother

He's the one who says, "It's just Mum, why do you have to make it a thing?" When you leave early, he texts that you've ruined the day.

He's easy to file as the enemy. Run the audience test first ([Is it you, or is it everyone?](ch:is-it-you-or-is-it-everyone)). Is he like this with everyone, or only with you? He smooths things over for everyone. He's done it since he was eight, and he flinches when Mum's voice goes up.

**A minimiser.** He's keeping the peace in a house where peace was expensive. That still costs you, and you're allowed to say so. He doesn't hold anything over you, though, and he's scared of the same thing you are.

Families hand out roles, and the roles outlast childhood. One child gets to be the good one, which the Decoder calls [golden child elevation](/playbook/golden-child-elevation). Another gets blamed for everything, which is [scapegoating](/playbook/scapegoating). A third ends up looking after the adults, which is [parentification](/playbook/parentification). Siblings in different roles grew up in different families under the same roof, which is why you can describe the same childhood and disagree about everything.

## Chair four: your grandmother

She has asked when you're getting married every year since 2009. It's less a question than a seasonal tradition, like the good plates. She thinks your tattoo is "a shame" and says so.

Weigh it with [Difference is not malice](ch:difference-is-not-malice). She has no power over your life. She controls nothing you need. When you change the subject, she lets you. She's speaking the dialect of a different century.

**Difficult, barely.** She costs you about ninety seconds.

## Chair five: you

There's one more chair, and it's yours.

You arrived with a script. You're braced for your mum before she's said anything, and you snapped at your dad about the gravy, which was a gravy comment. Some of what happens at this table is you, reacting to the family you had at fourteen instead of the one in front of you.

That doesn't cancel anything above. Your mum's prices are real. But [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history), and this is the room it was built in, so check it here more than anywhere.

## Am I the toxic one in my family?

Check the same way you checked them. What does your no cost other people? Do relatives adapt around your moods, or rehearse before telling you things? Are you like this with everyone, or only at home? Being the one who finally objects doesn't make you toxic. Charging people for their choices would.

[Am I the one doing it?](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it) asks the questions properly.

## Is my parent controlling or just difficult?

Difficult parents criticise, nag, sulk and repeat themselves, but your choices stay yours. Controlling parents need to approve them. Watch for three things: your no gets punished, they reach into your adult life (money, keys, partner, children), and other relatives get sent to deliver the message. Disapproval is allowed. Punishment is the line.

Add power. A parent who holds your deposit, your childcare or your inheritance can make an ordinary request work as an order. [Power changes the meaning](ch:power-changes-the-meaning) shows how.

## Why do I feel like a child again when I go home?

Because the house still runs on the old roles, and everyone, including you, slips back into them within an hour. That's normal, and it proves nothing about anyone. The question is whether you can step out of the role when you notice it. If stepping out gets you punished, that's the finding.

## Am I overreacting by stepping back from my parents?

Not if the step matches what's happening. Seeing them less, leaving earlier or skipping a holiday are choices about your own time, which makes them boundaries ([A boundary or a rule](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule) has the grammar). You don't need a verdict about your parents to see them less. [Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision) explains why the two can come apart.

## The table, sorted

Before you scroll up: difficult, controlling, or something else?

::: sort
- A relative rings to say your mum is "devastated" and you should apologise. || Proxy recruitment. The message has a sender. Talk to the sender.
- Your grandad makes old-fashioned comments and drops them when you change the subject. || Difference. He controls nothing you need.
- Your mum stops speaking to you for six weeks after you spend a holiday elsewhere. || Controlling. That's the price of a no.
- You snap at everyone within an hour of arriving, before anyone has said anything. || Check chair five. Your detector was built in this house.
- Your parents paid for your wedding and now decide where you spend Sundays. || Power turning gifts into orders. Watch what happens when you say no to a Sunday.
- You've told your parents you're not staying the whole week, and you're bracing for the reaction. What do you ask yourself? || When I say no to them, do I get a sigh or a bill?
- Your sister tells you that you're overreacting, and does the same for everyone. || A minimiser. It costs you, and she holds nothing over you.
- Your dad says your job isn't a real job, every Christmas, and nothing else happens. || Difficult. It costs patience. Your freedom stays put.
:::

## Saying no to family

Family arguments are hard to win because nobody's arguing about the thing. You're arguing about whether you love them. So keep the sentence about the plan, and keep the love out of the bargaining.

::: script With family
**You:** "We're spending Christmas with my partner's family this year. We'll come to you on the twenty-eighth."
**If they say:** "So we don't matter to you."
**You:** "You do. That's why we're coming on the twenty-eighth."
**If they say:** "Your father is devastated."
**You:** "He can tell me himself. The twenty-eighth is still on."
**If they say:** "After everything we've done for you."
**You:** "I know. I'm grateful. The twenty-eighth."
:::

Say the plan, say it again, and don't add reasons. Each reason is something new to argue with. Then watch the week, as you would with anyone else.

## The cost of no, at home

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Kerry's mum lent her fifteen thousand towards her flat. No paperwork. "Family doesn't need contracts." Since then her mum has had a key, an opinion about Kerry's boyfriend, and a standing expectation of Sunday lunch.

Kerry's new job has weekend shifts, and she starts to feel ungrateful. So she weighs her mum's chair.

Is it her mum with everyone? Kerry's brother, who borrowed nothing, skips Sundays all the time and gets a shrug. Power: her mum holds the loan and the key.

Kerry tells her she can do every other Sunday.

On Monday her mum texts that she "didn't realise the money bought so little." On Wednesday Kerry's brother rings to say Mum is "really hurt" and could she just come. On Friday her mum lets herself into the flat while Kerry's at work, "to check the boiler," and leaves a repayment schedule on the kitchen table, due in six months.

A sulk would have been disappointment. This is control: [financial control](/playbook/financial-control), [favour debt](/playbook/favor-debt), and a brother sent as the messenger.

Kerry changes the locks first. Then she rings a free debt advice service about repaying a family loan, and writes to her mum with a schedule she can actually keep.
:::

## If home isn't safe

::: warn If you're a child or teenager
If you're under eighteen and home doesn't feel safe, because someone hurts you, threatens you, or you're scared to go home, don't weigh this on your own. Tell a trusted adult: a teacher, a school counsellor or nurse, a doctor, a relative, or a friend's parent. You can also contact a children's helpline or the child protection service where you live. You're allowed to tell, even if you're not sure it's "bad enough".

If you're an adult and anyone in your family has been violent, has threatened you, or controls where you can live, stop weighing and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). [Find help](/longafter/find-help) shows where to look.
:::

A parent disagreeing with your choices is not control, and "you're controlling" can be used to shut down any opinion at all. If someone uses this chapter to tell you your family's prices are in your head, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).
:::

=== weighing-a-boss
title: Weighing a boss
question: Is my boss just bad at managing, or is this something worse?
seo: Bad boss vs toxic boss: how to tell if it's coercion
short: A bad boss is bad at the job: disorganised, moody, unclear, sometimes unfair, and it usually lands on the whole team. A toxic boss is good at you. They control what you can say, who you can talk to and what it costs to refuse, complain or leave, and it lands hardest on whoever objects. Review them on six lines.
---
Once a year, your manager fills in a form about you. This chapter hands you the pen.

Most bad bosses aren't villains. They were good at the job, got promoted for it, and now do a different job nobody trained them for, badly, while forwarding you emails titled "FW: FW: Re: urgent??". That's miserable to work for, and it needs a different fix from a boss who is coercive.

A bad boss needs managing around, or leaving. A coercive one needs a paper trail first.

## Bad boss vs toxic boss

A bad boss is bad at the work: unclear, disorganised, moody or unfair, and usually with everyone. A toxic boss, the kind this chapter calls coercive, controls you. Saying no costs you, raising a concern gets punished, talking to colleagues is discouraged, and leaving is made frightening. If the misery is general, it's bad management. If it targets whoever objects, it's coercion.

## The performance review, reversed

Score your manager from 1 to 5 on each line, with one dated example each. If you can't give an example, skip the line. Dreading Sunday nights is a good reason to fill this in. It doesn't count as an example.

::: compare The six lines
| Line | What you're scoring | A bad boss scores low because… | A coercive boss scores low because… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarity | Do you know what good work looks like? | They never said, or they said three things | They keep it vague on purpose, so you can always be wrong |
| 2. Consistency | Do the rules and targets stay put? | They forget what they asked for | The target moves every time you get near it, and only for you |
| 3. Credit and blame | Who gets which? | They're careless about it | Your wins become theirs and their misses become yours |
| 4. Your no | What happens when you decline extra work? | They grumble and give it to someone else | Your shifts, projects or reviews change afterwards |
| 5. Your voice | What happens when you raise a concern or talk to colleagues? | They get defensive, then forget | You're told to bring things "only to me", and the people you spoke to go quiet |
| 6. Your exit | Can you take leave, transfer or get a reference? | They're disorganised about the forms | Leaving is made frightening: a hint about your reference, a threat to your visa or your next job |
:::

Now look at where the low scores sit.

Lines 1 to 3 measure whether someone can manage. Lines 4 to 6 measure whether they're managing you: your no, your voice, your way out. Plenty of decent people score 2 on clarity. If you've ever managed anyone, you have too, probably in a meeting you called to "align". A boss who scores low on 4, 5 and 6 has stopped being bad at work and started being good at something else.

::: ask
Ask yourself: what did my last no to this person cost me?
:::

Each low score on lines 2 to 6 has a Decoder page. The moving target is [moving the goalposts](/playbook/moving-goalposts). Taking your wins is [credit theft](/playbook/credit-theft), and handing you their misses is [blame shifting](/playbook/blame-shifting). A no that changes your schedule is [punishment of boundaries](/playbook/punishment-of-boundaries). "Bring it only to me" is [controlled communication](/playbook/controlled-communication), and if it comes with cutting you out of meetings it's [isolation](/playbook/isolation). The reference hint is [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation).

## Is my boss bad or toxic?

Look at which half of the review holds the low scores. Low on clarity, consistency or credit, with your nos, your concerns and your exit still safe, is a bad manager: annoying, and possibly worth leaving. Low on what happens when you refuse, speak up or try to leave is control, and it calls for records and advice before anything else.

## One bad day isn't the finding

Researchers who study bad management have a name for the hostile end of it: **abusive supervision**. The management scholar Bennett Tepper defined it in 2000 as subordinates' perceptions of "the sustained display of hostile verbal and nonverbal behaviors, excluding physical contact" by their supervisors.

The useful word there is *sustained*. A manager who snaps at you once in a terrible week has had a terrible week. One who does it every week has a pattern. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) has the four blanks to fill in: how often, how long, getting worse, and what changed when named.

## Two tests that work well at work

**The audience test.** Is it the whole team, or only you? Only the people who've pushed back? A manager who is charming in the all-staff meeting and cutting in your private meetings is showing you selectivity, and [Is it you, or is it everyone?](ch:is-it-you-or-is-it-everyone) explains why selectivity is evidence. "My door is always open" is a sentence. What happens when you walk through it is the data.

**The power map.** "No pressure, but could you do Saturday?" from a peer is a question. From the person who writes your schedule and your reference, it's an instruction with the name taken off. [Power changes the meaning](ch:power-changes-the-meaning) works through exactly that sentence.

## Is my boss trying to get me to quit?

Sometimes. Look for several signs at once: targets that move only for you, sudden criticism after years of good reviews, being left out of meetings and emails, and a formal improvement plan nobody could pass. One of these is a bad patch. Several at once, starting after you objected to something, is a pattern worth documenting today.

The Decoder calls the slow version being [managed out through exhaustion](/playbook/managed-out-through-exhaustion). At Will has a chapter on [the improvement plan that is a termination](/atwill/the-improvement-plan-that-is-a-termination).

## Am I overreacting at work?

Use the same two rulers as anywhere else: does your reaction match the event, and who is telling you you're overreacting? If it's colleagues with nothing to gain, listen. If it's the manager whose behaviour you raised, every time, the word is closing a subject. [When people say you're overreacting](ch:when-people-say-youre-overreacting) has the tree.

## A bad boss, briefly

Priti's manager double-books meetings, forgets what he asked for on Monday, and once sent the whole team a spreadsheet called "FINAL final v3 USE THIS ONE". When Priti told him she couldn't take on the new client, he sighed, said "ugh, fine", and gave it to someone else. Nothing changed on her schedule. Her next review was late, vague and fair.

Lines 1 and 2: low. Lines 4 to 6: fine. Priti has a bad boss, and a decision to make about whether to stay. She doesn't need a lawyer.

=== weighing-a-friend
title: Weighing a friend
question: Is my friend just going through a hard time, or are they using me?
seo: One-sided friendship or a hard season? How to tell
short: Every friendship runs a ledger of give and take. In a hard season it tips one way for a while, and that's what friends are for. A one-sided friendship is a ledger that never tips back, even when their life is fine. Look at two seasons of the friendship, then ask for something small back and watch.
---
Your friend has rung you every night for six weeks.

You know their ex's new partner's name, her job, and the name of her dog. Meanwhile you've been promoted, moved flat and adopted a cat, and your friend knows about none of it, because every call opens with "okay, so he's posted again."

At this point you're a podcast they call into.

Is it a hard season, or is it who they are? Draw the ledger and find out.

## Is my friend using me?

Maybe, or maybe they're in a hard season. Look past now. In a hard season the giving runs one way for a while, there's a cause you can name, and small returns still happen. A one-sided friendship runs one way in the good times too. Ask for something small back, and watch what happens.

## The ledger

Everyone says friends don't keep score. Everyone also knows exactly who hasn't bought a round since 2019. If nobody springs to mind, it might be you.

A ledger isn't petty if you do it honestly, and honest means both columns. That's the difference from the rigged file in [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself). A rigged file only collects evidence against someone. A ledger records what they gave too.

Draw two columns: **what I gave**, **what I got**. Then draw it twice.

- **Season one** is now.
- **Season two** is another time: a stretch when their life was fine, or better still, a stretch when *yours* wasn't.

One season tells you almost nothing. Every friendship tips one way when someone's dad is ill or their marriage is ending. That's what the friendship is for. The second season is where the pattern shows.

::: ask
Ask yourself: when my life was the hard one, did they show up?
:::

## Hard season or using?

::: compare The two ledgers
| | A hard season | Using |
|---|---|---|
| The cause | You can name it: a death, a breakup, illness, a job lost, a new baby | Always a crisis. A new one arrives as the last one ends |
| They know | "Sorry, I'm all me at the moment" | It doesn't occur to them, or it's your job |
| Small returns | Still happen: a text on your interview day, a card in the middle of their chaos | Only when they want the next thing |
| Your good news | Heard, even if briefly | Ignored, topped, or turned back to them |
| When you're out of capacity | They manage, or ask someone else | They sulk, or tell others you've abandoned them |
| When it ends | The ledger tips back. "Your turn" | It doesn't end |
| Season two | They were there when yours was hard | They weren't, or they turned up for the gossip |
:::

Some people only ring when they need a lift to the airport. That's a taxi service with feelings.

The using column has Decoder pages. The friend who drains you and gives nothing back is [emotional parasitism](/playbook/emotional-parasitism). Wearing out everyone around them, one helper at a time, is [support system exhaustion](/playbook/support-system-exhaustion). Turning your news into a contest about who has it worse is [competitive victimhood](/playbook/competitive-victimhood). And the favour from three years ago that keeps getting cashed is [favour debt](/playbook/favor-debt).

## Ask for something small back

The friend version of [the cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no) has two halves.

**Say a small no.** "I can't do tonight, I'm wiped." A friend in a hard season is disappointed and finds another way through the evening. A user makes you pay for it: coldness, a guilt speech, or a message to someone else about how you've changed.

**Ask for a small thing.** "Can you come with me to look at the flat on Saturday?" A friend in a hard season says yes, or says "I can't this week, but I owe you," and means it. A user is suddenly busy, every time.

Neither half needs a confrontation. Life provides both within a fortnight.

## How do I tell a friend I can't be their therapist?

Say what you can give instead of what you can't, and name a time. "I love you, and I can't do nightly calls at the moment. Can we do Sunday evenings?" A friend in a hard season will take Sundays. If every limit is treated as abandonment, that's information too. Suggesting they also see a professional isn't rejecting them.

::: script Setting a limit with a friend
**You:** "I love you, and I'm running on empty. I can't do calls every night. Can we do Sundays?"
**If they say:** "God, of course. I've been a lot."
**You:** "You've had a lot. Sunday, seven?"
**If they say:** "Wow. I thought you were the one person I could count on."
**You:** "You can. On Sundays."
**If they say:** "Fine. I'll stop bothering you."
**You:** "You're not bothering me. Sunday, seven." Then leave it there.
:::

## Is it a toxic friendship, or am I a bad friend for pulling back?

Pulling back when you're empty doesn't make you a bad friend. It makes you a friend with limits, which is the only kind. A friendship is in trouble when your limits are punished, your news is ignored and the ledger never tips back. Check whether you're pulling back from a season or from a pattern before you decide anything bigger.

## The ledger has a mirror

Run it on yourself. Is there a friend whose ledger with you has tipped one way for a long time? Did you notice? Did you say "sorry, I'm all me at the moment," or did it not occur to you?

Being the one in the long season isn't a failing. Leaving a hard stretch, especially an abusive relationship, can take years, and the friends who carried you may be tired. Saying so to them is a kind of repair. [Am I the one doing it?](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it) has the wider self-check.

## Two seasons, read

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Maureen and Jackie have been friends for forty years. Since spring, Maureen has carried Jackie through a divorce at sixty-four: lifts to the lawyer, calls most nights. Maureen's sister says, "She's using you."

Maureen draws the ledger. Now: all one way. When Maureen was widowed twelve years ago, Jackie slept on her sofa for a week and made the funeral sandwiches. She rings every anniversary.

Maureen tries a small no. "I need tonight off, love."

"God, of course," Jackie says. "I've been a lot. Thursday?"

In month eight Jackie arrives with wine, coat still on. "Right. Your turn. Talk."

Maureen talks for two hours about her grandson's driving test.
:::

## Season or pattern?

Before you scroll up: a hard season, or using?

::: sort
- When your relationship ended, she turned up with soup and stayed till midnight. || Season two, in her favour. Put it in the ledger.
- Every time you share good news, she has a crisis by the end of the call. || Worth watching. Your news keeps getting replaced. Check season two.
- He only gets in touch when he needs the van, and he's been fine for two years. || Using, or close to it. The ledger never tips back.
- Your friend has called every night for a month and you're starting to resent it. What do you ask yourself? || When my life was the hard one, did they show up?
- He's been grieving for eight months and you're starting to feel tired. || Still a season. Grief runs on its own clock. Say what you can give, and keep giving that.
- You said you couldn't talk tonight and she told your group chat you've "gone cold on her." || The cost of no. Watch whether it happens again.
- Her mum is in hospital, she's calling every night, and she asked how your interview went. || Hard season. Small returns are still happening.
:::

If a friend tells you they might hurt themselves, or that someone is hurting them, it isn't a ledger question any more. If a friend's behaviour frightens you, or they've threatened you, stop weighing and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

And if you're auditing a grieving friend three weeks after the funeral, stop. That is a season by any measure. === weighing-a-group
title: Weighing a group
question: Is this group a healthy community, or is it something more controlling?
seo: Is it a cult? Signs of a high-demand group, and the test
short: The signs of a high-demand group build slowly. It asks for more each year, more time, money, obedience and distance from outside friends, until leaving costs you everyone. Compare what it gave you in month one with what it asks in month twelve. Then ask the clearest question there is: can you leave and still be spoken to?
---
Every group hands you a welcome pack.

Sometimes it's a real one: a tote bag, a lanyard, a laminated timetable. Sometimes it's a warm hello and three people who learn your name. A church, a running club, a wellness circle, an activist group, a start-up, a supplement "team", a forum that turned into a Discord that turned into your whole social life.

Most groups are just groups. They have annoying committee meetings and one person who always brings hummus. This chapter is about the few that aren't, and how to tell the difference without deciding what you think of anyone's beliefs.

## What a group believes is none of this chapter's business

A group with strange beliefs and an open door is fine. A group with ordinary beliefs and a locked door isn't.

So this chapter never asks whether the teaching is true, whether the supplements work or whether the leader really channels anything. It asks what the group does to your time, your money, your doubts, your friendships and your exit. Those can be checked from the outside, in any faith or none.

## Is it a cult?

That word ends conversations, so this book rarely uses it. Look for the signs of a high-demand group instead. Does the group ask for more of your time, money and loyalty each year, discourage outside friends and information, treat doubts as your failing, and make leaving cost you everyone? Then it's a high-demand group, whatever it calls itself.

## Month one: two welcome packs

::: compare Month one
| A healthy group hands you… | A high-demand group hands you… |
|---|---|
| A warm hello and a timetable | An overwhelming welcome. Everyone loves you instantly |
| "Come when you can" | "We've been waiting for you" |
| Answers when you ask who runs it and where the money goes | Answers that get vaguer the closer you get to the top |
| A few in-jokes you'll pick up | A whole vocabulary, with words that mean something new here |
| An easy way to drift off and come back | A buddy who checks in if you miss a week |
:::

Look how alike the packs are. A friendly group and a high-demand one are both lovely in month one, which is why people join them. The flood of warmth in the right-hand column has a name you've met before: [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing), the same move as in [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new), done by a whole room at once.

So don't judge the group by month one. Keep the pack and open it again later.

## Month twelve: what it asks now

::: compare Month twelve
| A healthy group asks… | A high-demand group asks… |
|---|---|
| The same as before, or less, as you settle in | More every season: more evenings, more courses, more levels, more stock |
| Nothing when you miss three months | A conversation about your commitment |
| You to disagree out loud, sometimes | You to bring your doubts to a leader, who explains what's wrong with you |
| Nothing about your outside friends | Why you still spend time with people who "don't get it" |
| You to read whatever you like | You to be careful about outside sources, especially former members |
| Nothing when you leave. You're still invited to the barbecue | Silence. Former friends stop replying, and your name stops being said |
:::

The healthy pack stays about the same size. The high-demand one keeps growing, and you pay for it in instalments small enough that each one seems fine. That's the gradient from [The gradient](ch:the-gradient), stretched over a year. Nobody joins a group that asks for everything. People join groups that ask for a little more than last time.

## Can you leave and still be spoken to?

If yes, it's probably just a group, however demanding it is. If leaving means old friends go silent, your name stops being said and people who left are treated as traitors, the group is using belonging as a lock. Start with this question.

In a healthy group, leaving is a change of schedule. You stop going, people say they'll miss you, and someone still sends you a birthday message. In a high-demand group, leaving is treated as betrayal, and the punishment is the whole social world you built inside it. The Decoder calls it [ostracism](/playbook/ostracism).

You can often answer the question without leaving. Think of the last three people who left. Are they still spoken to? Are they spoken *about*? In what tone?

::: ask
Ask yourself: if I stopped going tomorrow, who would still speak to me?
:::

## What is the BITE model?

The BITE model is a model of how high-demand groups control members, built by the counsellor and author Steven Hassan. It sorts control into four kinds: Behaviour, Information, Thought and Emotional control. Use it as a checklist of questions. It can't prove anything about a group on its own.

Hassan set out the four kinds in his 1988 book *Combatting Cult Mind Control* and in his 2020 doctoral dissertation. Some examples from his lists, in plain words:

- **Behaviour.** Needing permission for major decisions. Your schedule, diet, sleep or money set by the group. Individuality discouraged. The Decoder's page is [behaviour control](/playbook/behavior-control).
- **Information.** Outside sources discouraged. Some teachings kept for insiders. Members encouraged to report on each other. The Decoder's page is [information control](/playbook/information-control).
- **Thought.** Critical questions about the leader or teaching not allowed. Other beliefs labelled evil or worthless. A private vocabulary, including stock phrases that end a thought before it finishes. The Decoder has [thought control](/playbook/thought-control), [loaded language](/playbook/loaded-language) and [thought-stopping clichés](/playbook/thought-stopping-clich-s).
- **Emotion.** Feelings narrowed to the approved ones. Your needs called selfish. Fear of the outside world, of leaving, or of what happens to people who leave. The Decoder has [emotional control](/playbook/emotion-control) and [phobia indoctrination](/playbook/phobia-indoctrination-catastrophizing).

Treat it as one author's framework. Some researchers who study religious movements reject "mind control" language altogether, arguing that it underrates the choices people make in joining and leaving.

The sociologist Eileen Barker, who studied the Unification Church in the 1970s, found that most people who went to its recruitment workshops never joined, which is hard to square with irresistible mind control. Both things can be true. People do choose, and groups do make some choices much more expensive than others.

## Demanding or high-demand?

A rowing club that trains at 5am, a choir with three rehearsals a week, a religious order with vows: all of these ask a lot, and that's allowed. You chose a hard thing.

What separates them is the same thing that separated conflict from control in [Conflict or control](ch:conflict-or-control): what happens to your no and your exit. The rowing club is demanding. If you quit, the coach is disappointed and the group chat sends a farewell gif. A high-demand group can ask for less in hours and still cost more, because leaving it costs you everyone.

## Is an MLM a cult?

Usually it's a business, but some run like a high-demand group. Weigh it the same way: what it asks in month twelve, and whether you're still spoken to after you stop selling. The US Federal Trade Commission says most people who join legitimate MLMs make little or no money, and some lose money.

Watch for the particular shape. Your friends and family become your market. Buying stock becomes the price of staying "active". Doubts get called negativity or a "poverty mindset". And the money you've already put in becomes the reason to put in more, which the Decoder calls [sunk cost exploitation](/playbook/sunk-cost-exploitation).

## Twelve months in

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Shauna's gym friend, Nkechi, invites her to "the Collective", a wellness team that meets for Saturday brunch. Month one is lovely. Forty people learn her name in a fortnight. The group chat never sleeps, and everyone says she's glowing.

The Collective also sells supplements. To stay on the inner team she buys a monthly pack for a hundred and forty, and joins the Sunday "mindset calls". When she asks where the money goes, the leader, Brett, says that question is "coming from scarcity".

In month eleven she adds it up: eighteen hundred and fifty, and every Sunday since March. She opens the welcome pack again. Month one gave her brunch. Month eleven asks for her Sundays, her savings and her doubts.

She tells Brett she's stepping back to being a customer.

"Totally respect it," Brett replies. "I'll take you out of the team chat. It's for people who are committed."

Within a day, thirty-nine of the forty stop replying. One posts about people who "quit on their dreams". Shauna reads it twice. [Love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing) on the way in, [sunk cost exploitation](/playbook/sunk-cost-exploitation) in the middle, [ostracism](/playbook/ostracism) at the door: the Collective is a high-demand group.

On Tuesday Nkechi texts. "Coffee? Not Collective stuff. Just us."

Shauna says yes.
:::

Don't attack the group. Arguments about the teaching push people further in, because the group has usually warned them that outsiders would say exactly that. Keep the relationship warm and the door open. Ask questions they can answer privately: "What would happen if you took a month off?" The person who still speaks to them afterwards is often the reason they can leave. [Helping someone else weigh](ch:helping-someone-else-weigh) has the scripts.
