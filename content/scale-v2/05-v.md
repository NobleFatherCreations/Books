title: Am I the one doing it?
question: Am I the toxic one? How do I know if I'm the problem in my relationship?
seo: Am I the toxic one? An honest self-check on behaviour
short: To find out whether you're the toxic one, judge yourself the way you'd judge anyone else, on what you did rather than what you meant. Ask what people have stopped raising with you, what your no costs them, and what you do when cornered. Most people find a habit or two. Instant, total guilt is a warning sign too.
---
For forty-three chapters this book has tested other people's behaviour. Even the chapters about your detector were about how you read them. Now the tests turn round, onto what you do, before you act on anything the book told you.

Picture a dashcam. It doesn't know you were late, that the other driver started it, or that you're normally a very patient person. It records what the car did. That's the only footage this chapter accepts.

You've been judging other people on footage all along. You can't see inside them, so you watch what they do, how often, and what happens when someone names it. Yourself, you judge on the director's commentary. You know what you meant. You know how hard your week was. You know you'd never.

Everyone does this. In a series of studies, Emily Pronin and Matthew Kugler found that people judged their own bias by looking inward at their thoughts and motives, and judged other people's bias by their behaviour. Bias mostly doesn't show up when you look inward, so people rated themselves as less biased than their peers. Once participants were taught that introspection is unreliable, they stopped claiming to be the exception.

So the dashcam rule applies to you exactly as it applies to anyone:

::: handle
Behaviour, not intention.
:::

You don't have reliable access to your own intentions either, whatever it feels like from the inside. Intentions are the statement you give the insurance company. The footage is what happened.

## Am I the toxic one?

Maybe in places, and you can find out. Judge yourself on the last six months the way you'd judge anyone: specific acts, how often, and what happened when someone raised them. Most honest people find one or two real habits, under one kind of pressure, with one or two people. That's a habit you can fix, and fixing it is the point.

"Toxic" is a word that won't survive the next chapter. It's what people type at 1am, though, so here we are.

::: warn If you were trained to take the blame
Some people arrive at a self-check with the answer already loaded. If you've lived under sustained control, assuming it was your fault was probably what kept the peace, and the habit doesn't switch off because you opened a self-check. If your conclusion that you're the bad one is instant, total and effortless, that speed is the tell. A real self-assessment is slow, specific and mixed: some entries, no verdict.

Hold yourself to the book's own standard. What is the **evidence**? Which acts, how often, over what period, and what happened when they were named? Treat yourself as you'd treat anyone else in these pages, which means neither exemption nor conviction without a file.

If someone keeps handing you this chapter as proof that you're the problem, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). If you're afraid of them, stop the self-check and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## The mirror interview

Eight questions. Answer them about the last six months, on paper, in specifics: a date, a person, a sentence. "I think I'm generally fair" isn't an answer. It's a press release.

::: check The mirror interview
☐ When did you last go quiet at someone instead of saying the thing? How long did it last, and did they know why?
☐ When did you last tell a version of events that was true in every clause and misleading overall?
☐ What do the people close to you apologise for most often? Is it plausible they're all doing that much wrong?
☐ Who has stopped raising things with you? What happened the last time they tried?
☐ When someone tells you no, what does it cost them? Answer the way they would.

☐ Have you used what you know about someone, their fears, their family, their worst year, to win an argument?
☐ Who has less power than you, and how does your ordinary bad day land on them?
☐ What do you accuse other people of most often? Does it appear anywhere in your own six months?
:::

The last question is there because accusing others of the thing you do is common enough to have a Decoder page: [projection](/playbook/projection). Run it as a check on yourself, without the gotcha.

### What each question is looking for

Each one is a tactic seen from the inside, where it never feels like a tactic.

- **Going quiet** turns into the [silent treatment](/playbook/withholding-silent-treatment) when the other person can't tell why, or when it lasts until they apologise for something. From inside, it feels like needing space. Look at who has to end it.
- **The shaped version** is [half-truth framing](/playbook/half-truth-framing). From inside, it feels like telling your side.
- **Their constant apologising** means something in the room is expensive. It may be you.
- **The person who stopped raising things** is the most accurate report on you that exists. Nobody wrote it down, and it's there anyway.
- **The cost of your no** is often a sigh, a sulk, or a short speech about everything you do for them. That one is [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping), and it works so well that people do it without noticing. [The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no) has the full price list, and your prices count.
- **Using what you know** turns someone's trust into your ammunition. It's one of the clearest lines in the whole book.
- **Power** decides how heavy your bad day is. A snap from a flatmate is a bad moment. The same snap from the person who holds the lease is a reminder of who holds the lease. [Power changes the meaning](ch:power-changes-the-meaning) has the rest.

::: bridge repair:who-has-stopped-raising-things-with-you
[Who has stopped raising things with you](/repair/who-has-stopped-raising-things-with-you) in Repair turns question four into a whole document, and shows how to build it without asking anyone.
:::

## Reading what you found

::: tree
- If you found nothing at all → You read the questions without answering them. Everyone has entries, and a clean six months means the questions were admired from a distance. Go back and give each one a date.
- If you found two or three real entries → That's normal and workable. Look for the shape: usually one habit, under one pressure, with one or two people.
- If you found many, and they repeat with several people → Take it seriously. It's a pattern, and the rest of this chapter and [Repair](/repair) are written for you.
- If you'd decided you were the problem before question two → Stop there. That's a trained reflex, and findings take longer. Reread the warning above and weigh it the slow way.
:::

## How do I know if I'm being toxic?

Look at what happens around you. If the people near you rehearse before speaking, apologise constantly, have stopped raising things, or pay a price every time they say no, something you do is expensive for them. Then check whether it happens with one person or with everyone, and whether it changed when someone named it.

Two chapters do most of the work here. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) gives you four questions: how often, how long, getting worse, what changed when named. Run them on your own entries exactly as you'd run them on anyone's. And [Is it you, or is it everyone?](ch:is-it-you-or-is-it-everyone) has a version for this exact worry. If every partner, flatmate and boss has turned out to be the problem, one person was in every room.

## What if I'm both the one hurting and the one hurt?

It can be both, and one doesn't cancel the other. If you're asking because you both say you're the one being hurt, the fear test in [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) is built for it: who is afraid of whom, who adapts in advance, whose no gets charged for. Answer it as if they were answering too.

## The mirror, used twice

::: scene
*LABEL*

Hollis thinks of himself as the calm one. He doesn't shout. When Bram upsets him, he goes quiet until he's ready to talk.

He runs the mirror interview on a Sunday, mainly to prove a point.

Question one: last month, three days of near silence after Bram forgot his work dinner. Bram knew something was wrong but not what, and asked four times.

Question three: Bram apologises a lot. For being late, for the blender, for asking what's wrong.

Question four: Bram used to bring up money. He hasn't since March. The last time he did, Hollis went quiet for a weekend.

Question five: when Bram says no, Hollis doesn't argue. He gets very, very polite.

Every entry is the same move, timed and lasting until Bram apologises. From the inside it felt like needing space. On the footage, a weekend of silence is the price of raising money.

Yes, it was manipulation, and it was his: the silent treatment, charged as a fee. That evening he says, "When I go quiet for days, you end up apologising for things you didn't do. From now on I'll tell you when I need time, and for how long." Bram says, carefully, "Okay." The weeks will show whether it holds.
:::

::: scene
*LABEL*

Ngozi's husband tells her most weeks that she's the problem. Too sensitive, too critical, "exhausting." After a row about his weekend plans she finds this chapter and runs it, braced for the worst.

She makes herself give every answer a date.

Question one: she went quiet for an evening after Yaw mocked her presentation in front of his brother. He knew why. She told him the next morning.

Question four: nobody has stopped raising things with her. Yaw raises things daily.

Question five: when Yaw says no, she sulks for about ten minutes. She writes that down, because it's true.

Question seven: he earns most of the money and decides how it's spent.

Then she checks the other direction. She has stopped mentioning her sister, her overtime, and anything she buys. Each time she did, the evening went cold.

Her page has two small entries, both with dates. His accusations have no dates at all, only "always" and "everyone thinks so."

She isn't the problem he describes. She keeps the ten-minute sulk on her list, and starts paying attention to the things she no longer says out loud.
:::

## Sort the evidence

Before you look: what does the mirror accept as evidence, and what doesn't count? Decide for each one, then check.

::: sort
- "I'd never manipulate anyone. I'm far too honest." || Intention. Director's commentary. Check the footage instead.
- "Last Tuesday I didn't speak to her until she apologised for something she hadn't done." || Behaviour, with a date. That's an entry.
- "I'm the problem. I always have been. Everything is my fault." || Too fast and too total. Ask yourself for evidence the way you'd ask anyone.
- "My sister has stopped telling me about her boyfriend." || Behaviour around you. Ask what happened the last time she did.
- "I only did it because I was stressed." || Could be a cause or an excuse. Which one depends on what you do next week.
- "Everyone I've ever lived with has turned out to be impossible." || You were in every room. Run the audience test on yourself.
:::

## What to do with what you find

Most people find two or three real entries. A habit, not a character.

That's workable. Name it to the person it affects, plainly, the way [The naming test](ch:the-naming-test) taught you to name things in others: the act and its effect, then stop talking. Skip the self-flagellation. A huge apology performed at someone is its own imposition, because now they have to comfort you. Then change the behaviour and let the weeks show it. You know what real repair looks like by now. [Repair, and apology's counterfeit](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit) applies to you, all four features of it.

Don't go back through every relationship you've had and recast yourself as the villain. That's [the retrospective rewrite](ch:the-retrospective-rewrite) pointed inward, and it produces a story instead of a change. If you've accused someone of something you now think was yours, [Being wrong well](ch:being-wrong-well) has the words.

::: bridge repair:the-difference-and-why-its-practical
If you found an entry and immediately found a reason for it, Repair starts there. [The difference, and why it's practical](/repair/the-difference-and-why-its-practical) shows whether your reason is a cause, which tells you what to change, or an excuse, which tells you not to.
:::

::: bridge silence:am-i-the-one-doing-it
If you're a man who has been on the receiving end of control, [Am I the one doing it?](/silence/am-i-the-one-doing-it) in The Silence runs this audit for you directly, without softening it.
:::

## Who's holding the mirror?

A mirror you pick up is a tool. A mirror someone holds in front of your face every day, always pointing at you and never at them, is something else. Making you doubt your own reading of what happens is the core of [gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting), and "you're the problem" on repeat is one of its most efficient forms.

Run the interview on your own terms, in your own time, with dates. If the only entries you can find are the ones someone else wrote for you, that's a finding about them.

::: try Try it this week
Take question four alone. In three minutes, write down every subject the people close to you no longer bring up with you. Don't argue with the list while you write it. Then pick one subject and raise it yourself: "You used to talk to me about this and you don't any more. I think I made it expensive. I'd like to hear it." Listen to the end before you answer.
:::

Self-checks fail most often in the words you use. The vocabulary this book gave you can describe what's happening or win an argument, and from the inside the two feel identical: [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon).

=== the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon
title: The vocabulary as a weapon
question: Am I using words like narcissist, gaslighting and boundaries to win arguments?
seo: Weaponised therapy-speak: when words like gaslighting win
short: Therapy-speak becomes a weapon when a clinical word replaces a description. Narcissist, gaslighting, toxic and boundary arrive with borrowed authority and no burden of proof, so they end arguments instead of settling them. The fix is translation: say what happened in plain, checkable words. If you can't, what you have is a label.
---
This book handed you a vocabulary. Gaslighting. Narcissist. Trauma. Boundary. DARVO. Love bombing. Coercive control. Toxic.

Those words carry real weight, and weight doesn't care who's swinging it.

Picture a borrowed lab coat. Put one on, walk into a hospital, and people step aside, lower their voices and stop asking questions. The coat hasn't examined anybody. It makes everyone assume somebody did.

Clinical words work like the coat. "He's a narcissist" sounds like the end of an assessment. Usually it's the start of a mood.

## What is weaponised therapy-speak?

It's using psychological terms to win, end or dodge a conversation instead of describing what happened. "You're gaslighting me" about an ordinary disagreement. "I'm protecting my peace" to avoid a fair question. "That's a trauma response" to make a request unaskable. The words come from therapy. The job they're doing comes from arguments.

The Decoder has a page for it, [weaponized therapy-speak](/playbook/weaponized-therapy-speak), and it sits in the tactic list beside everything else this book describes. Learning the vocabulary doesn't exempt you from that list. It equips you for it.

## What a clinical word does to an argument

It ends it.

Once a behaviour has a clinical name, the other person is no longer disagreeing with you. They're disputing a diagnosis, which makes them sound defensive by construction. The word walks in with three things it didn't earn: authority borrowed from psychology, moral weight borrowed from real victims, and no burden of proof at all.

So it gets used for what arguments get used for. To win. To close a subject you'd rather not discuss. To relabel your own avoidance as a boundary. To turn an ex into a diagnosis, so that nothing they said needs answering. To shut down a fair complaint by pathologising the person who made it.

The words have been stretching, too. The psychologist Nick Haslam called it concept creep: ideas like abuse, bullying, trauma and mental disorder have widened over recent decades to cover more kinds of experience, including milder ones, than they first described. Some of that widening caught real harms that used to go unnamed. Some of it means a word that once meant something specific now means "a thing I didn't like." Both are true, and you're the one who has to tell which you're doing.

## The traps, one by one

- **"Narcissist" as an insult.** In everyday use it has mostly come loose from its clinical meaning and now means "selfish person I'm done with," said in the tone of a finding. A personality disorder is diagnosed by a clinician who has assessed the person. Your group chat met him twice, at a barbecue. [Weighing an expert](ch:weighing-an-expert) covers diagnosis at a distance.
- **"Boundary" covering a demand.** A boundary governs what you'll do. A rule governs what they must do. Demands can be fair, so call them demands, because a demand dressed as a boundary can't be answered. [A boundary, or a rule for someone else](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule) has the grammar test, and the Decoder files the dressed-up kind under [behaviour control](/playbook/behavior-control).
- **"Gaslighting" for disagreeing about facts.** Two people remembering an evening differently is a Tuesday. [Gaslighting](/playbook/gaslighting) is a sustained campaign to make someone distrust their own mind. Use one word for both and you've burned it for the people who need it. [I don't remember it that way](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way) sorts the two.
- **"Love bombing" for generosity.** A partner who is keen in month one isn't automatically running a playbook. [Love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing) shows itself in the pace and in what happens at the first no, and [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) has the timeline.
- **"DARVO" for any denial.** If every "that's not what happened" counts as [DARVO](/playbook/darvo), there's no answer the other person can give that doesn't convict them. Some denials are true.
- **"Triggered" and "traumatised" as trump cards.** Real and serious. Also usable to make any awkward conversation unaskable. The same move with a diagnosis, real or claimed, offered as the reason nothing can be discussed, has its own Decoder page: [trash-can diagnosis](/playbook/accountability-evasion-via-diagnosis-trash-can-diagnosis).
- **The unanswerable frame.** "Your denial is exactly what someone like you would say." A claim that treats denial as confirmation can never be wrong, and [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself) already told you what that means.
- **"Toxic."** It describes no act at all, which is why it's so popular. It means whatever the speaker needs it to mean this week.

::: handle
Say it in plain words, or it's only a label.
:::

## The translation exercise

Before you accuse anyone of anything, translate it out of clinical language into plain, observable description: who did what, when, how often, and what it did. Then say the plain version instead.

*"You're gaslighting me"* becomes *"Three times this month I've raised something and you've told me it didn't happen, and I've started writing things down because I don't trust my memory any more."*

The second version is more specific, harder to dismiss, and describes something real rather than asserting a category. It passes the lab-coat test: take the coat off and it still stands up.

Now you try. Cover the answers, write your own plain version of each label, then look.

::: sort
- "He's a narcissist." || "At my birthday he talked about his promotion for forty minutes and didn't ask anyone a single question." Rude, possibly a pattern, and no diagnosis needed.
- "She's love bombing me." || "We met eleven days ago, she's already booked us a holiday for June, and she went cold when I said I wasn't sure."
- "You're being controlling." || "You want a text every day when I leave work, and you were cold all evening when I forgot."
- "That's DARVO." || "When I said you'd read my messages, you called me paranoid and told my sister I was unstable."
- "I need to protect my peace." || "I don't want to talk about money tonight. Can we do Saturday morning?"
- "My boss is toxic." || "He presented my report to the directors as his own, twice this quarter."
- "I'm being triggered." || "When you raise your voice I can't think straight. I need ten minutes, then I'll come back."
- "My mother is emotionally abusive." || "She calls me selfish every time I can't visit, and last month she told my aunt I'd abandoned her." Possibly true, and now it's checkable.
:::

Look at what translation did. "I need to protect my peace" turned into a perfectly reasonable request, and reasonable requests are easier to grant. "That's DARVO" turned into something worse than the label, with a sister in it. Translation doesn't only shrink accusations. Sometimes it shows you have a case.

::: pull
**If it can't be said in ordinary words, you may not have a finding. You may have a label.**
:::

## Is it gaslighting, or just a disagreement?

Usually a disagreement. Gaslighting is a pattern of making you doubt your memory and your sanity over time, often with your records attacked and other people recruited. One argument where you remember things differently is two memories, and memory is unreliable in everyone. Translate it into plain events. If the plain version shows a repeating campaign, the word fits.

## The translation, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

Talia has a message to her ex typed and ready. "You're gaslighting me again, and honestly it's textbook narcissism."

The row is about Christmas. Dominic says he never agreed to have the kids on Christmas Day. She's certain he did.

Before sending, she tries the translation. What, specifically, did he do?

She searches her email. There it is, from October: "Fine, I'll have them on the 25th, you take Boxing Day." So the plain version is: "In October you agreed by email to have them on the 25th. Now you're saying you didn't."

Is that gaslighting? Once, about a date, with the email sitting right there? It might be. It might also be a man who agreed to something in October and forgot by December, which describes most of the adult population.

She deletes the first message and sends the plain one, with a screenshot of his email.

Twenty minutes later: "Ugh. You're right, sorry. I'll sort it with work."

The label would have bought her a three-day fight about whether he's a narcissist. The plain version had Christmas sorted before lunch.
:::

## When they're the right words

They aren't banned. They're precision instruments, and precision instruments have uses.

Use them for **yourself**, to organise what happened: realising that something done to you has a name is often the moment a person stops thinking they imagined it. Use them **with a professional**, where the shorthand does real work. And use them where a pattern has been **established**, with dates and repetition behind it, and the term is simply the accurate description.

The line is roughly this: the vocabulary is for **understanding** and for **describing an established pattern**. It isn't for **winning**, and the difference is usually obvious to you in the moment if you're willing to look.

::: check Before you use the word
☐ Could I say what happened without it, in one or two plain sentences?
☐ Do I have dates, or only a feeling with a name?
☐ Am I using it to understand this, or to end the conversation?
☐ Would I accept the same word, used the same way, about me?
:::

## When the vocabulary is aimed at you

It cuts both ways. A controlling person can learn these words too, and often learns them faster. Your request becomes "controlling." Your hurt becomes "trauma dumping." Your no becomes "abandonment issues." Your notes about what happened become "obsessive." Ask for the plain version once: "What did I do, and when?" If the answer is another label, you've learned something.

If someone uses this chapter to tell you that describing what happened to you is "weaponising therapy-speak," reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). The translation rule is for checking a word. It never asks you to stop saying what was done to you. And if you're afraid of someone, you don't owe them a precise vocabulary before you leave: go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

=== who-benefits-from-your-verdict
title: Who benefits from your verdict
question: My friends and family all have opinions about my relationship. Whose should I trust?
seo: Should you listen to friends about your relationship?
short: Everyone who helps you weigh a relationship has a stake in your conclusion: friends who never liked them, family who want peace, a new partner, even a therapist working only from your account. That doesn't make them wrong. But certainty that rises with every conversation, with no new facts, is applause. Find one person with no stake.
---
Almost nobody weighs alone. You talk to people, and the people you talk to have stakes.

Picture a boxer between rounds. The stool, the water bottle, three people leaning in and shouting. They're on your side. They're also the people who do well if you go back out and keep swinging. Not one of them is going to lean in and say, "To be fair, he's winning on points."

Talking it through is a lot like that. Most of the people in your corner are trying to help, and some of them are the reason you got out. But conclusions take the shape of the rooms they're formed in, and it's worth knowing which room you're standing in.

## Should I listen to my friends about my relationship?

Listen, then ask what each of them gets from the answer. Friends often see what you can't, especially changes in you. They also hear only your side, usually on your worst days, and some never liked the person. Give more weight to what they saw with their own eyes than to what they concluded from your stories.

## The stakeholder map

Here is everyone who usually ends up in your corner, and what each one gains. None of this makes them liars. It tells you which way each one leans.

::: compare Who's in your corner
| Who | What your verdict gives them | What they're good for |
|---|---|---|
| Friends who never liked them | Vindication, which feels lovely. They'll supply confirming evidence generously and ambiguous evidence never. | What they saw directly, especially changes in you. |
| Family who want it kept together | The family, the holidays, their own peace. It comes out as minimising, delivered as wisdom. | A long memory of who you were before. |
| A new partner | A great deal: the last one being a villain. Can be entirely sincere and still the least reliable analyst you have. | Showing you what ordinary feels like. |
| A therapist | Usually nothing in the outcome. But they hear your account only, and every framework finds what it was built to find. | Usually the best room you have. |
| A lawyer | A case. Excellent at their job and structurally uninterested in whether he meant it. | Knowing what will matter on paper. |
| The group chat | A story with a villain, and each message a little stronger than the last. | Turning up when you need people. |
| Anyone who wants your loyalty in a dispute | Your verdict is an asset to them. | Very little, for this question. |
| You | The conclusion that lets you leave, or stay, or keep the house, isn't neutral either. | The only person who was there for all of it. |
:::

## Why am I more sure every time I talk about it?

Often because talking with people who already agree makes everyone more extreme, including you. If your certainty has risen steadily and no new facts have arrived, repetition and agreement are feeding it. Confidence built by applause feels exactly like confidence built by evidence, and only one of them predicts anything.

This has a name. In his review of the research, Cass Sunstein calls it group polarisation: when people who already lean one way discuss something together, the group, and the individuals in it, tend to end up further in that direction than where they started. Nobody has to lie for it to happen. Each person hears a few more reasons for the side they already favoured, and hardly any for the other.

::: pull
Almost nobody in your corner is lying to you. The question is **which conclusion is being socially rewarded in the rooms I'm in?**
:::

## One person with no stake

::: handle
Find one person with no stake.
:::

Ask yourself: *who would be disappointed if I concluded the opposite?* Then find someone who wouldn't be. An old colleague, a cousin who lives abroad, someone at a support service who has never heard of either of you. Tell them the whole thing, including the parts that don't help your case. Especially those.

A neutral listener is one of the most valuable and least available things in this book. You'll know you've found one because they ask questions that annoy you.

## Survivor communities, carefully

They get their own section, because they're often life-saving and occasionally escalating, and both are true at once.

What they do well is almost impossible to get anywhere else: being believed straight away, by people who recognise the specifics without needing them explained, at the moment nobody else will. That's no small thing. It's often what ends the isolation.

What can go wrong is structural, not malicious. Attention flows to the worst stories, which quietly rewards escalation. Doubt reads as betrayal, so the ordinary correcting conversation stops being available.

::: pull
Reconciliation, or a softened conclusion, can cost you the community, which means **the community is now paying you to hold a verdict.**
:::

The test is the one this book keeps returning to. **Can you say an inconvenient thing here and still belong?** If yes, it's a good room and you're lucky to have it. If raising a doubt would cost you your place, you're in a room that will make your certainty rise whatever the truth is.

## Name the stake

Before you look: what does each of these people gain, and how much weight does their view deserve?

::: sort
- Your best friend, who called him "beige" the first night she met him, says she always knew. || Vindication. Ask what she saw herself, not what she always knew.
- Your mum says every couple has rough patches and you shouldn't throw away twelve years. || The family staying intact. A long memory, and a stake in the answer being "it's fine."
- The man you've been seeing for a month says your ex sounds like a psychopath. || The biggest stake in the room. Sincere, maybe. Neutral, no.
- A forum of strangers reads eight paragraphs and says it's textbook abuse. || A room that rewards certainty, reading your side only. Good for recognition, weak for a verdict.
- Your ex's sister says you're exaggerating. || Loyalty to her brother. Exactly as much of a stake as your best friend's, pointing the other way.
- A colleague who has met neither of you asks, "What happened when you said no to him?" || No stake, and the right question. Keep this one.
:::

## When the map has only one name on it

Sometimes you draw the map and the only person in your corner is the person you're trying to weigh. Your friends drifted. Your family "doesn't get him." Every outside view was explained away before you heard it. That isn't a neutral room either. A map whose only stakeholder is also the subject is what [isolation](/playbook/isolation) looks like from the inside, and that's a finding in itself.

The map runs the other way too. If someone keeps telling you that you're the problem, ask what they gain if you believe it. And if everyone agreeing with them was briefed by them first, that's [proxy recruitment](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment), not a consensus.

Disputes bring their own stakeholders. Once there's a court, a workplace investigation or a custody question, your verdict becomes a document, and some people use the system itself as a weapon. The Decoder's pages on [legal abuse](/playbook/legal-abuse) and [administrative escalation](/playbook/administrative-escalation) show how. A lawyer's stake is still a stake. It's one you want on your side.

## The stakeholder map, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

Anouk left Lars in February. By April she's sure he was a narcissist, and she's sure because everyone agrees.

Her best friend never liked him. Her new boyfriend thinks Lars sounds "properly dangerous." A forum she reads every night calls him textbook. Each conversation makes her a little more certain, and nothing new has happened since February.

She draws the map on the back of an envelope. Four names, four stakes. Nobody on it would be pleased if she concluded Lars was selfish and checked out.

So she rings her old manager, Esther, who met Lars once at a leaving do and has no view. Anouk tells her everything, including the parts that don't help: that she stopped trying in the last year too, and that Lars never stopped her seeing anyone.

Esther asks dull questions. "When you said no to him, what happened?" "Were you ever afraid of him?" He sulked. She wasn't afraid. He forgot her birthday twice and talked over her at every dinner.

By the end of the call her verdict has shrunk to "selfish, careless, and done." She still doesn't want him back. Now she has a verdict that will survive the next conversation, and she stops reading the forum.
:::

## The floor

You don't need a neutral listener before you protect yourself. Finding one is for weighing. If you're frightened, skip this chapter and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). And if someone uses this chapter to tell you that everyone who believes you is biased, so you should stop listening to them, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). Having a stake doesn't make a person wrong. It tells you which way they lean.

::: try Try it today
On paper, list everyone you've talked to about this. Next to each name, write what they'd lose if you concluded the opposite of what you think now. Circle anyone who'd lose nothing. If there are no circles, your next step is finding one person to circle.
:::

The map also works the other way round. Sometimes you're the one being weighed, and the verdict going round everyone's corner is about you: [When you're the one accused](ch:when-youre-the-one-accused).

=== when-youre-the-one-accused
title: When you’re the one accused
question: Someone has accused me of being manipulative, toxic or abusive. What do I do now?
seo: Accused of being toxic or abusive? What to do first
short: When you're accused of being manipulative, toxic or abusive, don't defend yourself in the first hour. Say you've heard it, and write down exactly what was said. ---
The text arrives at 11pm. Or the conversation starts in the car, or HR books a meeting called "a quick catch-up." Somebody is saying you did something. Manipulative. Controlling. Gaslighting. Abusive.

Your body answers before you do. Heat in the face, a speech assembling itself, and a list of everything *they've* done, alphabetised and ready to go.

Everything in this chapter is about not sending the speech yet.

Picture a parcel on the doorstep that you didn't order. You sign for it, which only means you received it. You carry it inside. You open it at the table, check each item against the delivery note, keep what's yours and send back what isn't, with a polite note on the return label. What you don't do is rip it open on the step and throw it at the driver.

This plan works whether the accusation is true, false, or, most often, partly both. It's the same plan for all three, which is the point of having one.

::: warn Before the plan
If the accusation involves violence, threats, or someone being afraid of you, this plan isn't enough on its own. Give them space, don't contact them to argue your case, and get help for yourself: [If you are dangerous](/repair/if-you-are-dangerous) in Repair is written for that moment.

If you're afraid of the person accusing you, and the accusation is the latest move in a pattern, your safety comes before any weighing. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

## What should I do if I'm accused of being abusive or toxic?

Don't defend yourself in the first hour. Say you've heard it and will think about it, then write down exactly what was said. Over the next day, weigh it with the tools you'd use on anyone: specific acts, how often, what happened when named. Then own what's true, dispute what isn't once and calmly, and don't counter-accuse.

## The first hour: sign for it

The only job in the first hour is to receive it. Winning, explaining, confessing and cross-examining can all wait.

::: script The first hour
**You:** I hear that you think I've been [their words]. I want to take that seriously, so I'd like time to think about it properly. I'll come back to you by [a specific time].
**If they say:** So you're not denying it?
**You:** I'm not answering yet. I want to answer properly, not defensively.
**If they say:** You always do this. You never take responsibility.
**You:** That might be part of what I need to think about. I'll still come back to you by [time].
:::

Then write down what they said, in their words. Not your summary of their words, which will already be leaning toward the version where they're being unreasonable. If they gave examples, write those down too. You'll need them in the morning, and by morning your memory will have quietly hired a defence lawyer.

Four moves wreck the first hour:

- **Defending.** A point-by-point rebuttal at 11pm. Even if you're right, you've answered before you've heard.
- **Counter-attacking.** "Well, what about when you…" Their complaint disappears under the one you swapped in. Done as a habit, it has a Decoder name: [DARVO](/playbook/darvo), deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender.
- **Collapsing.** "You're right, I'm a monster, I don't know why you stay." It looks like accountability and works like a [non-apology](/playbook/non-apology-apologies), because now they have to comfort you and nobody is looking at what happened.
- **Prosecuting.** "Give me three specific dates or this conversation is over." You'll want specifics later. Demanding them as an entry fee is a way of not listening.

::: handle
Listen before you defend. Weigh before you answer.
:::

## Does getting defensive make me look guilty?

Not to anyone weighing fairly. Flinching is the ordinary reaction to being accused, true or false, and innocent people flinch as hard as guilty ones. What counts is what you do after the flinch: whether you come back to the specific complaint later, or turn every attempt to raise it into a discussion about the person who raised it. The flinch is forgivable. The second one is the tell.

## The first day: open it at the table

Next morning, with a cup of tea and your notes, you weigh it, using exactly the tools you'd use on anyone else. The difference is that this time you're the subject, which makes you the least reliable witness in the room. [Am I the one doing it?](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it) explains why your intentions don't count as evidence here either.

::: check Open it at the table
☐ Translate it. What acts, in plain words, are they describing? If all you have is a label, ask them for the plain version. [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon) has the rule.
☐ Run the four questions. How often, how long, getting worse, and what happened when it was raised before? [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern).
☐ Give it three explanations: they're wrong, it's a misunderstanding, they're right. Then ask what would tell them apart. [The discipline of three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations).

☐ Check the echo. Has anyone else ever said anything like this? A partner, a sibling, a flatmate, a manager?
☐ Check the history. Is this the first time they've said it, or the first time they've said it loudly?
☐ Check the timing. Did it arrive straight after you raised something of theirs?
:::

Then sort the contents into three piles: **true**, **partly true** and **not true**. Almost every honest accusation lands in more than one. "You control the money" might break down like this. True: you check the bank app every day and comment on what she buys. Not true: you've never stopped her buying anything. Both piles are real, and both get an answer.

The timing check cuts both ways. An accusation that lands the day after you named something of theirs might be a counter-move; the Decoder calls that [pre-emptive accusation](/playbook/preemptive-accusation-complaint-weaponization). It might also be someone who finally felt able to say it because you went first. Timing is a reason to look closer. It's never a verdict.

## The first week: write the return note

Now you answer, in three parts, in this order.

**Own what's true, specifically.** The act, its effect, and what changes. "I check the bank app every day and comment on what you've bought. That's controlling, and I'm going to stop." The four features from [Repair, and apology's counterfeit](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit) now apply to you: specific, unconditional, changed, no bill.

**Dispute what isn't, once, with facts, and without touching their character.** "I don't accept that I've stopped you buying things. I don't think that's happened, and I'd want to hear about any time you think it did." Then stop. You don't need them to agree.

**Change the behaviour, and let the weeks argue for you.** You can't talk someone into believing you've changed. Six weeks of the new behaviour is the only argument that works.

And during the week, hold off on three things.

- **Recruiting witnesses.** Ringing mutual friends to get your version in first turns them into a jury. The Decoder files it under [proxy recruitment](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment), and it's no better when you're innocent.
- **Weighing at them.** Do the weighing privately. Never turn this book's tools on the person who accused you to make them doubt what they experienced: "Are you sure that isn't your anxiety talking?" The second chapter is blunt about this. Using calibration to make someone doubt an accusation about you is itself evidence. Reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands) if you feel the pull.
- **Demanding a verdict.** They may not accept your three piles. They may need weeks. You don't get to set their timetable.

## What if I'm falsely accused of manipulation?

Follow the same plan, because it protects you too. Write down what was said, weigh it honestly, and answer once, calmly, with facts. Don't agree just to end it, and don't counter-attack. An accusation on its own isn't evidence. A calm, specific answer, repeated if you need to, usually ages far better than a speech.

Being accused of something you didn't do is a real injury, and it doesn't heal on a schedule either.

If the same person accuses you of something new every time you raise something of theirs, that's a pattern worth weighing in its own right, and [When you both say you're the victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) has the fear test for it. If someone keeps telling you you're the problem, [Who benefits from your verdict](ch:who-benefits-from-your-verdict) asks what they gain if you believe it.

If it's formal, at work, in court or with social services, get proper advice before you write anything for anyone but yourself.

## How do I respond when someone says I'm gaslighting them?

Ask what they mean in plain events, then listen. "Which times? What did I say?" If you remember it differently, say so once, without telling them their memory is broken: "I remember it differently, and I might be wrong." Then check any records that exist. Honest disagreement welcomes the record, and gaslighting attacks it.

[I don't remember it that way](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way) has the full comparison.

## Sign, defend, collapse or attack?

Before you look: which of these is the first hour done right, and which is one of the four mistakes?

::: sort
- "Right, so I'm the villain now. Brilliant. What about when you read my emails?" || Counter-attack. Their complaint just vanished under yours.
- "You're completely right. I'm toxic. I ruin everything. Maybe I should just go." || Collapse. It ends with them comforting you.
- "Name three dates or I'm not discussing it." || Prosecuting. You'll want specifics later. Demanding them now is a way of not listening.
- "Okay. I hear that you feel I check up on you. I want to think about it properly. Can we talk tomorrow at seven?" || Signing for it. The first hour, done right.
- A two-thousand-word message at 1am, with timestamps. || Defending, at length, before any weighing.
- "I've thought about it. Two of those things are true, and I'm stopping. The third I don't recognise, and I'd like to talk about it." || The first week, done right: own, dispute once, change.
:::

## The plan, used twice

Yes, it was [guilt-tripping](/playbook/guilt-tripping), and it was his.

=== being-wrong-well
title: Being wrong well
question: I accused someone of something and I was wrong. How do I put it right?
seo: How to apologise after wrongly accusing someone you love
short: To put right a wrong accusation, name exactly what you accused them of, withdraw it with no "but", skip the big show of guilt, and let them take as long as they need. If you were only partly wrong, say both halves: what really hurt you, and what you inflated. Then be reliable for a while. That's the repair.
---
Calibration that can't reverse is only a slower route to the same verdict.

Picture the corrections box in a newspaper. It's small, it's on page two, and the good ones are brutally plain: "Yesterday's article said the councillor had resigned. She has not. We apologise for the error." There's no paragraph about the ambiguous press release that misled the reporter, and no column on the pressures of the newsroom. The fact, the withdrawal, and back to work.

That's the model for this chapter. It's short, because the work isn't complicated. It's only unpleasant.

## How do I apologise for wrongly accusing someone?

Name the exact accusation, say plainly that it wasn't true, and stop. No "but you can see why I thought it," no explanation of how they misled you, and no big display of guilt. Then let them take whatever time they need, and be reliable while they do. The specific withdrawal is the apology.

## The four moves

::: handle
Name it, withdraw it, don't perform it, accept their timing.
:::

Each move has a version that sounds like an apology and works like a continuing argument. Here they are side by side.

::: compare The four moves, bad and good
| Move | Sounds fine, isn't | The correction |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Name it | "I'm sorry things got so tense between us." | "I said you were deliberately undermining me in that meeting. That wasn't true." |
| 2. Withdraw it | "I was wrong, but you can see how it looked." "In fairness, you did go quiet." | "I was wrong. You weren't doing that." |
| 3. Don't perform it | "I'm a horrible person. I don't deserve you." | "I'm sorry. I got it wrong, and I want to put it right." |
| 4. Accept their timing | "I've apologised. Can we move on now?" | "Take as long as you need. I'm not asking you to be fine." |
:::

### 1. Name it

The specificity is the whole apology. Without it, nothing has actually been withdrawn, and the accusation is still sitting there, slightly blurred. "Sorry about everything" corrects nothing in particular.

### 2. Withdraw it, with no hedge

No "but you can see how it looked." No "in fairness, you did." Every qualifier turns the apology back into a negotiation, and the other person hears the negotiation instead of the apology.

The strongest temptation lives here: explaining how you came to be wrong in a way that makes it their fault for being ambiguous. "If you'd answered your phone, I'd never have thought it." Resist it. You can explain your reasoning later, if they want to hear it, once the withdrawal has been allowed to stand on its own for a while. A correction with a grievance stapled to it is a second accusation.

### 3. Don't perform it

Big displays of self-recrimination make the other person manage your distress, which is one more thing you're asking of them. You accused them of something false, and now they're on the sofa telling you you're not a terrible person. That's the self-flagellation counterfeit from [Repair, and apology's counterfeit](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit), applied to yourself.

### 4. Accept their timing

They don't have to be reassured, say it's fine, or put things back this week. Being accused of something you didn't do is a real injury, and it doesn't heal because the accuser has finished a process.

::: pull
You can't apologise someone back into trusting you. You can only stop being wrong and then be reliable for a while, which is exactly the standard you've been applying to everyone else.
:::

## The script

::: script Withdrawing an accusation
**You:** On Sunday I said you'd lied to me about where you were on Friday. You hadn't. I was wrong, and I'm sorry I said it.
**If they say:** Why would you even think that?
**You:** I'll tell you if you want to know, but I don't want it to come out as an excuse. The short version is that I got it wrong.
**If they say:** You always assume the worst about me.
**You:** That might be true. I'm going to look at it properly, and I'm not going to argue with you about it tonight.
**If they say:** Fine. I don't really want to talk about it.
**You:** That's okay. I'm not asking you to be fine.
:::

## What if I was only partly wrong?

Say both halves. Something real happened, and you built it into something bigger. Keep the true part, withdraw the inflation, and put both in one sentence: "What you did on Thursday hurt me, and I still don't want it to happen again. What I said, that you've been doing it on purpose for months, I don't think is true."

The partial misread is far more common than the total error, and far more tempting to leave alone. They did dismiss you. They were unfair. They did break the promise. And you made a pattern out of an incident, strategy out of thoughtlessness, [contempt](/playbook/contempt) out of exhaustion.

Keeping quiet is tempting, because the core was true and correcting the scale feels like giving ground. But the inflation is exactly what the other person experienced as unjust, and leaving it standing means the real grievance never gets dealt with on its own terms.

Both clauses. The second doesn't cancel the first. That's what precision is for.

## Grade the corrections

Before you look: which move does each one fail, if any?

::: sort
- "I'm sorry for what I said. But you have to admit you've been distant." || Move 2. The "but" started round two.
- "I'm sorry if anything I said upset you." || Move 1. It names nothing, and the "if" puts the doubt on their feelings. The Decoder calls it a [non-apology](/playbook/non-apology-apologies).
- Crying in the hallway: "I'm disgusting. I don't know how you can look at me." || Move 3. They're now consoling the person who accused them.
- "I said sorry three days ago. How long are you going to punish me for this?" || Move 4. The apology has turned into a bill.
- "I said you'd taken the money from the jar. You hadn't. I'm sorry." || None. Specific, withdrawn, no show, no deadline.
- "I said you were ignoring me on purpose. You weren't. If you'd only replied, though, I'd never have thought it." || Move 2. The grievance about being misled, stapled to the end.
:::

## Does being wrong once mean I can't trust my judgement?

No. One wrong call tells you about one call. Judgement is measured across many calls, which is why [Scoring your own past calls](ch:scoring-your-own-past-calls) has you write each one down before you learn the answer. Getting something wrong and correcting it cleanly is what good judgement looks like in practice. Second-guessing everything afterwards just swaps one error for the other.

## Being wrong well, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

At their dad's seventieth, in the car park, Dagny tells her brother Ruairi she knows he told their mum about her divorce before she was ready. "You couldn't wait, could you? You love being first with news."

Two weeks later her aunt lets slip that it was Dagny's ex's sister who told Mum, at the hairdresser's.

Dagny's first draft reads: "Turns out it wasn't you, sorry! In fairness, you do tell Mum everything." She looks at it for a long time and deletes the second sentence.

What she sends: "At Dad's party I said you'd told Mum about my divorce, and that you love being first with news. You didn't tell her, and the second part was unfair too. I'm sorry."

Ruairi replies the next day with a thumbs-up. Nothing else.

It stings. She wanted "no worries." She doesn't send a follow-up asking whether they're okay.

Three weeks later he rings about Christmas, a bit stiff at first, and by the end of the call he's laughing at her impression of their aunt.
:::

## When someone wants you to keep withdrawing

Being wrong well covers what you actually got wrong. It never means retracting something true because someone insists, or apologising again every time they bring it up. If you've withdrawn what was false and you're being pushed to withdraw what was accurate as well, that's the floor: reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). If you're always the one correcting and they never are, that's worth weighing in its own right. And if you're frightened of them, no correction is owed before you're safe: go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).

If you told other people the accusation, the correction goes to them too, briefly. [Changing your mind out loud](ch:changing-your-mind-out-loud) has the words for that.

## Why this chapter is the book's credibility

A book that demands evidence and never shows what losing looks like is asking for something it won't model. Being wrong well is the working condition of anyone who judges under uncertainty, which is everyone, permanently. It's what being right about people costs.
