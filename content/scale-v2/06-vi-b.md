=== weighing-an-expert
title: Weighing an expert
question: A therapist, a couples counsellor, a court report or someone online has given an opinion about us. How much should it count?
seo: Couples counselling with a controlling partner? Read first
short: Give a professional opinion the weight of what the professional actually saw. A therapist who has met only one of you, a counsellor seeing you both while one of you is afraid, a stranger diagnosing online, a report written after two interviews: each saw a slice. Ask what they saw, what they're trained to spot and whose account they heard.
---
A qualification is a better instrument. It isn't a pair of eyes in your kitchen.

Picture two mechanics. The first hands back your keys and says, "She's fine." The second hands you a job sheet: brakes checked, tyres checked, oil changed, exhaust not inspected because the ramp was broken. The second mechanic is the professional. She's told you what her opinion covers and where it stops.

Most expert opinions about people reach you like the first mechanic's. "My therapist says you're the problem." "The counsellor thinks we're both to blame." "This video says he's a covert narcissist." The gap between those and a job sheet is where readers get hurt.

## Can a therapist tell if someone is a narcissist?

A therapist can assess someone they've worked with, over time, for the things they're trained to spot. They can't assess a person they've never met, and they only see a client as that client presents. A diagnosis also wouldn't answer your real question, which is what this person does to you. Behaviour answers that.

::: handle
Credentials aren't a verdict. Ask what they saw.
:::

## The job sheet

Before any professional opinion gets the final word, run it through six questions. They work on a psychiatrist, a couples counsellor, a social worker, a life coach on Instagram, and the chatbot you pasted his texts into at 1am.

::: check The second-opinion checklist
☐ **What did they see?** Who was in the room, for how long, telling whose story?
☐ **What are they trained to spot?** Coercive control is a specialism.
☐ **Who is their client?** Your partner's therapist works for your partner.
☐ **What didn't they have?** The messages, the statements, you on your own.
☐ **Did it reach you directly?** Or as someone's summary of a room you weren't in?
☐ **Did they say where it stops?** Good professionals do, unasked.
:::

A court reporter works for the child, and a chatbot works for whoever is typing. If an opinion passes all six, give it real weight. Trained eyes catch things yours miss, and a good professional has seen hundreds of relationships shaped like yours. If it fails three, you're holding a mechanic's "she's fine."

## Four places it goes wrong

### The therapist who met one of you

Individual therapy is one of the best things a person can do, which is exactly why it gets quoted. A therapist hears their client's account, in their client's words, for fifty minutes a week. Their job is to help that person. Most won't hand down findings about someone who has never been in the room.

So when it lands at your end of the sofa as "my therapist agrees you're controlling," three things have happened. A professional heard a story. Your partner summarised what was said back. And the summary arrived mid-argument, which is where summaries go to get edited. That's [a secondhand account](ch:secondhand-accounts) with a certificate stapled to it.

Therapy vocabulary swung as a club has a Decoder page: [weaponised therapy-speak](/playbook/weaponized-therapy-speak). It works in both directions. Your own therapist has heard your version. Quoting them at your partner is the same move with the arrows reversed.

### Couples counselling when one of you is afraid

Couples counselling assumes two people who can both speak freely in the room and both go home safely afterwards. Where there's control, that assumption fails, and the counselling room becomes a place where honesty gets collected for later.

That's why many domestic abuse services advise against it. The US National Domestic Violence Hotline says it doesn't encourage anyone in an abusive relationship to go to counselling with their partner.

Its reasons: counselling can't fix the unequal power in an abusive relationship, what you say in the room can be used against you later, and the counsellor may not recognise the abuse and may side with the partner who presents better.

Battered Women's Support Services in Canada puts it more bluntly. Abuse is a pattern of control, it says, and communication and compromise don't resolve it. In an abusive relationship, couples therapy can be dangerous as well as ineffective.

One question settles most of it. *Would I say the true thing in that room, knowing who drives me home?* If the answer is no, the counsellor is working from an edited script, and their opinion is an opinion about the edit.

A careful couples counsellor sees each partner alone before working with both, and asks directly about fear. If yours never saw either of you on your own, the job sheet has a large blank on it. If you're afraid, start with help for you alone: an individual counsellor who knows this area, or the domestic abuse service where you live. [When you both say victim](ch:when-you-both-say-victim) has the fear test.

### The diagnosis from a distance

The internet will diagnose your partner for free, in under a minute, with background music. A woman in a ring light lists eleven signs of a covert narcissist. He has nine. So does your postman. A chatbot reads three of his texts, pasted in with your commentary, and finds "a pattern consistent with narcissistic abuse." It has never met him. It has never met anyone.

Psychiatry has a rule about the famous version of this. In 1973 the American Psychiatric Association wrote into its ethics code that psychiatrists shouldn't give a professional opinion about a public figure they haven't examined. It's called the Goldwater Rule, and psychiatrists still argue about where its edges are. The principle underneath is the job sheet. No examination, no finding.

If a psychiatrist isn't supposed to diagnose a senator from a press conference, a friend can't diagnose your boyfriend from a voice note. The labels themselves are covered in [The vocabulary as a weapon](ch:the-vocabulary-as-a-weapon), and a label thrown as a verdict has its own Decoder page: [trash-can diagnosis](/playbook/accountability-evasion-via-diagnosis-trash-can-diagnosis).

The good news is that you never needed the diagnosis. What he does, how often, and what happens when you say no will carry every decision you have to make. [Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision) explains why you need even less than that to leave.

### The court report

Family court reports, welfare reports, custody evaluations: the names change by country. A professional meets each parent, maybe the children, reads what they're given, and recommends. Many are skilled and badly overstretched. They see what's in front of them across a few interviews. A calm, charming parent interviews well. So does a frightened one who has learned to play everything down.

Courts have missed abuse. In 2020 a panel commissioned by the Ministry of Justice in England and Wales, with Rosemary Hunter, Mandy Burton and Liz Trinder as its lead authors, gathered more than 1,200 responses from people and organisations.

The evidence it received described domestic abuse being systematically minimised in children's cases, and courts putting undue priority on contact with the other parent. Reforms followed. Practice still varies by court, and by person.

If a report gets you wrong, the job sheet still applies, and so does the top rung of [What is evidence](ch:what-is-evidence): records beat adjectives. Answer it through a lawyer, with dates and documents, rather than with outrage at the hearing. [Family court, honestly](/longafter/family-court-honestly) in The Long After covers what helps.

## What a good expert sounds like

Careful language is the sign of competence here. [Certainty is the tell](ch:certainty-is-the-tell) explains why the confident version should worry you more.

::: compare The mechanic's "she's fine", and the job sheet
| Sounds sure, covers less | Sounds careful, covers more |
|---|---|
| "He's clearly a narcissist." | "From what you've told me, I'm worried about how often you feel afraid." |
| "You're both as bad as each other." | "I'd like to see each of you on your own first." |
| "Your anxious attachment is the real issue." | "Your anxiety is real. So is what you're describing. Let's look at both." |
| "The children are fine." | "In the hour I spent with them, they seemed settled. I didn't see a handover." |
| "You need to forgive and move forward." | "What would you need in order to feel safe?" |
:::

## Run the job sheet

Before you look: which question from the checklist does each one fail?

::: sort
- "My therapist says you're the one with the anger problem." || Did it reach you directly? It's your partner's summary of a room you weren't in.
- A couples counsellor who has only seen you together, while you're afraid to speak. || What did they see? An edited script.
- A therapist with two million followers says your husband's silences are "textbook covert narcissism." || What did they see? Nothing. They've never met him.
- A chatbot reads your summary of the argument and agrees with you. || What didn't they have? Everything except your account, typed at your angriest.
- A court reporter met each parent for an hour and never saw the messages. || What didn't they have? The record. Get it to them through your lawyer.
- Your own counsellor, after a year: "I've noticed you describe being frightened when he drinks." || Passes. She saw you over time, and she's describing what you've said rather than diagnosing him.
:::

## The job sheet, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

Pilar's husband, Rohan, books couples counselling. "Let a professional decide who's being unreasonable."

Three sessions in, the counsellor, who is warm and clearly good at her job, keeps coming back to money. Rohan budgets, Pilar resents it, they both need to meet in the middle. Rohan nods a lot.

What the counsellor hasn't heard is that Rohan checks Pilar's receipts every night, and holds her bank card. In session one Pilar said the word "receipts." In the car afterwards Rohan didn't speak to her for two days.

At home he says, "Even the counsellor thinks you're inflexible about money."

Pilar runs the job sheet on a sticky note in the bathroom. What did she see? Two people, one of them editing. What's she trained in? Pilar asks at the next session. Relationship work, mostly. Who's her client? The couple. What didn't she have? The receipts, the card, the two days.

Pilar contacts the domestic abuse service where she lives, from a work computer. They help her find a counsellor who knows coercive control, and she books a session on her own.

The new counsellor's first question is, "Is there anything you can't say in front of him?"

"Most things," Pilar says.
:::

=== helping-someone-else-weigh
title: Helping someone else weigh
question: My friend thinks they might be in a manipulative relationship. What do I say?
seo: How to help a friend in a toxic relationship: what to say
short: To help a friend weigh a relationship, ask questions instead of handing over a verdict. "What happened, exactly?" and "What do you want?" do more than "Leave him." Stay steady, keep your outrage out of the room, and keep the door open whatever they decide. If there's violence, a threat or fear, stop weighing and help them get safe.
---
You will be handed other people's situations. Once it's known that you've read a book like this, they arrive constantly. A friend at the kitchen table. A sister on the phone at eleven at night. A colleague who opens with "Can I ask you something odd?"

The instinct is to supply the verdict. You've read fifty-odd chapters, you have opinions, and they've just described a man who checks the mileage on her car. Resist it, for reasons that are practical rather than polite.

Picture your friend standing on a chair with their head through a loft hatch, in the dark. You're below, holding the torch. Your job is to put the light where they point and keep it steady. Your job isn't to shout what's up there before they've had a look.

::: handle
Steady beats fierce.
:::

## What do I say to a friend in a toxic relationship?

Ask before you tell. "What happened, exactly?" "How often?" "What happened when you told them how it landed?" "What do you want?" Then listen without adding adjectives. Say you're worried if you are, and that you're staying either way. Your friend needs a clearer view of their own situation, and a verdict from you blocks it.

## Why your verdict fails

**It ends the conversation.** The moment you say "that's abuse" or "he sounds fine to me," your friend stops thinking and starts reacting to your sentence. Whatever they were about to work out is gone.

**It's your judgement on their evidence.** You have one side, compressed, from someone inside it. That's the weakest position anyone can judge from in this whole book, and people confidently issue findings from it every day, usually over a second glass of wine.

**It transfers the conclusion.** If they leave because you said so, the decision belongs to you, and it won't hold. A verdict borrowed from a friend collapses the first time the other person is charming again.

**It costs them the way back to you.** If you told them to leave and they stayed, they now can't tell you what happened next. People vanish from their support at exactly the moment they most need it, because they can't face the friend who was told and ignored.

## Two ways to drop the torch

### The escalator

The escalator swings the torch round the loft yelling "Rat! That's a rat! It's definitely a rat!" Being the most outraged person in the room feels like loyalty. It's usually the least useful thing on offer.

Escalation makes the situation harder to describe honestly. Your friend starts editing what they tell you so as not to set you off, which means you get a worse account every time. It makes going back shameful, so if they stay, you lose them. And it puts your feelings into a room that already had plenty.

It's also how a friend who knows the partner from one wedding ends up casting him as a villain in a film only they've seen.

### The minimiser

The minimiser switches the torch off. "There's nothing up there. Every couple argues. He's lovely, though. Are you sure you're not reading into it?"

People minimise for understandable reasons. They like the partner. The couple is a fixture at every birthday. The truth would be inconvenient. Sometimes they're right, and nothing is up there.

But a minimiser teaches your friend that bringing it to you costs more than keeping quiet, so they stop bringing it. And if something is up there, you've just joined the chorus telling them their reaction is too big. The Decoder calls that [minimization](/playbook/minimization).

::: pull
Steady is more useful than fierce, and more useful than soothing. *"That sounds hard. Tell me more about what happened"* holds far more weight than *"That's disgusting, you have to leave,"* or *"Oh, he doesn't mean it."*
:::

## The questions that hold the light

::: script Questions for the kitchen table
**You:** What happened, exactly?
**If they say:** He was just being horrible, as usual.
**You:** What did he actually say? Word for word, if you can.
**You:** How often does that happen?
**You:** What happened when you told him how it landed?
**You:** What's it like when it's good?
**You:** What would you need in order to have that conversation with him?
**You:** What do you want?
:::

What each one does:

- **"What happened, exactly?"** Then stay there. Most of the work is getting from interpretation to events. "Horrible" is a verdict. "He told my sister I was unstable" is an event.
- **"How often?"** The [pattern questions](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern), asked gently.
- **"What happened when you told him?"** [The naming test](ch:the-naming-test), without anyone having to call it that.
- **"What's it like when it's good?"** Almost nobody asks this. The answer tells you a great deal, and it tells your friend you aren't running a prosecution.
- **"What would you need?"** It moves toward action without prescribing one.
- **"What do you want?"** Often the first time anyone has asked.

## When they ask for the verdict

Sooner or later they'll ask you straight. You can be honest without taking the decision off them.

::: script When they want you to decide
**If they say:** Do you think he's abusive?
**You:** I wasn't there, so I can't tell you what he is. I can tell you that what you've described worries me, and that I'm not going anywhere.
**If they say:** Should I leave?
**You:** That's yours to decide, and I'll back you either way. What would you need in order to be able to, if you wanted to?
**If they say:** You think I'm stupid for staying.
**You:** No. I think it's complicated, and I'd rather you told me the truth than told me what you think I want to hear.
:::

[Assessment is not decision](ch:assessment-is-not-decision) is worth handing over at this point. It's the chapter that tells someone they don't need a verdict to act, and don't need to act to have a view.

## Should I tell my friend to leave?

Usually not in those words. Telling someone to leave tends to end the conversation, and the decision only holds if it's theirs. Say what worries you, ask what they want, and stay. The exception is danger: if there's violence, a threat or fear, say plainly that you're frightened for them and help them get safe.

## If they go back

They might. Many people leave and return at least once before they leave for good. Keep the door open anyway, and don't make them earn their way back through it. The friend who can still be called is often the reason the next attempt works. [If you go back](/longafter/if-you-go-back) in The Long After is written for them, without judgment, if they want it.

The same rules hold if the "relationship" is a group: a church, a team, a coach with a following. Don't argue doctrine. Keep the relationship warm and ask questions they can answer privately. [Weighing a group](ch:weighing-a-group) has the one that matters most.

## Steady, fierce or switched off

Before you look: is each one holding the torch, swinging it, or switching it off?

::: sort
- "He sounds like a total narcissist. Block him tonight." || Swinging it. A diagnosis from a stranger and an instruction, in one breath.
- "Every couple has their stuff. You two are so good together." || Switched off. Now they know not to bring it up again.
- "What did he say, exactly?" || Holding it. Events, not adjectives.
- "I always knew there was something off about him." || Swinging it, backwards in time. A verdict nobody asked for, about the past.
- "What do you want to happen?" || Holding it.
- "Whatever you decide, I'm still here." || Holding it. The most useful sentence in this chapter.
:::

## The torch, held

::: scene
*LABEL*

Saskia mentions it over noodles, as if it were nothing. Anders "does the budget," so she texts him a photo of every receipt.

Esperanza has disliked Anders since he explained her own job to her at a wedding. The speech is ready in her mouth. She swallows it.

"What happens if you don't send one?"

"He goes quiet. Last time it was four days."

"How often does that happen?"

Saskia thinks. "Since the spring. Maybe five times."

"What happened when you told him it bothered you?"

A long pause. "I haven't. I don't think I'm allowed to." Saskia hears herself say it and puts her chopsticks down.

Esperanza doesn't say "that's control." She says, "That sounds hard. I'm not going anywhere."

Three weeks later Saskia texts. She has opened a bank account in her own name, and she'd like to keep the card at Esperanza's flat for now. Esperanza says yes, and asks nothing else.
:::

## When the person asking for help is the one doing it

Sometimes the request comes from the other side. Your friend's husband takes you aside: she's been "paranoid lately," could you help her see sense? Your sister's partner asks you to have a word, because she listens to you.

That's a request to carry their verdict to her, dressed as concern. The Decoder calls it [flying monkeys](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment). You can decline politely, and you can tell her, privately, that you were asked. If someone hands you this chapter to recruit you as a calm voice for their side, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).

## The exception, without ambiguity

::: warn When to stop calibrating
If what they're describing includes violence or a threat, a child at risk, being cut off from people who could help, being prevented from leaving, or fear that is physical rather than social, **you stop being a calibration partner and become a person who helps them get safe.**

That isn't the moment for three explanations. Say plainly that you're worried, that this isn't normal, and that you'll help. Know where to find the domestic abuse service where they live: [Find help](/longafter/find-help) shows where to look, and many services will also talk to a worried friend. Threats meant to frighten them have a Decoder page, [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation), but they don't need the name to get help. [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops) has the rest.

Then keep the door open, whatever they do next.
:::

:::

=== weighing-after-youve-left
title: Weighing after you’ve left
question: I left a controlling relationship and now I see red flags everywhere. How do I trust my own judgement again?
seo: Seeing red flags everywhere after a toxic relationship
short: After a controlling relationship your alarm is still set for the room you left, so it fires at almost everything. Don't switch it off, and don't obey every ring. Log what sets it off, name the specifics, check which room you're in and test small, over weeks. Keep it at full volume for the ex. Turn it down, never off.
---
You got out. Your smoke alarm came with you, still set for the old kitchen.

[The first chapter](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh) used the smoke alarm and the toast. After a controlling relationship, that alarm is wired to a hair trigger. A colleague's "Quick question?" is a house fire. A date who orders for the table is a house fire with sirens. Your new neighbour waves and you spend the evening wondering what she wants.

There are two quick fixes, and both are bad. You can pull the battery: decide you're healed, done with suspicion, and walk into the next charming person's kitchen with no alarm at all. Or you can obey every ring: stop dating, quit the job, move out of every kitchen in the world. This chapter is the third option. You turn the alarm down, you test it, and you keep it.

::: handle
Turn it down, never off.
:::

## Is it normal to see red flags everywhere after a toxic relationship?

Yes. Your alarm was set in a room where suspicion was correct, and it's still firing at that room's rate. That's an honest reading from the wrong room, and it can be reset. The aim is an alarm that rings at smoke and stays quiet for toast, which takes practice and time.

Why it was set that high is covered elsewhere. [Most people, most days](ch:most-people-most-days) explains the two rooms, and [Your detector has a history](ch:your-detector-has-a-history) digs up whose voice the alarm is using. The body's side of it, the startle and the scanning, is in [What your nervous system is doing](/longafter/what-your-nervous-system-is-doing) in The Long After.

## The recalibration plan

Twelve weeks, four stages. Treat the weeks as a practice schedule, never a recovery deadline. Some people need a month for each stage, and a bad day can send you back a stage without undoing anything.

::: compare Twelve weeks, four stages
| Weeks | What you do | What you're finding out |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Log every alarm. Change nothing. | How often it rings, and at what. |
| 3 to 4 | Name the smoke for each entry, and the room. | Which alarms are resemblance and which are behaviour. |
| 5 to 8 | Extend small trusts. Watch the first no. | How new people actually respond. |
| 9 to 12 | Read the log back. Check the real alarms still ring. | Whether you turned it down without switching it off. |
:::

### Weeks 1 to 2: log it

Every time the alarm goes off, write one line: the time, what set it off, what you did. No verdicts. You're counting rings, not investigating fires.

Expect the log to be long, and a bit embarrassing. *Tuesday, 9.14. Barista asked if I wanted "the usual." Alarm: he's been watching me.* That's fine. Nobody reads it but you, and the barista would be flattered to learn he's a person of interest.

Some entries will be real. Leave them in. The log is for counting, and sorting comes next.

### Weeks 3 to 4: name the smoke

Go back through each entry and ask three questions.

- **What exactly set it off?** A specific act, or a resemblance: the same aftershave, the same phrase, the same jaw? [The gut: sensor or smoke alarm](ch:the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm) has the test.
- **Which room am I in?** A new manager asking where you were at ten is the wide room's ordinary behaviour. Your ex asking was the local room's.
- **Whose voice is this?** If the alarm sounds exactly like the old relationship, mark it.

Most entries turn out to be resemblance, which is cheap. Behaviour is what costs. Mark the few that are behaviour, and keep watching them.

### Weeks 5 to 8: small trusts, small tests

Now extend things you can afford to lose. Lend a book. Accept a lift. Tell a new friend something small and true. Say no to a minor plan and watch what it costs. The price of a small no is the best single reading you can take of a new person, and [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) has the timeline for dating.

Write the results in the log. "Said I couldn't do Saturday. She said 'next time' and meant it." Those lines matter more than the alarms, because they're the alarm being told it was wrong, with evidence.

### Weeks 9 to 12: test the alarm

Read the whole log in one sitting. Look for two things.

First, the ratio. How many alarms were resemblance, and how many were behaviour? Most people find the first group shrinking, slowly, as the weeks go on. Some don't, and that's information too: it may be time for help, which is what [Finding a therapist who understands this](/longafter/finding-a-therapist-who-understands-this) is for.

Second, and this is the point: did the real alarms still ring? If someone in those twelve weeks made a cruel joke, pushed past a no, or got cold when you saw your friends, and you noticed, your alarm works. You turned the volume down and kept the circuit live, which is the whole job.

## The room where it stays on full

Turning the alarm down is for the world. For the person you left, keep it exactly where it is.

Contact from an ex after leaving often arrives sweet: an apology, a memory, a crisis only you can help with. The Decoder files that under [hoovering](/playbook/hoovering-relief-bait). It also arrives through other people. His mother is worried about him. Her best friend thinks you should talk. That's [flying monkeys](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment), and the alarm should ring at it whatever the messenger's tone.

If you share children, a home, a business or a court case, the contact may not stop for years. [What is post-separation abuse?](/longafter/what-is-post-separation-abuse) in The Long After covers what keeps going after the relationship ends.

## How do I stop seeing red flags everywhere after a toxic relationship?

Sort them instead of trying to stop them. Write each one down, name exactly what set it off, and ask whether it's a resemblance or a behaviour. Then extend small trusts and record how people respond. Over weeks, the resemblance alarms quieten while the real ones keep ringing. That's recalibration.

## The other mistake: pulling the battery

The year after leaving is when the alarm is loudest, and also when people most want it gone. Someone very charming arrives, and the relief of finally not suspecting anyone feels like healing. A coach or a forum arrives with an explanation for everything, and the relief of finally understanding feels like clarity. [Certainty is the tell](ch:certainty-is-the-tell) covers why that moment is when you're easiest to recruit.

Being "done with all that" is the battery out of the alarm. Pace in a new relationship is still information, and a big early rush still deserves a look. The Decoder's page on [love bombing](/playbook/love-bombing) describes the version to watch for.

## Resemblance, behaviour, or the ex's room

Before you look: where does each alarm belong?

::: sort
- "He wears the same aftershave as my ex." || Resemblance. A smell isn't a behaviour.
- "My new manager asked where I was at ten this morning." || Probably the wide room. Managers ask that. Check whether she asks everyone.
- "My date got annoyed when I said I'd drive myself." || Behaviour. Log it, and watch the next no closely.
- "A friend didn't reply for two days." || Resemblance, until it's a pattern. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) has the four blanks.
- "My ex's sister messaged: he's struggling and one call would help." || The ex's room. Full volume, whoever is carrying the message.
- "Someone at the pottery class was kind to me and I felt sick." || Your history talking. Note it. Kindness isn't evidence either way.
:::

## The plan, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

Fourteen months after leaving Ciaran, Yasmin is three dates into seeing Leon. On the third, he says, "You should wear green more. It really suits you."

Her stomach drops. Ciaran used to lay her clothes out on the bed.

She logs it on the bus home. *Leon, clothes comment. Alarm: big.* Then she names the smoke. One compliment, about a colour. No instruction, no sulk attached. Which room? People say "that suits you" all the time. Whose voice? Ciaran's, completely.

She runs a small test. On the fourth date she wears black, and when he mentions green again she says, "I liked hearing it, but black's my colour."

"Fair enough," Leon says. "You'd know." He doesn't mention clothes again.

That same week, Ciaran's mother messages. "He's not sleeping. One call from you would help him so much."

Yasmin doesn't turn that alarm down. She screenshots it, doesn't reply, and adds a line to her log.
:::

::: warn When the alarm is right
If your ex is still threatening you, following you, turning up, or contacting you after you've asked them to stop, you don't have a calibration problem. Keep the alarm at full volume and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). [A safety plan for after you've left](/longafter/a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) covers your home, your routines and your phone.

If anyone tells you your fear of your ex is "just trauma" and you should turn it down for him, or a new partner answers a real concern with "That's your ex talking," reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). Recalibration is something you do to your own alarm, by choice. Nobody else gets to adjust it.
:::

If you're thinking about dating again, [Dating when you're ready, and when you're not](/longafter/dating-when-youre-ready-and-when-youre-not) in The Long After deals with the two pressures, rushing to prove you're fine and waiting for a perfect "ready", and the early signs worth knowing.

=== when-weighing-stops
title: When weighing stops
question: When should I stop trying to work out what's happening and just get safe?
seo: When to stop analysing and get safe from domestic abuse
short: Stop weighing and get safe if there has been violence or a threat of it, a child is at risk, you're being cut off from people who could help, leaving is being made difficult or dangerous, or your fear is physical. You don't need proof. In danger, call your local emergency number. Otherwise, contact the domestic abuse service where you live.
---
Everything in this book assumes you have room to think. This chapter is about the conditions where you don't, and where thinking is the wrong activity.

It appeared as rule three in [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). It appears again here, at full strength, because you now have a book's worth of tools and might otherwise use them somewhere they don't belong.

::: warn danger Weighing stops entirely here
- **There has been violence, or a threat of it.** Including toward property, pets, children, or themselves, used as leverage.
- **A child is at risk.** Any child, including one you're not related to. This is the one condition where you act on suspicion rather than evidence, and where being wrong is an acceptable cost.
- **You are being cut off from people who could help.** Actively, deliberately, over time.
- **Leaving is being made difficult, dangerous or impossible.** Money, documents, immigration status, transport, threats about the children, threats about what would happen if you left.
- **Your fear is physical rather than analytical.** Not "this is a bad relationship," but your body doing what it does when a door opens.
:::

If any line is true, you don't need to read further before acting. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number. [If you're not safe right now](/longafter/if-youre-not-safe-right-now) in The Long After lists emergency numbers and helplines for several countries.

::: handle
In danger, act first. Understand later.
:::

## What should I do if I'm scared of my partner?

Treat the fear as enough. Get somewhere safe, tell one person outside the relationship, and contact the domestic abuse service where you live. If you're in danger now, call your local emergency number. You don't need to prove anything, name what's happening, or decide anything about the relationship before you reach out.

## What to do instead of weighing

Three things, in order.

**Distance first.** Physical separation, even for a night. Everything else is easier from somewhere else, and nothing is easier from inside.

**One person outside who knows.** Not for advice. For the fact of someone knowing. [Isolation](/playbook/isolation) is the mechanism, and one person who knows breaks it. Tell them plainly, including the parts that sound exaggerated.

**People whose job this is.** Domestic abuse services, child protection, legal aid, and their equivalents wherever you are. They exist in nearly every country. Most are free, they have heard this before, and they won't be shocked. They also know things about money, housing and legal options that no book can tell you, because those answers are local.

- To find the domestic abuse service where you live, start at [Find help](/longafter/find-help). You don't need to be sure it's abuse to contact them.
- If talking out loud isn't safe, ask whether they offer text or online chat.
- If someone checks your phone or computer, use one they can't see: a friend's, a work one, a library's. [Surveillance and technology](/longafter/surveillance-and-technology) in The Long After explains how to check your own devices.
- If a child is at risk, contact the police or the child protection service where you live.
- If you're planning to leave, or have left, [A safety plan for after you've left](/longafter/a-safety-plan-for-after-youve-left) covers your home, your routines, your phone and who to tell.

Leaving can itself be a dangerous time. In a large US study of women killed by a partner, led by Jacquelyn Campbell, separation from a controlling partner was linked to higher risk. That is why services help people plan the leaving itself. The signs services treat as most serious, such as strangling, threats to kill and access to weapons, are listed in [Warning signs to take seriously](/longafter/warning-signs-to-take-seriously).

If you can keep a record safely, keep it: messages, dates, photographs, stored somewhere the other person can't reach. If keeping it isn't safe, don't. Your safety comes before your evidence. [What to document and how](/longafter/what-to-document-and-how) covers the method.

## Is it abuse if they've never hit me?

It can be. Threats, [intimidation](/playbook/intimidation), cutting you off from people, controlling money or documents, and making you afraid are all on this chapter's list without any physical violence. You don't need a bruise, or a word for it, to contact a domestic abuse service. [Conflict or control](ch:conflict-or-control) explains the difference from ordinary fighting.

Threats to report you to the authorities over something small, or to use your visa, your job or the courts against you if you leave, belong here too. The Decoder covers them as [administrative escalation](/playbook/administrative-escalation) and [legal and administrative threats](/playbook/legal-administrative-threats).

## Why the tools have to stop, not slow down

Every tool in this book slows you down, and slowness is exactly the wrong property in these conditions.

Three explanations, patterns over months, waiting for a second instance, the honest admission that you can't read intent: each one is right in peacetime, and each one costs time you may not have. A method that produces good judgement at the price of a delay is a bad trade when the delay is the danger.

That includes the tests. [Before you test: is testing safe?](ch:before-you-test-is-testing-safe) is the gate. If any line of this chapter is true, no test in this book applies to you.

::: pull
Acting on an incomplete read is not a failure of discernment. In these conditions **it is discernment**, correctly applied to a situation where the cost of waiting is the only thing that matters.
:::

## Weighing stops, in practice

::: scene
*LABEL*

For a year, Dorota has kept a notebook about Magnus's rages. Each one gets three explanations: he's exhausted, he's worried about money, she pushed him. She's been waiting for the pattern to become clear.

On a Thursday night he punches the wall beside her head. Then he says, quietly, "Next time it won't be the wall."

She reaches for the notebook out of habit, and stops. There has been violence, and a threat of more. There is nothing left to weigh.

On Friday, after he leaves for work, she takes the children and their passports to her mother's flat across town. From her mother's phone she contacts the domestic abuse service where she lives. The adviser doesn't ask her to prove anything. They talk about a safety plan, about her job, and about getting legal advice.

The notebook is still in the kitchen drawer. She hasn't needed it.
:::

## The sentence to keep

People in these situations often say that what kept them in place wasn't only the danger. It was the standard they held themselves to: the sense that they weren't allowed to act until they could prove it, justify it, or explain it to someone else's satisfaction.

So here is the sentence, one more time. It outranks everything else in this book.

::: pull
**You do not owe anyone certainty before you protect yourself.** Not them, not your family, not a court, not a friend, and not this book.
:::

Understanding can wait. It will still be there afterwards, and it's much easier from somewhere safe.

=== changing-your-mind-out-loud
title: Changing your mind out loud
question: How do I tell people I was wrong about someone, or tell a friend they were right?
seo: How to admit you were wrong about someone: what to say
short: To change your mind out loud, tell the people who heard your first verdict, briefly: what you said, what you think now, and that you don't need a reply. Telling a friend "you were right about him" works the same way. Keep it short, skip the backstory and the self-punishment, and never retract something true because someone pushes you to.
---
A verdict about a person travels. It goes to your sister, your group chat, your mum, the friend who tells everyone everything. When your view changes, the verdict keeps travelling without you, because nobody announced the update.

Picture a station announcement. "Passengers for the 17.42 to Bristol: this train will now depart from platform nine." The old information, the new information, what to do about it. Nobody explains the signalling fault, apologises for the history of the railway, or asks the passengers to say it's all right. Then the tannoy goes quiet, and everyone moves to platform nine.

::: handle
Update in public, briefly.
:::

## How do I tell people I was wrong about someone?

Tell the same people who heard the first verdict, in roughly the same way you told them. Say what you said, say what you think now, and stop. Skip the long explanation of how you got it wrong and the request for reassurance. If the person you misjudged doesn't know yet, tell them first.

## Why bother

Because the verdict is still out there doing its job. You told nine people your brother's new wife was cold and calculating. You've since learned she's shy, and works nights. The nine people have not been informed. Two of them have told others. Your old opinion is on tour, and it's selling out venues you've never heard of.

[Being wrong well](ch:being-wrong-well) covers the apology to the person you misjudged. This chapter is for everyone else who heard it, and for the harder announcement nobody practises: telling someone they were right.

## The four announcements

### "I was wrong about them"

The most common one. Same volume as the original: a verdict delivered in the group chat gets corrected in the group chat, and a verdict told to your dad over Sunday lunch gets corrected to your dad.

::: script To the people who heard it
**You:** A while ago I told you Ana was cold and only with Gareth for his money. I was wrong. I've got to know her, she's shy and works nights, and I was unfair. I didn't want that hanging around.
**If they say:** Ha, I did wonder.
**You:** Fair. Anyway, that's the update.
**If they say:** Are you sure? You were really convinced.
**You:** I was. I was also wrong. That's all.
:::

You don't need to explain how you came to think it. The explanation is where people sneak the original opinion back in. "In fairness, she does have a resting face" undoes the whole announcement.

### "You were right"

Harder, because it costs pride instead of time. A friend warned you about someone, you defended them, maybe you went quiet on the friend for a while. Now you know.

::: script To the person who warned you
**You:** You told me last year you were worried about how Callan talked to me. I didn't want to hear it. You were right, and I'm sorry I pushed you away for saying it.
**If they say:** I'm just glad you're okay.
**You:** Me too. Thank you for still being here.
:::

Short is kinder here as well. They don't need the full account of how bad it got unless they ask, and they definitely don't need you to grade yourself as an idiot. The point is that they stop wondering whether they lost you for telling the truth.

### "I was right after all"

Sometimes the update runs the other way. You raised a worry, people said you were overreacting, you half believed them, and now it's confirmed. The temptation is a victory lap. Don't take one. Say what's changed, and put the attention where it belongs.

::: script When the worry turned out to be true
**You:** I said last spring I was worried about how Uncle Martin treats Aunt Jen. Some of you thought I was overreacting. She's left him, and she's told me some of what happened. I'm not saying this to be right. I'm saying it because she'll need people, and I'd like us to be those people.
:::

### "I've changed my mind about staying, or going"

Maybe you told everyone you were leaving and then didn't. Maybe you told everyone it was fine and now you're leaving. People who change their minds about a relationship often go silent, because they can't face the friends who heard version one. That silence costs more than the announcement would.

::: script When your decision has changed
**You:** I know I said I was done with him. We're giving it another go. I'm not asking anyone to agree. I'd just rather tell you than have you hear it.
:::

[Can people change?](ch:can-people-change) has the audit for giving it another go with your eyes open, and none of this needs anyone's permission.

## What you don't owe

**A retraction of something true.** If someone pushes you to take back an accurate account of what they did, publicly or privately, the platform hasn't changed. Being pressured to announce that you were wrong is not evidence that you were.

**An update that puts someone in danger.** If you were right about someone dangerous, an announcement can reach them. Telling his family, or posting about it, could make things worse for the person at risk. Talk to that person first, or to the domestic abuse service where they live, before you say anything wider.

**A performance.** The update is information. A tearful hour on someone's sofa makes them manage your feelings about your mistake. [Being wrong well](ch:being-wrong-well) calls that the self-flagellation counterfeit.

## When someone updates you

Sometimes you're on the platform. Your sister finally says she was wrong to take your ex's side. A friend admits she thought you were exaggerating.

Take it. You can say thank you without saying it didn't hurt, and you don't have to rebuild everything that afternoon. What helps nobody is making them crawl. Someone who changed their mind out loud did the thing this whole book asks for. If you punish it, you'll teach them to keep their next update to themselves.

## Grade the announcement

Before you look: would each one get everyone to the right platform?

::: sort
- In the group chat: "Update: I was wrong about Josh. He's not a cheat, the receipts were his brother's. Sorry, all." || Yes. Same place as the verdict, old and new, done.
- "I was wrong about Josh, but honestly, can you blame me after how he acted at Christmas?" || No. The "but" reboards the old train.
- A three-page message about your trust issues, your childhood and your ex, ending "so yes, I was wrong about Josh." || Too long. The update is buried under the signalling fault.
- Telling one close friend you were wrong, when you'd originally told twelve. || Too quiet. Eleven people are still on the wrong platform.
- To the friend who warned you: "You were right. I'm sorry I went quiet on you." || Yes. Two sentences, and they stop wondering.
- "As I always said, she was trouble." || No. That's a victory lap, with a rewrite of what you always said.
:::

For two years Lorcan defended his brother-in-law, Achille. Achille fixed Lorcan's boiler, remembered his kids' birthdays, and rang him now and then, worried. "Moira's been so up and down. I just want her to get help."

So when his sister Moira said Achille checked her phone and decided which friends she could see, Lorcan told the family chat she was being dramatic. "He's the most patient man I know."

In March Moira leaves. She shows Lorcan the messages: hundreds, telling her where to be, who she'd be "punished" for seeing, and that her own family thought she was unstable. Several quote Lorcan, word for word.

It lands all at once. Those worried calls weren't concern. Achille had been feeding Lorcan the story, so that he'd carry it to Moira for him.

Lorcan rings his sister first. "You were right, and I didn't listen. I'm sorry. I'm on your side."

Then the family chat, which Achille isn't in. "I told you all Moira was overreacting about Achille. I was wrong. I've seen what he sent her. I believe her."

Yes, it was manipulation, and Lorcan had been one of its [flying monkeys](/playbook/flying-monkeys-proxy-recruitment) without knowing it.

Two days later Moira asks him to come with her to the solicitor's. He does.

=== trust-as-a-skill-you-rebuild
title: Trust as a skill you rebuild
question: If I can never be certain about anyone, how am I supposed to trust people again?
seo: How to trust again after being manipulated, step by step
short: You trust again the way everyone with good relationships already does: in steps. Extend small things you can afford to lose, watch what happens, then climb or step back. Watch three signals: can things be named, does repair happen, what does a no cost. Weighing won't stop you ever being wrong. It makes being wrong survivable.
---
Here is the question the whole book has been walking toward, and it's usually asked with some despair.

*If I can never really be certain about anyone, if impressions aren't evidence, if intent can't be read, if my own instrument is bent and the tests take months, how am I ever supposed to trust another person again?*

The answer is that you were never doing it the way you think you were.

## Trust was never a verdict

People imagine trust as a state you enter once you've established that someone is safe. A conclusion, reached, and then relaxed into.

Nobody has ever done this. The certainty it needs doesn't exist. What people do instead, including everyone you know with good relationships, is extend trust in increments, watch what happens, and revise.

::: pull
Trust is a **practice**: give a little, watch, give a little more. Nobody has ever had proof, and everybody has managed anyway.
:::

## How do I trust again after being manipulated?

Start small and go up in steps. Rely on someone for something minor, share something low-stakes, say a small no, and watch what happens each time. Climb when they handle it well, step back when they don't. Expect to be wrong sometimes. The point is to be wrong at a height you can survive.

## The staircase

Picture the stairs in an old house. You don't take them three at a time in the dark. You put a little weight on each step, feel it hold, then move up. If one creaks badly, you step back down and go round. Nobody calls that paranoid. It's how people climb old stairs.

::: handle
Trust in steps you can afford to lose.
:::

Here's a staircase for a new person: a friend, a partner, a colleague. The order matters more than the speed.

::: compare The steps
| Step | What you give | What you watch |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Small reliance | A lift, a lent book, "meet you at seven." | Do they turn up? Does the book come back? |
| 2. Small confidence | Something true and minor about you. | Does it stay with them? |
| 3. A small no | Turning down a plan or a favour. | What does it cost, over the next few days? |
| 4. A real disagreement | Saying something they did bothered you. | Can it be named? Does repair happen? |
| 5. Bigger reliance | Keys, money, the children, your plans. | Does it get used as leverage? |
| 6. The soft parts | Your history, your fears, what hurt you. | Does it ever come back to you as a weapon? |
:::

Steps three and four are the ones people skip, because they're uncomfortable. They're also the three signals this book keeps returning to. Can things be named ([The naming test](ch:the-naming-test))? Does repair happen ([Repair, and apology's counterfeit](ch:repair-and-apologys-counterfeit))? Does a no cost you ([The cost of "no"](ch:the-cost-of-no))? Those cover most of what matters, and none of them needs you to read anyone's mind.

## Rules for the stairs

- **Start with what you can afford to lose.** Small confidences, small reliances. These aren't traps. They're the ordinary increments every friendship is built from, and they happen to be information too.
- **Match the stakes to the evidence.** Six months of good behaviour earns six months' worth of trust. That sounds cold, and it's simply proportion. You already do it with money.
- **Revise in both directions.** Up as well as down. A reader who only ever downgrades is bracing, and calling it weighing.
- **A creak isn't a collapse.** One forgotten promise, owned and repaired, is a step that held under strain. That's better news than a step that's never been tested.
- **Accept that you will be wrong sometimes.** This is the acceptance the rest stands on. Not might be: will be, more than once, over a life.

## Can you ever trust someone 100%?

Not in the sense of certainty, and you never needed to. People with good relationships trust in proportion to a long record, and they keep paying gentle attention without treating it as suspicion. After years of steps that held, trust feels total, because the record is long. It was built one step at a time.

## What weighing is for

Calibration, from [the first chapter](ch:you-learned-to-see-this-is-how-you-learn-to-weigh), means matching how sure you are to how often you're right. Its job was never to stop you being deceived. Nothing does that, and any book claiming otherwise should be checked against [Certainty is the tell](ch:certainty-is-the-tell).

Its job is to make trust affordable. A person with no way to weigh has two strategies, and both are terrible. Trust everyone, and get wrecked on a regular schedule. Or trust nobody, and be safe and alone.

Someone who can weigh has a third option. They can extend trust early, because they'll notice. They can be wrong on a Tuesday and correct by Friday. They can risk being close to people, because being wrong is no longer catastrophic. It's recoverable, and recovery is a skill they have.

::: pull
The calibrated person is usually the most **relaxed** person in the room, because they aren't relying on getting it right the first time.
:::

## Climb or step back

Before you look: is each one a sensible step, a skipped one, or a staircase nobody is climbing?

::: sort
- Three dates in, he asks you to lend him £800 for his car. || A skipped step, and he's the one doing the skipping. Step five, asked for at step one.
- After a year of kept promises, you give your friend a spare key. || A sensible step. The evidence and the stakes match.
- She repeated something you told her, apologised specifically, and hasn't done it since. You tell her something small again. || A sensible step. One creak, repaired, and you've gone back up carefully.
- Eighteen months of good evidence, and you still won't tell your partner anything about your past. || Nobody's climbing. The bottom step feels safe, and it keeps you alone.
- "If you really loved me, you'd trust me with your passwords." || Someone demanding you skip steps. That's information about them.
- You tell a new colleague a small, true thing about your week, and watch whether it travels. || Step two, done right.
:::

## The staircase, used

::: scene
*LABEL*

Two years after she left, Clemence joins a choir and finds herself next to Signe, who sings flat and apologises to the whole alto section about it.

Step one: Clemence lends her a coat after a wet rehearsal. It comes back dry-cleaned, with a chocolate in the pocket, which feels like overdoing it, and also nice.

Step two: over a drink, Clemence mentions she's divorced. She waits to hear it come back from someone else. It never does.

Step three: Signe invites her away for a weekend. Clemence says no. "No worries," Signe says, and two weeks later asks about something else.

Meanwhile a colleague repeats a small thing Clemence told her about her ex, in the work kitchen. Clemence drops that step, says nothing more personal at work, and notices she doesn't conclude anything about the rest of the world.

Step four comes in spring. Signe forgets to come to her birthday, then rings the next day. "I forgot, I've no excuse, and I'm sorry. Can I take you out on Saturday?"

In June, walking home after a concert, Clemence tells her the whole thing. Signe listens to all of it, and on Thursday she's flat again, and apologising.
:::

Anyone who demands you skip steps, with "If you loved me you'd trust me," is telling you something about how they treat a no. And if someone hands you this chapter to argue that you owe them trust, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). Nobody is owed a step you aren't ready to take.

## What this was for

The rest of this library taught you to recognise something that had been happening to you without a name. That was necessary, and it wasn't enough. Recognition on its own produces a person who sees the machinery everywhere and can't get close to anyone, which is the same [isolation](/playbook/isolation), arrived at voluntarily.

The point of learning to weigh was never to see through everyone. It was to stop having to.

You can put the instrument down now. It'll be there when you need it, for the days when something starts to feel wrong. It's a tool for difficult days, and a person who runs it all the time isn't safer. They're only further away.

Go and be close to people. Extend a little more than you can prove. Notice what happens. Revise.

That's the whole skill, and you have it now.

::: try One step this week
Pick one person you'd like to trust a little more. Choose one step you can afford to lose: lend something, share one small true thing, or say one small no. Take it this week, and write one line afterwards about what happened.
:::

For the difficult days, the method fits on one screen in [The 60-second weigh](ch:the-60-second-weigh), and every handle in this book sits on [The field card](ch:field-card). Keep the card on your phone. Then close the book, and go and be close to someone.
