=== the-boring-explanations
title: The boring explanations
question: Is this manipulation, or are they just tired, stressed or bad at this?
seo: Manipulation or just tired? Check the boring causes first
short: Most behaviour that looks like a tactic has a cheaper cause: thoughtlessness, exhaustion, distraction, mismatched expectations, logistics, a body having a bad day. Manipulation needs planning and upkeep, so it's an expensive explanation. Check the boring causes first, then try a boring fix. Tiredness improves with sleep. A tactic survives it.
---
Manipulation is an expensive explanation.

For it to be true, someone has to want something from you, plan for it, keep it going for weeks, and remember what they told you and what they told everyone else. They have to do all of that while also holding down a job and remembering to buy milk. Some people do run that operation. Most people can't keep track of their own passwords.

Expensive explanations shouldn't be the first thing you reach for when cheap ones fit the same facts. A trained eye skips straight past the cheap ones, which is why they're written down here, as a countdown. The order is my bet about how often each one turns up, not a survey. Like any chart show, the biggest hit comes last.

## Is it manipulation, or are they just tired?

Usually they're tired, or distracted, or bad at hard conversations. Those causes are far more common than a plan, and they produce the same short replies, forgotten promises and vague answers. Check them first. Then watch what a boring fix does: rest, a clear request, a plain conversation. Tiredness improves with sleep. A tactic survives it.

## The countdown

### 10. Their own grief or stress

Money trouble, a parent's diagnosis, a job that's wobbling. People rarely announce these, and they bend everything: patience, memory, how much warmth is left at the end of the day. You often find out months later, and rewrite the whole spring in a second.

### 9. Fear

People who are frightened of your reaction, of losing you, or of a conversation going badly will lie, avoid, minimise and go vague. All of that looks exactly like manipulation, and it comes from the opposite of power. The person dodging your question may feel like the weakest person in the room.

A lie told out of fear is still a lie. [Can you tell when someone is lying?](ch:can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying) covers how to check one. Fear only changes what the lie was for.

### 8. Clumsy care

Some people apologise badly, comfort badly and ask badly. The intent is good and the delivery lands like a dropped tray. "Sorry you feel that way" can be a [non-apology](/playbook/non-apology-apologies). It can also be a man who has never once seen a real apology, doing his best impression of one.

### 7. No skill at hard conversations

Most people are bad at difficult conversations. Nobody taught them, so they improvise. What looks like evasion is often a person who has run out of words and is hoping the subject will leave on its own, like a wasp.

### 6. The body

Pain, illness, hunger, a bad night, a new medication, a hangover nobody's admitting to. Bodies drive behaviour far more than any of us like to admit. Someone with a migraine gives three-word answers and wants the lights off, which from the doorway looks a lot like the silent treatment. [The state you're judging from](ch:the-state-youre-judging-from) covers what a body does to judgment, and it applies to theirs as much as yours.

### 5. Logistics

The message didn't send. The calendar didn't sync. They thought dinner was Thursday because you said Thursday, in a voice note, over a boiling kettle. A surprising share of "he ignored me" turns out to be a grey tick and a train through a tunnel.

Logistics is the easiest cause to check, so check it before you diagnose a person.

### 4. Mismatched expectations

Two people running different unwritten rules about replies, punctuality, money, birthdays or affection, each experiencing the other as inconsiderate. In her family you ring on birthdays. In his, a text counts and a card is showing off. Neither is wrong, and both have been keeping score for three Christmases. [Difference is not malice](ch:difference-is-not-malice) goes further into this one.

### 3. Distraction

They were thinking about something else. That's the whole explanation, and it covers a huge share of slights. The cold look across the table was a person trying to remember whether they'd locked the car.

### 2. Exhaustion

The single largest cause of behaviour mistaken for strategy. Tired people are short, forgetful, withdrawn and terrible at reading others. Put a decent person on four hours' sleep for a week and they'll produce a near-perfect imitation of someone who doesn't care about you, without meaning to and without noticing.

### 1. Plain thoughtlessness

They didn't think about it. The thought never arrived, so there was nothing to choose. This is the most common cause of hurt feelings between people who love each other, and the hardest one to accept, because "it never crossed his mind" can feel worse than malice. Malice at least means you were on his mind.

::: pull
Every item on that list produces behaviour that **looks identical** to a tactic. That's no coincidence. Tactics work because they copy ordinary human failure closely enough to be deniable.
:::

::: handle
Boring first.
:::

## The economy test

Ask one question: *how much work would this have to be, for it to be strategy?*

Write the bill for each reading. The hostile one needs the person to have planned three moves ahead, kept a story straight, predicted your reaction, and held all of it together through a week when they were also working and sleeping. The boring one needs them to have been tired.

Both readings explain the same facts, and the boring one costs a fraction as much. Start there.

This isn't proof. Some people really do run the expensive version, and this book won't tell you they don't exist. Boring first is where you start. Where you stop depends on what the evidence does next.

## The boring-fix test

Boring causes respond to boring fixes. That's how you tell a real one from a tactic using it as cover.

::: compare What a boring fix should do
| The cause | The boring fix | If it was boring | If it wasn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exhaustion | Raise it after they've slept, or after the bad week ends | It eases | Rested and relaxed, they do it anyway |
| Didn't know it mattered | Say so, plainly, once | It changes, maybe clumsily | It continues, or you pay for asking |
| Logistics | Check the calendar, the message, the signal | The facts line up | The facts never line up, and there's always a new glitch |
| Clumsy care | Say the apology didn't land | They try again | "I said sorry, what more do you want?" |
| Their stress | Ask, then give it time | It lifts when the stress does | The stress ends and the behaviour stays |
:::

A boring explanation used every time, for months, to cover the same thing has stopped being an explanation. It's a cover story with excellent attendance.

The Decoder has names for that. When "I'm just useless at that" means the job always lands on you, it's [weaponized incompetence](/playbook/weaponized-incompetence). When "I was only tired" is used to shrink something serious, it's [minimization](/playbook/minimization). [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) shows how to count whether you're there.

## Some behaviour has no boring version

A few acts don't come in a cheap model. Tiredness doesn't install a tracking app. Distraction doesn't read your messages every night. Bad people skills don't take your bank card away. If what you're looking at is [monitoring](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance) or [financial control](/playbook/financial-control), skip the countdown. It was never built for those.

## Red flag or normal?

Most annoying behaviour is normal: late replies, forgotten plans, a bad mood, a clumsy apology. It becomes a red flag when it shrinks your freedom, repeats after you've named it, or comes with a price for saying no. Check the boring causes first, then the pattern. [Red flag, flaw or dealbreaker](ch:red-flag-flaw-or-dealbreaker) has the bins.

## Is my partner manipulative or just bad at communicating?

Bad communicators are bad with everyone, and they improve a little when you tell them clearly what you need. Manipulative communication is selective. It shows up where it gets them something, and it gets worse, or turns on you, when you name it. So ask what changed the last time you said it plainly.

Whether it's only you who gets this treatment is a test of its own, in [Is it you or is it everyone?](ch:is-it-you-or-is-it-everyone)

## What it costs when you get it wrong

The sharpest reason to care has little to do with fairness to them.

If you treat exhaustion as strategy, you'll fight a war against someone who isn't in one. They'll be accused of things they didn't do, with no way to disprove an intent they never had. They'll get defensive, which will read as confirmation. The relationship will get worse, which will read as more confirmation. And the whole structure will rest on a Tuesday when they hadn't slept.

::: pull
**You can lose people this way. Good ones.**
:::

It's worth thirty seconds with the countdown first.

## The countdown, run for real

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Asha's boyfriend, Gabe, is late with his half of the rent for the third month running. Each time he pays a few days later, with a vague apology. Asha has started to wonder whether this is about money, or about control, or about seeing what she'll let slide.

She runs the countdown on the bus. Stress: he's been on night shifts since August. Exhaustion: yes, obviously. Logistics: worth checking.

So she tries the boring fix. "I need the rent by the first. Can we set up a standing order together tonight?"

Gabe goes red. He gets his banking app out at the kitchen table, and they find it. His bank changed his account details in a merger in July. The old standing order has been bouncing every month, and the warning emails went to an address he stopped using at university.

They set up the new one together. On the first of the next month, it lands.

Three months of behaviour that looked deliberate, produced by a man with the financial planning skills of a houseplant. Asha keeps the standing order and drops the theory.
:::

## Find the boring cause

Before you tap, name the likeliest boring cause and the fix you'd try. One of these has no boring version.

::: sort
- She's replied "ok" to everything since Tuesday, the week her dad went into hospital. || Their own stress. Ask how her dad is, and leave the "ok"s alone for now.
- He says he never got your message about Saturday. || Logistics, maybe. Check it was sent. If messages go missing four times a month and only the awkward ones, that's no longer plumbing.
- Your flatmate never replaces the loo roll. || Thoughtlessness, almost certainly. Say it plainly, once. This one will outlive us all.
- He snapped at you on his third night shift in a row. || Exhaustion. Raise it after he's slept, and see whether he does it rested.
- She checks your location every evening "in case something's happened." || No boring version holds. Worry doesn't need daily [location tracking](/playbook/location-tracking). Weigh it as monitoring.
- He apologised with "I'm sorry you took it that way." || Clumsy care, or a non-apology. Tell him it didn't land, and watch what the second attempt looks like.
:::

::: warn Boring first is a starting point, never a requirement
None of this applies if you're frightened of someone. Fear doesn't need a countdown, and you don't need to rule out tiredness before you leave. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). If someone hands you this chapter to prove their behaviour is "just stress", reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). Boring first is yours to use, privately, by choice.
:::

::: try Try it on one thing tonight
Take the thing that's bothering you and go down the countdown from ten to one. Tick every cause that could fit. Pick the likeliest, and choose its boring fix: a night's sleep, one plain sentence, a check of the calendar. Do it this week, and write down what changed. It takes about three minutes to plan.
:::

Even when the cause turns out to be boring, it still hurt. Whether that hurt counts if they didn't mean it is the next question, and [Intent, impact and the mind-reading trap](ch:intent-impact-and-the-mind-reading-trap) answers it.

=== intent-impact-and-the-mind-reading-trap
title: Intent, impact, and the mind-reading trap
question: Does it matter what they meant, if what they did hurt me?
seo: Intent vs impact: does it matter what they meant to do?
short: Intent is what someone meant. Impact is what their action did to you. The impact is knowable, because you felt it. The intent usually isn't, because it lives in their head. Arguing about it hands them the one subject only they can speak to. You don't need to prove intent to ask for a change or set a boundary.
---
"He didn't mean it, so it doesn't count."

That sentence has kept more people stuck than most of the tactics in the Decoder. It sounds fair. It's wrong in a very specific way, and so is its twin, "He meant it. I can tell."

Both sentences make the same mistake. They fuse three different questions into one.

## The three boxes

Picture three boxes on a table.

**Box one: what they did.** Knowable, and usually not in dispute. It's the version a camera would have caught, the one [What is evidence](ch:what-is-evidence) asks you to write down.

**Box two: what it did to you.** Knowable, and yours to report. Nobody else gets a vote on whether you were hurt. They can argue about whether your reaction was reasonable, but that's a separate conversation.

**Box three: what they meant.** Usually not knowable, and where nearly every overreach lives.

Now draw a thick line through box three. It matters. You just can't open it.

::: handle
Defend what happened, not what they meant.
:::

## What is intent and impact?

Intent is what someone meant to do. Impact is what their action actually did to you. They're separate questions. You can know the impact for certain, because you felt it and can describe it. You can rarely know the intent, because it lives inside their head. A request or a boundary can rest on impact alone.

## The mind-reading trap

Confident claims about someone else's inner state are the most common failure in amateur analysis. *He knew exactly what he was doing. She said that to make me feel small. He wanted me to react.*

You don't have access to that. Nobody does. What you have is a behaviour and a plausible motive. The certainty attached to the motive comes from exactly the machinery [The gut: sensor or smoke alarm](ch:the-gut-sensor-or-smoke-alarm) took apart. The motive arrives fully formed and feels like seeing, when it's guessing with confidence.

Your friend who has met him twice will supply a motive within four minutes. So will the internet, which has diagnosed every man it has never met.

Worse, and this part is uncomfortable, claiming to know someone else's inner state is itself a move in the tactic lists. Telling a person what they really think, what they really wanted and what they're really like, while refusing their own account of it, is a recognised form of control. It doesn't become acceptable when someone who has read this library does it.

::: pull
You can defend **what happened** and **what it cost you**. You cannot defend **what they meant**, and staking your case on it hands them the one ground on which they are the only authority.
:::

## Impact stands on its own

This part frees you more than it restricts you.

::: pull
You do not need to prove intent to be entitled to a boundary.
:::

That holds for a request, a boundary, leaving a room, ending a relationship, or deciding what you'll put up with. It holds with partners, family, friends and bosses. It doesn't decide what anyone else must believe about the person, and it isn't a verdict on their character. It's permission to act on what happened.

::: bridge loop:index
[The Loop](https://noblefathercreations.com/loop) links to that exact sentence as its own foundation for separating intent from impact, applied to the apps and feeds that shape what you see.
:::

"When you did X, the effect on me was Y, and I need it to stop" is complete. It needs no theory of their psychology. It can't be beaten by "that's not what I meant," because it never claimed to know what they meant. And it's the version most likely to change behaviour, because they can accept the request without first accepting a description of themselves.

Look at how much this frees you from. People stay stuck for months trying to establish intent, building the case that he *knew* or she *meant* it, when intent was never required for anything they wanted.

## Saying it out loud

::: script
**You:** "When you told the story about my driving at your sister's, I felt small in front of everyone. Please don't tell it again."

**If they say:** "That's not what I meant."

**You:** "Maybe not. I'm not arguing about what you meant. I'm telling you what it did, and asking you not to do it again."

**If they say:** "You're too sensitive."

**You:** "Maybe. It still landed that way, and the request still stands."

**If they say:** "So I'm a terrible person now?"

**You:** "I haven't said anything about what kind of person you are. I've asked you to stop one thing."
:::

Every line in that script stays in boxes one and two. That's why it's hard to argue with.

## Does intent matter at all?

Yes, for one question: *is this going to keep happening?* A person who didn't know they were hurting you can learn, and usually changes once told. A person who meant it usually won't. You still can't see intent directly. You find it out from what they do next, over weeks, after you've named it.

But you don't settle intent by inference in the moment. You settle it by **pattern over time**, which is [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern), and by **what happens when it's named**, which is [The naming test](ch:the-naming-test). Intent leaves tracks. You read the tracks, never the mind.

Intent also matters to how you feel about them and whether you forgive. That's yours to decide, and it can wait until the tracks are in.

## "I didn't mean it" isn't an answer, in either direction

The rule cuts both ways. When you hurt someone, your good intentions don't cancel what it did to them. "I didn't mean it like that" may be true, and the right next sentence is still about the impact: what it did, and what you'll change. If you're checking your own behaviour, [Am I the one doing it?](ch:am-i-the-one-doing-it) runs the same three boxes on you.

## Impact is real, and it isn't a trump card

Impact tells you something landed. It doesn't prove a wrong.

Someone can say your boundary hurt them, or that being asked where the money went felt like an attack. They may well have felt it. That doesn't oblige you to drop the boundary or the question. Hurt that comes from being told no, or from being asked a fair question, is real hurt about a fair thing.

Watch for the swap. You raise an impact, and within a minute the conversation is about the impact *your raising it* had on them. That reversal has a name in the Decoder, [DARVO](/playbook/darvo). And if anyone uses "impact is all that matters" to make you stop doing ordinary, reasonable things, that's the move [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands) describes.

## The three boxes at a family lunch

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

At every family lunch, Mira's brother Fergus says something about her body. This time it was "Someone's found the biscuit tin," loud enough for their mum to hear.

That night Mira drafts a text. "You do it on purpose to humiliate me in front of Mum because you've always been jealous of me."

She reads it back and sorts it into the boxes. Box one: he commented on her body, in front of their mum, at four lunches since spring. Box two: she now dreads the lunches and nearly skipped the last one. Box three: jealousy, humiliation on purpose. She crosses it out.

The text she sends says: "At lunch you said I'd found the biscuit tin. Your comments about my body make me dread coming. Please stop making them."

Fergus replies within a minute. "Wow. It was a joke. I didn't mean anything by it."

"Maybe not," she writes. "It still stings, and I'm asking you to stop."

He goes quiet for a day, then sends "fair enough. sorry."

At the next lunch he opens his mouth, catches her eye, and talks about the cricket instead. Mira still doesn't know what he meant. She never needed to.
:::

Motive is one thing people try to read off a face. The other is whether that face is lying, and [Can you tell when someone is lying?](ch:can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying) has the research on how well anyone manages it.

=== can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying
title: Can you tell when someone is lying?
question: How can I tell if someone is lying to me?
seo: How to tell if someone is lying, and why faces don't help
short: Mostly, not from their face. Across 206 studies, people judged lies and truths correctly about 54% of the time, barely better than tossing a coin. Eye contact, fidgeting and eye direction tell you almost nothing. What works is slower: checkable detail, core facts that hold or move over time, the record, and other people.
---
You can't tell by looking. Almost nobody can.

That's one of the steadiest findings in the research on lying, and the internet has spent twenty years selling you the opposite. The face-reading course is the horoscope of psychology, with more diagrams.

In 2006, Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo pulled together 206 studies in which 24,483 people judged whether someone was lying. Average accuracy was 54%. A coin manages 50%.

People did better at recognising truths, about 61%, than lies, about 47%. Most of us lean towards believing what we're told. That lean is mostly useful, because most of what people tell us is true. It also means a confident liar starts with the odds on their side.

Two more findings from the same review close the obvious escape routes. Experts did no better than anyone else: police, judges and other professionals stayed under 55%. And knowing someone well didn't rescue people either. Judgments of long-term acquaintances stayed within a few points of 50%, and people were slow to suspect anyone familiar.

So if you've been reading his face for weeks, the face has told you roughly what a coin would have.

## How to tell if someone is lying

Stop watching their face and start checking their story. Body language is close to useless for spotting lies. What helps is asking for specific, checkable detail, comparing their account with facts you already have, noticing whether the important parts change between tellings, and giving it time. Most lies come out later, through facts and other people.

## The myths

The anchor for this chapter is a face on a screen with the sound off. Everything the myths below rely on is in that picture, and the research says the picture tells you very little.

::: myth
- **Myth:** Liars avoid eye contact. / **True:** It's the most popular belief about lying on earth. A 2006 survey across 75 countries and 43 languages found "liars avert their gaze" was the dominant belief across cultures. A 2003 review combined 32 estimates of eye contact while lying and found an effect of almost exactly zero.
- **Myth:** Liars look up and to the right. / **True:** That one comes from neuro-linguistic programming. In 2012, Richard Wiseman and colleagues tested it in three studies, including footage of real press conferences. Eye direction didn't separate liars from truth tellers, and people taught the rule were no better at catching lies.
- **Myth:** Liars fidget. / **True:** The same 2003 review found only a small effect for fidgeting in general, which varied a lot from study to study, and none for scratching and self-touching. Fidgeting is a poor guide to lying.
- **Myth:** A nervous answer is a guilty one. / **True:** Being doubted makes honest people tense too. Nerves tell you someone is nervous.
- **Myth:** I'd know if he was lying. I know him. / **True:** People who know each other do barely better than strangers, and lean harder towards believing.
- **Myth:** Trained professionals can tell. / **True:** In the 2006 review, experts were no more accurate than students. They were just more suspicious.
:::

If you can tell your partner is lying because he touches his nose, the research has bad news for you and great news for anyone with hay fever.

## What actually works

The face is out. Four moves are in.

::: handle
Don't read faces. Check facts.
:::

### Move one: check facts, not faces

In 2002, Hee Sun Park, Timothy Levine and colleagues asked people to describe a time they'd discovered someone lying to them, and how. Very few lies were spotted in the moment from how someone behaved. Most came out hours, weeks or months later, through other people, physical evidence, facts that didn't fit what the person already knew, or a confession. Most of those lies came from friends, partners and family.

Lies are mostly caught by receipts. The bank statement, the friend who was actually there, the date that doesn't fit. That's the top rung of the ladder in [What is evidence](ch:what-is-evidence), and it's where a lie is most exposed.

### Move two: ask for detail you could check, once, calmly

Aldert Vrij and colleagues have spent years testing what does separate liars from truth tellers in interviews. The idea underneath is cognitive load. Lying is harder mental work than telling the truth, because the liar has to invent, remember and monitor all at once. Questions that add load make a lie harder to hold together.

Two of their findings are worth knowing. In 2008, asking people to tell their story backwards produced more signs of deceit, and police officers watching those interviews got better at spotting the liars.

In 2009, pairs of liars and pairs of truth tellers were questioned separately about a meal they'd supposedly shared. On the questions liars expected, both pairs matched. On unexpected questions about where they sat and what happened when, and when asked to sketch the restaurant, the liars' accounts stopped matching each other. Up to 80% of people were classified correctly that way.

Those are interview techniques tested in labs. Your kitchen is not a police station, and you shouldn't run it like one. Question someone the way a detective questions a suspect and you'll make an honest person look guilty, which [The file that builds itself](ch:the-file-that-builds-itself) explains. Repeated grilling has its own Decoder page, [interrogation](/playbook/interrogation).

The kitchen version is one natural question for a specific, checkable detail. *Who else was there? Which place was it?* Honest people usually answer easily. Vagueness or irritation in reply to an easy, specific question is worth a note. It isn't a conviction.

### Move three: watch the story over time

Memory rebuilds itself every time it's used, so small details drift in honest accounts. Whether it was Tuesday or Wednesday, who ordered what. That's normal, and [I don't remember it that way](ch:i-dont-remember-it-that-way) shows why.

Core facts shouldn't move. Who they were with, where they were, how much it cost. When the important facts change between tellings, write down each version with the date. Detail drifts. Core facts hold.

### Move four: weigh the record and the pattern

Lying isn't spread evenly across people. In a 2010 survey of 998 American adults, Kim Serota, Timothy Levine and Franklin Boster found that 60% reported telling no lies at all in the previous 24 hours. Half of all the lies reported came from just 5.3% of people. It was a self-report survey, so treat the numbers as rough, but the shape is the point. Most people lie a little. A few people lie a lot.

So one white lie about liking the jumper doesn't make someone a liar. A record of lies about things that matter is the best predictor you'll get. This person's track record tells you more than any face. [One thing is not a pattern](ch:one-thing-is-not-a-pattern) has the form for counting it.

## How to tell if someone is lying over text

Text removes the face, which barely helped anyway. In Bond and DePaulo's review, people who could only see the speaker did close to chance. Over text, look at content. Easy questions answered vaguely, a different question answered, details that change between messages. Then scroll up, because a text thread is a record you can compare.

One small diary study of 30 students, by Jeff Hancock and colleagues in 2004, found people told the fewest lies by email and the most on the phone. The authors' explanation was that email leaves a record. Whether that holds for every app is unknown, but the record is still yours to read. What a message's tone can and can't tell you is in [Reading a text message](ch:reading-a-text-message).

If the person exists only on your screen, checkable facts matter even more. Someone who has avoided every live video call for three months, or whose story always needs money, is giving you facts to weigh. The Decoder covers [catfishing](/playbook/catfishing) and the long online con it calls [pig butchering](/playbook/pig-butchering-long-con-digital-fraud).

## Is my partner lying about cheating?

You can't settle it from their face, their tone or how fast they deny it. Look at what you can check without spying: specific accounts that hold or don't, money and times that fit or don't, what other people tell you directly, and whether the core story changes. You don't need proof to protect yourself, or to leave.

A denial is a claim, like any other. Don't believe it because it was calm, and don't disbelieve it because it was flustered. Weigh it against the record and the pattern, the same as everything else. Searching their phone is a separate decision with costs of its own. This chapter is about what you can weigh without it.

## Checking facts, for real

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Cerys has spent three weeks reading Ollie's face. He's been doing "overtime" on Thursdays since March. When she asks about it, he scratches his neck, and she has read that liars touch their necks. She has also read that they don't.

So she stops watching his neck and tries the methods. Over dinner she asks, lightly, "Who else is on Thursdays?" He says Kemal and "the new lad." A fortnight later, asked in passing, it's Joanne from accounts.

Then she checks one fact that's already in front of her. Ollie's wages go into their joint account. The March, April and May payslips show no overtime at all. The joint savings are £2,300 lighter than in February.

She puts the payslips on the table. Ollie says she can't know he's lying. He has seen her reading articles, and they all say you can't tell from someone's face.

"I'm not reading your face," Cerys says. "I'm reading the payslip."

He admits it that night. There was no overtime. Thursdays were with someone he'd met in January, and the money paid for it.

Yes, he was lying, for three months, and his face never once gave it away. The payslips did.
:::

## When the liar has read this too

The Decoder's page on [lying](/playbook/lying) covers what deliberate lies tend to protect, and [lying by omission](/playbook/lying-by-omission-gaslighting-by-omission) covers the version where nothing false is said and the truth is simply left out.

::: warn If someone quotes this chapter at you
A liar can quote this research too. "You can't tell lies from faces, so you can't know." The research says faces don't reveal lies. It says nothing in favour of their story. It moves the question to the facts, the record and the pattern, so go there. If someone is using doubt to keep you from acting, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands). And if you're frightened of the person, you don't need to prove a single lie before you protect yourself. Go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops).
:::

::: try Try it this week
Write down the one thing you suspect someone is lying about. Underneath, list only what you could check: a date, an amount, a place, a person who was there. Cross out anything that depends on their face or tone. What's left is your real evidence. If the list is empty, you have a feeling to watch, not a lie to prove. It takes about five minutes.
:::

A lot of lies, and a lot more imagined ones, arrive by text, where there's no face to misread and every message gets read eleven times. [Reading a text message](ch:reading-a-text-message) is about what a message can and can't tell you.

You've read it eleven times. It hasn't changed. It still says "Sure." with a full stop, and you're still deciding whether the full stop is a threat.

A text arrives with the face cut out. No voice, no expression, no half-second pause before "sure." Your brain hates a blank, so it pastes a face into the hole. When you're anxious, the face it reaches for is the worst one it owns.

That picture, a message with the face cut out and you holding the scissors, is the whole chapter.

## You're worse at this than you feel

In 2005, Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues ran a set of studies on reading tone in email. People sent short messages, some sarcastic, some serious, and guessed how many would be read the way they meant. Senders expected about 78%. Readers actually got the tone right about 56% of the time, not much better than guessing. And readers believed they'd got it right about 90% of the time.

Knowing each other well didn't fix it. The overconfidence held whether or not people knew each other.

So when you're certain what a text "obviously" means, you're running at roughly coin-toss accuracy with the confidence of a judge.

::: handle
Text strips the face. Don't fill it with your worst guess.
:::

## What does this text mean?

Usually less than you think. A text carries words and almost nothing else, so tone, mood and intent get supplied by the reader, and an anxious reader supplies the worst. Before you decide, list three readings that fit the words. Then ask what you'd need to know: how they text everyone, what's happening for them, and whether this is new.

## Six messages, three readings each

Each of these is a screenshot in words. For each, there's the worst guess, the boring guess, and the reading about you, then the thing you'd actually need to know. It's the drill from [The discipline of three explanations](ch:the-discipline-of-three-explanations), aimed at your phone.

### 1. "k."

*Reply to four paragraphs you sent about your week.*

- **Worst guess:** they're furious, or they don't care.
- **Boring guess:** they read it in a queue, and "k." meant "got it, proper reply later." Later never quite arrived.
- **About you:** four paragraphs is a lot to answer from a queue.

**What you'd need to know:** Is "k." how they reply to everyone? Does a real answer turn up later? Did anything in your four paragraphs actually need one?

### 2. Read 14:12. No reply.

- **Worst guess:** they're ignoring you, and they want you to know they've seen it.
- **Boring guess:** they opened it in a meeting, meant to answer when they could think, and forgot. Opening a message and replying to it are two different jobs.
- **About you:** you've checked that "Read" more times today than they've checked their phone.

**What you'd need to know:** How long do they usually take? Did your message ask something that needs thought? Do they do this with everyone, or only with you?

The read receipt tells you that someone saw a message, and nothing else. Somehow we've all agreed to treat it as a confession.

### 3. They used to reply in seconds. Now it's hours.

- **Worst guess:** they're losing interest, or there's someone else.
- **Boring guess:** a new job, a new rota, or the end of the early weeks, when everyone replies like they're being paid by the minute. Nobody keeps that pace forever.
- **About you:** you set the speed limit during the first fortnight and you're still enforcing it.

**What you'd need to know:** Did anything else change? Are they as warm in person? Slower texting with the same warmth face to face is a phone habit. Slower everything is information. If this is someone new, [Weighing someone new](ch:weighing-someone-new) covers what pace does and doesn't tell you.

### 4. "👍"

*In reply to a question that mattered to you.*

- **Worst guess:** dismissive. A door closed politely.
- **Boring guess:** to plenty of people it means yes, and nothing more. Your dad uses it to mean "message received, love you, drive safely."
- **About you:** you asked something important and got an emoji, and that would sting whatever the emoji meant.

**What you'd need to know:** How do they use it elsewhere? If it matters, ask: "Is that a yes, or a whatever?" Habits like this vary a lot by age and family, which [Difference is not malice](ch:difference-is-not-malice) goes into.

### 5. "Can we talk later?"

- **Worst guess:** a break-up, or you're in trouble.
- **Boring guess:** they want to sort out holiday dates and they're on a bus.
- **About you:** your stomach dropped because the last time someone sent this, it ended something.

**What you'd need to know:** Which kind of talk it is, and you're allowed to ask. "Good talk or bad talk? Happy to wait, I'd just like to know which." Nothing in the language makes a stomach drop faster, and it's usually about a boiler.

### 6. "Where are you? Why aren't you answering? Send me a photo of who you're with."

This one doesn't need three readings. The words are doing all the work. A demand for proof of where you are and who you're with is [monitoring](/playbook/monitoring-surveillance), whatever tone it was typed in and however worried the sender says they were. Tone-reading is for messages that could mean several things. This one means one.

If messages like this come with threats, or leave you frightened, stop reading tone and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). Keep the messages. [What to document and how](/longafter/what-to-document-and-how) in The Long After shows how to store them safely.

## Things that tell you almost nothing

Typing dots that appear and vanish tell you someone started typing. That's all. Maybe a call came in. Maybe they deleted a joke. Maybe the bus arrived.

"Online at 1am, didn't reply to me" tells you their phone was on at 1am. They could have been answering a group chat, failing to sleep, or watching a man restore a rusty axe.

A full stop, a missing kiss, a shorter message than usual: all of these change with the sender's mood, age, phone and thumbs. One instance tells you very little. A change that lasts for weeks, and shows up off the phone too, is worth asking about.

## What a text can tell you

Texts are bad at tone and good at content. A text is reliable on:

- what someone actually said they'd do, and when;
- whether the core facts of a story change between messages, because you can scroll up;
- threats, demands and rules about your life, which mean what they say;
- patterns over weeks, which no single message can show.

And it's very good at carrying one plain question. "Not sure how to read that. All good?" settles more than eleven rereads.

## Does reply speed mean anything?

On its own, very little. Reply speed follows jobs, moods, habits and phones far more than feelings. A change can mean something when it lasts, comes with other changes such as less warmth in person or cancelled plans, and doesn't shift when you ask about it plainly. Otherwise a slow reply is a busy afternoon.

## Can you tell if someone is lying over text?

Not from tone, punctuation or reply speed. Look at content instead: vague answers to easy questions, answers to a different question, and core facts that change between messages. A text thread is a record, so compare it. The full method, with the research behind it, is in [Can you tell when someone is lying?](ch:can-you-tell-when-someone-is-lying)

A little lying over text is ordinary. In a 2009 study of nearly 7,000 instant messages, senders rated about one in ten as containing a lie. About a fifth of those were "butler lies", small social excuses such as "got to go" or "I completely forgot", told to start, end or dodge a conversation. The modern classic, "sorry, only just seen this", belongs to the same family. It may well be a lie, and it's almost always a harmless one.

## The full stop

::: scene
*Made up, but you've met these people.*

Wren sends her new boyfriend, Raf, a photo of her new haircut at lunchtime. Two hours later, he replies.

"Nice."

Full stop. No kiss. By four o'clock she's decided he hates it, and possibly her.

On the walk home she runs the three readings. Worst: he hates it and is being polite. Boring: he's at work, typing between calls. About her: she's anxious about the haircut, which is shorter than she planned.

What would she need to know? How Raf texts in general. So she scrolls up.

Raf ends every message with a full stop. "On my way." "Pizza?" "I love you." Raf texts like he's paying by the letter.

That evening he rings, says the haircut looks great, and asks why she went quiet all afternoon. She tells him about the full stop. He laughs for a full minute, then admits he didn't know you could text without one.
:::

## Read these without scissors

Before you tap, decide what you'd need to know, or whether the message needs reading at all.

::: sort
- "Fine." after you cancelled your plans. || Could be any of the three readings. Is "Fine." their usual style? Did they say anything else? How are they next time you see them?
- Typing dots appeared and vanished three times, then nothing. || Tells you they started typing. Nothing more. Wait, or ask.
- "Who's Sam? Why is he liking your photos? Unfollow him." || No reading needed. That's a rule about who you can talk to. [A boundary, or a rule for someone else](ch:a-boundary-or-a-rule) explains the difference.
- Online at 1am, and didn't reply to you. || Their phone was on at 1am. That's the whole finding.
- Three weeks of short replies, and in person they're short too, and they've cancelled twice. || Now it's a pattern across channels, not a phone habit. Ask about it directly, in person.
- "We should talk about the money thing." || Content, not tone. Ask when, and get ready to talk about the money thing.
:::

::: bridge loop:variable-reward
If you can't stop checking for the reply, the checking may be doing something of its own. [Variable reward](/loop/variable-reward) in The Loop explains why an unpredictable reward keeps you checking, and why "the reply might be there" is such a strong pull.
:::

::: warn Tone-reading never comes before safety
If texts from someone frighten you, you don't need to work out their tone before you act. Save them, tell someone you trust, and go to [When weighing stops](ch:when-weighing-stops). If someone tells you that you're "reading too much into" messages that threaten or control you, reread [This book in the wrong hands](ch:this-book-in-the-wrong-hands).
:::

::: try Try it next time a text lands badly
Before you reply, write the three readings: the worst, the boring, and the one about you. Add one line saying what would tell them apart. If that thing can be asked, ask it in plain words: "Not sure how to read that. All good?" Then put the phone face down for ten minutes. It takes about three minutes, plus the ten.
:::

Texts get screenshotted, cropped and forwarded, and a crop can make a message mean almost anything. [Screenshots and what they leave out](ch:screenshots-and-what-they-leave-out) shows how to ask what's outside the frame.
