So that's all of it. Twenty-one guides. Two hundred and seventy scenarios. And if you've made it here, you've either been through one guide and come looking for the end of the whole thing, or you've genuinely gone through the lot — in which case, thank you, and also please go outside.
Let me pull the entire series back into one piece, because the individual guides matter, but the shape underneath all twenty-one of them matters more.
Here's the thing I noticed writing this, and it's the single most useful sentence in the whole series. Almost none of these two hundred and seventy scenarios are about a villain in a mask. They're about somebody using the best parts of this culture as a tool. Trust that forms fast. Community that opens its arms wide. Generosity that doesn't keep a ledger. A strong shared instinct not to be the person who makes it awkward. Every single one of those things is genuinely good, and genuinely worth protecting — and every single trap in this series runs directly on one of them.
That's why so much of this looks the same from twenty-one different angles. The promoter whose main stage is always next year. The camp whose contribution only ever expands in one direction. The manager who handles everything and ends up owning every relationship you had. The helpful stranger at the tent who has learned your shift pattern. The supervisor whose complaint route runs through the supervisor. The person who waits for hour twelve to ask, because hour twelve is when people say yes.
And notice what almost all of them needed from you. Not naivety. Just tiredness, and good manners.
So nothing in this series ever asked you to trust people less, or to arrive at a festival braced. What it asked for was a set of small, cheap, slightly unglamorous things, all of which have to be done *before* — before the night, before the crush, before the dose, before the contract, before you're exhausted. Terms in writing. Tents located on Thursday. A second reporting name collected on day one. A buddy named before gates open. Your own account of an incident written the same hour. A break taken while it's still busy. A decision deferred to the morning. A debrief booked with a name and a time.
And every one of those is easy. That's the whole problem with them. They're easy at the exact moment they feel unnecessary, and they're impossible at the exact moment you need them. So the entire design of this series is just: decide the hard calls in a calm room, in advance, so the tired version of you at hour twenty doesn't have to invent one from nothing.
Here's the second thing worth saying, and it applies across every guide. The standards don't push the right people away. They push the wrong people away. The promoter who won't put it in writing. The shop that won't sign the consignment terms. The camp that calls a defined cost a bad vibe. The person who responds to a kind, firm no with guilt. Every single one of them has just filtered themselves out — for free, in advance, and before it cost you anything. That's not cynicism. That's the cheapest information you will ever be handed.
And the third thing, which is really the point of all of it. Look at how many of these two hundred and seventy scenarios turn on somebody nearby doing something small. Not a hero. Not a confrontation. A distraction. A charger. A question asked properly, with eye contact — "are you actually okay, because I'm asking for real." A radio call instead of a grab. A friend who stayed. That's the entire safety infrastructure of a festival, and it isn't the Rangers and it isn't the fence. It's people who decided in advance that they'd be the one who stops.
So if you take one thing out of the whole Festie Bible, take that. You are going to walk past something. Everybody does. And the version of you that stops isn't braver or better informed than the version that keeps walking. It's just the version that already decided.
