# L.E.N.S. — PHOTOGRAPHERS & MEDIA
### Photographers, Filmers & Media Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 262 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is L.E.N.S.: Photographers, Filmers & Media Edition. You hold two things at once: a camera that gives you access, and a file that belongs to somebody else's life. This guide is about handling both honestly.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **L** — License Is Not Implied By Location. Being in public is not consent to be published. Legality and ethics are different questions and only one of them protects the person in the frame.
- **E** — Everything You Shoot Is Someone's Monday. That frame outlives the weekend. Who sees it, where it lands, and what it costs them at work or at home is your call before you post, not theirs after.
- **N** — No Means Delete It Now, In Front Of Them. A withdrawal of consent is not a negotiation about a good shot. Delete it while they watch, and say you have.
- **S** — Sell Your Work, Not Your Rights. Unpaid, unlimited, perpetual, all media. Festival media contracts take everything by default and most shooters sign without reading.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*Access as a lure, the shoot that becomes something else, exposure as payment.*

### 1. “I CAN GET YOU BACKSTAGE”
*The Camera As A Key / Access Offered In Trade — clinically, access exploitation / quid pro quo grooming.*

Here's who this is. Has a laminate, a long lens, and an unusual amount of freedom of movement. Offers to shoot you, offers to get you in, offers to introduce you — and the offers arrive in a sequence that keeps having a next step.

Picture this. He says your look is incredible and he wants to shoot you properly, and he can get you into the artist area because he shoots for them. The first shoot is professional and the pictures are genuinely the best you have ever had of yourself. The second one he suggests somewhere private for the light, and mentions the festival he shoots in the spring, and asks whether you would want to be involved with that.

Here are the signs.

- Access, introductions and opportunity all route through one person
- Shoots move from public to private across sessions
- Wardrobe or nudity is introduced gradually rather than agreed up front
- The next opportunity is always just ahead and is always in his gift
- No call sheet, no assistant, no third person, no written brief
- The work is good, which is what makes the arrangement hard to leave

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A camera is one of the most efficient access tools at a festival — it explains proximity, justifies attention, licenses photographing strangers, and moves through restricted space. Nearly everyone carrying one is doing the job. But the same properties make it attractive to someone who wants proximity for other reasons, and the pattern is the classic one from the modelling and music industries: real opportunity, real skill, real photographs, with each step requiring slightly more and each step justified by the last. The tell is not creepiness. It is the absence of the ordinary professional furniture — no brief, no third person, no rate, nothing in writing.

Here's your check. The E check: “Is there a third person, a written brief, and a stated rate?” — Professionals have all three. Their absence is not informality, it is the design.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Photographs Are The Hold. The photographs themselves become the leverage, and that is often the point rather than a side effect. Once someone holds images of you — especially anything you agreed to when you were tired, altered, flattered, or halfway through a shoot you did not want to ruin — they hold something that can be released, hinted at, or simply remembered out loud. Nothing has to be threatened. If you are in this: the files existing is not the end of your options, and the fastest way to defuse a hold is to stop it being a secret — tell one person now, in writing, before anything is asked. A secret with two keepers is worth far less to the person holding it.

Here's what you do. Insist on the professional furniture: a written brief, a rate or a clear trade, a third person present, and no private locations for a first shoot. Bring somebody. Somebody wanting to shoot you properly will not mind an assistant; somebody wanting the room will mind a great deal, and that is your answer.

Say this, out loud. “Sounds great — I'll bring a friend, and can you send me a quick brief and what the usage is? I keep it all written down, it's just how I work.”

Here's the truth underneath it. A real photographer wants an assistant, a brief and a release. The ones who want the room empty are telling you what the shoot is for.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*The frame you did not earn, altered subjects, the long lens.*

### 2. “SHE WON'T MIND, SHE'S HAVING A GREAT TIME”
*The Frame You Did Not Earn / Shooting The Incapacitated — clinically, non-consensual capture / exploitation of impairment.*

Let's talk about who does this. You, on a good night, with a great frame in front of you and a legal right to take it and a rationalization already forming. This page is aimed at the photographer, not at the subject.

Here's how it plays out. It is four in the morning and she is beautiful and completely gone, sitting on the grass laughing at nothing with her top half off, and it is the best light of the entire weekend. Nobody would stop you. It is a public field. She is not in a position to object and will not remember either way, and you already know that is the reason the shot is available.

Watch for these.

- The subject could not consent right now if you asked
- You are relying on them not noticing rather than on them agreeing
- The frame's value comes from their state rather than from the moment
- Long lens, from distance, on someone who has not clocked you
- You are constructing a justification before you have taken it
- You would not show them the shot tomorrow and ask

Here's what's really going on. Legality and ethics part company here completely. In most jurisdictions you may photograph people in public and publish it, and that permission is exactly why the profession needs a standard above it — the law is not doing the work of deciding whether a frame should exist. A person who is incapacitated cannot consent to being photographed any more than they can consent to anything else, and a photographer relying on their impairment has made the same move as everybody else in this book who waits for capacity to drop. The test is simple and it is the one the whole trade runs on: could you show them tomorrow, and would they say yes.

Ask yourself this. The L check: “Could this person consent right now if I asked?” — If not, there is no frame here. The camera does not create a permission the situation does not contain.

Here's what's underneath all of it. You Are The Risk On This Page. Most of this book is about somebody doing something to the reader. This page is the other way round, and it is in here because festival photography has a genuine, ongoing problem with it: the near-nude, the overwhelmed, the passed-out, the person in a crisis at the harm-reduction tent, shot at distance and posted as atmosphere. Those frames circulate for years. They cost people jobs, custody arrangements, and their sense of safety in a place they loved. The photographer is usually not malicious — they are a person who wanted a good shot and let the law answer an ethical question. Do not be that. Tomorrow-test every frame, and when a frame fails it, do not take it rather than not posting it, because files leak and drives get shared.

Here's the play. Apply the tomorrow test before the shutter, not before the upload. If someone is impaired, in distress, or in a medical or harm-reduction context, put the camera down entirely — and if you have already shot it, delete it now rather than filing it.

Here are the words. “Not shooting that one. — [to a colleague] Mate, she's gone, leave it. Get the crowd instead.”

Remember this. The law says you may. The law is not your ethics, and a photograph of somebody's worst night outlives both of you.

### 3. “ALONE, AT NIGHT, WITH FOUR GRAND OF GEAR”
*The Photographer As The Target — clinically, occupational exposure / high-value equipment risk.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Whoever has been watching the person with the obvious camera bag walk back to the media tent alone at three in the morning for the third night running, on the same route, at roughly the same time.

Here's the scene. You shot the headliner, you are carrying two bodies and three lenses, and the walk back to the car park goes through the dark bit behind the production compound because it is quicker. You do this every night. You have never thought about it, because you are thinking about the edit.

Here's what to watch for.

- The same route, the same time, alone, every night
- Visible high-value equipment and no discreet bag
- Working at the crowd's edges and in unlit areas by the nature of the job
- Head in the camera — the one posture that removes your situational awareness
- Nobody knows your route or when you are due back
- Somebody has been unusually interested in what you are carrying

Here's what's actually happening. Photographers occupy the highest-risk position at a festival and almost never think of themselves as at risk: they carry the most valuable and most portable items on site, they work alone at night by necessity, they are in the unlit margins rather than in the crowd, and the job itself requires them to spend long stretches with one eye in a viewfinder and no awareness of what is behind them. The equipment loss is the visible risk. Being followed to a vehicle in an unlit car park at three in the morning is the one that matters more.

Run the check. The E check: “Does anybody know my route and my ETA tonight?” — If the answer is no, and it is the same route as last night, that is two problems rather than one.

Here's the dark reality. Vary The Route, Tell Somebody The Time. The pattern is what does it. Somebody who wants your gear does not need to find you; they need to watch you once and then wait in the right place the following night, and a photographer is the most predictable person on a festival site — same stages, same pits, same exit, same hour. Two changes remove almost all of it, and neither costs anything: alter the route and the timing, and have a named person expecting you with a time. The gear is insured. Being alone in an unlit car park at three in the morning with somebody who has been planning it since Friday is not.

Here's your move. Vary your route and your times. Use an unbranded bag. Walk out with someone — another shooter, crew, security — and agree an ETA with a named person each night. Insure the kit properly and keep serial numbers and photographs.

Here's exactly what to say. “Walking out at half two — anyone going the same way? — [message] Heading to the car park now, back at the hotel by three, I'll text when I'm in.”

And here's the truth. You are the most predictable, most valuable and least accompanied person on this site. Change two of those three tonight.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Footage held over people, rights grabs, the unpaid perpetual license.*

### 4. “IT'S GREAT EXPOSURE”
*The Rights Grab / Unpaid, Unlimited, Perpetual — clinically, IP appropriation / unconscionable contract terms.*

Here's who this is. The media accreditation form nobody reads, and the production manager who genuinely believes a wristband is payment because everybody has always said it is.

Picture this. The accreditation form is four paragraphs and you are filling it in on your phone in a queue. One of those paragraphs assigns the festival a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free, sublicensable license to everything you shoot, in all media, for any purpose including advertising, with no credit obligation. You tick it because the gate opens in ten minutes and because everyone ticks it.

Here are the signs.

- “Perpetual”, “irrevocable”, “royalty-free”, “all media now known or hereafter devised”
- Sublicensable — meaning sponsors and brands get your work too
- No credit obligation, or credit at their discretion
- Payment described as exposure, access, or a wristband
- The form arrives at the gate, under time pressure, on a phone
- No usage window, no territory limit, no carve-out for your own portfolio

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Festival media terms are among the most aggressive rights grabs in working photography, and they are accepted almost universally because they are presented at the moment of least leverage — at the gate, in a queue, with the job about to start. The result is a marketing archive built for free out of freelancers' work, resold to sponsors, with no credit. This is the M.A.R.K.E.T. guide's exposure problem with a contract stapled to it, and the difference is that here you are signing away the asset itself rather than just working cheap. The good news is that these terms are usually negotiable, because the person holding the clipboard has never been asked.

Here's your check. The S check: “What exactly am I licensing, to whom, for how long?” — Three answers before you tick. If you cannot state them, do not sign at the gate.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. You Signed Away The Archive, Not The Weekend. The cost is not this festival, it is the decade afterwards. A perpetual sublicensable license means the frame you shot at twenty-three can be sold to a drinks brand at thirty-three without a call, a credit or a fee, and you have no standing to object because you ticked a box in a queue. Shooters discover this years later when their own image turns up on a billboard for something they would never endorse. Ask for the form in advance, by email, before you travel. That one request — the most junior-sounding thing on this page — is the whole defense, because a term you negotiate on Tuesday is a term, and a term you meet at the gate is an ultimatum.

Here's what you do. Request the media terms by email before you commit to the trip. Propose a limited license: twelve months, editorial and social only, credit required, no sublicensing to sponsors, and you keep everything else. Most will accept, because almost nobody asks.

Say this, out loud. “Can you send the accreditation terms over before I book travel? I usually license twelve months editorial with credit rather than perpetual all-media — happy to work to that.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Exposure has never once paid an invoice. The license is the deal, and the gate is the worst place on earth to read one.

### 5. “TAKE IT DOWN OR I'LL MAKE IT A THING”
*The Subject Who Turns / Demands, Threats And Pile-Ons — clinically, post-publication conflict / reputational coercion.*

Let's talk about who does this. Someone in a frame you published — sometimes with an entirely legitimate grievance you should act on immediately, sometimes escalating to demands for money, and occasionally mobilizing a pile-on that arrives before you have read the first message.

Here's how it plays out. The message says take it down. You took it at a public stage, you asked at the time, and you have the reply. The second message says you owe them money for using their image. The third says they have screenshotted your account and people are going to hear about this, and by the morning there are forty comments on a photograph of somebody else entirely.

Watch for these.

- A takedown request — which you should simply honour, immediately
- A demand for payment attached to the takedown
- Threats about your reputation rather than about the image
- Claims that shift each time you answer them
- Public escalation before any private conversation
- You have no record of the consent you actually obtained

Here's what's really going on. Two different things arrive in the same inbox and telling them apart is the whole skill. A person asking for their image to come down is exercising something you should grant without argument, whatever the law says and whatever you paid to be there — that is the profession's own standard and it is also the cheapest possible resolution. A demand for money, or a threat about your reputation, is a different transaction, and the correct response to that one is not negotiation but documentation.

Ask yourself this. The N check: “Is this a request to remove, or a demand for something else?” — Remove instantly for the first. Do not pay the second.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Delete First, Document Everything. The sequence that keeps photographers out of trouble is counterintuitive: comply completely and immediately on the takedown, and only then deal with the rest. Arguing about whether you were entitled to publish is the thing that converts a two-minute fix into a public campaign, because to everyone watching you are the person with the camera refusing to delete a picture. Take it down, say you have, apologize for the distress without conceding any claim, and keep every message. And build the thing that makes all of this short next time: a consent log, with a name, a date and what was agreed, which turns a he-said-she-said into a record.

Here's the play. Remove it immediately and say so. Do not pay, do not argue the law, do not respond publicly. Screenshot everything with timestamps. Keep a consent log from now on so the next one is a thirty-second conversation.

Here are the words. “It's down — removed as soon as I saw your message, and I'm sorry it upset you. I'm not able to pay anything, but the image is gone and it won't be used again.”

Remember this. Take it down first and sort out who was right afterwards. The photograph was never worth the week.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The consent practice, the delete rule, the contract you actually read.*

### 6. “THE CONSENT PRACTICE”
*How Working Photographers Actually Do This — clinically, consent protocol / ethical capture practice.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For every shooter, from the phone in the crowd to the one with the laminate. None of this slows you down after the first weekend, and all of it is what separates a photographer people trust from one they warn each other about.

Here's the scene. The whole practice is four habits and they take about three seconds each. The reason to build them now is that the moment you need them — somebody upset, somebody's partner asking, somebody who saw themselves on an account — is not a moment when you want to be inventing a policy.

Here's what to watch for.

- Ask before, not after — a raised camera and a look is often enough
- Say where it is going: personal, editorial, client, socials, with credit or not
- If they are impaired, in distress, or with kids: no frame, no exceptions
- If a no arrives later, delete it while they watch and tell them you have
- Hand over a card or a handle so people can reach you
- Never post a stranger's face with a caption that mocks them

Here's what's actually happening. Consent practice is what makes the difference between a photographer who gets access and one who gets asked to leave, and it compounds: people who have been treated well introduce you, tip you off, and let you shoot things nobody else gets to shoot. It is also the cheapest professional insurance available, because almost every media dispute at a festival starts with somebody who was photographed without being asked and had no way to reach the person who did it. Being findable is half the practice. Deleting on request is the other half, and it costs you one frame out of two thousand.

Run the check. The N check: “If they asked me to delete this right now, would I do it without arguing?” — If the answer is no, you are treating the file as more important than the person, and you will eventually be the story.

Here's the dark reality. The Delete Is The Whole Reputation. Deleting in front of somebody, immediately and without negotiation, is the single most reputationally valuable thing a festival photographer can do, and it is also the one most of them get wrong. The instinct is to explain — that it is a great shot, that you would not post it, that you shoot for the festival. Every word of that reads as a negotiation over whether their no counts, and it is remembered that way. Delete first. Say you have. Explain nothing. People talk to each other, and the photographers who become trusted in a scene are trusted for exactly this, not for their portfolio.

Here's your move. Build the four habits until they are automatic, and put your handle on a sticker on your camera strap so anybody can find you afterwards. Practise the delete — do it once, cheerfully, and notice it costs you nothing.

Here's exactly what to say. “Mind if I grab one? It's for my own stuff, I'd tag you. — [later] Course, deleting it now, look — gone. All good.”

And here's the truth. One deleted frame costs you nothing. The reputation for deleting without arguing is worth more than any shot you will take this weekend.

## Accountability — what you owe when the power is yours

*What you owe the people in your frames.*

### 7. “WHERE THAT PHOTO LANDS”
*What You Owe The People In Your Frames — clinically, publication ethics / downstream harm awareness.*

Here's who this is. You, at home on the Tuesday, with two thousand frames and a deadline and no one watching what you select.

Picture this. There is a frame in there of somebody mid-breakdown at the edge of a crowd, and it is the best photograph you took all weekend. It says something true about the whole event. Nobody would ever know who they are except everyone who knows them, which at a festival of this size is probably four hundred people.

Here are the signs.

- Would this person be fine with their employer, family or ex seeing this
- Are they identifiable to people who know them, even if not to strangers
- Are they in a medical, harm-reduction, welfare or crisis context
- Is anybody in the frame a minor, and is anybody outed by it
- Would you be comfortable sending it to them before you post it
- Is this frame about the festival, or about them specifically

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Selection is where the ethics actually live — the shutter is a reflex, the edit is a decision made with time and without pressure, and that is the moment you are accountable for. Festival crowds contain people who are not out, people whose employers do not know they are here, people in custody disputes, people in recovery, people who are somewhere they told someone else they were not. None of that is visible in the frame and all of it is downstream of your upload. You cannot check for every case, which is exactly why the general rules exist: no crisis contexts, no minors without a parent's yes, no identifiable faces in compromising states, and send it to them first when you are unsure.

Here's your check. The E check: “Am I willing to send this to the person in it before it goes up?” — If not, that reluctance is the answer, and you already know what it means.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Best Frame Is Usually The One To Kill. The photographs that do the most damage are almost never the careless ones — they are the excellent ones, because those are the ones that travel. A brilliant frame of somebody at their worst gets shared precisely as far as it is good, and the person in it spends a year meeting it. If you shoot festivals for long enough you will take a frame that is genuinely the best work of your career and should never be published, and what you do with that frame is the whole of your professional character. Nobody will ever know you killed it. That is rather the point.

Here's what you do. Make the edit a separate session with the rules in front of you, not a scroll at 1am. When a frame is strong and you are unsure, send it to the subject and let them decide — most say yes, and the ones who say no are the ones that would have been the problem.

Say this, out loud. “Hey — took this of you on Saturday, think it's a great shot. Only posting it if you're happy, no hard feelings either way.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your best photograph is not automatically a photograph you are allowed to publish. The edit is where you find out what kind of photographer you are.

### 8. “THERE ARE KIDS IN THAT FRAME”
*Photographing Children At A Public Event — clinically, child safeguarding / image consent for minors.*

Let's talk about who does this. You, in the family field, where the light is good and the pictures are genuinely lovely and every single one of them has a safeguarding dimension you are responsible for.

Here's how it plays out. A child on someone's shoulders at golden hour with her hands in the air is one of the great festival photographs and you have a clear shot of it. You do not know her, you have not spoken to her parents, and in about four hours you will be deciding whether to put a recognizable image of somebody else's child on a public account.

Watch for these.

- A recognizable minor, no parent asked
- The frame would identify them to anyone who knows them
- You are relying on the setting being public as your permission
- A child in distress, undressed, or in a medical context
- You have no written consent and are about to publish commercially
- You would not be comfortable if a stranger did this to your own child

Here's what's really going on. Photographing children is the one area of event photography where the legal position and the professional standard diverge most sharply. In most public settings it is not unlawful, and every festival's media policy, every safeguarding lead, and every reputable agency nevertheless requires parental consent for identifiable minors — because the risk is not to you, it is to a child whose image you are putting into circulation without anyone who is responsible for them having agreed. Custody arrangements, safeguarding orders and families who are not findable are all invisible from behind a lens.

Ask yourself this. The L check: “Has a parent said yes, by name, to this specific use?” — Public setting is not permission where a child is concerned.

Here's what's underneath all of it. You Cannot See The Reason It Matters. The specific families this hurts are the ones you cannot identify from where you are standing: a child in a custody dispute whose location must not be public, a family with a safeguarding order, a parent who has moved away from someone. A photograph of a child geotagged at a named festival on a public account can undo arrangements that took years to build, and the photographer will never learn that they did it. That is the whole argument for asking, and it is why the standard is consent rather than judgement — you are not in a position to assess the risk, so you do not get to decide it is low.

Here's the play. Ask the parent before you shoot, and get a name and a contact if you intend to publish. Default to backs of heads, crowd-scale, or unidentifiable framing in family areas. Never shoot a child in distress or in a medical context. If in doubt, send it to the parent and let them decide.

Here are the words. “Lovely moment — do you mind if I take it? I'd only post it if you're happy, and I'll send it to you either way. Can I grab your number?”

Remember this. A public field is not consent for somebody else's child. Ask the parent, or shoot the back of the head.
