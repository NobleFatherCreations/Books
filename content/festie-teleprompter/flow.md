This is F.L.O.W.: Flow Artists & Performers Edition. You hand your body to gravity, fire, and other people's hands. This guide is about who you hand it to.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **F** — Fuel, Rig, And Gear Are Yours To Check. You inspect what you hang from, what you burn, and what you spin. Every time, personally. Nobody's feelings outrank that check.
- **L** — Lights Mean You Are Being Filmed. Assume a camera on every burn and every set. Decide what you consent to before you are lit, not after the clip is up.
- **O** — Overrides Are The Pattern. Spin injured, one more burn, skip the safety brief, it's fine. Watch who benefits from every override — it is almost never you.
- **W** — Who Holds You Is A Safety Decision. The rigger, the spotter, the safety, the base. That person holds your body. Choosing them is a technical decision, not a social one.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*The teacher with access, the base who makes you dependent, gear as a gift with strings.*

### 1. “I'LL TEACH YOU THE REAL STUFF”
*The Mentor With A Waiting List / Skill Access As Currency — clinically, mentorship grooming / skill gatekeeping.*

Here's who this is. Genuinely good, visibly senior, and the reason half this circle can do what they do. Teaches selectively. Makes it clear that being taught is a thing you are chosen for, and that the choosing is ongoing.

Picture this. He has been doing this for fifteen years and the thing he does with a staff is the reason you started. He offers to work with you properly — not in the jam, at his camp, after, when it's quiet and there's space. The first session is the best hour of instruction you have ever had. The second session he corrects your stance with his hands and holds the correction slightly longer than the correction needs.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Teaching happens privately and late rather than in open practice
- Access to instruction is framed as being chosen, and is reviewable
- Physical correction escalates in duration or location over sessions
- Discourages you from training with others — they'll teach you bad habits
- Mentions what he could do for your booking or your profile
- The lesson quality is real, which is what makes leaving expensive

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Skill is the currency in this scene and a person who controls access to it controls a great deal. The mechanism is the same as every other access trap in this book, but it has an unusual protection: the teaching is genuinely valuable, so the cost of walking away is real and everybody can see it. That asymmetry is what gets exploited. It is also why the correction-by-touch escalation works — in a discipline where hands-on adjustment is legitimate and constant, the line between instruction and testing is exactly where a person can operate while retaining deniability, and where you will second-guess yourself for weeks.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The O check: “Would this lesson be identical if it happened at 2pm in the open jam?” — If the answer is no, the privacy is doing something the teaching does not need.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Adjustment That Tests You. The escalation is deliberately gradual and it is designed to be unreportable. A hand on the hip is technique. The same hand, a beat longer, is a test — and if you do not react, the next one is longer. Every individual step is defensible and the whole staircase is not, which is precisely why it is built in steps. Dance, martial arts, aerial, and acro have all documented this shape, and it works the same way here. The counter is not deciding where the line is in the moment, when you are mid-correction and unsure. It is deciding beforehand that instruction happens where people can see it, and treating any pressure to move it somewhere quieter as the finding rather than as a scheduling preference.

Here's what you do. Train in the open. Take instruction from at least two people so no single person is your route to progress. If a session has already moved somewhere private, bring someone — “my friend wants to watch” is unarguable and tells you a great deal by how it lands.

Say this, out loud. “I learn better in the jam — more eyes, more feedback. Can we do it there? I'll bring Kai, they've been wanting to work on the same thing.”

Here's the truth underneath it. A teacher worth having will teach you in daylight with people watching. The ones who need the room quiet are not protecting your focus.

### 2. “TAKE IT, IT'S YOURS”
*The Gear Gifter / Props With A Ledger — clinically, reciprocity debt / material obligation.*

Let's talk about who does this. Generous, well-equipped, and quick to notice what you cannot afford. Hands you a set of pro-grade props, a rig, a costume, a fire kit. Does not want anything for it, says so repeatedly, and mentions its value twice.

Here's how it plays out. Your poi are entry-level and everyone knows it. He has a spare set of the ones you have been saving for — seven hundred dollars of them — and he wants you to have them, no, really, take them, he has three sets. Two weeks later there is an afterparty you do not want to go to and the sentence in your head, unprompted, is that he gave you the poi.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- The gift is expensive enough to be uncomfortable and given fast
- Refusing is treated as an insult rather than a choice
- Its value gets mentioned more than once, casually
- Given in front of other people, so the generosity is witnessed
- Followed within weeks by an ask that is hard to decline
- Taking it back is floated, lightly, when you say no to something

Here's what's really going on. Reciprocity is the strongest lever in the book — a received gift creates a felt obligation that operates below deliberation, and it does not care whether you asked for the gift or wanted it. Gift economy culture supercharges it, because the frame says a gift is pure, which removes your permission to be suspicious of one. Expensive gear is a particularly efficient version: it is high value, it is useful daily, it visibly improves your practice, and every time you use it you renew the debt. Most gifts in this scene are exactly what they look like. The tell is not generosity. It is generosity that arrives before the relationship has any other content.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Would I be comfortable saying no to this person tomorrow?” — If the gear makes that harder, the gear is doing something other than spinning.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Ledger Nobody Admits Exists. The damage is not the moment the ask arrives — it is the weeks before, when you quietly start being easier to be around. You stop raising the thing that bothered you. You go to the thing you did not want to go to. Nobody has threatened anything and there is nothing to report, and if you told a friend they would say he sounds nice. That self-editing in advance of a demand that has not been made is the whole mechanism, and it is the most reliably reported experience of people who later realized they had been managed. If you want to test it cheaply: decline something small this week and watch whether the temperature of the friendship changes.

Here's the play. Price the gift and settle it — pay something, trade something, or return it. If none of that is possible, say out loud and early what the gift does not buy, which converts an unspoken ledger into a stated one and removes its power.

Here are the words. “These are amazing and I'm not comfortable with a gift this size — let me pay you half, or I'll take them as a loan for the season and get them back to you.”

Remember this. A real gift survives you saying no to the giver a week later. Test it early, while the test is cheap.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Safety-third pressure, performing injured, adjustments that stop being technical.*

### 3. “SAFETY THIRD”
*The Culture That Jokes About The Thing That Kills You — clinically, normalized risk / social override of safety protocol.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Not a person — a room. The collective eye-roll when someone asks for a safety brief. The laugh when you check your fuel. The lightly impatient veteran waiting while you do a rig inspection everybody else skipped.

Here's the scene. You are about to burn. The safety is a guy you met an hour ago who is holding a duvetyne blanket like it is a beach towel, and when you ask whether he has done this before he says yeah, loads, and looks at his phone. The circle is waiting. Asking again in front of everyone would make you the difficult one. You have about four seconds to decide whether being the difficult one is worse than the other thing.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Safety checks treated as a personality flaw rather than procedure
- A designated safety who cannot describe what they would actually do
- Fuel handled near lit tools, or depot uncovered
- “Safety third” said in a context where it is not a joke
- Nobody has asked who is calling stop, or what stop sounds like
- The social cost of pausing is visibly higher than the cost of proceeding

Here's what's actually happening. Normalized deviance is the documented process by which a group repeatedly gets away with skipping a step until the skipped step becomes the standard. Every fire and aerial community runs on it to some degree, because the consequence is rare and the friction is constant, and the people most exposed are the newest — who have the least standing to pause and the most to lose by being seen as green. The joke is doing real work here: it pre-frames anyone who insists on the check as humorless, which means the insisting costs something, which means fewer people insist. That is not culture, it is a mechanism, and it runs whether or not anybody in the circle intends it.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The F check: “Have I personally checked my fuel, my depot, and my safety — by asking them what they would do?” — Secondhand assurance is not a check.

Here's the dark reality. The Four Seconds. Burn injuries in this scene cluster around exactly this moment — not around difficult tricks, around skipped checks under social pressure with an audience waiting. The specific failure is almost always mundane: fuel too close, a depot uncovered, a safety who had never actually done it, nobody having agreed what the stop call was. And the reason it is mundane is the reason it is preventable. The four seconds of being the difficult one is the entire intervention. People who have been burned describe those four seconds with total clarity afterwards, every time — they remember deciding not to ask again. Ask again. The circle waits, and then forgets by morning, and you do not have a graft.

Here's your move. Run your own check out loud, every time, in the same words, so it becomes your known habit rather than a comment on this circle: fuel, depot, safety, stop call. Ask the safety one question they have to answer — what do you do if my wick catches my sleeve? Their answer is the check.

Here's exactly what to say. “Quick one before I light — what's the stop call, and what do you do if it goes up my arm? Not being difficult, I just do this every time.”

And here's the truth. The circle's patience is not a safety system. Being briefly annoying is cheaper than being briefly on fire.

### 4. “YOU'RE ON IN TWENTY, IT'S FINE”
*The Show Must Go On / Performing Through Injury — clinically, injury minimization / occupational pressure.*

Here's who this is. The organizer with a running order, the troupe lead with a headcount, and — most often — you, doing the arithmetic about letting people down and arriving at the wrong answer.

Picture this. Your shoulder went on the last rehearsal and you can feel it in a way that is new. You are on in twenty. There is no understudy because there is never an understudy. The set is four minutes. Four minutes is nothing. You have already decided you are doing it, and you are now assembling reasons, which is a different mental activity from deciding and it is worth noticing that you are doing it.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- No cover, no contingency, and that is treated as normal
- Your injury is reframed as a scheduling problem
- “It's only four minutes” applied to a load-bearing body part
- Someone else's disappointment is the thing being weighed, not the joint
- You are assembling reasons rather than reaching a decision
- Pulling out has a reputational price everyone can name

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Performing-arts and circus injury research keeps finding the same thing: the significant injuries are disproportionately not freak accidents but re-injuries and compensations, sustained by people who continued while compromised. The economics drive it — no cover, no sick pay, gigs awarded by reputation, and a culture that reads reliability as showing up regardless. In a festival context it is sharper still, because the gig is often unpaid or trade, so you are risking a shoulder for a wristband and the thing keeping you in the air is not money but not wanting to be the person who dropped out.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The O check: “Am I deciding, or justifying?” — If you are listing reasons, the decision already happened and it was not made by your judgment.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What Four Minutes Costs. The ugly arithmetic is that four minutes of a compromised shoulder, rotator cuff, or ankle can end a practice you spent a decade building, and the people asking you to do it will have forgotten by the next event. Aerialists lose careers to exactly this. The second half is worse: performing impaired — injured, exhausted, or altered — also puts your base, your spotter, and anybody underneath you at risk, so the loyalty argument that got you into the air is actually pointing the other way. The culture that will pressure you to go on is the same culture that will not cover your physio. Decide accordingly.

Here's what you do. Set the rule before the season, not in the twenty minutes: a named list of injuries that mean you do not perform, agreed with yourself while nothing is at stake. Then it is a policy you are following rather than a judgment you are making under pressure.

Say this, out loud. “Shoulder's gone, so I'm out tonight — that's firm. I can still do the ground set or safety for someone else if that helps you fill it.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Nobody in that crowd will remember the set. You will be carrying the shoulder for thirty years.

### 5. “TRUST ME, I'VE GOT YOU”
*The Base / Partner Acro And The Hands That Are Already On You — clinically, consent in physical-contact disciplines / positional vulnerability.*

Let's talk about who does this. Your base, your flyer, or the person spotting. The discipline requires hands on your ribs, hips and inner thighs, so the contact is legitimate, constant and unremarkable — which is the whole difficulty.

Here's how it plays out. You are inverted with your entire weight on his hands and he adjusts his grip, and the adjustment goes somewhere that was not necessary for the position, and you are upside down four feet off the ground and the only thing keeping you there is the person who just did that. You come down. Neither of you says anything. He books you for the next jam.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Grip adjustments that are not required by the position
- New moves introduced without describing the contact first
- Contact continues after you have landed and are stable
- Objecting is framed as not trusting your base, which is a safety word here
- Sessions move from open practice to private space
- You come out of a wash feeling odd and cannot point to a single thing

Here's what's really going on. Partner acro, aerial and any spotting discipline share a structural problem: consent to hold has to be given in advance and continuously, while the person giving it is frequently inverted, load-bearing, or in a position where objecting is physically unsafe. Every acro community that has addressed this has landed on the same protocol — name the contact before the move, no new contact mid-position, and trust means trusting the hold rather than trusting the person's intentions. The word trust is the thing to watch, because it is doing safety work and social work simultaneously and somebody can lean on the second to get the first.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The W check: “Was this contact named before the move started?” — Unnamed contact is the finding, regardless of where it landed.

Here's what's underneath all of it. You Cannot Object At Four Feet. The reason this is not like other unwanted contact is the physics. You cannot step back, you cannot pull away, and reacting sharply while inverted risks a genuine injury to both of you — so your body overrides the objection to keep you both safe, and afterwards you will read your own stillness as consent. It was not; it was self-preservation. This is exactly why the protocol lives before the move rather than during it, and why the right move afterwards is not to relitigate what happened in the air but to change the practice: named contact, open space, and a different base. You do not owe anybody an accusation to be allowed to stop flying with them.

Here's the play. Agree the contact map before any new move, out loud, every time — where hands go, and where they do not. Practise in open space. Change base without explaining yourself; you never owe a reason for who holds your body.

Here are the words. “Before we try it — where are your hands going? Ribs and hips, nothing else, and tell me if that changes. — I'm going to work with Sam on this one, cheers.”

Remember this. Trust in acro means trusting the hold. It has never meant losing the right to say where the hands go.

### 6. “THEY GRABBED ME MID-SET”
*The Crowd / Performing Is Not Consent To Be Touched — clinically, performance boundary violation / audience contact.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The crowd, at about one in the morning, drunk and delighted and not thinking — grabbing a hoop, stepping into a fire circle for a photograph, putting hands on a stilt-walker's legs, reaching for an aerialist's foot.

Here's the scene. You are three minutes into a fire set and a man steps inside the circle to film himself next to you. He is about a metre from a lit wick moving at speed and he has no idea, and you now have to decide, mid-spin, whether to stop the set or keep going while somebody stands in the burn radius filming.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- People entering the performance space for photographs
- Props grabbed, touched or stepped on mid-set
- Costume, wings, stilts or rigging treated as public property
- No safety perimeter, no marshal, and no marked boundary
- Contact that would be obviously unacceptable if you were not performing
- The venue treats crowd management as your problem

Here's what's actually happening. Performing in public gets read as an open invitation, and for fire and aerial that misreading is a physical danger to the audience as well as to you — a person inside a fire circle is inside a burn radius, and a grabbed hoop or a knocked stilt takes a performer down onto hard ground. The professional answer is that crowd management is the event's job and a perimeter is a condition of the booking, not a nicety. The individual answer is that you are allowed to stop, and stopping is safer than performing around an obstacle.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The O check: “Is there a real perimeter and somebody holding it?” — If not, that is a condition to fix before you light, not a thing to absorb during.

Here's the dark reality. Stopping Is The Professional Response. The trained instinct is to keep going, because performers are conditioned that stopping is failure. Unlearn that one. A set continued around a member of the public standing in the burn radius ends, when it ends badly, with a burned stranger, an insurance claim, and a performer explaining why they did not stop — and the explanation will be that it would have been awkward. The second half of this belongs to the event: a perimeter and a marshal are a condition of booking for fire, and if a promoter will not provide them the correct answer is that you do not perform, not that you perform more carefully.

Here's your move. Make the perimeter and a named marshal part of accepting the booking. Stop the set the moment the boundary is broken — kill it, say why, restart when it is clear. Agree a stop signal with your safety before you start.

Here's exactly what to say. “I need a marshal and a three-metre perimeter or I can't light — that's not negotiable for fire. — [stopping] Set's paused, mate, you're standing in the burn circle. Back behind the line and we'll go again.”

And here's the truth. You are working, not on display. A set that has to be performed around an obstacle is a set that should have stopped.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Rigging and safety as leverage, circle gatekeeping, image and footage control.*

### 7. “I RIGGED IT MYSELF”
*The Rigger Who Owns The Point / Life Safety As Leverage — clinically, safety-critical dependency / technical gatekeeping.*

Here's who this is. The one with the hardware, the certification everyone assumes he has, and the only ladder. Rigs for everybody. Decides whose point goes up, when, and how high. Has never been asked to show anything.

Picture this. You want to fly and he is the only person here who can hang you. He is warm about it, competent-looking, and slightly dismissive when you ask what the anchor is rated to — he says he has been doing this since before you were spinning. Later he mentions, in the same warm voice, that some people get priority because they are part of the crew and you could be part of the crew.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- One person rigs everything and no second competent person exists on site
- Deflects or jokes when asked about ratings, inspection, or load path
- Rigging priority tracks social closeness rather than the running order
- No written check, no second pair of eyes, no logbook
- Access to flying is described as something you earn from him socially
- Asking technical questions is treated as an insult rather than as normal

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Aerial rigging is life safety — a single point failure is a fall from height — and the entire discipline's professional practice is built on redundancy, documentation, and independent inspection precisely because competence cannot be established by reputation. When a festival has one unsupervised rigger, two things are true at once: the technical risk is unmanaged, and a person now controls something people want badly enough to trade for. The second follows from the first. It is the accessible-platform pattern from the A.C.C.E.S.S. guide, with the stakes raised from a good view to your spine.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The W check: “Can this person tell me the load path and the rating, without irritation?” — Competence explains itself. Only insecurity gets offended by the question.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Two Failures Sharing One Cause. An unchecked rigger is both a fall risk and a coercion risk, and they are the same structural fact: nobody is watching, and the thing he controls is something you will trade for. That is how these situations run for years without a complaint — the people who could complain need the point, and the people who could check him were never appointed. If you take one thing from this page: a competent rigger will answer a technical question with relief, because it means somebody else in the room is thinking. The irritation is the signal. It means he has not been asked before, and that is the actual finding — not about him specifically, about the whole arrangement.

Here's what you do. Ask the three questions before you ever go up: what is the anchor, what is it rated to, and who checked it besides you. Do not fly on a point nobody but its own rigger has inspected. Find the second competent person on site on arrival — if there is not one, that is your answer about flying this weekend.

Say this, out loud. “Before I go up — what's the anchor and the rating, and who's your second pair of eyes on it? I ask everyone, it's not about you.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Redundancy is the whole discipline. A rig with one person and no check is not a rig, it is a bet.

### 8. “THE CLIP'S ALREADY UP”
*The Filmer / Your Body As Somebody Else's Content — clinically, image appropriation / non-consensual distribution.*

Let's talk about who does this. Always filming, often good at it, and operating on the assumption that a public performance is a public license. Sometimes a friend with a phone. Sometimes an account with two hundred thousand followers and a monetization deal.

Here's how it plays out. You burned last night. This morning there is a clip of it on an account you have never heard of, forty thousand views, your face clearly visible, your name nowhere, a brand tagged in the caption. The comments are mostly about your body. Somebody has stitched it. You did not know a camera was there, and the account owner, when you message, is genuinely baffled that you are not pleased.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Films without asking and treats performing as blanket consent
- Cannot say where footage will end up or who else gets it
- Credits the venue and the brand but not the performer
- Reacts to a takedown request as though you are being ungrateful
- Monetized account, unmonetized subject
- Costume, aerial, and fire performers get framed body-first

Here's what's really going on. Performing in public does not transfer your rights in your own image, and it never has — but the practical position is that enforcement is slow, cross-border, and expensive, so the operative protection is the one you set up in advance rather than the one you litigate afterwards. What makes this acute for flow and aerial performers is the combination of revealing costume, physical spectacle, and a distribution system that rewards exactly the framing you did not choose. The version that does real harm is not the friendly clip, it is the reposted one, where it travels without context, without your name, and often into spaces you would never have agreed to.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The L check: “Have I said, out loud, before I start, what I consent to being filmed and posted?” — Silence gets read as a yes by people who wanted a yes.

Here's what's underneath all of it. It Does Not Come Back Down. Once a clip is stitched, reposted, and mirrored, there is no version of this where you get it back. The realistic goal is containment, not removal — which is why the work is all front-loaded. State it before the burn, to the organizer and to anyone visibly filming, and get the organizer's media policy in writing when you agree to the gig. The second thing worth knowing is that this is often where a different kind of leverage starts: somebody holding footage of you, and mentioning it. Footage held over a performer is coercion, it is not a grey area, and the response is the same as for any other documentation threat — tell one person now, in writing, so the footage stops being a secret that can be spent.

Here's the play. Set your policy before you are lit, in one sentence, to the organizer and the circle. Get the event's media terms in writing when you take the booking. Watermark or claim your own footage first so the canonical version is yours.

Here are the words. “Happy to be filmed, not happy to be posted without a tag — credit me or don't put it up. Who's your media lead so I can send them that in writing?”

Remember this. A performance is not a release. Saying so takes six seconds and it is the only part of this you control.

### 9. “IT'S A GREAT LOOK FOR YOUR REEL”
*The Unpaid Booking / Spectacle For Exposure — clinically, labor exploitation / exposure as substitute payment.*

Here's who you're dealing with. A promoter with a budget for sound, lights, security and bar staff, and a line about community for the people doing the dangerous thing in the middle of the field.

Here's the scene. He wants three fire performers for the Saturday headline changeover. There is no fee. There are wristbands, and he says it in a way that makes the wristbands sound generous. He also mentions that a lot of people get seen at this one, and that the photographer is really good, and every single other trade at this event is being paid.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Wristbands offered as the fee for skilled, insured, dangerous work
- Every other supplier at the event is on a rate
- No contract, no call time in writing, no fuel or travel budget
- No mention of insurance, or an assumption that you carry it
- “Exposure”, “the community”, “great for your reel”
- The photographer is paid and you are not

Here's what's actually happening. Fire and aerial performance is skilled, insured, equipment-heavy, physically dangerous labour, and it is the single most reliably unpaid element of a festival's programme because performers love doing it and promoters know that. The comparison that settles it is on-site: the sound engineer, the bar staff, the security and the photographer are all on rates. If a budget exists for everyone who touches the event except the people doing the thing with fire in it, that is a decision rather than a constraint.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The L check: “Who else working this event is unpaid?” — If the answer is only the performers, the budget is not the problem.

Here's the dark reality. The Insurance Question Is The Real One. The part that outlives the argument about money is liability. Unpaid, uncontracted, uninsured fire performance means that when somebody is burned — you, a colleague, or a member of the public who walked into the circle — there is no contract establishing who was responsible for the perimeter, no employer, and frequently no cover. Promoters who do not pay performers also, reliably, have not thought about any of that, and the person standing in the footage holding the lit tool is the one who will be answering for it. Ask about public liability in the first conversation. The answer tells you everything about the event's professionalism, before money is even discussed.

Here's your move. Quote a rate like any other supplier and ask two questions early: what is the fee, and who carries public liability for this performance. Get the call time, perimeter and marshal in writing. Decline cheerfully and early rather than resentfully and late.

Here's exactly what to say. “My rate's [x] per performer plus fuel and travel. Also — who's carrying public liability for the fire, you or us? I need that confirmed before I commit.”

And here's the truth. Everyone else at this event is being paid. Your work is more dangerous than most of theirs, and a wristband is not a fee.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

### 10. “THE THIRTY-SECOND PRE-BURN”
*The Check You Run Every Time, Out Loud — clinically, safety protocol / pre-performance checklist.*

Here's who this is. For every fire, aerial, and prop performer, at every skill level. The veterans who have been hurt are the most rigorous people in any circle, and that is not a coincidence — it is what the injury taught them.

Picture this. You are about to go. There is a circle waiting, a running order slipping, and a version of you that wants to skip this because everything looks fine and everything usually is. This page exists because “usually” is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Fuel: which fuel, how old, where is the depot, is it lidded and upwind
- Tools: wicks sound, no loose hardware, chains and leaders intact
- Body: hair tied, no synthetics, nothing loose, sober enough to be doing this
- Safety: who, do they have duvetyne, can they say what they would do
- Stop: what is the call, who can make it, does everyone know it
- Rig: anchor, rating, second inspection, and a spotter who is watching you

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Checklists work for the same reason in every high-consequence discipline: they take the decision out of the moment and out of the social context. Reading a list is not a judgment call and cannot be read as a comment on the people present, which is precisely what makes it survivable under an impatient circle. Saying it out loud does the second job — it makes the check public, which means the circle adjusts to it rather than you adjusting to the circle, and it quietly gives permission to the newer performer behind you who was not going to ask.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The F check: “Did I run all six, this time, for this burn?” — Last burn's check does not cover this one. Fuel moves, safeties change, hardware fails between sets.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Check Is Also Social Cover. There is a second reason to do it out loud and it is not about fire. A performer who visibly runs their own checks, asks technical questions, and is comfortable being briefly annoying in front of a circle is a conspicuously bad target for every pattern in this guide — the mentor with the private lesson, the rigger who does not like being asked, the person who wants the footage. Not because it makes you unpleasant, but because all of those depend on you being someone who will not make a small public fuss to avoid an awkward moment. The checklist trains exactly that muscle, thirty seconds at a time, on something where you already know you are right.

Here's what you do. Run the six out loud, in the same order, every single time, until it is your known habit and the circle expects it. Teach it to the newest person there — that is how it becomes the culture rather than your quirk.

Say this, out loud. “Fuel, tools, body, safety, stop call, rig. — Right, what's the stop call tonight?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Every burned performer in this scene can tell you exactly which item they skipped. Run all six.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*Real jam culture versus a circle with an owner.*

### 11. “WHOSE CIRCLE IS THIS”
*Real Jam Culture Versus A Circle With An Owner — clinically, authentic practice space vs gatekept hierarchy.*

Let's talk about who does this. The good version has no owner, teaches sideways, and lets terrible beginners take up space. The other version has a centre, and the centre is a person, and everyone's position is measured by distance from him.

Here's how it plays out. Two circles at the same festival. In the first, a woman who has been spinning for twenty years spends eleven minutes helping somebody who picked up poi on Thursday, then goes back to her own practice and never mentions it again. In the second, everyone is subtly performing for one man at the edge, and when he leaves for ten minutes the energy of the whole circle changes, and everybody notices that it did.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Real: beginners get space and nobody is ranked
- Real: teaching flows in every direction, including upward
- Real: you can leave, be bad, or sit out with no cost
- Owned: one person's approval organizes the room
- Owned: access to gear, instruction, or gigs routes through him
- Owned: people describe the circle by his name

Here's what's really going on. A practice circle is one of the genuinely great things this scene produces — skill transmitted for free, across ages and levels, because somebody once did it for you. It is also a structure with an obvious failure mode: circles with a centre concentrate access, and concentrated access is what every pattern in this guide runs on. The test is identical to the one in every other guide here, which is not an accident: can you decline, be unimpressive, or leave without it costing you? In a real jam the answer is yes and nobody registers it. In an owned one, everybody registers everything.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/flow/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The W check: “If I sat out tonight and said nothing, would anything change for me here?” — If sitting out has a price, this circle has an owner.

Here's what's underneath all of it. When The Circle Is The Supply Chain. It gets serious when the circle is also where the gigs, the gear, and the instruction come from, because then leaving costs your practice and not just your evening. That is the configuration to watch for — not a charismatic person, which is normal and often lovely, but one person sitting across all of it at once. Everybody in that circle can feel it and nobody names it, because naming it is the thing that costs the most. If you are reading this and recognizing your own circle: the others already know, the conversation is easier than you think, and the fix is usually structural rather than confrontational. Two teachers. A second rigger. Somebody else who books.

Here's the play. Keep your practice plural on purpose — more than one circle, more than one teacher, more than one route to a gig. Then hand skill downward for free, because a circle where everybody teaches cannot develop a centre.

Here are the words. “Come spin with us, we're rubbish and nobody cares. — Here, let me show you the grip, it took me a year to work out and it shouldn't have.”

Remember this. A jam that needs a specific person to be good is not a jam. It is an audience with props.
