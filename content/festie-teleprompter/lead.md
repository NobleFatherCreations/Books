# L.E.A.D. — CAMP LEADS & ORGANIZERS
### Camp Leads & Organizers Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 259 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is L.E.A.D.: Camp Leads & Organizers Edition. The power you hold in your camp is real. Use it to protect people, not to own them.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **L** — Lead With Accountability. The standard you walk past is the standard you set. What you allow in your camp becomes what your camp is.
- **E** — Earn Trust, Don't Assume It. Your role gives you authority. Authority is not the same as trust. Trust is earned through what you do when it costs you.
- **A** — Access Creates Responsibility. You control housing, resources, placement, belonging. That control is a tool for protection — or a lever for harm.
- **D** — Debrief and Reflect. Hard situations happen in every camp. What you do with them afterward shapes whether they happen again.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*How camps attract and collect people — including predatory dynamics.*

### 1. “JOIN OUR FAMILY”
*Camp Recruitment and What It Signals — clinically, manufactured belonging / dependency creation.*

Here's who this is. Camps recruit new members every season. The pitch — community, infrastructure, belonging, access — is real when the camp is genuine and a trap when the camp is extractive. Understanding which kind of camp you are running starts with honest assessment.

Picture this. You recruited twelve new members this season. The pitch was warm, the infrastructure is real, and most of them are having a genuinely good experience. Three of them are doing significantly more camp labor than others and seem reluctant to raise concerns. One new member has been spending most of their time with a senior camp member in ways that look more like dependency than connection. You have not looked closely at these dynamics because camp is running well overall.

Here are the signs.

- New members doing disproportionate labor compared to senior members without discussion
- New members who seem reluctant to raise concerns or push back on anything
- Dependency dynamics forming between new members and specific senior members
- Camp culture that treats questioning the leadership as disloyalty rather than healthy input

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Camp leaders are often the last to see problematic dynamics because they are managing the whole operation and because the overall experience is positive. The patterns to watch for in your own camp are the same ones that signal predatory structures in other contexts: unequal contribution without discussion, reluctance to raise concerns, dependency dynamics, and cultures that treat questioning as betrayal. Your camp is only as healthy as what you are willing to look at.

Here's your check. The L check: “What dynamics in my camp am I avoiding looking at because things are running well overall?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Camp Health Self-Assessment. Honest questions to ask yourself about your camp: (1) Can any member raise a concern with me without social cost? (2) Is labor distributed equitably and has that been discussed explicitly? (3) Are there specific senior members whose relationships with new members I should look at more closely? (4) Do members feel free to leave the camp during the festival without it affecting their standing? (5) Has anyone ever pushed back on a camp decision and how was that received? (6) If I am honest, do any members seem afraid of me or of consequences from me? Your answers to these questions tell you what kind of camp you are running.

Here's what you do. Look honestly at the dynamics you have been avoiding. The health of your camp depends on what you are willing to see.

Say this, out loud. “"I want to do a camp culture check-in before the festival — I want to hear honestly from everyone about how things are going."”

Here's the truth underneath it. You cannot protect your camp from dynamics you are not willing to look at.

### 2. “THE PREDATOR IN YOUR CAMP”
*When Someone in Your Camp Is Causing Harm — clinically, safeguarding / internal accountability.*

Let's talk about who does this. Someone in your camp is causing harm to other members or to festival attendees. They may be a long-standing member. They may be well-liked. The situation is complicated. This page is about what you do anyway.

Here's how it plays out. A newer member comes to you with a concern about someone who has been in the camp for four years. She is specific about what she observed and what happened to her. You believe her. You also know that the person she is describing is one of your most reliable camp contributors, is well-liked, and has been part of the community for years. The situation has no easy resolution. But it does have a right next step.

Watch for these.

- A member with concerns is coming to you directly — this is the outcome of the trust you built
- The person being reported is well-integrated into the camp — this is what makes it hard
- The temptation to manage it informally to protect the camp's cohesion
- The understanding that how you respond right now defines your camp culture permanently

Here's what's really going on. Every camp leader will eventually face a situation where a trusted and integrated member has caused harm. The response to that situation defines the camp culture more than any other single event. Camps that protect long-standing members at the expense of the people they harmed build a culture where harm is tolerated when the person causing it is valuable enough. Camps that hold everyone to the same standard build a culture where people feel genuinely safe to report.

Ask yourself this. The L check: “Am I responding to this in a way that protects the person who came to me — or the person the report is about?” — the standard you set when it’s hard is the only standard that actually matters.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Responding To Harm Reports In Your Camp. When someone brings you a concern: (1) Believe them enough to take action, even if you cannot verify everything immediately. (2) Document what they told you in writing, with their knowledge and consent. (3) Separate the accused person from access to the reporting person immediately and without drama. (4) Consult with the event's safety or security team — this should not be handled entirely within the camp. (5) Do not share the reporter's identity with the accused person without the reporter's explicit consent. (6) Follow up with the reporter. (7) Make a decision about camp membership that reflects the seriousness of the report. There is no version where you do nothing and maintain the trust of your community.

Here's the play. Believe enough to act. Document. Separate. Consult. Follow up. Make a decision. In that order.

Here are the words. “Thank you for telling me. I am going to take this seriously. Here is what happens next.”

Remember this. The standard you set when it is hard is the only standard that matters.

### 3. “THIS IS HOW WE DO THINGS HERE”
*Camp Culture and What Builds It Badly — clinically, organizational culture / norm setting.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Camp culture is built by what you reward, what you tolerate, and what you address. It is built more by your behavior in difficult moments than by your stated values in calm ones. This page is about the specific ways camps build cultures that hurt people.

Here's the scene. Your camp has a norm of not talking about what happens in camp outside camp. It started as a way to protect privacy and maintain a sense of internal safety. Over time it has also functioned as a way to prevent accountability — things that happen in camp stay in camp because "that's our culture." Someone who was harmed by a camp member felt they could not report it externally because of this norm. The norm that was meant to protect people is now protecting the person who harmed them.

Here's what to watch for.

- Privacy norms that function as reporting suppression
- Loyalty norms that equate questioning the camp with betrayal of the community
- Contribution norms that create unpayable debt for new or less senior members
- Norms that make it harder rather than easier to raise concerns or exit the camp

Here's what's actually happening. Every camp norm was created for a reason and serves a function. The question is whether the function it currently serves is the one it was created for — or whether it has evolved into a protection mechanism for the camp's reputation rather than its people. Privacy norms that prevent reporting, loyalty norms that suppress dissent, contribution norms that extract labor — all of these start somewhere reasonable and can drift somewhere harmful. Examining your norms honestly is camp leadership.

Run the check. The E check: “Do my camp’s norms make it easier or harder for members to raise concerns and seek help when something has gone wrong?” — the norms you build in calm times are the ones that show up in the hard ones.

Here's the dark reality. Camp Norm Audit. Take each of your camp's major norms and ask: (1) What was this norm created for? (2) Is it currently serving that purpose? (3) Who does this norm protect when there is conflict? (4) Does this norm make it easier or harder for harmed people to seek help? Specific norms to examine: confidentiality norms, loyalty norms, contribution expectations, seniority hierarchies, and any norms around how conflict is handled. If your honest audit reveals that a norm is primarily protecting the camp's reputation or the comfort of senior members at the expense of accountability — it needs to change.

Here's your move. Audit your camp norms honestly. Change the ones that protect the camp at the expense of its people.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I want us to look at our camp norms together and be honest about whether they are serving the people in this camp or protecting the camp itself."”

And here's the truth. The norms you build in calm times define what happens in the hard ones.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*How camp culture gets built and what builds it badly.*

### 4. “I BUILT THIS CAMP”
*Founder Syndrome / When Leadership Becomes Ownership — clinically, founder syndrome / authority abuse.*

Here's who this is. The specific dynamic where a camp founder or long-standing leader begins to treat the camp as personal property — where their vision overrides community input, their comfort overrides member wellbeing, and accountability flows downward but not upward.

Picture this. You have run this camp for seven years. You have poured extraordinary amounts of time, money, and love into building it. You know the infrastructure better than anyone. You also notice that your decisions rarely get questioned, that members seem careful about how they raise concerns with you, and that the last two people who pushed back significantly on a camp decision are no longer in the camp. You tell yourself they were not a good fit. You have not considered whether there is a pattern.

Here are the signs.

- Decisions are made unilaterally and questioning them is received as ingratitude or disloyalty
- People who push back on decisions tend to leave or be pushed out
- Your comfort and vision consistently override member input in conflicts
- The camp's accountability structures apply to members but not clearly to you

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Founder syndrome in camp culture is the accumulation of unchecked authority over time — a process so gradual it is often invisible to the person experiencing it. The camp leader who started with genuine community intention can become, through years of carrying the weight of the camp, someone who confuses their investment in the camp with ownership of it and ownership of the people in it. The antidote is structural: accountability that includes you, decision-making that includes others, and the willingness to hear things about yourself that are hard.

Here's your check. The E check: “Am I accountable to my camp in the same ways my camp members are accountable to me?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Structural Accountability For Camp Leadership. Practical accountability structures for camp leads: (1) At least one other person in the camp has authority to raise concerns about your behavior without social cost. (2) Major camp decisions involve input from members before they are made, not just communication after. (3) There is a clear process for raising concerns about camp leadership specifically. (4) Camp finances are transparent to at least two members beyond yourself. (5) Exit from the camp is genuinely free — members can leave without social consequences. (6) You actively seek honest feedback after events rather than only welcoming positive feedback. These structures do not diminish your leadership. They make it trustworthy.

Here's what you do. Build accountability structures that include you. The camp that cannot hold its leader accountable cannot protect its members.

Say this, out loud. “I want to create a formal way for camp members to raise concerns about my decisions and my leadership. Here is what I am proposing.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The camp you built belongs to the community in it as much as to you. Lead accordingly.

### 5. “HOUSING AS LEVERAGE”
*When What You Provide Becomes Control — clinically, resource control / manufactured dependency.*

Let's talk about who does this. Camp leads who provide housing, infrastructure, or festival access control something that members genuinely need. That control becomes leverage — sometimes deliberately, sometimes not — when members feel they cannot exercise independence or raise concerns without risking what you provide.

Here's how it plays out. You provided accommodation and festival access to six members of your camp. You did this generously and genuinely. You also notice that the members whose accommodation you control raise concerns less often, agree with your decisions more consistently, and seem to navigate their interactions with you more carefully than others. You have not used housing as leverage explicitly. But the dynamic it creates is real regardless of your intentions.

Watch for these.

- Members whose accommodation or access you control behave differently from those who are independent
- Concerns from accommodation-dependent members are rare even when issues exist
- The implicit threat of losing housing is present even when you have never voiced it
- Your decisions as a camp lead are harder to push back on when members depend on you for basics

Here's what's really going on. Resource control creates leverage regardless of intent. When members depend on you for housing, festival access, or other basics, the power differential is real even if you never use it deliberately. People who need something from you will navigate that need carefully — which means raising concerns carefully, agreeing more often than they might otherwise, and staying in situations they might leave if they were independent. Acknowledging this dynamic is the beginning of mitigating it.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Do the members who depend most on what I provide feel the most or least free to push back on me when something’s wrong?” — generosity that creates dependency isn’t generosity. It’s leverage that happens to be warm.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Managing Resource Dependency Ethically. If you provide housing, access, or other resources to camp members: (1) Make explicit that their place in the camp and in the accommodation is not conditional on agreeing with your decisions. Say this out loud at the start of each season. (2) Create separate channels for concerns about camp decisions that do not go through you directly. (3) Be more careful about your behavior with members who depend on you, not less — the power imbalance means your actions land harder. (4) Never reference housing or access in conversations about camp disagreements or member behavior — even implicitly. (5) If someone wants to leave the camp, support that — the accommodation does not bind them.

Here's the play. Acknowledge the leverage your resources create and actively work against it rather than ignoring it.

Here are the words. “Your place here is not conditional on agreeing with me. I want to be clear about that and I want you to feel free to push back.”

Remember this. Generosity that creates dependency is not generosity. It is leverage that happens to be warm.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Power abuse camp leads may not recognize in themselves.*

### 6. “THE CAMP THAT OWNS YOU”
*When Camp Membership Becomes a Control Structure — clinically, high control group dynamics / BITE model.*

Here's who you're dealing with. This page is for camp leads who may be running — without full awareness — a camp that functions more like a high-control group than a genuine community. The signs are specific and worth examining honestly.

Here's the scene. You care deeply about your camp and your members. You also have high standards for participation and strong views about what it means to be a good member of the community you have built. You have removed members who were not a good fit. You discourage members from talking about camp dynamics with people outside the camp. You feel that deep loyalty to the camp is an important value. These individually can be reasonable. Together they warrant examination.

Here's what to watch for.

- Leaving the camp carries significant social cost — former members are characterized negatively
- Talking about camp dynamics with outsiders is discouraged as a loyalty issue
- Questioning camp leadership is framed as a character issue rather than legitimate input
- Deep loyalty to the camp is valued above members' individual wellbeing and judgment

Here's what's actually happening. High-control group dynamics in camps develop gradually and are usually invisible from the inside. The characteristics — discouraging outside input, penalizing exit, equating questioning with disloyalty, valuing camp loyalty above individual judgment — serve a genuine function for the person running the camp: they reduce conflict and maintain cohesion. They also prevent the accountability and self-correction that healthy communities require. If several of these are present in your camp, it is worth examining honestly.

Run the check. The L check: “Can members of my camp leave freely, talk to outsiders freely, and question decisions freely, without a cost attached to any of it?” — a community that needs control to hold together isn’t a community. It’s a structure.

Here's the dark reality. The Bite Model — A Self-Assessment Tool. The BITE Model (Behavior, Information, Thought, Emotional control) is used to assess high-control groups. Applied to camps: Behavior control — are member behaviors controlled beyond what the camp function requires? Information control — is information about the camp restricted from outsiders? Thought control — is questioning the camp framed as a thought problem in the member? Emotional control — are members' emotions managed through guilt, shame, or fear of loss? Answering yes to any of these is worth examining. freedomofmind.com has resources on high-control group dynamics that apply to informal communities as well as formal organizations.

Here's your move. Examine your camp honestly against these criteria. Make changes where the examination reveals them.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I want to make sure our camp is a place people feel genuinely free in — including free to leave, free to question, and free to talk to outsiders."”

And here's the truth. A community that requires control to maintain cohesion is not a community. It is a structure.

### 7. “WHO'S HOLDING THE CAMP MONEY”
*Dues, Floats And The Quiet Financial Hold — clinically, financial control / unaccounted collective funds.*

Here's who this is. Whoever collects for the generator, the truck, the food, the sound system and the pitch — often the founder, often for years, often with no accounting anybody has ever seen.

Picture this. Everyone pays in two hundred at the start of the season and nobody has ever seen a spreadsheet. It is almost certainly fine. It is also the reason that when somebody wants to leave the camp, or disagrees with him about something, there is an unspoken question about what happens to what they have put in, and nobody asks it out loud.

Here are the signs.

- One person collects and spends with no reporting
- No receipts, no ledger, no statement at the end of the season
- Members do not know what they have collectively paid for
- Assets — the shade structure, the sound system, the truck — are in one name
- Leaving the camp means losing money nobody can quantify
- Asking about it would be treated as an accusation

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Camp money is usually handled by a trusted person acting in good faith, and the problem is structural rather than moral: unaccounted collective funds create an exit cost that nobody can name, and an exit cost is leverage whether or not anybody intends to use it. It compounds with the housing-as-leverage dynamic elsewhere in this guide — if the same person controls where you sleep, what you have paid in, and the assets you helped buy, then disagreeing with them has a price that is real and unquantifiable, which is the worst combination.

Here's your check. The E check: “Could any member see where the money went, without asking a favour?” — If not, trust is doing a job that a spreadsheet should be doing.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Transparency Protects The Person Holding It. If you are the one holding the money, the accounting is for you before it is for anybody else. The moment a camp has a falling-out — and camps do — an unaccounted fund becomes the thing people reach for, and years of genuine generosity get reinterpreted in a group chat by people doing arithmetic from memory. A shared sheet, receipts photographed into a folder, and a five-minute end-of-season summary makes that impossible. And put the significant assets somewhere collective, because a shade structure everybody paid for that lives in one person's garage is an ownership question waiting for a bad year.

Here's what you do. Run a shared spreadsheet anybody can open, photograph receipts into a shared folder, and post a short end-of-season summary without being asked. Name a second signatory. Write down who owns the major assets and what happens to them if the camp splits.

Say this, out loud. “Putting the camp finances on a shared sheet this year — not because anything's wrong, just so it's never a question. Anyone want to be second pair of eyes on it?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Unaccounted money is an exit cost nobody can name, and it is leverage even when nobody means it to be.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*Accountability practices, conflict resolution, reporting.*

### 8. “THE CAMP SAFETY INFRASTRUCTURE”
*Practical Structures That Make Your Camp Genuinely Safe — clinically, preventive structure / protocol design.*

Let's talk about who does this. This page is practical. Specific structures that make the difference between a camp that talks about safety and a camp that actually provides it.

Here's how it plays out. The briefing before gates open is the most important ten minutes of the festival. What you say in that briefing — and what structures you establish in it — shapes what members feel able to do and say for the entire weekend. The camps with the best safety records are not the ones with the most rules. They are the ones where the culture of safety is established before anything difficult has happened.

Watch for these.

- Pre-festival briefing: consent expectations, reporting process, code words, daily check-in times
- Designated safety contact who is not you — someone members can go to with concerns about camp leadership
- Clear, simple reporting process that does not require members to confront the person they are concerned about
- Post-festival debrief: what happened, what worked, what needs to change next season

Here's what's really going on. The structures you build before the festival are the ones that work during it. A reporting process established in a calm briefing is infinitely more useful than one improvised in a crisis. A designated safety contact who is not you means members have a path for concerns about your behavior as well as others. A code word system means members can signal for help without having to explain themselves in the moment. These are not elaborate systems — they are ten minutes of planning that change everything.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Have I built safety infrastructure into my camp that works even when I am the problem?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Pre-Festival Camp Safety Briefing Template. Cover in your briefing: (1) Consent expectations — specific, not just "be cool." (2) Reporting process — how to raise a concern, who to go to, what happens next. (3) Code word — one word that means "I need help right now, come find me." (4) Daily check-in time — when and how. (5) Exit is free — anyone can leave the camp any time for any reason with no social cost. (6) What to do if something goes wrong and you are not sure who to tell. (7) Resources: Harm Reduction tent location, Ranger station location, medical tent location. Ten minutes. Do it before gates open. Every season.

Here's the play. Build the safety infrastructure before you need it. Run the briefing before gates open. Every season.

Here are the words. “Before gates open — ten minutes for our safety briefing. This is how we take care of each other this weekend.”

Remember this. The camps with the best safety records are the ones that built the infrastructure before anything went wrong.

### 9. “HOW YOU ACTUALLY REMOVE SOMEONE”
*The Conversation Camp Leads Avoid For Years — clinically, exclusion process / safe removal practice.*

Here's who you're dealing with. You, holding a decision you have already made and have been postponing for eleven months because you do not know how to do the next part without it becoming a scene.

Here's the scene. You know he cannot come back. You have known since August. What you do not have is a way of saying it that does not turn into a two-hour argument in front of the camp, or a group chat meltdown, or him turning up anyway because nothing was ever said clearly enough to be enforceable.

Here's what to watch for.

- The decision is made and only the delivery is missing
- You are waiting for one more incident to make it defensible
- The camp is quietly organizing itself around avoiding him
- People have started not coming, and you know which people
- There is no written camp agreement to point at
- You are planning to do it in person, alone, at the event

Here's what's actually happening. Most camp exclusions go badly for procedural reasons rather than emotional ones: delivered verbally, at the event, by one person, with no written basis and no defined scope, which makes them arguable, unenforceable and humiliating for everyone present. Every organization that does this well does the same four things — decide off-site, deliver in writing, be specific and short about what and for how long, and do not debate. The last one is the hardest and the most important: the decision is not being negotiated, it is being communicated.

Run the check. The A check: “Is this decided, and is it written down?” — If it is not written, it is a disagreement rather than a decision, and it will be treated as one.

Here's the dark reality. Do It Before The Event, Not At It. Doing it on site is the mistake that produces every bad outcome. He arrives having paid, packed and travelled, in front of an audience, with nowhere to go and nothing to lose — and that is the version that becomes a confrontation, or a scene the whole camp remembers, or a safety issue at two in the morning. Send it weeks out, in writing, and tell the event's safeguarding lead that you have done it so somebody official knows before he turns up. Also protect the person who raised it: never name them, and never let the reasoning be specific enough to identify them, because the single biggest deterrent to anyone else ever reporting anything is watching what happened to the last person who did.

Here's your move. Decide with two other people, not alone. Send it in writing weeks before the event, short, specific, with a clear scope and no invitation to discuss. Tell the event's safeguarding lead. Brief the camp on what is happening without the detail and without names.

Here's exactly what to say. “You're not camping with us this year. This is a decision, not a discussion, and I'm not going to debate it over messages. If you turn up, we'll be asking the event to handle it.”

And here's the truth. Waiting for one more incident means one more person gets hurt. Decide off-site, write it down, and do not debate it.

### 10. “TWO OF MY PEOPLE, ONE STORY EACH”
*A Report About Somebody Inside Your Own Camp — clinically, internal report handling / conflict of interest.*

Here's who this is. You, holding a disclosure from one camp member about another, both of whom you like, in a structure with no process, no training and no separation of roles whatsoever.

Picture this. She told you what happened on Friday. He has been your friend for nine years. You are now the investigator, the judge, the friend of both parties and the person who has to keep this camp functioning for another two days, and you have no idea what you are doing.

Here are the signs.

- You are close to both parties and there is no independent person
- Your first instinct is to get them in a room together
- You are trying to establish what really happened
- The camp has begun taking sides before anything is decided
- No notes have been taken and it is already three days ago
- The reporting person has asked you not to do anything

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Camp leads are handed reports with none of the structure that makes handling them possible, and the two most common instincts are both wrong. Mediation — getting both parties in a room — is actively harmful where there is any power imbalance or alleged harm, because it requires the person who was harmed to negotiate with the person who harmed them. And investigating, in the sense of establishing the truth, is not your role and you are not equipped for it; your role is immediate safety, accurate notes, and routing it to someone independent.

Here's your check. The L check: “Am I trying to find out what really happened?” — That is not the job. Safety, notes, and handing it on is the job.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What The Reporting Person Controls And What They Do Not. Two things have to be held together and it is uncomfortable. The reporting person controls their own disclosure — who they tell, whether they make a formal report, whether they involve police — and you do not override that. But you do control your camp, and if there is an ongoing safety risk you can act on that without their consent, as long as you do it in a way that does not identify them. Separating sleeping arrangements, changing a rota, or removing somebody can all be done as a camp decision rather than as a response to a specific report. And write the notes tonight, factual and dated, because in four months this will come back and memory is not evidence.

Here's what you do. Ask what she wants and what she needs to be safe tonight, and do the safety part immediately. Take dated factual notes the same day. Separate them physically without announcing why. Route it to the event's safeguarding lead. Do not mediate, do not investigate, and do not tell the camp.

Say this, out loud. “Thank you for telling me. Two things: what do you need tonight to feel safe, and what do you want to happen next — that part's yours to decide. I'm going to move some sleeping arrangements either way, and I won't say why to anyone.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You are not an investigator and you are certainly not a mediator. You are safety tonight, notes today, and a handover tomorrow.

## Protection — what real leadership looks like

*Building a camp that is genuinely safe for everyone in it.*

### 11. “THE CAMP LEAD WHO DOES THE WORK”
*What Genuine Leadership in This Context Looks Like — clinically, accountable leadership / repair practice.*

Let's talk about who does this. This final page is about what it looks like when camp leadership is done right — not as a set of rules but as a practice of genuine care that includes accountability, humility, and the willingness to be wrong.

Here's how it plays out. The best camp leads you know have several things in common: they take concerns seriously regardless of who brings them. They hold themselves to the same standards they hold their members. They have built ways for members to push back on their decisions. They have removed people from their camps when those people caused harm — even when it was complicated. They are honest about the limits of their own judgment. They debrief after hard weekends and make changes.

Watch for these.

- Takes concerns seriously and follows up on every single one regardless of who brought it
- Holds themselves to the same behavioral standards as every other camp member
- Has built accountability structures that include themselves not just their members
- Makes decisions about camp membership based on behavior not on seniority or contribution

Here's what's really going on. Genuine camp leadership is a practice of consistent accountability — to your members, to the community, and to yourself. The camp leads who build the safest camps are not the ones who have the best rules. They are the ones who model what accountability looks like, who take the same risks with their own behavior that they ask their members to take, and who treat every person in the camp as someone whose wellbeing they are genuinely responsible for.

Ask yourself this. The D check: “When I look at my camp honestly — am I the kind of leader I would want my members to describe honestly, if they were asked?” — the best camp lead isn’t the one who never gets it wrong. It’s the one who keeps working to get it more right.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Ongoing Practice Of Accountable Leadership. Ongoing practices for camp leaders: (1) Post-event honest debrief with at least one person who will tell you the truth. (2) Annual review of camp norms — are they still serving the people in the camp? (3) Regular check-ins with members specifically about how safe they feel in the camp. (4) Seek out critical feedback actively — not just welcome positive feedback. (5) When you get it wrong, say so and change. Out loud. To your members. (6) Invest in your own learning about group dynamics, safeguarding, and consent culture. The work of being a good camp lead is not finished when the event ends. It continues in how you build for the next one.

Here's the play. Do the ongoing work of accountable leadership. It is never finished and it is always worth it.

Here are the words. “After every event I want to hear honestly about what worked and what I could do better. I mean that.”

Remember this. The best camp lead is not the one who never gets it wrong. It is the one who keeps doing the work to get it more right.
