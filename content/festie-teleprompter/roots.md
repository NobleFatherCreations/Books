This is R.O.O.T.S.: BIPOC Attendees Edition. The scene calls itself a family. This guide is about the weekends where that is completely true and the moments where it is not — and about having a plan for both.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **R** — Read The Room Before It Reads You. Who is here, who is staff, who would be believed. Knowing the answer on Thursday is worth more than deciding it at two in the morning.
- **O** — Ownership Of Your Body And Your Hair. Nobody touches you without asking. Not your hair, not your skin, not for a photo. Being at a festival is not a waiver.
- **O** — Options That Do Not Assume Police. The standard escalation ladder ends at security and police. Yours may need to stop earlier. Build the ladder that is actually safe for you.
- **T** — Tokenizing Is An Opening Move. Exoticization, the compliment about your look, the fascination — these are approaches. Warmth aimed at a category is not warmth aimed at you.
- **S** — Solidarity Is Findable. Affinity camps, crews and regulars exist at nearly every festival. Finding them on day one changes the whole weekend.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*Fetishization as an approach, tokenizing warmth, the collector.*

### 1. “I'VE ALWAYS WANTED TO”
*The Collector / Fetishization As An Approach — clinically, racial fetishization / objectification as opening move.*

Here's who this is. Intense, immediate, and full of enthusiasm about a version of you he assembled before you spoke. Compliments a category. Has a type and tells you so as though it were a gift he is handing over.

Picture this. He has been talking for four minutes and every single thing he has said has been about how you look, in language that keeps reaching for somewhere — a country, a continent, a word he clearly thinks is a compliment. He asks where you are really from. He tells you he has always wanted to be with someone like you. There is no point in this conversation where you have been a person with a name.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Compliments a category rather than anything you chose, said, or made
- “Where are you really from”, and a guess afterwards
- Announces a preference for people like you as though it flatters you
- Reaches for your hair, your skin, or a photo within minutes
- Knows nothing about you and is nonetheless certain about you
- Gets cold fast when you are ordinary rather than the thing he pictured

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Fetishization does the same work as any other idealization in this book: it replaces a person with a symbol, and symbols are not allowed boundaries. It is also unusually efficient as an opening move, because it arrives disguised as enthusiastic admiration, which makes declining it look like you are the one being difficult. The practical tell is not the subject matter, it is the direction of attention — someone interested in you asks questions and updates on the answers; someone interested in a category delivers conclusions and does not update at all, because your actual self is not the point and is in fact slightly in the way.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The T check: “Has he asked me anything, or only told me things?” — Interest asks. Collecting announces.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Being A Type Means Being Interchangeable. The thing worth understanding early is what fetishization does to the aftermath. Somebody attracted to a category rather than a person has, by definition, no particular attachment to you — which means the ordinary brakes that come with actually knowing someone are not installed. It also gives him a ready explanation for anything that goes wrong: a misunderstanding, a cultural thing, you took it the wrong way. And if you raise it afterwards in a scene that prides itself on openness, you will frequently be met with the suggestion that he was just really into you and you have made it about race. That pre-built defense is part of why this approach gets used. None of it means you have to litigate it. You can simply leave a conversation that has never once been about you.

Here's what you do. Test it once, cheaply: give him a real detail about yourself that has nothing to do with how you look, and watch whether he picks it up or steers back. Then leave, without a verdict and without a debate — you are not obliged to prove it to him or to anyone standing nearby.

Say this, out loud. “I'm going to find my people — have a good one. — [if pressed] Not interested, mate. That's the whole answer.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Someone who wanted you would ask you something. You are allowed to walk away from an audition you never entered.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Hypervisibility, unsolicited touch, colorblind framing that erases what happened.*

### 2. “CAN I JUST TOUCH IT”
*The Reacher / Your Body As Public Property — clinically, non-consensual contact / bodily autonomy violation.*

Let's talk about who does this. Frequently a stranger, frequently delighted, frequently already reaching while asking — which means the question was decorative. Often drunk, often a woman, often genuinely baffled that this lands badly.

Here's how it plays out. Her hand is already in your hair. She is saying it is amazing and asking how you get it like that and whether it is all yours, and three people are watching and smiling, and the entire social weight of this moment is arranged so that the rude person in it would be you. You have about two seconds and the good options all cost something.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Asks while already reaching, or does not ask at all
- Treats your hair, skin, or features as a topic for public discussion
- Does it in front of people, so objecting has an audience cost
- “Is it real”, “how do you get it like that”, “I've never touched”
- Wants a photo with you rather than of the moment
- Reacts to a no as though you have been unkind to them

Here's what's really going on. Unsolicited touching is a boundary violation regardless of intent, and the specific version aimed at Black women's hair is documented well enough to have prompted its own body of workplace and school policy. The mechanism at a festival is the environment: everyone is close, warm, uninhibited and touching each other constantly, which supplies a ready-made explanation for the contact and makes objecting feel like importing outside-world rules into a space that is supposed to be free of them. That framing is the problem. A culture of physical affection is consent-based or it is not a culture of affection, it is just a culture where some people get touched.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Did the question stop the hand?” — If the hand kept moving, it was not a question, and you owe it nothing.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Cost Is The Accumulation. Any one of these is small. The reason it belongs in a safety guide is what a weekend of them does: by Sunday you have absorbed forty small interactions in which your body was treated as a shared object and you were required to be gracious about each one, and that steady grinding down of your standing to say no is precisely the ground condition every predator in this book is looking for. Someone who has watched you accept unwanted contact all weekend, smiling, has learned something about you that they should not have been allowed to learn. Practising the flat no on the small stuff is not pettiness. It is keeping the muscle available for the night you need it.

Here's the play. Block with your body rather than your words — step back and put a hand up before you speak. Then a flat, short sentence with no apology and no explanation, and move on immediately so there is no space for a discussion.

Here are the words. “No — don't touch my hair. — [flat, no smile, then turn away] Anyway. Have you seen where the water point is?”

Remember this. You are not a texture. A space that needs you to be gracious about being handled is not actually free for you.

### 3. “IT'S JUST A COSTUME”
*Your Culture Worn Around You / Appropriation As Ambience — clinically, cultural appropriation / environmental othering.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Not a predator. Hundreds of people, none of whom thought about it, wearing your grandmother's dress, a headdress, a bindi, a keffiyeh, a sacred pattern, as festival wear — and one person who will argue with you about it if you say anything.

Here's the scene. You are walking to the second stage and you pass a war bonnet, two bindis and somebody's idea of a kimono, and you are doing the thing you always do, which is deciding forty separate times an hour whether to spend energy on it. Then somebody in your own camp asks you — warmly, as the resident expert — whether her outfit is okay, and now it is your job.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Sacred or ceremonial items worn as decoration
- You are asked to adjudicate, because you are the nearest person of colour
- Objecting is treated as policing rather than as information
- “It's appreciation” offered as though it settles the question
- The energy cost is continuous and invisible to everyone around you
- Somebody wants absolution more than they want an answer

Here's what's actually happening. This belongs in a safety guide for one reason: the cumulative cost. Any single instance is minor and arguable. Forty a day, every day, while also being asked to educate people about it, is a continuous background tax on the same attention you need for everything else in this guide, and depleted attention is the precondition every other pattern relies on. There is also a specific trap in being made the arbiter — once you are the person who rules on this, every conversation about it becomes work you did not agree to do, performed for an audience that wants reassurance rather than information.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The T check: “Am I being asked for information, or for permission?” — Information is a conversation. Permission is a job, and you can decline it.

Here's the dark reality. You Are Not The Ethics Department. The sharpest version is the person who will not let it go until you approve. They are not asking what you think; they are looking for a member of the group to sign it off so that the discomfort goes away, and your reluctance reads to them as you being difficult rather than as you being tired. That is the moment to notice that a request for education has become an obligation to perform it, in public, for free, about your own culture, at a festival you paid to attend. You are allowed to hand it back. Declining to be the arbiter is not conceding the argument; it is refusing an unpaid role.

Here's your move. Decide your own policy in advance and apply it without deliberating each time — most people pick one thing they will always say something about and let the rest go, and that is a legitimate and sustainable choice. Hand the adjudication back with a resource rather than a ruling.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm not the person to green-light that, and I'd rather not be. Have a look up why it's a thing — there's loads written by people who actually want to explain it.”

And here's the truth. Deciding forty times a day whether to say something is the cost nobody else can see. Spend it on purpose, not by default.

### 4. “SO WHAT HAVE YOU GOT”
*Assumed To Be The Dealer, The Staff, Or The Security — clinically, racialized misidentification / role assumption.*

Here's who this is. Strangers, all weekend, who have decided what you are doing here before speaking to you. Sometimes it is a drinks order. Sometimes it is a bin bag handed to you. Sometimes it is somebody asking, very directly, what you are holding.

Picture this. You are standing near the edge of a crowd, doing nothing, and a man comes up close and asks quietly what you have got. Then later somebody hands you an empty cup as though you were staff. Then somebody asks where the toilets are in the tone people use with stewards, and you are wearing exactly what everybody else is wearing.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Approached about substances by strangers who have no reason to
- Mistaken for staff, security or crew while in ordinary clothes
- Spoken to in an instructional register by people who want service
- Security attention that does not match what you are doing
- It happens repeatedly, to you and not to the people you came with
- Correcting it makes the other person embarrassed, and then annoyed

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Each instance is small and the pattern is not, and there is a specific risk hiding inside the most common version. Being repeatedly approached about substances by strangers puts you in conversations about drugs, in public, that you did not initiate and did not want — and undercover officers work festivals. A person who is approached fifteen times a weekend has fifteen chances to be observed having exactly the wrong conversation, and the observation does not come with the context that you were approached rather than approaching.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The R check: “Am I in a conversation I did not start, about something I am not doing?” — End it flatly and physically, not with an explanation.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Conversation You Did Not Start. This is where the misidentification stops being an indignity and becomes a legal exposure. The differential-enforcement problem from the security page applies directly: if a conversation about substances goes wrong, whose account gets believed is not a neutral question, and being the person who was standing there is a worse position than being the person who walked off. So the move is not wit and it is not a lecture about assumptions. It is ending the exchange in under three seconds and physically relocating, every time, no matter how tedious that becomes by Sunday — and having witnesses who can say you walked away.

Here's what you do. Two flat words and a step away. Do not engage, do not joke, do not correct at length. Stay near the people you came with, because the approaches drop off sharply when you are visibly in a group.

Say this, out loud. “Nothing, mate. — [stepping away] Not staff, sorry. — [to your group] That's the fourth one today, I'm sticking with you lot.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You did not start that conversation and you do not owe it a reply. Walking off is the whole technique.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Security called on you, being disbelieved, escalation routes that are not safe.*

### 5. “THEY CALLED SECURITY ON YOU”
*When The Escalation Route Points At You — clinically, differential enforcement / weaponized reporting.*

Let's talk about who does this. Someone in a dispute with you who has correctly worked out that involving staff is a move that costs them nothing and costs you a great deal. Sometimes deliberate. Sometimes just a person who reached for the phone without thinking about who would be believed.

Here's how it plays out. There was an argument at your camp. It was nothing. Now there are two radios and a torch and the questions are coming at you rather than at the person who started it, and the specific thing you notice, standing there, is that everybody is being perfectly polite and the outcome has somehow already been decided before anybody asked you anything.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Security arrives asking you the questions rather than asking everyone
- The other party is treated as the reporter rather than as a participant
- Your tone becomes the subject rather than what happened
- Someone reaches for staff or police unusually fast over something small
- You are asked to leave to resolve it while the other party stays
- You are, or are close to, the only one of you in the immediate area

Here's what's really going on. This book's standard advice is to escalate — tell a ranger, tell security, involve staff. That advice assumes the escalation route is neutral, and for a lot of readers it is not: the same call produces different outcomes depending on who is standing where, and a police involvement that resolves one person's evening can be a materially dangerous event for another. Saying so is not cynicism about festival staff, most of whom are decent and trying. It is the difference between a safety plan built for an average reader and one built for you. A plan that ignores this is not neutral — it is a plan that works for other people.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “If this escalates to staff, who does that help?” — Ask it before the moment, because in the moment there is no time.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Build A Ladder That Stops Where You Need It To. The practical version of this is not avoid all staff — it is have more rungs before that one, and know which specific humans they are. Camp leads, welfare, the harm-reduction tent, an artist liaison, a named accessibility or community lead: these are escalation routes that are generally safer and almost always more effective than a radio call, and they exist at nearly every festival. The other half is witnesses. The single biggest change in how one of these encounters goes is whether two other people are standing there, and the second biggest is whether somebody is recording. Neither requires a confrontation. Both require having thought about it before the torch is pointing at you, which is the entire reason this page exists.

Here's the play. On arrival, get two names that are not security — welfare and a camp or community lead. In any dispute, get a witness beside you before it escalates, stay still and boring, and write it down immediately afterwards with the time and the names. Do not resolve it alone in the dark.

Here are the words. “I'm happy to talk about this with welfare present. — [to a friend] Stand here with me a minute, would you.”

Remember this. Escalation is a tool, not a reflex. Know which rung is safe for you before you need to climb.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The escalation ladder you can actually use, documentation, the anchor crew.*

### 6. “THE LADDER AND THE ANCHOR”
*Building The Plan That Actually Fits You — clinically, personal risk mapping / escalation planning.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For anyone whose safety plan cannot be copied straight out of a generic guide — which, at some events, is most of the people reading this one.

Here's the scene. Thursday afternoon, before anything has happened, while the site is calm and the people you would need are all findable and not busy. Twenty minutes now buys you the thing that is impossible to assemble at two in the morning with a torch in your face, which is a set of names and a route you already trust.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Two named non-security contacts: welfare, and a camp or community lead
- Two people on site who will physically stand next to you on request
- One person off-site who expects a check-in and knows where you are
- Your own transport, or a lift that is not in anybody else's control
- A note app for time, place, names — written the same hour, not the next day
- The affinity camp or crew, found on day one rather than on the bad night

Here's what's actually happening. Everything in this guide is cheaper to arrange in advance than to invent under pressure, and the reason is the same one that runs through this whole book: the moment is designed to be one where you cannot think. A prepared ladder converts a judgment call into a lookup. The witness rule does the most work of anything on that list — accounts of these encounters diverge sharply depending on whether anyone else was present, and a second person standing beside you changes both what happens and what can be said about it afterwards. None of this is about expecting trouble. It is the same logic as knowing where the medical tent is.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The R check: “Can I name my two non-security contacts right now?” — If not, that is the twenty minutes, and it is best spent on Thursday.

Here's the dark reality. Write It The Same Hour. Documentation is the part people skip and the part that matters most, because these situations are decided later by whose account is more specific. A dated note written within the hour — time, location, names, what was actually said, who else was there — outperforms any amount of remembered detail weeks on, and it does not require anyone to find you credible in the moment. Send it to someone off-site so it exists outside this weekend and outside your own phone. It takes ninety seconds. Do it even when you are fairly sure nothing will come of it, because the cases where something does come of it are exactly the ones where you did not expect it to.

Here's your move. Get your two non-security names in the first hour on site. Agree the stand-next-to-me signal with two friends. Write anything that happens down the same hour and send it off-site. Keep your own way home.

Here's exactly what to say. “Who's the welfare lead this year, and how do I reach them directly? — If I text you a pin, just come stand next to me, you don't have to say anything.”

And here's the truth. A plan built for the average reader is not built for you. Build yours on Thursday, out of real names.

### 7. “IF YOUR STATUS IS THE RISK”
*Immigration Status, Visas, And Why The Advice Changes — clinically, immigration exposure / differential consequence.*

Here's who this is. For anyone whose worst-case outcome from any encounter with authority is not a fine or a night in a cell but a removal from the country, a visa consequence, or a family separation.

Picture this. Everything else in this book says get help, tell security, make a report. For you, every one of those sentences has a second half nobody wrote down, and you have been quietly translating the whole guide as you read it. This page does the translating out loud.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Any police contact carries a consequence unrelated to what happened
- You avoid medical and welfare because you are unsure who they report to
- You do not carry ID, or you carry documents you cannot afford to lose
- Someone knows your status and has mentioned it more than once
- A dispute would end with you being asked questions the other party will not be asked
- No one at home knows exactly where you are this weekend

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A safety guide that assumes a neutral escalation route is giving some readers a plan that raises their risk. Medical and welfare at festivals are generally not immigration functions and treating people is their job, but the calculation is legitimately different, and pretending otherwise is not kindness. The practical answer is the same as the escalation-ladder page, with more rungs: welfare, harm reduction, camp and community leads, artist liaison — all of which resolve most situations and none of which is a police interaction.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The O check: “What is my worst-case outcome from this escalation — not the average person's?” — Plan from your answer, not from the generic one.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Status As Leverage. The thing to watch for is somebody who knows and mentions it. Status is among the most effective coercive levers that exists — it needs no threat, it silences reporting completely, and it is why people in precarious immigration positions are targeted deliberately for unpaid work, for sexual coercion, and for keeping quiet about both. If somebody has brought up your status in a conversation that was about something else, that is not conversation, and you are not the first person they have done it to. Get advice from an immigration charity rather than from anyone in the situation, and get it before you need it.

Here's what you do. Know the non-police rungs by name before you need them. Carry a copy rather than an original document, and leave the original somewhere safe. Have one person off-site who knows where you are and who to call. Find an immigration advice line now and save the number, while nothing is happening.

Say this, out loud. “I'd rather handle this with welfare than with anyone else — can you get them? — [if your status is raised] We're not talking about that. What's actually the issue here?”

Here's the truth underneath it. The standard advice was written for somebody whose worst case is a fine. Build the version whose worst case is yours.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*Affinity space, real solidarity, and the festivals that get it right.*

### 8. “FINDING THE OTHERS”
*Affinity Space, And The Festivals That Get It Right — clinically, affinity community / protective social infrastructure.*

Let's talk about who does this. The camp with the sound system and the good food that has been coming for eleven years. The crew who clocked you at the gate and nodded. The promoters and collectives who built something deliberately and did the work of making it real.

Here's how it plays out. Saturday, and you have spent two days being the only one in every frame, doing the low-level arithmetic that costs more energy than anyone who does not do it understands. Then you walk past a camp and three people look up and one of them says come and eat something, and you feel your shoulders drop about two inches, and you had not registered they were up.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Real: you are not explained, introduced as a demographic, or asked to represent
- Real: you can be ordinary, annoying, or quiet with no change in standing
- Real: people share practical information without ceremony — who is sound, who is not
- Performative: your presence gets mentioned to other people as a credit
- Performative: the event's diversity is discussed with you rather than built
- Performative: you are the only one there and nobody has ever asked why

Here's what's really going on. Affinity space is protective for reasons that are entirely practical rather than sentimental: it replaces hypervisibility with ordinariness, it supplies witnesses who do not need the situation explained to them, and it is where the informal warnings circulate — which promoter is sound, which crew to avoid, what happened last year. Every mechanism in this guide works better on someone isolated, and the fetishizer, the reacher and the weaponized report all operate most freely on a person with nobody standing near them. This is also the page where the scene deserves credit: there are collectives and festivals doing this properly, and they are findable.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/roots/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The S check: “Have I found my people here, or am I still doing this alone?” — Day one. Not the night it goes wrong.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Shoulders Down Is Data. Worth naming plainly: the constant low-level vigilance has a cost, and because it is continuous it becomes invisible to the person paying it. You will not notice you have been braced all weekend until you stop, and the stopping is what tells you. That fatigue is not fragility and it is not you being difficult — it is the predictable result of running a background process everyone around you has switched off. It also degrades judgment, the same way sensory load and sleep loss do elsewhere in this book, which means finding the people who let you put it down is not a comfort measure. It is a safety measure, and it belongs on day one alongside knowing where medical is.

Here's the play. Find the camp, the crew, or the collective on day one, before you need anything. Ask the regulars what they know — the informal information network is the best safety infrastructure at any festival. And go back to the events that actually built something, because they are the ones that respond to being supported.

Here are the words. “First time at this one — who's sound round here, and is there anything I should know? — Come find us at the camp with the blue flag, we're cooking Saturday.”

Remember this. You came for the music, not to be a subject of interest. Find the people who let you be ordinary, on day one.
