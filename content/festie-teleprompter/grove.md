# G.R.O.V.E. — WOMEN ATTENDEES
### Women Attendees Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 249 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is G.R.O.V.E.: Women Attendees Edition. A field guide for women in the festival world — written by festies, for festies.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **G** — Gut Check. What's your body actually saying right now? Tight chest, held breath, urge to get small — that's data, not drama.
- **R** — Rapid Pace? Is this moving faster than YOU decided? Speed set by someone else is the first red flag. Real connection can survive a slower pace.
- **O** — Out Alone? Where are your people? Can you find camp without his help? Separation from your crew is how isolation starts.
- **V** — Verify Later. You know their festival self. That is not the same as knowing them. Three days of magic is not three months of character.
- **E** — Exit Is Mine. Can you walk away freely right now? If leaving feels complicated — that IS the answer.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*How they hook you before you see it coming.*

### 1. “YOU'RE MY PERSON”
*The Festival Boyfriend / Instant Soul Sister — clinically, love bombing / accelerated attachment.*

Here's who this is. Full send from minute one. Flowers in your hair before you know their last name. Telling strangers you're "festival family" fourteen hours in. Could be any gender. Charming as hell — and that's exactly what makes it work.

Picture this. You met him at the silent disco Friday night and by Saturday breakfast he's saved your spot, got you coffee, and told his whole camp you're "the one." He keeps saying "we" — we should catch that set, we should stay together tonight. By Sunday you haven't seen your actual people since gates opened. You feel chosen, warm, slightly dizzy. He wants to share his tent because "the connection is just too real, babe."

Here are the signs.

- "I've never felt this way" before he knows your last name
- "We" language before you agreed to a "we"
- You feel electric and slightly like you can't catch your breath — that rush is chemistry, not character
- You're suddenly part of his plans, his camp, his crew — you didn't choose that, it just happened

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Love bombing is overwhelming affection deployed fast — before you can actually clock who someone is. The festival bubble supercharges it: nervous system already wide open, time doing that weird thing, culture normalizing fast intimacy. He might not know he's running this pattern. Doesn't matter — the effect is the same. You feel bonded to someone you've known for one sunrise. Chemistry is real. It is not the same as character. Pace is protection.

Here's your check. The R check: “Is this moving faster than I chose?” — if the speed is his, not yours, that's your first signal.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Isolator. Some festival boyfriends are deliberately running a playbook. By Sunday you're in their camp, dependent on their phone charger, can't find your friends, and your own tent feels far away. This is not accidental. Isolation is the setup. Connection is the costume. If you're three days in and can't easily get back to your own people without asking him — that IS the situation.

Here's what you do. Enjoy the spark. Let the pace be yours. A connection that's real will still be real after you check in with camp.

Say this, out loud. “I'm feeling this energy — I also need to flow freely this weekend. Let's vibe without locking anything down.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Real connection can survive a slower pace. Anyone who can't wait is applying pressure, not offering magic.

### 2. “I GOT YOU, BABE”
*The Gifter With a Ledger — clinically, reciprocity trap / loan-sharking gifts.*

Let's talk about who does this. The generous one. Always has extra. Covers your wristband, feeds you, shares her stash, lends her charger. Feels like pure festie abundance — until you try to leave or say no.

Here's how it plays out. She covered your wristband at the gate when your card declined. "Don't stress, we're family." Over two days she shares her hammock, food, connects. Every time you try to wander toward your crew she gets that look — "After everything I've done for you?" By Sunday you're in her orbit, your new friends are her people first, and the idea of leaving feels like doing something wrong.

Watch for these.

- Gifts arrive before any actual friendship — fast, often in front of witnesses
- Every generous act gets quietly logged and referenced when you try to have independence
- You feel a vague obligation you didn't agree to and can't name
- Attempts to spend time with your own people are met with hurt feelings

Here's what's really going on. Reciprocity is one of the deepest human wiring patterns — when someone gives us something we feel compelled to give back. Festival gifting culture makes this extra potent because giving IS sacred here. The predatory version flips it: the gift is an investment with a return expected. When the "return" gets called in, saying no feels ungrateful. That feeling of owing something you never agreed to? That was engineered.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Am I still connected to my actual people — or has dependency on her replaced that?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. When The Gift Is The Substance. Most dangerous version: the person who provides your substances becomes the person you have to stay near. "I'll get you sorted" sounds generous until you've been in their camp for 18 hours, your phone is dead, and leaving means losing access. This is dependency grooming. Your consciousness should never be owned by someone who wants something from you. Test your stuff. Know your source. Independence is harm reduction.

Here's the play. Receive kindness warmly. Leave freely. Gratitude is a feeling — not a transaction that purchases your loyalty.

Here are the words. “That was genuinely kind — thank you. I'm gonna find my camp. Catch you at the main stage.”

Remember this. In real gifting culture the gift is complete the moment it leaves your hand. Anyone who picks it back up was never giving.

### 3. “THIS IS FATE, BABE”
*Manufactured Destiny / The Cosmic Setup — clinically, manufactured destiny + exclusive meaning.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The Soulmate Architect. Knows your rising sign within the hour. Says he dreamed about meeting someone like you. Makes the connection feel cosmically pre-ordained — and therefore unchallengeable.

Here's the scene. "I never come to this stage. I had a vision in ceremony last month about a woman in a red hat and here you are." He's holding your hands. Completely certain. You feel seen in a way that bypasses your brain and lands in your chest. By the fire he's planning the retreat in Oaxaca you'll do together. Something whispers this is fast. Something else says — but what if the universe actually did this?

Here's what to watch for.

- Spiritual or cosmic language to explain why YOU specifically were chosen
- "I never do this" as a uniqueness hook
- Future plans appear within 48 hours — travel, living together, ceremonies
- Questioning the connection gets framed as blocking your own expansion

Here's what's actually happening. Manufactured destiny wraps urgency in spiritual clothing. It bypasses your "wait, do I actually know this person" reflex by making the connection feel pre-decided — by the universe, by your higher selves. Slowing down starts to feel like betraying something sacred. Your pace is sacred. Your sovereignty over your own story is sacred. If the connection is real it will survive three days of verification instead of three hours.

Run the check. The V check: “Do I actually know this person — or just the character they're performing this weekend?”

Here's the dark reality. Ceremony Grooming — The Plant Medicine Predator. Festivals with plant medicine attract a specific predator: someone who uses Spiritual authority and the vulnerability of a ceremonial state to access people. "You're supposed to be with me — the medicine showed me." If someone is making moves during or immediately after a ceremony, that is not spirituality. That is predation wearing a headdress. Real facilitators have strict ethics about this. Zero romantic or sexual contact with participants. If someone violates that — they are not a real practitioner.

Here's your move. Let the feeling be real. Let the verification also be real. Destiny that's true is still true in three weeks.

Here's exactly what to say. “I love this energy — I also want to actually know you. Tell me something real about your regular Tuesday.”

And here's the truth. If the universe truly aligned to bring you together, it can handle you taking your time.

### 4. “YOU'RE NOT LIKE OTHER GIRLS”
*Pedestal Ploy / The Chosen One Setup — clinically, pedestal ploy / isolation primer.*

Here's who this is. He puts you above every other woman at the festival — "you actually get it," "you're different," "most people here are just here to party but you're really feeling it." The compliment is real and it lands hard. It also quietly separates you from other women who are your most natural support network.

Picture this. "Most women I meet here are just looking for a hook-up. You have depth." It feels so good to be seen that way. By Saturday you've quietly distanced yourself from the other women in your camp — they seem kind of surface level compared to this energy he's offering. By Sunday you're alone with him in a part of the festival you don't know and the women who would have had your back are somewhere on the other side of the grounds.

Here are the signs.

- Compliments that specifically separate you from other women
- Your female friends become characterized as "basic" or "not on your level"
- You start to feel mildly embarrassed of your own people
- The flattery requires staying close to him to maintain the specialness

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The pedestal ploy isolates you from your natural support network by making that network feel beneath the version of yourself he's reflecting back. Other women at the festival — especially the ones you came with — are your most reliable safety net. Anyone who positions themselves between you and that solidarity, however flatteringly, is removing your protection. The "not like other girls" frame is also a trap for you: it trains you to compete with women instead of trust them.

Here's your check. The O check: “Where are my women right now — and when did I last actually see them?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Other Women Are Your Greatest Safety Asset. The most protective thing at any festival is a tight circle of women who are watching out for each other. The "not like other girls" framing specifically targets this. Real festie culture is women pulling each other into bathrooms to check on them, women appearing next to strangers who look uncomfortable, women sharing phone chargers and walking each other to their tents. That solidarity is what predators need to dismantle to get access. Don't let flattery be the tool they use to do it.

Here's what you do. Receive the compliment. Stay with your women. Specialness that requires isolation isn't specialness.

Say this, out loud. “That's really sweet — I'm gonna go check in with my girls. Find me at the set tonight.”

Here's the truth underneath it. A man who needs you to be above other women needs you to be without them. Notice that.

### 5. “THIS IS ONCE IN A LIFETIME”
*Scarcity & Urgency / The Closing Window — clinically, artificial scarcity / pressure closing.*

Let's talk about who does this. The Opportunity Creator. Always has something exclusive about to disappear — a private ceremony, a backstage pass, a connection you'd be crazy to miss. The window is closing. You need to decide right now. You're already moving before you chose to.

Here's how it plays out. "I don't do this for just anyone but there's a private ceremony happening in twenty minutes — only eight people, real medicine holders, you'd have to come right now." He's already walking. You're following before you've decided to. Something in his certainty made the decision feel already made. You don't know where you're going. You haven't told anyone.

Watch for these.

- "Right now" / "only tonight" / "this won't happen again"
- Decision must happen before you can find your friends or think clearly
- The opportunity only exists in his world, on his timeline
- You feel excited AND vaguely railroaded simultaneously

Here's what's really going on. Urgency is a discernment bypass. When something must happen RIGHT NOW, your prefrontal cortex — your judgment — literally cannot keep up. The pressure is the point. Real once-in-a-lifetime moments don't require you to abandon your people and your pace to access them. Anything that evaporates the moment you ask to slow down was never safe to begin with.

Ask yourself this. The G check: “What is my gut saying — and do I have time to text someone where I'm going?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Private Ceremony Trap. "Private ceremony" in a location you don't know, with people you've just met, without telling anyone where you're going — is one of the highest-risk situations at any festival. Real ceremonies have known facilitators, registered locations, and don't require you to disappear immediately with a stranger. Before going anywhere private with anyone you just met: text your location to a friend. Two sentences. Takes 30 seconds. If he's impatient about those 30 seconds — that impatience is your answer.

Here's the play. Stop. Breathe. Text someone where you're going. If it evaporates in five minutes — it wasn't real.

Here are the words. “If it's real it can wait two minutes for me to text my camp where I'm going.”

Remember this. Anything that disappears the moment you slow down was never safe to begin with.

### 6. “GOOD VIBES ONLY”
*PLUR Weaponized / Your Boundary Is the Bad Vibe — clinically, frame control / weaponized community values.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The PLUR Police. Uses the language and values of the culture — openness, presence, ego dissolution, unity — to make your "no" sound like a trauma response, a Spiritual blockage, or a vibe kill.

Here's the scene. "I thought you were an open person." Said softly. Disappointed. You just said you didn't want to go back to his camp. "This festival is about releasing ego. Your resistance is just fear — I'm not pushing you, I just think you're blocking your own growth." You didn't feel blocked. You felt managed. But now you're wondering if he's right.

Here's what to watch for.

- Your "no" is reframed as your trauma, ego, or fear — not a valid decision
- Healing language is weaponized to pathologize your limits
- "I'm not pressuring you" said in a way that IS pressure
- You leave the conversation questioning your judgment instead of his behavior

Here's what's actually happening. Frame control — the manipulator defines what the conversation is "about" in a way that makes your truth impossible to speak without sounding like the problem. When your boundary becomes evidence of your woundedness, he's moved the topic from his behavior to your psychology. You're defending yourself instead of evaluating him. That shift is the mechanism. Real PLUR means your "no" lands without negotiation. If someone has to invoke PLUR to get your yes — they don't have your yes.

Run the check. The G check: “What is my body saying — separate from the story his words are telling me?”

Here's the dark reality. The Consent Culture Predator. Some predators deliberately position as consent culture advocates — loudly talking about PLUR, boundaries, sacred sexuality, holding space. This creates social proof they're safe. It also gives them vocabulary to talk someone into a yes they didn't actually feel. If someone understands consent culture well enough to talk about it constantly but their actions require you to override your gut — the words are the cover story. Watch behavior. Full stop.

Here's your move. Trust the body over the frame. If it felt like pressure, it was pressure — regardless of vocabulary used.

Here's exactly what to say. “My boundaries are part of my healing too. I'm good with my decision.”

And here's the truth. Real PLUR means your no is honored without a negotiation. The end.

### 7. “HE'S BEEN IN OUR GROUP FOR YEARS”
*The Insider / The Predator Who Is Already Vouched For — clinically, in-group perpetrator / pre-established trust exploitation.*

Here's who this is. Not a stranger. Your friend's partner, the camp regular, the one who has been coming since before you did. Vouched for by people you trust, which is the entire advantage, and which is why he does not have to run any of the approaches in the earlier pages of this guide.

Picture this. It is not a stranger at the rail. It is the guy who has been in this group for six years, who everyone loves, who your best friend introduced you to — and who was in your tent doorway at four in the morning saying he just wanted to check you got back okay, standing slightly too close, for slightly too long, in a way you will spend the next three weeks trying to describe to yourself.

Here are the signs.

- Contact happens at the edges — late, in transit, in the tent, when you are alone
- Individually deniable each time, and there is always a reason
- He is closer to your friends than you are, and you both know it
- Other women in the group have gone slightly quiet about him
- Your account would cost the group more than his would
- Nothing he has done would sound like anything if you said it out loud

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Most sexual harm is committed by somebody the person already knows, and festival crews are exactly the trust structure that makes it work — years of shared history, a group identity, and a strong collective incentive not to have this be true. The insider does not need charm or a technique, because vouching has already done the work that an approach would otherwise have to do. What he has instead is the thing a stranger never gets: repeated, unremarkable proximity, and a group that will find it easier to reinterpret your discomfort than to lose him.

Here's your check. The G check: “Am I explaining away something I would flag instantly in a stranger?” — Do the substitution deliberately. If a man you met yesterday had done it, you would already know.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Group Is The Reason It Keeps Working. Here is the part that makes this the hardest page in the guide. When someone finally says something, groups usually do not split over whether it happened — they split over what it costs, and the person who spoke is generally the one who pays. Everybody can feel that in advance, which is why it takes years, and why when it finally comes out three other women say quietly that he did the same to them. If you are at the start of that: you do not have to accuse anybody of anything. Say the specific thing, once, to one woman in the group, factually. The overwhelming likelihood is that she has been waiting for somebody else to speak first, and two accounts change everything that one account cannot.

Here's what you do. Write it down with the date while it is fresh, in plain factual language with no adjectives. Then tell one woman in the group, privately, as a fact rather than a verdict. Stop being alone with him — no lifts, no late walks, no tent doorways — without announcing a policy.

Say this, out loud. “I want to tell you something and I'm not asking you to do anything with it. On Saturday at about four, Callum did [the specific thing]. I'm writing it down. Has anything like that come up for you?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Vouched for is not the same as safe. It only means nobody has said it yet.

### 8. “HOLD THIS FOR ME”
*The Substance Gatekeeper / The Plug Who Owns You — clinically, resource control / dependency grooming.*

Let's talk about who does this. The one with the connect. He controls access to what you're taking and uses that access to manufacture proximity, obligation, and dependency. You keep ending up near him because you keep needing to find him.

Here's how it plays out. He found you Thursday night and what he had made the music feel like it lived inside your ribcage. Friday he appeared right when you were looking. By Saturday you haven't slept in your own tent, you don't know anyone else's camp, your phone is at 3%, and his is the only number you've memorized. When you mention leaving to find your friends he gets quiet. "I thought we were vibing." Something about that makes you stay.

Watch for these.

- He is the exclusive or primary source of what you're consuming all weekend
- Access to substances is physically tied to being near him
- He gets quiet, cold, or hurt when you try to spend time with others
- Your world has contracted to his orbit without a conscious decision

Here's what's really going on. Substance gatekeeping is dependency grooming — slowly engineering a situation where you need someone in a way that makes saying no feel complicated. Altered states increase trust, lower threat-detection, create real neurochemical bonding. Add isolation and resource control and by Sunday the manipulation is structural. You're not in it because you're naive. You're in it because it was designed carefully.

Ask yourself this. The G check: “What is my body telling me about this person — separate from what they're providing me?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. Whose Name Is On It. The part nobody says out loud: if it is in your bag, it is your charge. Not his. Constructive possession means the person holding it is the person arrested, and “it wasn’t mine” is not a defense that works at a gate search — it is a sentence people say on the way to a county holding cell. The person asking you to hold knows this. That is frequently the entire reason he is asking, and the reason he asks the newest person in the camp rather than his oldest friend. Then there is the second layer: once he is your supply, leaving his camp costs you your weekend, and he knows that too. Carry your own, in your own bag, in the amount you chose while sober. “I don’t hold for anyone” is a complete answer and it does not require a follow-up.

Here's the play. Know your own supply or know your source independently of anyone who also wants something from you.

Here are the words. “I'm good for tonight — heading to find my camp. I'll catch you at the set tomorrow.”

Remember this. Your consciousness is yours. Nobody gets to be the gatekeeper of your inner world.

### 9. “THAT'S NOT WHAT HAPPENED”
*Morning After Gaslighting — clinically, gaslighting / reality rewriting.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The Revisionist. What happened last night is now disputed this morning — calmly, certainly, like your memory is the confused one. Festival environment gives him extra ammunition: sleep deprivation, altered states, time distortion.

Here's the scene. You wake up feeling wrong. Something happened that didn't feel right and you're trying to piece it together. You bring it up. He's completely calm: "You were into it, babe. You said you wanted to come to the tent." You're not sure of the full timeline but you're sure of the feeling. He looks at you like you're confused. "I think you just need water. You always get like this coming down." Now you're wondering if he's right.

Here's what to watch for.

- Your memory is disputed with calm authority — not anger, but certainty
- Your emotional state is attributed to substances rather than reality
- "You wanted it, you initiated" when you have no clear memory of consent
- You leave more uncertain about your own experience than when you started

Here's what's actually happening. Gaslighting makes you the unreliable narrator of your own life. In a festival context it has bonus ammunition: substances, sleep deprivation, emotional openness, time distortion all genuinely affect recall — creating plausible deniability. But your body holds memory that your conscious mind loses. If your nervous system says something is wrong, that signal is real data — even when you can't reconstruct a perfect timeline. You do not need a perfect memory to know something happened.

Run the check. The G check: “My body is the first source of information. If it feels wrong — that's real.”

Here's the dark reality. Spiking — It Happens And It's Not Rare. Drink and substance spiking at festivals is underreported and more common than most people openly discuss. Signs: dramatically more impaired than what you took should explain, losing chunks of time, feeling paralyzed while semi-conscious, waking up not knowing how you got somewhere. If this happened: tell a Harm Reduction worker or festival medic — not security first. RAINN (1-800-656-4673) has 24/7 support. Your experience is valid without a perfect memory.

Here's your move. Trust your body's signal. You do not need a perfect memory or a witness to know something was wrong.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm not debating what happened. I know how I feel and I'm going to honor that.”

And here's the truth. Your body's memory outlasts the timeline. If it feels wrong, it is enough.

### 10. “HOT AND COLD ALL WEEKEND”
*Intermittent Reinforcement / The Addictive Cycle — clinically, intermittent reinforcement / trauma bonding.*

Here's who this is. The one who blows hot and cold on a loop — intense and present, then distant and cold, then back to magic. The unpredictability doesn't kill the attachment. It deepens it. Your nervous system starts organizing the whole weekend around getting back to the warm version.

Picture this. Friday night was electric — the most connected you've felt in years. Saturday morning he was barely there. Cold, distracted, barely looked at you. Saturday afternoon he reappeared like nothing happened — warm, funny, magnetic. You felt such relief to have him back that you didn't ask about the morning. By Sunday you realize you've spent the whole weekend trying to figure out how to keep him in the warm phase.

Here are the signs.

- Intense warmth followed by withdrawal without explanation
- You feel relief — not just happiness — when he comes back around
- You're adjusting your behavior to manage his temperature
- The cold phases make the warm phases feel even more valuable than they are

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Intermittent reinforcement is the most powerful conditioning mechanism in human psychology — it's why slot machines are more addictive than ones that pay out every time. Random reward creates compulsive seeking. When warmth is unpredictable, you start to crave it more than you would if it were consistent. The cold isn't distance — it's the tool. The warmth isn't the reward — it's the hook being set deeper. A weekend of this wires your nervous system in ways that outlast the festival.

Here's your check. The G check: “Am I feeling relief when he's warm — or genuine happiness? Those are different things.”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Trauma Bonding In 72 Hours. Festival timelines compress everything — including the bonding that normally takes months to develop. Three days of intermittent hot-cold cycles can create a real trauma bond. Signs you're in one: you feel anxious when he's not around, his approval feels more important than your own comfort, you're making excuses for behavior that would be unacceptable from a friend. This feeling is neurochemical and real — and it does not mean you're in love. It means you've been conditioned. That can be unwound. It starts with naming it.

Here's what you do. Notice whether you feel relief or joy when he returns. Relief is not connection. Relief is a warning.

Say this, out loud. “[To yourself]: "I deserve warmth that doesn't come with cold attached to it."”

Here's the truth underneath it. Consistency is not boring. Consistency is the baseline. Unpredictability is not chemistry — it's a mechanism.

### 11. “YOUR FRIENDS DON'T GET IT”
*Tent Isolation / Cutting You Off From Camp — clinically, isolation / manufactured separation.*

Let's talk about who does this. The Gradual Drift Engineer. Doesn't drag you away dramatically — makes your world slowly contract around him until your own camp starts to feel like an interruption of the main event.

Here's how it plays out. It's Sunday morning and you realize you haven't seen your campmates since Friday lunch. Every time you mentioned them he had something better lined up. He called your best friend "a bit of a wet blanket." Now your phone is dead, you're deep in a part of the festival you don't know, his friends are your only social context, and finding your people would require asking him for help. When did that happen?

Watch for these.

- Your actual friends get subtly characterized as negative or "not on your level"
- Better plans always materialize right when you're about to reconnect with your crew
- You've been in his orbit for most of the festival without deciding to be there
- Getting back to camp now requires his help — you've stopped navigating independently

Here's what's really going on. Isolation is the infrastructure of control. It doesn't need to be dramatic. A series of small redirections over 72 hours can leave you completely dependent on one person in unfamiliar territory with a dead phone. The criticism of your friends is the tell — it's not about them being bad for you. It's about making you distrust the people who know you, love you, and would notice if something was off. Connection that requires losing your existing connections is not connection. It's replacement.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Can I find my camp right now without his help? If not — that is the situation.”

Here's what's underneath all of it. Camp Safety Infrastructure — Build Before You Arrive. Before gates open: set a daily check-in time with at least one campmate ("if I haven't texted by 2pm, come find me"). Set a physical meeting spot everyone knows. Establish a code word meaning "come get me now, no questions." Make sure your camp lead knows your general plan each night. Write your camp location in pen on your arm. These feel like overkill until they're not. Build them before you need them.

Here's the play. Make reconnecting with your camp a daily non-negotiable ritual. Your people are your root system.

Here are the words. “"I need to go do my daily check-in with my camp — it's our thing. I'll find you at the main stage tonight."”

Remember this. A connection that requires your isolation to survive is not a connection. It's a cage with better lighting.

### 12. “YOU CAN CHARGE IT AT MINE”
*The Power Bank / Your Phone As The Lever — clinically, communication control / resource dependency.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The person with the generator, the solar rig, the van with a cigarette lighter, or simply three power banks and a lot of generosity about them.

Here's the scene. You are on eight percent by Friday afternoon, which is the ordinary condition of every phone at every festival. He has power. Charging means leaving it in his van for a couple of hours, or sitting in his camp while it charges, and both of those are completely normal and both of them mean that for two hours the thing that connects you to everyone you know is in somebody else's hands.

Here's what to watch for.

- Offers to take your phone away to charge rather than to charge it beside you
- Your phone is repeatedly in his possession, briefly, with good reasons
- Notices and comments on who is messaging you
- Power access is framed as a favour that accrues
- Discourages you from getting a power bank of your own
- You have found yourself uncontactable for stretches and did not choose that

Here's what's actually happening. A dead phone removes your map, your torch, your group chat, your ability to call anyone, your record of the weekend, and your capacity to prove where you were. At a festival it is the single most consequential piece of equipment you carry, and the site is designed — unintentionally — to drain it. Whoever solves that problem for you has a hold, and the hold does not require any intent at all to be dangerous: an unreliable friend with your phone produces the same eight hours of unreachability that a deliberate one does. The physical access is the second issue, and the more serious one, since a few unattended minutes is enough to install something or to read everything.

Run the check. The V check: “Is my phone in my own possession, charged enough to call someone?” — If it lives somewhere else for hours at a time, so does your ability to get help.

Here's the dark reality. Uncontactable Is A Condition Somebody Can Arrange. Think about what an eight-hour gap in your reachability enables: nobody at home expects to hear from you at a festival, your friends assume you are at a stage, and you cannot be located by anyone who would come looking. Every isolation pattern in this book works better against a woman with a dead phone, which is why the offer to solve it is worth noticing rather than just accepting. The stalking guides make the same point from the other side — physical access to a handset, even briefly, is how location sharing gets quietly switched on. Charge it yourself, in your own hands, and set a PIN that is not your birthday.

Here's your move. Carry two power banks and charge them before you travel — it is the cheapest safety equipment at any festival. If you must use somebody else's power, stay with the phone. Keep a paper note of two numbers and your camp location in your pocket for when it dies anyway.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'll sit with it while it charges if that's alright — I'm paranoid about losing it. — Got my own bank, thanks though.”

And here's the truth. Your phone is your exit, your map and your witness. It does not go in anybody else's van.

### 13. “SMILE FOR ME”
*Photography as Predation — clinically, boundary violation / digital coercion + stalking.*

Here's who this is. The Photographer With Access. Could be credentialed, could be a guy with a phone. Uses the camera to create proximity, extract intimacy, and build a collection you may not know exists.

Picture this. He took a gorgeous shot of you dancing Saturday and showed you on his screen — genuinely beautiful. Said he'd send it. Asked to get a few more, closer, "just your energy." Later your friend shows you his Instagram stories. Photos of you that you don't remember being taken. One from behind. One that feels intimate in a way you can't fully articulate. Everyone says he's a great photographer. You feel like you're being dramatic. You're not.

Here are the signs.

- Photos taken without asking, normalized as "just capturing the moment"
- Escalation from group shots to solo shots to closer, more intimate framing
- Promised photos create a reason to stay in contact and find you after the festival
- Images appear on social that you didn't know were being taken of you

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The camera creates three manipulation vectors: proximity (he needs to get close), intimacy (he frames you personally), and post-festival leverage (he has your image and a reason to contact you). In festival culture photography is celebrated and normalized — which makes predatory documentation easy to disguise as art. Your image is yours in every context. "Everyone was being photographed" is not consent. A camera pointed at your body without your knowledge is a violation regardless of cultural context.

Here's your check. The E check: “Can I freely ask him to delete photos I'm uncomfortable with? What happens when I do?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Post-Festival Digital Predator. Festival photography is often setup for what happens after: unsolicited DMs, location tagging your images, sharing photos you didn't consent to, or using intimate images as leverage. Before leaving any photographer's presence: "Can you show me everything you took of me?" You have the right to ask for deletions on the spot while you watch. If they post images of you without permission after — that's reportable on every platform. Screenshot before reporting. Document everything.

Here's what you do. Your image is yours. You can ask anyone, anytime, to delete a photo of you — no explanation required.

Say this, out loud. “Hey — I'm not comfortable with that one. Can you delete it right now while I watch?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Consent for one photo is not consent for all photos forever in all contexts. Each image needs a yes.

### 14. “I'M HEALING YOU”
*The False Healer / Body Access Through Spiritual Authority — clinically, authority abuse / spiritual coercion.*

Let's talk about who does this. The Self-Appointed Healer. Uses therapeutic, somatic, or spiritual language to create a context where physical touch seems appropriate and expected — while skipping the part where you actually agree to it.

Here's how it plays out. "You have blocked energy in your hips — I can feel it from here. I do somatic release work." He's already reaching. The ceremony just ended. You're tender and wide open. Three people told you he's legit. He has a beautiful altar. You don't want to block your own healing. His hands are on your body before you said yes. You didn't say no either. But you also never said yes.

Watch for these.

- Physical contact initiated under therapeutic framing without your explicit verbal yes
- Your hesitation reframed as resistance to healing — not a valid limit
- Timing targets peak vulnerability: post-ceremony, post-peak, tender moments
- Credentials asserted verbally but impossible to verify in the moment

Here's what's really going on. Spiritual authority abuse weaponizes the healer role to bypass the consent conversation. It works because festival culture values healing (the frame seems legit), the therapeutic context makes touch feel appropriate, and questioning a healer feels like questioning healing itself. Real somatic practitioners and ceremony facilitators are trained to obtain clear, uncoerced consent before any physical contact. That standard exists to protect you. If someone skips it — the reason is not that they're too advanced for it.

Ask yourself this. The G check: “What is my gut saying about this person — underneath the role they’re performing right now?” — if the answer doesn’t match the title, trust the gut over the role.

Here's what's underneath all of it. How To Actually Vet A Practitioner. "Legitimate" does not mean "has a following," "has an altar," or "has people here who vouch for them." Real vetting means asking for their training lineage and how long they've practiced, asking what their consent protocol is before they work with anyone, and ideally talking to people who know them outside festival contexts. A real practitioner welcomes these questions. Anyone who responds to "what's your consent process?" with spiritual deflection is giving you your answer.

Here's the play. No practitioner — no matter how credentialed — initiates physical contact without your explicit verbal yes. That is the baseline.

Here are the words. “I appreciate the offer. I'm not available for bodywork right now.”

Remember this. Your healing belongs to you. Nobody gets to access your body in the name of your own expansion.

### 15. “YOU'RE KILLING THE VIBE*”
*Tone Policing / Your Discomfort Becomes the Problem — clinically, tone policing / credibility theft.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The Vibe Sheriff. Not the person who did the thing — the person who responds to you naming it. Your discomfort, your concern, your calling-it-out gets treated as the drama. His behavior disappears. Your reaction becomes the whole story.

Here's the scene. You told your campmates the guy next door kept touching you after you moved away. One sighs: "He's always been chill with us. Are you sure you're not just in a weird headspace? We don't want drama — this is our happy place." You came for support and now you're defending your own perception. By the end of the conversation you're apologizing for bringing it up.

Here's what to watch for.

- HOW you said it becomes the focus instead of WHAT you said
- "Drama" is the word used to describe your legitimate safety concern
- Group comfort is placed above your signal
- You end up softening or retracting a report that needed to be heard clearly

Here's what's actually happening. Tone policing is credibility theft. It moves the conversation off what happened and onto how you're expressing it — then uses your emotional state as evidence you're not reliable. In festival culture it shows up as "no drama," "good vibes," "you might just be coming down." Function is identical regardless of the outfit: your testimony gets discounted before anyone has to engage with what you're actually describing. Your camp dismissing you is information about your camp. Not about your experience.

Run the check. The G check: “My nervous system logged something real. Other people's comfort does not un-log it.”

Here's the dark reality. Undercovers & Buying/Selling Safety. Buying or selling anything at a festival — legal or otherwise — carries real risk. Undercover officers work festivals and look exactly like attendees: kandi, festival wear, the whole vibe. Red flags: someone approaches YOU to buy or sell (not the other way), they push quickly, they want to go somewhere private, they seem weirdly interested in quantities. If something feels like a setup — it might be. "Actually I'm good" ends any transaction. Walk toward people. Never follow anyone to a secluded location for a deal.

Here's your move. Your discomfort is the data. You don't need group consensus to trust your own signal.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I hear that this feels uncomfortable for the group. I'm still going to report it. Those aren't the same issue."”

And here's the truth. Your nervous system logged something real. Other people's comfort does not un-log it.

### 16. “I'M YOUR RIDE”
*The Driver / Transport As A Hold — clinically, transport control / isolation by logistics.*

Here's who this is. Whoever owns the vehicle, the ticket, or the only spare seat. Often perfectly nice for three days. Becomes something else at the exact moment your plans and his stop matching.

Picture this. He drove. It is four hours each way and you are ninety miles from the nearest station with no signal in the car park. On Saturday you want to stay with the people from the neighbouring camp and he says that's fine, a bit flatly, and then mentions that he was thinking of heading off early tomorrow, and you feel the whole weekend quietly reorganize itself around not annoying him.

Here are the signs.

- One person controls the only realistic way home
- Departure time shifts in response to your choices rather than to logistics
- Your ticket, wristband, or bag is in his vehicle
- “I could just go without you” floated as a joke
- You are editing what you ask for to keep the lift viable
- No independent route exists and you have not checked whether one does

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Transport is the cleanest leverage at a festival because it is invisible — nobody has threatened anything, there is nothing to report, and the pressure is purely structural. It works the same way as medication or accommodation control: whoever holds the thing you cannot do without sets the terms of every other conversation. Most people who hold it never use it. The ones who do rarely say anything explicit, because they do not have to; you will do the arithmetic yourself and adjust, and that adjustment is the entire mechanism.

Here's your check. The E check: “If I had to leave this festival in the next two hours without him, could I?” — If there is no answer, your exit is somebody else's property.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Car Park Is The Trap. The dangerous shape is not the weekend, it is the journey. A car with one other person in it, four hours of road, no signal, and no way out at seventy miles an hour is a genuinely enclosed space, and people who want an enclosed space know that. This is why the lift home is where a lot of these situations actually turn — the festival was populated and watched, and the drive is neither. Decide the return arrangements before you travel, tell somebody off-site who you are travelling with and when you are due back, and make it entirely normal for them to be expecting a message when you land.

Here's what you do. Arrange a second way home before you go — a coach time, a rail replacement, a friend in the next county, sixty pounds set aside for a taxi to a station. Keep your ticket, ID, phone and keys on your body, not in his boot. Text your arrival to an off-site person both ways.

Say this, out loud. “I've got a coach booked back as a backup either way, so don't wait on me if you want to head off — go when you like.”

Here's the truth underneath it. An exit that depends on somebody else's mood is not an exit. Buy the backup ticket; it costs less than the weekend does.

### 17. “I'LL WALK YOU BACK”
*The Escort Home / The Last Two Hundred Metres — clinically, opportunistic isolation / terminal isolation point.*

Let's talk about who does this. Someone who has been around all night, pleasant, unremarkable, and is now offering the single most normal thing at a festival — which is also the thing that ends with him knowing exactly which tent is yours.

Here's how it plays out. It is late, the site is enormous and dark, and walking back alone genuinely is the thing everybody tells you not to do. He offers. The offer is correct. And somewhere between the last stage and the campsite the crowd thins out and it is just the two of you on a path, and the thing you were avoiding by not walking alone is now walking next to you.

Watch for these.

- Insists on walking you specifically rather than the group
- Discourages you from texting anyone that you are heading back
- Wants to come in “just for a minute”, or to see your camp
- Now knows your tent's exact location and that you sleep there alone
- Times the offer for when everybody else has already gone
- Waves off the alternative — a camp mate, a friend, walking with a group

Here's what's really going on. The walk back is the last unwitnessed stretch of the night and it is where a disproportionate amount of festival harm occurs, precisely because the safety advice — do not walk alone — creates a socially compulsory role that anybody can volunteer for. The escort is not a bad idea; being escorted by whoever happened to be standing there at 3am is. The other half of the cost is durable: after tonight, one more person knows which tent is yours, and tent location is the single most useful piece of information anyone can hold about you at a festival.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Am I about to be alone with this person somewhere nobody is watching?” — The walk counts. That is the whole point of the walk.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Who Knows Where You Sleep. Two separate things end here and it is worth keeping them apart. The first is tonight, which is about being alone on a dark path with somebody you have known for six hours. The second is every night after, which is about tent location — information that cannot be withdrawn once given, and that turns up again in the small hours later in the weekend when you had forgotten the conversation entirely. Handle both with the same move: walk back with people you arrived with, and if a stranger walks you, be walked to a landmark near your camp rather than to your door. It is not rude. It is the same reason you would not give a stranger your home address.

Here's the play. Pre-arrange the walk before you need it — leave when your camp leaves, or text a camp mate to meet you at a landmark. If someone unexpected is escorting you, stop at the food stall or the water point, thank them, and let them leave first.

Here are the words. “Cheers — Mel's meeting me at the big oak, so I'm good from there. — This is me, thanks, night!”

Remember this. Not walking alone is good advice. Walking with whoever volunteers at three in the morning is a different piece of advice entirely.

### 18. “THE FESTIVAL EX RETURNS”
*Hoovering / The Annual Reappearance — clinically, hoovering / trauma bond reactivation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The person from last year — or the year before — who hurt you, who you finally processed, who shows up at the same festival like the circuit is their hunting ground. They know your world. They know your people. They know exactly what to say.

Here's the scene. You saw him across the fire Saturday night and your whole body went electric — not in a good way. Then he appeared next to you with that smile. "I've been thinking about you. I think I was in a really different place last year. I've done a lot of work." He sounds different. He looks softer. And some part of you that spent six months healing wants desperately to believe that the work is real.

Here's what to watch for.

- Reappears at the same festivals — he knows your circuit
- Opens with a change narrative: "I've done the work," "I've grown a lot"
- Creates urgency: "I need to talk to you this weekend before you leave"
- The feelings that come back are old trauma activation, not new information

Here's what's actually happening. Hoovering is the return of a previous manipulator after a period of absence — timed for maximum impact. Festival reunions are particularly potent because the environment is already emotionally heightened, your nervous system associates the setting with connection, and seeing someone in a context where you once felt magic reactivates those neurochemical pathways faster than almost anything else. "I've done the work" is a sentence. Changed behavior is a pattern. You need months of data, not a weekend of charm.

Run the check. The V check: “Do I actually know who this person is now — or am I responding to who I hoped they were?”

Here's the dark reality. The Rule Of Months Not Moments. Changed people change in their regular life — in how they treat people when nothing is on the line, when they're stressed, when they're not in a magical environment trying to win you back. The festival weekend is the worst possible place to assess whether someone has actually changed because everything is heightened, everyone is performing the best version of themselves, and the environment itself creates false intimacy. "I need to see this in your regular life over months, not this weekend" is a full and complete response to any change narrative.

Here's your move. Let the encounter be pleasant. Do not let the encounter be a reopening. You already did that chapter.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I'm really glad you're doing the work. I'm also going to protect what I built after you."”

And here's the truth. Your healing is not available for disruption every time they decide they're ready.

### 19. “TEST EVERYTHING”
*Substance Safety Is Sovereignty — No Shame, All Sense — clinically, harm reduction protocol.*

Here's who this is. This is not a judgment page. It's a life page. Fentanyl is in the supply chain, pills are pressed to be indistinguishable, and testing is the single most effective thing you can do to protect yourself and your people.

Picture this. "Someone in our camp tested their stuff Saturday morning and it came back completely different than what they thought they had. We wouldn't have known." This happens at every major festival. The person who handed it to you may not have known either. Testing isn't about distrust — it's about knowing what's actually in what you're putting in your body. You wouldn't eat a random mushroom you found in the woods without identifying it first. Same exact energy.

Here are the signs.

- Fentanyl test strips: dissolve tiny amount in water, one line = fentanyl present, stop
- Reagent kits (Marquis, Mecke, Simon's): color reactions tell you what's actually in it
- Start with a small test dose and wait — especially anything pressed or from a new source
- Never take something handed to you by someone you just met without testing it yourself first

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A lethal dose of fentanyl is invisible. People die at festivals every year from substances that looked, smelled, and tasted exactly like what they expected. Testing costs under $40 for a full kit from DanceSafe. Harm Reduction tents at most major events have strips free. Naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid overdose — available at many pharmacies without prescription, small enough to carry. Know how to use it. Having it in your camp has saved lives.

Here's your check. The G check: “What is my gut saying about this substance, this source, this situation — before I put it in my body?” — test it anyway, even when the gut says yes. Verification isn’t distrust of magic, it’s respect for your own body.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. If Someone Is In Trouble Right Now. Signs of overdose: unresponsive or barely responsive, slow or stopped breathing, blue lips or fingertips, gurgling sound. What to do: call festival medics immediately, administer Narcan if available (nasal spray — one puff per nostril, repeat in 2-3 min if no response), put them in recovery position on their side, stay with them. Good Samaritan laws in most US states protect you when seeking overdose help. Saving someone's life matters more than anything else.

Here's what you do. Test your stuff. Every time. No exceptions. Thirty seconds can save your life.

Say this, out loud. “Hold on — let me test it first. I've got strips in my kit. Two minutes.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You can love the magic of this world AND verify what goes into your body. These are not opposites.

### 20. “THE EXIT IS ALWAYS YOURS”
*Getting Out of Any Situation — No Explanation Required — clinically, exit planning / coercive control countermeasure.*

Let's talk about who does this. Exit scripts are for any situation: a conversation, a camp, a tent, a ceremony, a transaction, a connection that stopped feeling right. You don't need a reason that passes someone else's inspection. The exit is always yours.

Here's how it plays out. You're in a situation that doesn't feel right and you're spinning trying to figure out how to leave without causing a scene, without being rude. Here's the thing: the energy you're spending on the perfect exit is energy you could spend walking out the door. You don't owe anyone a seamless departure. You don't need to explain yourself. You don't need them to agree that leaving is okay. You can just go.

Watch for these.

- "I need to go find my people" — no further explanation needed, ever
- Pre-arranged code word with your camp: one text means "come get me, right now"
- The fake phone call: totally valid, completely legitimate, use it freely
- Festival Rangers and Harm Reduction staff exist specifically for moments like this

Here's what's really going on. The difficulty of leaving is almost always manufactured — through social pressure, physical positioning, altered states, and the norm of not causing a scene. Your right to leave does not require agreement from the other person. It does not require a valid excuse. It does not require a good reason. The more someone works to make leaving complicated, the more important it is that you leave. That is the metric.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “Exit Is Mine” — can I leave right now, freely? The answer tells you everything.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Build Your Exit Infrastructure Before You Need It. Before gates open: find the Ranger station and Harm Reduction tent locations. Establish a camp meeting point everyone knows. Set a daily check-in time. Pick a code word. Make sure one person knows your general whereabouts each night. Write two emergency numbers in pen on your arm — yes, actually. Download an offline map of the festival grounds. Systems built before you need them are the only ones that work when you actually need them.

Here's the play. Stand up. Say "I need to go." Walk. You can be kind AND leave. These have always been compatible.

Here are the words. “"I need to head out — thanks for the time." Then go. No elaboration required.”

Remember this. The exit is always yours. Nobody gets to make leaving complicated enough that you stay.

### 21. “SEE SOMETHING, HOLD SOMETHING”
*Bystander Activation in Festival Culture — clinically, active bystander intervention.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The witness who acts. At some point this weekend you will see something that doesn't look right. What you do in that moment is part of what kind of community this is.

Here's the scene. You're walking to the water station and you see a woman sitting with a man who seems agitated. She looks small. Her body is closed. He's leaning into her space and she's looking at the ground. You're not sure if it's anything. You almost keep walking because you don't want to make it weird. But something caught. This is the moment. This is the one. What do you actually do right now?

Here's what to watch for.

- THE DISTRACTION: walk up to her like you know her — "Oh my GOD I've been looking everywhere for you!"
- THE RESOURCE: find a Ranger or Harm Reduction worker, describe location and what you saw
- THE CHECK-IN: wait for a private moment, ask her directly: "Hey — are you okay?"
- You do NOT have to confront the person causing concern — not required, not always safe

Here's what's actually happening. The bystander effect is real — in groups, individuals are less likely to act because everyone assumes someone else will. Festival culture adds "no drama" making people hesitate to name what they see. But intervention doesn't have to be dramatic. The distraction approach is subtle, friendly, and requires zero accusation. You're not the police. You're a human who saw something and chose to show up. That is enough.

Run the check. The O check: “Is someone out alone in a way that seems involuntary?” — sometimes the most important thing you do all weekend is the thirty seconds you spend checking on a stranger.

Here's the dark reality. After A Disclosure — What To Actually Do. If a friend tells you something happened: you do not need answers or a plan right now. "I believe you. I'm here. What do you need in this moment?" is complete and powerful. Don't investigate. Don't minimize. Don't immediately jump to reporting unless they want that. Follow their lead. Stay with them. Harm Reduction tents are confidential, non-judgmental, and not police. RAINN's hotline (1-800-656-4673) has 24/7 counselors trained specifically for this.

Here's your move. If something looks wrong, you have options. Distract. Alert. Check in. Pick one and do it.

Here's exactly what to say. “[Natural, warm, to her]: "Oh my god — there you are! I've been looking everywhere. Come with me, I found the good bathrooms."”

And here's the truth. The community is only as safe as what we each choose to do in the moment we see something.
