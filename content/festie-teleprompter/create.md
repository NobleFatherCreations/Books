# C.R.E.A.T.E. — LIVE PAINTERS & ARTISTS
### Live Painters & Artists Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 254 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is C.R.E.A.T.E.: Live Painters & Artists Edition. Your art is yours. Your body is yours. Your worth is not for sale.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **C** — Contract First. Handshake deals are not deals. If it's not written, it's not real. Every wall, commission, collab — get it in writing.
- **R** — Rate Is Sacred. 'Exposure' doesn't pay rent. Know your rate before every conversation and don't negotiate it under pressure.
- **E** — Exit Clause. Can you leave this arrangement if it stops being okay? If the answer is no or complicated — that is the problem, not your hesitation.
- **A** — Art Stays Yours. Your image, your work, your process. Nobody photographs it, sells it, or claims credit without your explicit yes.
- **T** — Test The Room. Who's in your creative space while you work? Attention during creation is intimate. Not everyone watching has earned that access.
- **E** — Enough Is Enough. Burnout, boundary erosion, over-giving are the slow drain. You're allowed to stop.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*Exposure traps, false collabs, the fan who won't leave.*

### 1. “PAINT FOR FREE, GET FAMOUS”
*The Exposure Promoter — clinically, reciprocity trap / future faking.*

Here's who this is. The promoter, organizer, or influencer who wants your art for their event and offers exposure instead of payment. The opportunity sounds real. The exposure rarely materializes. The art is already on their wall.

Picture this. "We can't pay for the live painting but your work will be seen by 20,000 people this weekend and we'll tag you in everything — this could really launch you." You spend 16 hours creating something extraordinary on their main stage wall. The photos go everywhere — 40,000 likes. You get tagged in two of them. The painting stays on their wall. You leave with nothing except the work experience, which you already had.

Here are the signs.

- Payment replaced with "exposure," "experience," or future opportunity — all unquantifiable
- The beneficiary of your labor is clearly defined; your benefit is vague and future-tense
- No written agreement about tagging, credit, reproduction rights, or ownership
- The ask comes under time pressure — event is this weekend, they need an answer now

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Exposure is a real form of value in some contexts. It is also one of the most systematically used tools to extract free labor from artists. The test: would this person pay a photographer, sound engineer, or caterer in "exposure"? No — because those are recognized as labor. Live painting is also labor. The festival ecosystem has a long history of treating visual artists as ambient decoration. You are not decoration.

Here's your check. The C check: “Is there a written agreement — or just a verbal promise that evaporates after the event?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Live Painting Minimum Contract. Every engagement should have in writing: your fee or explicit agreement that this is donated (your choice, not their default), who owns the finished piece, photo/video rights and credit requirements, whether they can reproduce commercially, and what happens if they cancel. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts (vlany.org) offers free legal help. A legitimate organizer will sign it. The ones who won't are telling you everything.

Here's what you do. Know your rate before every conversation. Exposure is a supplement, not a replacement.

Say this, out loud. “I'd love to be involved — my rate for a live painting is [X]. What's your budget?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your art is your labor. Your labor has a price. You get to know what it is.

### 2. “LET'S COLLAB, BABE”
*The Collab That Becomes Unpaid Labor — clinically, forced teaming / credit theft.*

Let's talk about who does this. The person who wants to "collaborate" — which in practice means they contribute a concept or their presence while you contribute the actual skill, time, and materials. Credit gets split equally. Labor doesn't.

Here's how it plays out. "I have this incredible vision — I can see it so clearly — I just need someone with your skills to bring it to life. We'd be equal partners." You spend the next day executing their vague concept with your specific technical ability, your materials, your time. The finished piece gets posted with both your names. Their 8,000 followers see it first. The caption says "our piece." When you mention the execution was entirely yours, the vibe goes cold.

Watch for these.

- They bring concept or personality; you bring skill, time, materials, and execution
- Credit proposed as 50/50 before any conversation about actual labor split
- Pushback about contribution equity received as you being difficult or insecure
- Their platform/following positioned as their "equal contribution"

Here's what's really going on. Platform is not the same as labor. A concept is not the same as execution. In the art world these distinctions matter enormously for credit, compensation, and professional record. The collab frame is used to extract skilled labor under the cover of partnership. A genuine collaboration is one where both parties' contributions are named honestly and the credit reflects actual work.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Does my name appear clearly on this work in a way that reflects my actual contribution?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. Photo And Video Credit — Non-Negotiable. Every time your work appears online, your name should appear in the caption — not just a tag that can be removed, a written credit. Before any event or collab, establish in writing: (1) all posts featuring your work credit you by name in the caption, (2) you receive advance notice before your work appears in any commercial context, (3) you retain rights to your own images of the work. Screenshot every post featuring your work.

Here's the play. Define the contribution split before you start creating. If they can't have that conversation — that's your answer.

Here are the words. “I'm into the idea — let's talk about what each of us is actually bringing so we're clear on credit before we start.”

Remember this. Equal credit requires equal contribution. Concepts and execution are not the same thing.

### 3. “I KNOW PEOPLE”
*The Connector Who Wants a Cut of Everything — clinically, access control / parasitic middleman.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The "connector" — not quite a manager, not quite a promoter — who positions themselves as the key to your next level of exposure and begins expecting ongoing commission on work they introduce you to, then on work they didn't introduce you to.

Here's the scene. "I'm going to connect you with everyone this weekend — I know all the right people." He does introduce you to two legitimate leads. He also begins referring to himself as your "rep" in conversations with those leads and starts mentioning a 20% "arrangement" that you never agreed to. By the time you realize what's happening, the leads think he's already your manager.

Here's what to watch for.

- Introduces themselves as your "rep" or "manager" in conversations without your agreement
- Commission expectations appear after introductions, not before — retroactively
- Scope of what they expect commission on keeps expanding
- You're now in a complicated position with leads who believe a deal exists

Here's what's actually happening. The parasitic middleman move works by creating facts on the ground before you've had a chance to define the relationship. By the time you understand what they're claiming, they're already representing you to the people who matter to you professionally. Clear, early conversation about what any assistance means in terms of compensation is the only protection. If someone introduces themselves as your representative without your agreement — correct it immediately and directly.

Run the check. The C check: “Have I agreed to any representation arrangement — or is someone claiming one that...

Here's the dark reality. Correcting False Representation. If someone is representing themselves as your manager or rep without your agreement: (1) Contact the leads directly and privately: "I want to clarify — [name] is not my manager/rep. I'm handling my own bookings." (2) Tell the person directly: "I haven't agreed to any representation arrangement and I need you to stop representing yourself that way." (3) Document this conversation in writing immediately after. False representation can create enforceable implied contracts in some jurisdictions — address it fast.

Here's your move. Define every assistance relationship before it becomes a representation relationship.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I appreciate the intros — I want to be clear that I'm handling my own arrangements. Any intros are just that — intros, not representation."”

And here's the truth. Anyone who represents themselves as your representative before you've agreed to that is telling you who they are.

### 4. “I JUST LOVE WATCHING YOU CREATE”
*The Hoverer / The Fan Who Becomes a Fixture — clinically, boundary erosion / manufactured intimacy.*

Here's who this is. The person who camps near your workspace all weekend under the banner of admiration. Creating in public is inherently intimate — your focus makes you less boundaried — and predatory hovering exploits all of that.

Picture this. He's been near your painting station since you set up. Brings you water. Makes comments about your process that show he's genuinely paying attention. It feels nice to be witnessed. By Sunday he feels like a fixture of your weekend — he knows your whole process, has opinions about your choices, offered to help break down. When he starts steering the direction of the piece and you redirect, he takes it personally. You feel like you owe him something.

Here are the signs.

- Presence near your workspace extends across the whole weekend without invitation
- Witness role gradually becomes advisory role — opinions about your work appear uninvited
- Small acts of service (water, help) build invisible debt over time
- Redirecting their input or asking for space is received as rejection

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Creating in public is an intimate act — you're in a focused, slightly vulnerable state. This is a window that predatory observers specifically exploit. Real appreciation respects the artist's process and follows their lead about interaction. Hovering that gradually expands into advisory territory, then into physical presence in your workspace, then into emotional debt — is not admiration. It's occupation disguised as appreciation.

Here's your check. The T check: “Who is actually in my creative space right now — and did I invite them there?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Substances And Creative Work — The Vulnerability Window. Many live painters work with substances as part of their creative practice — completely valid. Your altered state while working is a specific vulnerability window. You're focused on the work, your social threat-detection is lower, and your boundaries are softer. People who offer substances to you while you're creating are not always doing it neutrally. Know what you're taking, test it before you're in the flow state, and have a trusted person in your orbit who is less altered than you while you work.

Here's what you do. Name your workspace boundaries clearly and early. "I go pretty internal while I work" is a complete sentence.

Say this, out loud. “I really appreciate the support — I need to be heads-down for a while. I'll find you when I come up for air.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Admiration that respects your process from a distance is real. Admiration that requires access to your workspace is not admiration.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Substance vulnerability while working, validation weaponized.*

### 5. “SEXY ARTIST ENERGY”
*Sexual Attention as Artistic Validation — clinically, objectification / sexual coercion via validation.*

Let's talk about who does this. The specific way sexual attention gets entangled with artistic recognition for visible artists. When someone's interest in your art and interest in your body arrive in the same package, receiving one without the other becomes complicated. That confusion is the tool.

Here's how it plays out. He's been complimenting your work genuinely all weekend — knowledgeable, specific, clearly understands what you're doing. It feels good to be seen by someone who gets it. Saturday night he asks if you want to "continue the conversation" at his camp. When you decline he says "I guess I misread the creative energy between us." Suddenly the whole weekend of genuine-feeling artistic recognition has a retroactive question mark over it.

Watch for these.

- Artistic interest and sexual interest arrive in the same package from the same person
- Declining sexual interest causes withdrawal of artistic validation — they're linked
- Retroactive reframing of the whole interaction when you say no
- You start wondering which of your artistic connections this weekend were actually about your work

Here's what's really going on. For visible artists — especially women creating in public — sexual attention and professional attention are frequently bundled together. Being seen as an artist is meaningful and the confusion of that with being desired is a specific manipulation: it makes you question your artistic worth every time you decline someone's sexual interest. Your art's validity is entirely separate from anyone's desire for you.

Ask yourself this. The T check: “Is the appreciation of my work actually about my work — or is it cover for something...

Here's what's underneath all of it. Your Work Being Shared Is Not Your Body Being Available. Live painting is public art. Photographs of your work circulate. Your face and body are often in those photographs. None of this is consent for contact, pursuit, or sexual attention. If someone's engagement with images of your work has crossed into following, gathering information about you, or sexual fixation — that's stalking behavior regardless of the artistic frame around it. Screenshot. Block. Report. Document before you report.

Here's the play. Receive genuine artistic recognition with full confidence. Decline sexual interest without apology. These are completely separate.

Here are the words. “I'm going to keep those two things separate — I'm glad you connected with the work. I'm not available for the rest.”

Remember this. Your art being beautiful does not make your body available. Full stop.

### 6. “WE JUST NEED THIS ONE THING”
*Scope Creep / Moving Goalposts on the Brief — clinically, moving goalposts / manufactured obligation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The commission or project that starts with a clear brief and expands, shifts, or escalates with each interaction — while the original agreed compensation stays fixed. Each change seems small. The cumulative impact is significant.

Here's the scene. "We love the direction — can you also just add a panel on the left? And can the main figure be slightly different? And can you include our camp's logo somewhere subtle? And can you do a smaller companion piece for the kitchen?" Each ask arrives after the previous one is done, when you're already invested and it feels petty to push back on "just one more thing." You've delivered four times what was originally scoped for the original price.

Here's what to watch for.

- New requests arrive after previous work is completed and your investment is high
- Each individual ask seems small and reasonable in isolation
- Original compensation stays fixed as scope expands
- Pushing back on changes is framed as being difficult or not collaborative

Here's what's actually happening. Scope creep in creative work is one of the most common forms of exploitation precisely because each individual expansion seems minor and the accumulated investment makes pushing back feel like losing everything. The professional protection is a written brief with explicit terms: this is what is included, this is what additional work costs. Changes to scope trigger a new conversation about compensation. Every time. Without apology.

Run the check. The C check: “Is what I'm being asked to do now what I agreed to do — and is the compensation still...

Here's the dark reality. The Change Order Conversation. "That's a new element that's outside the original scope — I'm happy to include it, my rate for additions is [X]." Say this every time a new request appears after the brief is agreed. You don't need to be apologetic or elaborate. A change order is a completely normal part of professional creative work. Clients who push back on this are clients who planned to expand scope without paying for it. That's your information about whether you want to work with them again.

Here's your move. Changes to scope trigger a new compensation conversation. Every time. Without apology.

Here's exactly what to say. “That's a new element outside the original brief — happy to include it, my rate for additions is [X]. Want to proceed?”

And here's the truth. Your time and skill are finite. Scope creep is not creative collaboration — it's extraction in slow motion.

### 7. “THE CAMP GAVE YOU A WALL”
*Camp Ownership / The Wall With Strings — clinically, resource control / manufactured obligation.*

Here's who this is. The camp or art collective that offers you a wall, a stage, a platform — in exchange for labor, loyalty, compliance, or silence about what happens inside that space.

Picture this. "We're giving you the main wall — that's a massive opportunity. We just need you to also help with camp setup, do a couple smaller pieces for the kitchen, and be around for our events as artist-in-residence." What started as one wall becomes three pieces, ten hours of camp labor, mandatory presence at events, and a growing sense that saying no to anything risks losing the whole opportunity. By Sunday you're exhausted and haven't made anything you actually wanted to make.

Here are the signs.

- The initial opportunity expands after you've committed — goalposts keep moving
- The space or wall is used as leverage whenever you express a limit
- "After everything we've given you" appears when you push back
- Your artistic choices are subject to camp approval rather than your own vision

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Offering a platform and then expanding the terms of accessing it is a classic exploitation structure — you've already committed, walking away means losing what you came for, and each small additional ask seems reasonable. This is scope creep as manipulation. A real artist-in-residence opportunity has defined terms before you arrive. If the terms are defined verbally and keep expanding in practice — you're not a resident artist. You're camp labor with an art cover story.

Here's your check. The E check: “Can I walk away from this arrangement right now without losing something I shouldn't...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Who Actually Owns The Finished Piece. When you paint on someone's wall, at someone's event, on someone's surface — who owns the finished work? Physical custody of a wall is not the same as copyright ownership of the art on it. Before you paint: establish in writing whether you retain reproduction rights to your own images of the work, whether the piece can be painted over without your consent, whether your name must accompany any reproduction, and whether they can sell images or prints. US Copyright Office (copyright.gov): your work is automatically copyrighted the moment you create it.

Here's what you do. Define the terms before you commit. Changing terms after commitment is pressure, not negotiation.

Say this, out loud. “I'm excited about the wall — can we nail down exactly what's included on both sides before I commit?”

Here's the truth underneath it. A platform with undefined terms is not an opportunity. It's a blank contract you're signing in paint.

### 8. “IT'S FINE, THE TENT'S OPEN AT ONE END”
*Solvents, Aerosols And Four Days In A Marquee — clinically, occupational chemical exposure / inadequate ventilation.*

Let's talk about who does this. The environment, plus a schedule that wants eight hours of live painting a day inside a structure designed to keep rain off rather than to move air.

Here's how it plays out. You are spraying in a marquee with the sides down because it is raining. There are three other painters doing the same thing and a crowd standing in it watching, and by the fourth hour you have the headache you always get and have stopped noticing the smell, which is the part that should worry you most.

Watch for these.

- Aerosols or solvents used in an enclosed or half-closed structure
- You have stopped smelling it, which means saturation rather than safety
- Headache, dizziness, nausea, or a sore throat by mid-afternoon
- No respirator, or a dust mask being used as though it filtered vapour
- The public standing in the same air with no ventilation plan
- Consecutive long days, because the exposure is cumulative

Here's what's really going on. Solvent and aerosol exposure is a genuine occupational hazard with cumulative effects on the nervous system and the airways, and live painting at festivals reproduces almost every condition that makes it worse: enclosed space, long consecutive days, no extraction, and no employer carrying a duty of care. The critical detail is that a dust mask does nothing against organic vapour — it is the wrong class of protection entirely, and it produces a false sense of safety that leads people to work longer in worse air.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “Can I smell it, and is air actually moving through here?” — If you have stopped smelling it and nothing is moving, leave now rather than at the end of the piece.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Acclimatization Is Not Tolerance. The dangerous signal is the one that feels like good news. When the smell fades you have not adapted, your olfactory receptors have saturated — and you are now breathing the same concentration with the warning system switched off, which is why people work through it and why the worst exposures happen to the most experienced painters on the longest days. Cumulative solvent exposure over years of festivals is a known occupational pattern and it does not announce itself. Buy the right cartridge respirator, insist on open sides, and take the piece outside when the rain stops.

Here's the play. Carry an organic-vapour cartridge respirator, not a dust mask, and know when the cartridges expire. Insist on cross-ventilation as a condition of the pitch. Break outside every hour. Use water-based paint where the piece allows it, and keep the public out of the immediate air.

Here are the words. “I need the sides up on at least two walls or I can't spray in here — it's a vapour thing, not a preference. — Taking twenty outside, back at four.”

Remember this. When you stop smelling it, your warning system has failed, not the fumes. That is the moment to go outside.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Camp walls that own you, credit theft, financial manipulation.*

### 9. “WE'LL PAY YOU AFTER THE EVENT”
*Payment Delays / Non-Payment for Creative Work — clinically, financial exploitation / payment manipulation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Getting paid what you were promised, on the timeline agreed, is the baseline. It is also one of the most commonly violated terms for creative workers at festivals. The variations are endless. The pattern is always the same.

Here's the scene. "The accounting is still getting sorted — payment will be in your account by end of month." End of month: "we had some unexpected costs, we're going to need to adjust the final payment." Or: you delivered the commission, the client went quiet, three weeks later you're following up for the third time. Or: the event was three months ago and the check that was "in the mail" has never arrived.

Here's what to watch for.

- Payment delayed past agreed date without prior communication
- "Unexpected costs" or "accounting issues" appear after the work is delivered
- Communication becomes vague or slow after the work is delivered
- Each follow-up produces a new reason and a new timeline

Here's what's actually happening. Cash flow manipulation is a feature, not a bug, of predatory event economics. Your leverage decreases dramatically once you've delivered the work — which is why deposits exist. Get 50% before you start. Invoice immediately on delivery. Send written payment requests with your contract attached. Paper trail everything. Most non-payers rely on artists being too tired, too embarrassed, or too non-confrontational to pursue it. Pursue it.

Run the check. The C check: “Do I have a signed agreement with payment terms — so I have something to enforce?”

Here's the dark reality. When They Don't Pay — Your Options. Step 1: written demand email with contract attached, specific payment deadline (7-14 days). Step 2: small claims court handles disputes up to $10,000-$25,000 depending on state — filing fee usually under $100, you represent yourself. Step 3: for larger amounts, one attorney demand letter (often $150-300) frequently resolves it. Step 4: publicizing non-payment in artist and vendor communities is legal and effective — many predatory organizers rely on silence. Document everything before going public.

Here's your move. 50% deposit before you start. Invoice immediately on delivery. Paper trail everything.

Here's exactly what to say. “Per our agreement, the remaining balance of [X] is due within 7 days. Please confirm receipt of this.”

And here's the truth. You created the work. You get paid what was agreed. This is not complicated and it is completely enforceable.

### 10. “YOUR ART ON OUR MERCH”
*Image and IP Theft / Unauthorized Reproduction — clinically, intellectual property theft / copyright violation.*

Here's who this is. Your designs, your process images, your finished work appearing on someone else's merchandise, social media, or promotional materials — without your permission, without credit, and often generating revenue for them while you receive nothing.

Picture this. You find your work on a festival's merchandise table — a tote bag and two t-shirts featuring a painting you created for them two years ago. You were paid for the original painting. You were not paid for any reproduction rights. Nobody asked. When you bring it up you're told "but we paid for the painting" — as if payment for the original work included unlimited commercial reproduction rights forever. It didn't.

Here are the signs.

- Your work appearing on merchandise, promotional materials, or commercial contexts without notice
- "We paid for the painting" used to justify unlimited reproduction rights
- Your work appearing in advertising, sponsorship materials, or branded content
- Digital versions of your work being sold, licensed, or reproduced without your knowledge

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Payment for an original artwork does not transfer reproduction rights unless explicitly stated in writing. Copyright in the original work belongs to you. The buyer purchased the physical object — not the right to reproduce it commercially. This is not a gray area. It is copyright law. The protection is a written agreement before any commission that specifies exactly what rights the buyer receives and what they do not.

Here's your check. The A check: “Has anyone asked me in writing for permission to reproduce this work — or did they just...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Reproduction Rights — What To Include In Every Commission. Your commission agreement should specify: (1) what rights the buyer receives — typically "right to display the original artwork" only, (2) that all reproduction rights remain with the artist, (3) any commercial use (merchandise, advertising, print) requires a separate license with additional fee, (4) your name must appear with any digital reproduction. US Copyright registration ($65 at copyright.gov) gives you the ability to sue for statutory damages ($750-$30,000 per infringement) — making enforcement financially viable.

Here's what you do. Define reproduction rights in writing before any commission. Payment for the original piece is not payment for reproduction rights.

Say this, out loud. “Reproduction rights are separate from the original — if you'd like to use this work on merchandise we'll need a separate licensing agreement.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You created it. You own the right to reproduce it. Every commercial use requires your permission and your compensation.

### 11. “YOUR ART IS YOUR LABOR”
*Rates, Rights, and Protecting What You Make — clinically, professional sovereignty / artist rights.*

Let's talk about who does this. This is the practical page. Rates, rights, contracts, credit. The unglamorous infrastructure that means you can keep making art without being slowly drained by every festival you attend.

Here's how it plays out. The artists who thrive on the festival circuit long-term are not the ones who work hardest. They're the ones who figured out their value early, put it in writing consistently, and stopped apologizing for having a professional framework inside a culture that frames everything as family and love. You can love this community AND operate professionally within it.

Watch for these.

- Know your day rate and piece rate before any conversation — not during it
- Every engagement in writing: fee, deliverables, credit requirements, ownership, cancellation
- Photograph your own work before, during, and after — your documentation is your record
- Watermark your digital images before sharing widely online

Here's what's really going on. Festival culture's "we're all family" framing is beautiful and also systematically used to remove the professional frameworks that protect artists. Family doesn't charge family. Family doesn't need contracts. Family gives freely. This creates the conditions for systematic exploitation of your labor. You can operate with full love for the community AND maintain professional clarity about how your work is valued. In fact, artists who know their worth make the community stronger by modeling that art has value.

Ask yourself this. The R check: “Is my rate for this engagement something I decided — or something that got decided...

Here's what's underneath all of it. Resources For Artists. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts (vlany.org): free/low-cost legal help on contracts, copyright, and disputes. US Copyright Office (copyright.gov): automatic copyright on creation, registration ($65) enables enforcement. Graphic Artists Guild Handbook — Pricing and Ethical Standards: industry reference for rates. CERF+ (cerfplus.org): emergency assistance for working craft artists facing crisis. Artists' legal clinics exist in most major cities — search "volunteer lawyers for the arts [your city]."

Here's the play. Set your rate. Put it in writing. Say it out loud without apologizing. Repeat.

Here are the words. “My rate for live painting is [X] for the weekend. Does that work with your budget?”

Remember this. Knowing your worth and saying it out loud is not ego. It is what keeps you in the game.

### 12. “WE RAN YOUR STUFF THROUGH A MODEL”
*Your Style, Regenerated Without You — clinically, generative appropriation / style replication.*

Here's who you're dealing with. A promoter, a brand, or a former client who has discovered they can get something that looks like your work without booking you — and who genuinely does not understand why you would object, because no file of yours was copied.

Here's the scene. The poster for this year's event is unmistakably yours. The palette, the linework, the way you do figures. You did not make it. When you ask, they say they made it in-house, and then, cheerfully, that they used your last three pieces as references, and they say it as though that were a compliment rather than a description of what they did.

Here's what to watch for.

- Work that reproduces your recognizable style without your involvement
- “In the style of”, or your name used as a prompt reference
- A former client who stopped booking you and whose visuals did not change
- Your live-painting footage or process shots used as training references
- A contract clause licensing your work for “any purpose including machine learning”
- They are baffled by your objection and cite that nothing was copied

Here's what's actually happening. Style is not protected the way a specific work is, which makes this legally murky and commercially devastating — a festival that can generate something close enough to your look does not need to book you, and the thing that made you employable becomes the thing that replaces you. The practical defence is therefore contractual and evidential rather than legal after the fact: what you license, to whom, for how long, and whether machine learning uses are excluded. Most artists have never read that clause and most event contracts now contain one.

Run the check. The A check: “What exactly did I license, and does it exclude training and generation?” — If you have never checked, assume it does not.

Here's the dark reality. The Clause You Signed In A Queue. Look at the media and artist agreements you have already signed, because the grabbing happens there rather than later. Perpetual, worldwide, sublicensable, all media now known or hereafter devised, for any purpose — that language predates all of this and covers it completely, and the artists most exposed are the ones who signed it years ago and never looked again. Add an exclusion in writing to every future agreement, register the significant pieces where registration exists in your country, and document your process with dated footage. Provenance is what you will have instead of a remedy.

Here's your move. Add an explicit exclusion to every contract — no training, no generative reuse, no style derivation — and get it in the email even when you cannot get it in the document. Watermark and register key work. Keep dated process footage. Raise it once, in writing, with the actual decision-maker rather than publicly.

Here's exactly what to say. “My licence excludes any machine-learning or generative use, including style references — can you confirm that in writing before I invoice? — That poster is derived from my work. I'd like to talk about a fee or its removal.”

And here's the truth. They did not copy a file, they copied the decade. Put the exclusion in the contract, because there is no remedy afterwards.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*Contracts, rates, saying no, protecting your work and rights.*

### 13. “THE REAL COLLAB”
*What Genuine Creative Partnership Actually Looks Like — clinically, authentic collaboration vs extraction.*

Here's who this is. Real collabs exist. They're some of the best creative experiences in the festival world. Here's how they're different from the extractive version — so you can recognize and pursue the genuine ones.

Picture this. She approaches you and says she's been watching your work and she does immersive sound installations and she thinks your visual language and her sonic language could be interesting together. "I'm not sure what it would look like — want to just experiment and see? No output pressure, no specific deliverable, just exploration. And if we make something we both want to show, we figure out the terms then." No agenda. No pitch. Just curiosity. That's different.

Here are the signs.

- Both parties' contributions are named honestly before work begins
- Decision about whether to share the output happens AFTER creation — not before as leverage
- Credit structure is proposed as a conversation, not a demand
- The person is genuinely interested in you as an artist, not in what you can do for their project

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Real collaboration is characterized by mutual curiosity, defined terms, and shared investment in the outcome. The distinction from extraction is visible in who carries the risk, who defines the terms, and whether both parties' contributions are named honestly. Real collaborators are comfortable with the credit conversation because they're not trying to hide the imbalance. Real collaborators celebrate each other's other work and connections rather than centralizing everything through themselves.

Here's your check. The A check: “Is this person interested in my whole artistic practice — or just in what my hands can do...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. How To Propose A Real Collab. When you want to genuinely collaborate with another artist: (1) Express specific interest in their work and why you think your practices could be interesting together. (2) Propose exploration without a predetermined outcome or output obligation. (3) Agree on credit and ownership structure BEFORE you create anything you might want to share. (4) Be comfortable with "this didn't produce something we want to show" as a valid outcome. The best creative partnerships in the festival world start with this kind of honest, low-stakes curiosity.

Here's what you do. Pursue real collabs with full enthusiasm. Protect yourself from extraction with clear terms. Both can be true.

Say this, out loud. “"I'd love to explore this — before we create anything, let's agree on how we'd handle credit and ownership if something comes out of it worth sharing."”

Here's the truth underneath it. The real collabs are out there. Protect yourself from the fake ones so you have the energy to find them.

### 14. “DIGITAL PROTECTION FOR ARTISTS”
*Your Online Presence, Your Work, Your Safety — clinically, digital security / IP protection.*

Let's talk about who does this. Your digital presence as an artist at festivals creates specific vulnerabilities: your work being appropriated without credit, your images being used without consent, and your online presence being used to locate or contact you in ways you didn't invite.

Here's how it plays out. Someone has been using your work without credit for three months. You've found it on their Etsy, their promotional materials, their social media. You've found your face — from a photo someone else took at a festival — being used in a "free creative" stock image collection. You've received DMs from a man who clearly knows your festival schedule, your booth location, and details about your process that feel like surveillance. Each thing is individually manageable. Together they paint a picture.

Watch for these.

- Your work appearing online without credit or permission in commercial contexts
- Photographs of you from festivals used in contexts you didn't authorize
- Someone who seems to know your festival schedule, locations, and movements in detail
- DMs that reference specific details about your process or whereabouts that weren't publicly shared

Here's what's really going on. Digital exploitation of artists operates on multiple simultaneous tracks: IP theft (your work), image rights violations (your likeness), and stalking behavior (your location and schedule). Each track requires different tools. All of them require documentation. Screenshot everything that might be relevant before you report it — platforms remove content on reports and you lose your evidence if you don't capture it first.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Have I documented this situation before reporting it — do I have screenshots and dates?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. Digital Safety Basics For Festival Artists. (1) Watermark your digital work — your name and website embedded in the image itself, not just the caption. (2) Use a dedicated email for professional inquiries — not your personal email. (3) Don't share your festival schedule publicly beyond the event you're at. (4) If you feel someone is tracking your location or movements: document it, tell a trusted person, contact festival security, and if it escalates contact local law enforcement (Stalking Prevention Awareness and Resource Center: stalkingawareness.org). (5) Your festival address, vehicle, and home location are private. Treat them that way.

Here's the play. Watermark everything. Document anything concerning. Report before you think you should.

Here are the words. “[To festival security]: "Someone has been tracking my location and movements across events. I'd like to make a report."”

Remember this. Your online presence is an extension of your work. Protect it with the same intentionality you bring to your art.

### 15. “I DON'T WANT TO PAINT THAT”
*The Brief You Object To / Declining Without Burning The Circuit — clinically, creative autonomy / reputational risk in refusal.*

Here's who you're dealing with. A promoter, a brand or a camp with a brief that conflicts with something you actually hold — imagery you find appropriative, a sponsor you object to, a message you do not want your name on.

Here's the scene. The brief lands and it is a good fee and there is a piece of it you cannot do: a symbol that is not yours to paint, or a brand you have publicly criticized, or a theme you think is straightforwardly bad. And the circuit is small, and saying no to this promoter means something for next season, and you are already assembling a version where you do it and put it in a different corner of your portfolio.

Here's what to watch for.

- The brief conflicts with something you would defend in public
- You are negotiating with yourself rather than with them
- The fee is doing the arguing
- Nobody has offered a route to modify the brief
- You are planning to leave it off your portfolio, which is the tell
- You expect refusal to cost you future bookings

Here's what's actually happening. This is a live-painting problem more than a studio one, because you make the work in public, with your name on the pitch, in front of the audience whose opinion is your actual asset. A piece you are uncomfortable with is not a private compromise; it is a public statement made in your hand, photographed, and attributed to you for years. The practical question is rarely whether to refuse outright — most briefs have a component that can be changed — and the useful move is to separate the part you object to from the job and offer an alternative.

Run the check. The T check: “Would I be happy for this to be the piece people associate with me?” — Planning to leave it out of your portfolio is the answer arriving early.

Here's the dark reality. Refusing Well Is A Skill Worth Having. The real risk is not refusing, it is refusing badly — late, publicly, or with a moral framing that makes the promoter the villain in a small circuit where everybody talks. That is what costs bookings, and it is avoidable. Decline early, privately, warmly, with an alternative attached and no lecture, and you will usually keep the relationship and occasionally get the brief changed. Nobody is owed your explanation for why, and delivering one is what turns a professional no into an argument. The artists who last on a circuit are the ones who can say no in a single friendly sentence and still be booked the following year.

Here's your move. Decline early and privately, name only the specific element, and offer a concrete alternative. Do not explain your ethics at length and do not go public. If the fee is genuinely tempting, ask for the brief to change rather than negotiating with yourself.

Here's exactly what to say. “Love the rest of it and I'm keen — the one thing I can't paint is [element], it's not mine to use. If you're happy with [alternative] in that space I'm in, and if not, no hard feelings, I'd still love to do next season.”

And here's the truth. You make your work in public with your name on it. A piece you would hide is a piece you should have declined.

### 16. “BURNOUT IS NOT A BADGE”
*Over-Giving as a Control Dynamic — clinically, compassion fatigue / boundary erosion.*

Here's who this is. The pressure to create publicly, generously, and constantly — for the community, for the vibe, for the love of it — while ignoring the reality that your creative capacity is finite and the constant demands on it are not always coming from a neutral place.

Picture this. You came to the festival to create and you've made twelve pieces, done two free workshops, gifted four finished works, painted a wall for the camp, contributed to a collab you didn't really want to be part of, and helped three other artists with their setups. It's Sunday and you're empty. You feel proud and resentful simultaneously. You gave everything and you're not sure you gave it to the right people or the right places or even because you wanted to.

Here are the signs.

- Requests for your creative labor accumulate until you can't find your own work in your schedule
- "For the community" is used to override your "I need to stop now"
- Your giving is received as a baseline expectation rather than a choice
- You feel proud and resentful simultaneously — the resentment is information

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Giving is a value in festival culture and creative communities. The shadow side is that it can be systematically exploited — where one person's generosity is treated as an extractable resource rather than a chosen gift. When the word "no" starts to feel selfish inside a community that calls itself loving, that community is using your values against you. Resentment is the signal that giving has crossed from chosen to coerced.

Here's your check. The E check: “Enough is enough — am I still giving from abundance or am I giving from depletion?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What Sovereign Creative Giving Looks Like. Sovereign giving is giving from fullness — you have something to give, you choose to give it, and the giving leaves you replenished rather than depleted. The test: do you feel genuinely good after giving, or do you feel relief that the request has been satisfied? Relief is not generosity. Relief is appeasement. You are allowed to say: "I'm not available for this right now." You are allowed to charge for your time. You are allowed to choose what you make and for whom. These are not failures of community. They are the conditions that make sustainable community possible.

Here's what you do. Give from fullness. Stop when you're depleted. Resentment is information, not ingratitude.

Say this, out loud. “I'm not available for any more projects this weekend — I need to be protective of my own creative energy.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your creative capacity is finite. Treating it like an unlimited public resource is not community. It's extraction.
