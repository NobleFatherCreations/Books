# A.C.C.E.S.S. — DISABLED & NEURODIVERGENT
### Disabled & Neurodivergent Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 257 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is A.C.C.E.S.S.: Disabled & Neurodivergent Edition. The scene says everyone belongs. This guide is for making that true for you — and for spotting the person who uses your access needs as a door.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **A** — Access Is Not A Favor. Your accommodations are not gifts. Anyone who frames them as generosity has set up a debt you never agreed to — and a thing they can withdraw.
- **C** — Control Of Your Equipment Is Yours. Your chair, your cane, your meds, your hearing aids, your AAC device. Nobody moves, holds, or “looks after” them without you saying so, every time.
- **C** — Capacity Changes And That Is Data. A yes at hour two is not a yes at hour twenty. Fatigue, pain, and sensory load all move the line. Somebody tracking when you are most depleted is not being attentive.
- **E** — Exit Requires A Route. An exit you cannot physically use is not an exit. Know how you leave — terrain, distance, lighting, who pushes — before you agree to be anywhere.
- **S** — Support People Are Chosen. Whoever helps you should be someone you picked, who can be replaced without your weekend collapsing. One irreplaceable helper is a single point of failure.
- **S** — Sensory Overload Can Be Aimed. Sometimes the wall of sound and light is just the festival. Sometimes it is where you were walked on purpose, because you decide worse there and you both know it.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*The helper who becomes the gatekeeper, access needs as leverage, inspiration framing.*

### 1. “I'LL BE YOUR HANDS THIS WEEKEND”
*The Volunteer Carer / The Helper Who Becomes The Gate — clinically, caregiver grooming / manufactured dependency.*

Here's who this is. Shows up early, helps beautifully, and keeps helping until helping is the only way anything gets done. Learns your routine faster than your friends did. Frames every act of assistance as devotion rather than logistics, so that by day three declining anything feels like rejecting a person instead of declining a task.

Picture this. He offered to push on the gravel stretch Thursday and he was genuinely good at it — read the terrain, asked before every curb, never grabbed the handles without saying so. By Friday he is carrying your meds because your bag was full. By Saturday he knows the schedule better than you do, and when you mention going to the silent disco with the two people from your camp, he says he doesn't mind, he'll come, it's easier, they don't know how to do the transfer properly.

Here are the signs.

- Helps with one thing, then quietly becomes the route to all things
- Learns your routine and your medication times unusually fast
- Frames assistance as love or devotion rather than as a task anyone could do
- Discourages other people from helping — “they don't know how you like it”
- Is present for every interaction you have with anyone else
- Reacts to you making a plan without him as though it were a betrayal

Here's the mechanism underneath it. This is dependency grooming with an accessibility costume on. The mechanism is not the helping — the helping is often real and skilled. The mechanism is consolidation: the steady narrowing of who can assist you until one person controls movement, medication, and access to everyone else. Disabled people are victimized at substantially higher rates than non-disabled people, and the perpetrator is disproportionately someone occupying a care or assistance role, because that role supplies proximity, privacy, physical contact, and a ready-made explanation for all three. The tell is never a single act. It is the direction of travel: every week he is load-bearing for one more thing, and every week there is one fewer person who could take over.

Here's your check. The S check: “If this person left right now, would my weekend still work — or would it stop?” — If it would stop, that is not closeness. That is a single point of failure with feelings attached.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Helper Nobody Questions. The reason this role is chosen is that it is socially armored. A man pushing your chair reads as a good man to every single person who walks past, which means he can stand closer, stay longer, and be alone with you more than anyone else at this festival — in full view, with everyone approving. If you later say something was wrong, you are contradicting a thing a hundred people watched and admired. That is the actual asset, and it is why the same role attracts the same people at every event. None of this makes the good ones bad. It makes the question worth asking early, while the answer is still cheap: who else could do this, and do they have my number?

Here's what you do. Keep at least two people who can do any given task, and use both in the first twenty-four hours so it is normal rather than a statement. Split the functions on purpose — the person who pushes is not the person who holds meds is not the person who knows the plan. If someone objects to redundancy, that objection is the finding.

Say this, out loud. “I run a two-person system for everything — it's how I stay independent, not a comment on you. Can you take the morning and let Rae take the evening?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Help you can only get from one person is not help. It is a bottleneck, and bottlenecks are where people who want control go to stand.

### 2. “YOU'RE SO INSPIRING”
*The Admirer / Inspiration As An Opening Move — clinically, objectification via idealization / inspiration framing.*

Let's talk about who does this. Approaches with enormous warmth and a compliment about your existence rather than anything you did. Talks about your courage within ninety seconds of meeting you. Wants to tell you what seeing you here means to them, at length, and wants you to receive it.

Here's how it plays out. You are in the line for water. A woman you have never met takes your hand — takes it, does not ask — and tells you that seeing you out here has genuinely changed her whole weekend, that she was complaining about the walk and then she saw you, and she just had to say it. She is crying a little. Three people are watching. You have about four seconds to decide whether to be gracious about a thing you did not agree to.

Watch for these.

- Compliments your existence rather than anything you chose or made
- Touches you, your chair, or your service animal while saying it
- Uses words like brave, courage, inspiring about you getting through a normal day
- Wants a photograph, sometimes before asking your name
- Positions your presence as being about their emotional experience
- Reacts badly to a flat “thanks” — the gratitude is what was being purchased

Here's what's really going on. Inspiration framing converts a person into a symbol, and symbols do not get to have boundaries. Once you are a source of someone's uplift, declining anything reads as cruelty — you are refusing to be the good thing that happened to them today. It is usually not predatory. It is usually a person feeling something clumsily out loud. But it does exactly the same work as a deliberate opening move: it establishes touch without consent, creates an audience that expects grace from you, and installs a debt in a stranger's favor inside two minutes. People who are running something deliberate use it because it works, and because the follow-up — I just feel a connection with you — is already half-built.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Is this about something I did, or about what my existence is doing for them?” — Warmth about your work is a compliment. Warmth about your existence is a job you did not apply for.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Audience Is The Trap. The spectators are not incidental — they are the pressure. A stranger who wanted a private moment would not do this in a queue. Doing it in front of people means any “please let go of my hand” has a cost, and both of you know it. This is the same architecture as the public grand gesture that makes a no expensive, and it is the reason the follow-up ask so often arrives about ninety seconds later, while the audience is still there and still approving. You are allowed to be the one who breaks the scene. The discomfort belongs to the person who started it.

Here's the play. Answer the content, not the frame, and keep your hands to yourself first: pull back, say something flat and unmemorable, redirect to a task. Flatness is the tool. A symbol cannot survive a boring answer.

Here are the words. “Ah, thanks. I'm just here for the music, same as you. — Hey, could you pass me that?”

Remember this. You did not come here to be somebody's feeling. Being ordinary in public is a thing you are allowed to insist on.

### 3. “LET ME CARRY YOUR MEDS”
*The Keeper / Medication As A Leash — clinically, medication control / coercive access to essentials.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Reasonable, practical, and correct that your bag is full. Offers to hold the thing you cannot go twelve hours without. Sometimes a partner, sometimes a friend, sometimes the helper from page one who has already been given three other jobs.

Here's the scene. Your bag is genuinely overloaded and he is genuinely trying to help and the pouch with the afternoon dose genuinely does fit better in his pocket. Then it is 4pm and he is at the other stage and not answering, and you are doing the arithmetic about how bad this gets, and discovering that the answer to “how long can I wait” is now a question about somebody else's schedule.

Here's what to watch for.

- Offers to hold medication, insulin, inhalers, or a charger you medically depend on
- Is slow to return them, with a good reason each time
- Suggests you don't need a dose right now, or that you seem fine
- Uses the meds as a reason you should come with him rather than bringing them to you
- Gets wounded at the suggestion you keep your own
- Is the only person who knows where they are

Here's what's actually happening. Whoever controls access to something you cannot skip controls your movements, your timing, and eventually your willingness to argue. This is textbook coercive control and it is well documented in intimate-partner contexts involving disabled partners — withholding medication, mobility equipment, or assistive technology is one of the most common forms it takes, precisely because it is invisible to outsiders and easy to explain away as forgetfulness. At a festival it needs no cruelty to work. Ordinary flakiness produces the same dependency, and a person who wants leverage only has to notice how well it worked and do it again.

Run the check. The C check: “Am I holding everything I cannot skip — meds, dose, charger, spare?” — If the answer routes through somebody else's pocket, that is a leash regardless of who is holding it.

Here's the dark reality. The Dose You Have To Ask For. The second half of this is the part people miss: once the asking starts, the asking itself becomes the leverage. You begin choosing your battles — not raising the thing that annoyed you, not saying you want to go to the other stage — because you have to ask this person for something at six o'clock and you do not want it to be awkward. Nobody threatened anything. Nobody had to. That quiet self-editing around a person who holds something essential is the clearest early sign of coercive control there is, and at a festival it can install itself in a single weekend. Carry your own. Carry a spare somewhere else. Tell one other person where the spare is.

Here's your move. Split your supply: primary on your body, spare in a second location with a second person, and a photograph of the label and dose on your phone. Never consolidate. If someone is already holding them, retrieve them in daylight, in company, cheerfully, with a logistics reason and no accusation.

Here's exactly what to say. “I keep my own — pharmacy rules, and I've been burned before. Can I grab that back off you? I'll take the water bottle instead, that's the heavy bit.”

And here's the truth. Anything you cannot skip lives on your body. That is not distrust. That is the same reason you keep your own passport.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Equipment and medication control, sensory positioning, masking exhaustion.*

### 4. “LET'S GET YOU SOMEWHERE QUIETER”
*The Overload Navigator / Sensory Load As A Steering Wheel — clinically, environmental coercion / engineered decision fatigue.*

Here's who this is. Notices — correctly — that you are struggling with the noise and light, and offers the relief. Sometimes that is kindness and the quiet place is the medical tent. Sometimes the quiet place is his tent, and the route there went past the loudest stage on site for longer than it needed to.

Picture this. Four hours in, the bass from the main stage is doing the thing where it stops being sound and becomes a physical pressure in your jaw. Your ability to hold a sentence together is going. He says he knows somewhere quiet. You would agree to almost anything right now to make it stop, and some part of you clocks that, and the part of you that would normally clock it properly is the part that is currently offline.

Here are the signs.

- Waits until you are visibly overloaded before making the ask
- The quiet place is private rather than designated — his tent, not the chill dome
- Takes a route that extends the overload rather than shortening it
- Discourages the sensory tools that would fix it without him — loops, defenders, the quiet zone
- Frames leaving as an emergency requiring immediate agreement
- Is unbothered by asking you to make decisions in a state where deciding is hardest

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Decision-making degrades measurably under sensory load, and for autistic and sensory-sensitive people the degradation arrives sooner and steeper. A person who has noticed this has a reliable instrument: they do not need to argue you into anything, they only need to wait, and to ask at the bottom. Nothing about this is exotic — it is the same mechanism as the 3am ask, the twentieth-hour ask, the ask after the long walk. What is specific here is that the pressure is ambient and continuous, the venue supplies it for free, and steering you through it looks exactly like helping.

Here's your check. The S check: “Am I agreeing to this, or agreeing to make it stop?” — Those feel identical from the inside. Only one of them is a decision.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Relief Is Not Consent. The cruelty of this one is that it uses your own coping strategy against you. Getting out of overload is the correct instinct — your body is right — and that correctness is what makes the offer so hard to refuse. Afterwards it is also what makes it hard to name: you went willingly, you wanted to go, you were relieved to go. All true. And none of it means you agreed to what happened once the noise stopped. Wanting the noise to end is not consent to whatever is on the other side of the noise. If the quiet place is private and the person offering it is the reason you are deciding badly, the answer is a designated quiet space with staff in it, every time.

Here's what you do. Pre-commit while you are regulated: name the quiet places on the map Thursday, put loops or defenders somewhere you can reach one-handed, and agree a signal with one person that means walk me to the chill dome and stay. Pre-committing is the whole technique — you are deciding now for a version of you who will not be able to.

Say this, out loud. “I'm at capacity, so I'm not deciding anything right now. I'm going to the quiet zone with Sam and I'll find you after.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Decisions made to end sensory pain are not decisions. They are escapes. Know your exits before you need them, and make them ones that do not require agreeing to anything.

### 5. “YOU SEEMED FINE EARLIER”
*The Mask Auditor / Using Your Coping Against You — clinically, masking exploitation / capacity misattribution.*

Let's talk about who does this. Has seen you hold it together and now treats that as your baseline. Cites your good hours back at you when you set a limit in your bad ones. Sometimes genuinely confused. Sometimes using it.

Here's how it plays out. Friday you were sharp, funny, on it for six straight hours, because you were masking hard and it cost you everything you had. Sunday you cannot process a sentence with three clauses in it and you need to leave. He says you were fine on Friday. He says you managed then. He says, and this is the one that lands, that it feels like you only get tired when there is something you do not want to do.

Watch for these.

- Cites your best hour as proof of your real capacity
- Treats a variable condition as a chosen inconsistency
- Suggests your limits are convenient or strategic
- Is surprised by fatigue after long social effort, repeatedly, having been told
- Uses “but you did it before” as an argument rather than a question
- Never asks what it cost

Here's what's really going on. Masking and the crash that follows it are well documented, and so is the cost: sustained camouflaging is associated with exhaustion and worse mental health outcomes, and the better you are at it the less anyone believes the crash. Fluctuating and invisible conditions generate the same problem — a person who saw your good hour now has evidence, and evidence is what gets used on you. Most people doing this are not malicious; they are pattern-matching badly. But it produces exactly the conditions someone deliberate needs: your own limits become debatable, and each time you defend them you spend energy you did not have, until conceding is cheaper than arguing. That is the mechanism, whoever is running it.

Ask yourself this. The C check: “Am I explaining my capacity, or defending it?” — Explaining is information. Defending means somebody has made it a negotiation, and it was never theirs to negotiate.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Believed On Friday, Doubted On Sunday. This is the groundwork for the thing that matters later. A person who has spent a weekend establishing that your limits are inconsistent has also established that your account of things is inconsistent — and if something happens, that is the frame already sitting in everyone's head. It is the same reason disabled people's reports get discounted in general: the credibility was undermined before the incident, usually by nothing more sinister than repetition. Notice who argues with your capacity. Not because arguing means they will hurt you, but because the ones who will need that groundwork laid, and they lay it in public, in advance, sounding reasonable.

Here's the play. Stop justifying. State the limit as a fact with no evidence attached, because evidence invites cross-examination. Then use your people: the ones who saw Friday's cost are the ones who can say so without you spending anything.

Here are the words. “Friday cost me today. That's how this works for me. I'm heading back — not up for discussing it.”

Remember this. A limit is not a claim that needs supporting. You do not owe anyone the receipts for your own body.

### 6. “CAN I SAY HELLO TO YOUR DOG”
*The Distractor / Interference With A Working Animal — clinically, assistance animal interference / functional disruption.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Mostly delighted, drunk, well-meaning people, arriving at a rate of roughly one every four minutes all weekend. Occasionally somebody who has worked out that distracting the dog is a way to get at you.

Here's the scene. She is in her vest and she is working and a man crouches down in front of her going hey, hey gorgeous, clicking his fingers, and behind him two other people are also crouching, and one of them is offering her a chip. Your dog's whole job is to notice things about you and she has just spent forty seconds noticing him instead.

Here's what to watch for.

- Approaching, touching, feeding or calling a dog in a working vest
- Continuing after being told she is working
- Treating your no as unfriendliness rather than as a medical instruction
- Using the dog as an opening to keep talking to you
- Letting their own off-lead dog approach yours
- Photographing her, or you, without asking

Here's what's actually happening. A distracted assistance dog is a disabled piece of equipment. An alert dog that misses a signal, a guide dog that loses the line, a mobility dog that breaks position — these are functional failures with real consequences, and a festival supplies distraction at a density found almost nowhere else. Feeding is the most dangerous version because it can make a working animal ill for the rest of the weekend, which removes your support entirely. The legal position in most places is clear that interference is not permitted; the practical position is that fifty people a day will do it anyway.

Run the check. The C check: “Is my equipment currently able to do its job?” — A dog is equipment while the vest is on, and this is the one piece of equipment strangers will touch without asking.

Here's the dark reality. The Dog As An Approach. There is a version of this that is not about the dog at all. An assistance animal is a guaranteed conversation starter with a person who cannot easily leave, and somebody who wants sustained access to you has an unlimited, socially approved reason to keep returning — asking after her, bringing her water, wanting a photo. It is the inspiration approach from earlier in this guide with a better cover story, and it is harder to refuse because refusing looks like you are being precious about a dog everybody can see is lovely. Watch for the person who comes back. Once is a stranger; the fourth time is a pattern.

Here's your move. Put a bright DO NOT DISTRACT patch on the vest so the sentence is pre-said, and use a flat one-liner without a smile so it does not open a conversation. Keep her water and her food entirely under your control, and do not accept treats from anyone.

Here's exactly what to say. “She's working, please don't — and please don't feed her, it makes her ill. — [flat, turning away] Thanks.”

And here's the truth. She is not a pet this weekend. She is the reason you can be here, and a stranger's forty seconds of fun costs you her attention.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Being disbelieved, dependency lock-in, the accessible space that becomes a closed one.*

### 7. “NOBODY'S GOING TO BELIEVE THAT”
*The Discrediter / Pre-Loading Your Unreliability — clinically, testimonial injustice / credibility undermining.*

Here's who this is. Has been telling a gentle, affectionate story about you all weekend — that you get confused, that you exaggerate, that you had a lot on board, that you are a bit much by Sunday. Says it fondly. Says it to everyone. Says it before anything has happened.

Picture this. You tell a camp mate what happened. You watch her face do a thing. Not disbelief exactly — something slower and worse, a recalculation. And you realize you have heard the groundwork for that face all weekend, in the affectionate way he kept saying you were away with it, in the way he checked with other people whether you had taken anything, in the three separate times he mentioned to somebody else that you get muddled.

Here are the signs.

- Narrates your reliability to third parties when you are not there to hear it
- Frames your condition, meds, or substance use as a reason to discount you
- Is warm about it — concern is the delivery mechanism
- Establishes the frame before any incident, not after
- Positions himself as the calm one and you as the one who needs interpreting
- Speaks for you in conversations you are physically present for

Here's the mechanism underneath it. This is testimonial injustice — the documented pattern where a person's account is given less weight because of who they are rather than what they said — and disabled people, particularly those with cognitive, psychiatric, or communication disabilities, are among the most consistently affected. The barrier to reporting most often named by disabled survivors is the expectation of not being believed, and that expectation is usually correct and usually pre-installed. What makes the festival version sharp is speed: a weekend is long enough to seed a reputation in a group of strangers and short enough that nobody has a competing impression to weigh it against.

Here's your check. The A check: “Is somebody explaining me to people, when I am standing right here?” — Being interpreted to others is how being doubted gets built.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Why They Pick Us. Say the ugly part plainly, because knowing it is protective: a person looking for someone to hurt and not be caught for is looking for a low-credibility target, and our whole culture pre-supplies that. Not because of anything about us — because of what people already assume. That is the actual reason victimization rates are higher, and it has nothing to do with vulnerability in the sense people mean when they say it. The counter is not becoming more credible, which is not available to you and is not your job. The counter is documentation, which does not care whether anyone finds you credible: a timestamped message to somebody not at this festival, sent at the time, saying who you are with and where. It is boring and it is the single most useful thing in this guide.

Here's what you do. Build a record that exists outside this weekend and outside your own account. Text one off-site person a name and a location each time you go anywhere with someone new — short, dated, ordinary. If the discrediting is already running, go to a specific person with a specific factual sentence rather than to the group.

Say this, out loud. “I'd rather speak for myself, thanks. — [to your off-site person] With Dane, north camp, 11:40pm. Tell me you got this.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You cannot argue somebody into believing you. You can make a record that does not need them to.

### 8. “THE ACCESSIBLE VIEWING PLATFORM”
*The Closed Room With A Ramp / When The Safe Space Has One Gatekeeper — clinically, controlled environment / access gatekeeping.*

Let's talk about who does this. The person with the wristband list, the radio, or the key to the lift. Sometimes staff, sometimes a volunteer, sometimes the guy who has simply been doing it for years and nobody checks. Decides who gets up there, who stays, and who is being difficult.

Here's how it plays out. The platform is genuinely the best thing about this festival for you — sightlines, space, somewhere to be that is not a crowd. It is also small, elevated, roped off, and run by one man who controls who comes up. He has started saving you a spot. He has started mentioning that he saved you a spot. On Saturday he asks, warmly, in front of the others up there, whether you are going to come find him later.

Watch for these.

- One person controls entry and there is no posted process or named supervisor
- Access is framed as a personal favor rather than an entitlement
- Your place is “saved” for you, repeatedly and audibly
- Other disabled attendees have been quietly excluded and nobody explained why
- The same person is there every shift, every year, and “just handles it”
- Raising anything would cost you the only place on site that works for you

Here's what's really going on. Any resource that is scarce, essential, and controlled by one unsupervised person becomes leverage regardless of the intentions of whoever holds it. Accessible platforms, accessible camping, the golf cart, the lift, the interpreter roster — these are exactly that shape, and they sit in a blind spot because they are framed as provisions rather than as power. The risk is not that such spaces are unsafe; they are often the best-run part of a festival. The risk is structural: when the cost of complaining is losing the one thing that makes the weekend possible, complaints do not get made, and the absence of complaints gets read as everything being fine.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “If I had a problem with this person, who else could I go to — by name?” — If there is no second name, the space has a gate, not a policy.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Cost Of Complaining Is The Whole Weekend. This is why these situations run for years. A person in this role who is exploiting it is protected by arithmetic that everybody can do: reporting him means losing platform access, possibly for the rest of the event, possibly at every event he works. So people wait, and compare notes afterwards, and discover it was all of them. The pattern repeats across every gatekept accommodation in every scene, and it ends the same way each time — not because one person was brave, but because several found each other. If you are reading this and recognizing it: you are not the first one, and the others are findable.

Here's the play. Find the second name on arrival — the accessibility coordinator, the duty manager, the number on the back of the wristband — before you need it, while asking is neutral. Introduce yourself to another regular on the platform. Two disabled people who know each other's names is already a network, and it is the thing gatekeeping depends on not existing.

Here are the words. “Who's the accessibility lead on shift, and what's the best way to reach them? I like knowing before I need it.”

Remember this. An accommodation that depends on one person's goodwill is not an accommodation. It is a favor, and favors can be withdrawn.

### 9. “WE'LL JUST LIFT YOU OVER”
*Being Moved Without Consent / Terrain As A Trap — clinically, non-consensual physical handling / mobility deprivation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Helpful, strong, and already reaching for your chair's handles or your arms. Genuinely trying to solve a problem — the mud, the step, the gap — that the event should have solved and did not.

Here's the scene. The path has turned to mud and you are stuck, which was entirely predictable in October. Three men decide to help. Nobody asks how your chair lifts. Somebody takes the armrest, which is not a lifting point and now bends, and you are briefly airborne and entirely at the mercy of people who mean well and have no idea what they are doing, and your footplate is being used as a handle.

Here's what to watch for.

- Hands on you or your equipment before anyone asked
- Lifting by armrests, footplates or wheels rather than the frame
- “We've got you” said while you are trying to explain how it works
- Being carried somewhere you did not agree to go
- Terrain the event never made passable, treated as your problem
- The offer of help proceeds regardless of your answer

Here's what's actually happening. Two failures stack here. The event's, which is that accessible routes are an obligation and mud is foreseeable; and the crowd's, which is that helping a disabled person is treated as something that does not require permission. Being lifted incorrectly causes real injuries to both people and to expensive, hard-to-replace equipment, and a chair damaged on Saturday cannot be repaired before Monday, which turns a bad moment into the end of your weekend and possibly your independence for weeks.

Run the check. The E check: “Did I plan a route I can actually use, in this weather?” — And in the moment: “Did anybody ask me before touching me?”

Here's the dark reality. Moved Is Not The Same As Helped. Underneath the practical risk is the one that matters more. A person who can be picked up and relocated by strangers without agreement has had their ability to leave taken away, and being carried somewhere you did not choose is the single most efficient way to isolate a disabled person — no coercion needed, no conversation, just a friendly group and a mud patch. Most of the time it is exactly what it looks like. The habit worth building is the one that holds in both cases: nobody moves you or your equipment without your instruction, and the instruction includes where you are going.

Here's your move. Say stop first and instruct second — people follow clear instructions and freeze at arguments. Know your chair's actual lift points and say them out loud. Pre-plan hard-standing routes and ask the accessibility team about wet-weather alternatives on arrival, before the mud exists.

Here's exactly what to say. “Stop — hands off, please. If I want lifting I'll tell you where to hold. It's the frame here and here, and we're going to the hard path, not the tent.”

And here's the truth. Help you did not agree to is just being moved. You decide whether, how, and to where.

### 10. “YOUR ACCESS PLAN”
*The Fifteen Minutes That Make The Weekend Work — clinically, preventive planning / access infrastructure.*

Here's who this is. This page is the boring one and it is the one that works. Everything here is done before you arrive, in a calm room, by a version of you who is not tired, not overloaded, and not being asked anything by anyone.

Picture this. It is the Tuesday before. You have fifteen minutes and a notes app. Everything that will go wrong on Saturday night is currently a thing you can fix in one sentence, and on Saturday night it will not be, because the whole point of Saturday night is that you will not have the capacity to invent a plan from nothing.

Here are the signs.

- Terrain and distance: where you sleep, where the accessible toilets are, how far, what surface
- The two-person rota: who does what, and who is the backup for each
- Meds on your body, spare elsewhere, photo of the label and dose on your phone
- The quiet places, located on the map before you need one
- The accessibility coordinator's name and contact, obtained on arrival
- One off-site person who expects a check-in and will notice if it does not come

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Everything in this guide is easier to counter in advance than in the moment, because every mechanism in it works by targeting a depleted version of you. Pre-commitment is the general answer: decisions made Tuesday hold on Saturday in a way that Saturday's decisions do not. This is also the part where you get to stop treating your access needs as a negotiation. Written down as logistics — surfaces, distances, timings, names — they stop being a thing you justify to people and become a thing you operate. That shift in framing is doing as much work as any individual item on the list.

Here's your check. The E check: “Can I answer, right now, how I physically leave from where I will be tonight?” — If not, that is the fifteen minutes.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Plan Is For The Version Of You Who Cannot Make One. Here is the reason this page is not optional filler. Every mechanism in this guide — the helper who consolidates, the quiet place that is private, the ask at the bottom of an overload — works by arriving when your capacity is lowest. None of them work on Tuesday. That asymmetry is the whole game, and the plan is the only move that plays on your side of it, because it lets a rested version of you make the decisions and hands them to a depleted version who only has to follow. People skip this page because nothing has gone wrong yet. That is precisely when it costs fifteen minutes instead of a weekend.

Here's what you do. Write the six lines above in a note titled with the festival's name. Share it with your two people before you travel. Photograph your meds. Get the coordinator's contact at the gate, not on Sunday.

Say this, out loud. “Here's my access plan for the weekend — two minutes to read. Tell me if you can't cover the bit I've put you down for, that's completely fine, I'd just rather know now.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The plan is not because you expect something to go wrong. The plan is so that a tired version of you does not have to invent one.

### 11. “SCRIPTS FOR WHEN WORDS ARE EXPENSIVE”
*Pre-Written Sentences for Low-Capacity Moments — clinically, self-advocacy scripting / cognitive load reduction.*

Let's talk about who does this. For anyone whose speech, processing, or patience goes first when things get hard — which by Sunday is most people, and for a lot of us is Thursday. Scripts are not weakness. They are the same reason pilots read checklists out loud.

Here's how it plays out. Somebody is standing too close, asking something that needs a real answer, and the part of your brain that builds sentences has gone. You know what you want. You cannot currently construct it. Every second of silence makes the next one harder, and the person in front of you is reading the silence as agreement.

Watch for these.

- You freeze or go flat when you most need to say no
- You say yes to end an interaction and regret it inside a minute
- Your speech gets harder exactly when the stakes get higher
- You need processing time and people fill it
- Written or typed communication is easier than spoken, and nobody offers it
- You have said “it's fine” about something that was not fine, this weekend

Here's what's really going on. Under load, generating a novel sentence is expensive and retrieving a memorized one is cheap. That difference is the entire technique. Pre-written lines survive shutdown, overload, pain, and the specific pressure of an audience, because they do not require you to compose anything — you are reading, not writing. They also remove the part that usually costs the most, which is deciding how much to explain. The scripts below explain nothing. That is deliberate: explanation invites negotiation, and negotiation is the thing you do not have the resources for right now.

Ask yourself this. The C check: “Do I have a sentence ready, or am I going to have to build one?” — Ready is the difference between a no and a yes you did not mean.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Silence Gets Read As Yes. The part that does real damage is not the freeze itself — it is what other people do with it. A pause while you assemble a sentence gets read as consideration, then as agreement, and somebody who wants a yes will take it and move. Afterwards it is extremely hard to explain that you did not agree, you just could not answer in time, because from the outside those look identical and the other person will say honestly that you did not object. This is why non-speaking and intermittently-speaking people are disproportionately affected, and why the counter is not courage. It is having the sentence already built, so that answering costs nothing you do not currently have.

Here's the play. Put four of these in your phone's notes, pinned, in large text, so you can hold the screen up instead of speaking if that is where you are. Practise saying two of them out loud before you travel, so your mouth already knows the shape.

Here are the words. “I need a minute before I answer. — I'm not deciding anything tonight. — No, and I'm not going to explain it. — I need you to step back, please. — Can you type it? I'm not processing speech right now.”

Remember this. A sentence you prepared on Tuesday is still your sentence on Saturday. Borrowing from your own calm self is not cheating.

### 12. “IF YOU CANNOT HEAR THE ANNOUNCEMENT”
*Deaf And Hard-Of-Hearing At A Music Festival — clinically, communication access / emergency information exclusion.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For Deaf and hard-of-hearing attendees, and for anyone whose hearing aids or implants have to survive four days of dust, rain and a hundred and ten decibels.

Here's the scene. Every piece of safety information at a festival is delivered by tannoy: the weather warning, the stage closure, the evacuation route, the missing child, the thing that has just happened at the other end of the site. All of it is audio, all of it is over a distorted PA, and none of it reaches you. You will find out what happened when the crowd starts moving.

Here's what to watch for.

- Emergency and safety announcements are audio-only with no visual equivalent
- No interpreter listed, and no text alert or app notification option
- Staff default to talking louder rather than to writing it down
- Your aids or processor have no dry storage, spare batteries, or backup
- Nobody in your group has agreed a visual signal for we are leaving
- You have not told welfare that you will not hear a tannoy

Here's what's actually happening. Deaf attendees are systematically excluded from the emergency information layer of an event, and that is a safety issue rather than an inclusion one — evacuation instructions, severe weather warnings and stage cancellations are all broadcast in the one modality you cannot receive. Most events will provide interpreters, text alerts or a welfare contact if asked in advance, and almost none provide them by default. The equipment problem is separate and severe: rain, dust and a dead battery are all far more likely here than at home, and a processor lost at a festival is a five-figure object in a field.

Run the check. The A check: “How will I find out if something goes wrong on this site?” — If the only answer is the tannoy, you have no route to emergency information.

Here's the dark reality. Evacuations Are Announced Out Loud. The scenario that matters is the rare one. A crowd evacuation, a severe weather call, a stage closure that redirects forty thousand people — these are announced by PA, once, urgently, and then everyone around you starts moving in a direction you do not understand for a reason you have not been told. Moving in a dense crowd without knowing why is how people get hurt. Arrange the alternative in advance, in writing: ask the event for text alerts, register with welfare so they know to find you, and agree a physical signal with your group that means leave now, follow me, no questions.

Here's your move. Email the event before you travel about interpreters and text alerts. Register at welfare on arrival so somebody knows you will not hear a tannoy. Bring a dry box, spare batteries and a backup, and agree a visual emergency signal with your group.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm Deaf and I won't hear announcements — can you register that at welfare and tell me how safety info gets out here? — [to your group] If we need to go, this signal. Don't explain, just do it and I'll follow.”

And here's the truth. The safety information at a festival is audio-only. Arrange your own channel before you need it, because there will be no time afterwards.

### 13. “FOUND YOUR PEOPLE VS BEING SOMEBODY'S PROJECT”
*Real Crip And ND Community, And How To Tell — clinically, authentic community vs benevolent tokenization.*

Here's who this is. The good version is other disabled and neurodivergent people, and the non-disabled people who have been around them long enough to have stopped performing. The other version means well, sometimes intensely, and needs you to be receiving something.

Picture this. Two things happen the same weekend. A woman with a cane and a folding stool sees you doing the arithmetic about the distance to the second stage, and says without ceremony that the north path is flat and there is a bench two-thirds of the way, and then talks to you about the lineup for twenty minutes and never mentions either of your bodies again. And a man tells you three times that his camp is a safe space for you and asks whether you are comfortable and asks again and asks whether you are sure.

Here are the signs.

- Real: gives you information and then moves on to the actual subject
- Real: assumes competence — asks before helping, accepts no without a second attempt
- Real: you are allowed to be annoying, wrong, or boring without losing standing
- Project: needs you to confirm they are doing well at including you
- Project: helps without asking, and is hurt when that lands badly
- Project: your presence in their camp gets mentioned to other people

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Real community is identifiable by one thing: whether you can decline without it costing you. In genuine crip and ND space, refusing help, leaving early, going non-verbal, or simply being unimpressive changes nothing about your standing — there is no ledger. Tokenizing warmth is transactional even when it is sincere, and the transaction is that your comfort confirms someone's self-image, which means your discomfort becomes a problem for them rather than for you. The scene has an enormous amount of the real thing in it. It also has people for whom your inclusion is a credential, and the two can sit in the same camp.

Here's your check. The S check: “Can I say no here and have it change nothing?” — If declining costs you standing, it was never community. It was an arrangement.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. When Your Inclusion Is Somebody's Credential. The uncomfortable version: a person whose self-image depends on being good to you has a stake in you staying in a position where they can be good to you. That rarely looks like malice. It looks like discouraging the other camp, being hurt when you make plans elsewhere, reminding people how welcome you are here. The consequence is the same as the deliberate version on the first page of this guide — your options narrow — and it is harder to name because everyone involved, including them, believes it is kindness. The test stays the same and stays cheap: decline something small and see whether the temperature changes.

Here's what you do. Test cheaply and early: decline one small offer and watch what the relationship does. Then find the other disabled and neurodivergent people on site, because they are the network that makes everything else in this guide survivable — the platform regulars, the quiet-zone regulars, the ones with the good folding chair.

Say this, out loud. “Thanks — I'm good, I've got a system. — Hey, are you here every year? What's the terrain like round the back stages?”

Here's the truth underneath it. The people who are actually your people will let you be ordinary. That is the whole test, and it works in under a minute.
