**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 261 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is S.O.B.E.R.: Sober & In Recovery Edition. You do not need a substance to be here and you never did. This guide is about the people who need you to be the exception.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **S** — Sober Is Not An Explanation You Owe. “I'm good” is a complete sentence. Anyone who requires the story behind it is asking for something other than information.
- **O** — Open Containers Are Not Yours. You do not accept a drink you did not watch being made. This is the same rule everyone should follow, and for you the consequences are larger.
- **B** — Being The Reliable One Has A Price. The sober friend gets handed everything. Care is a gift you choose, not a duty assigned to you because you are the one who remembers the night.
- **E** — Exit Before The Turn. Every night has an hour where it changes. Yours is earlier than theirs. Leaving early is not missing out, it is how you get the next twenty years of this.
- **R** — Relapse Is Not A Character Verdict. If it happens, it is a medical and practical event with a next step. It is not proof of anything about who you are — and shame is what keeps people from the next step.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*The convincer, the spiked drink, sobriety as a target.*

### 1. “ONE WON'T HURT YOU”
*The Convincer / Your Sobriety As Their Problem — clinically, peer coercion / social proof pressure.*

Here's who this is. Usually not malicious. Frequently drunk. Genuinely believes he is being welcoming, and is also, underneath it, uncomfortable that somebody nearby is not doing what he is doing. Persistent in a way he would not be about anything else.

Picture this. Fourth time tonight. He is holding it out again, doing the thing where he waggles it slightly, saying he is not trying to pressure you but it is a festival, and asking — warmly, in front of five people — what actually happened, like your sobriety is a story he is owed and the story will settle whether your no is reasonable.

Here are the signs.

- Asks more than once after a clear answer
- Wants the reason, and frames your reason as the thing that would justify the no
- Does it in front of an audience, so declining has a social cost
- Reframes your boundary as a mood you could be talked out of
- “Just this once”, “it's a festival”, “you're no fun like this”
- Gets cold or wounded when the fourth no lands

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The pressure is usually about him rather than about you — a person drinking heavily next to someone who is not is being handed an unwelcome mirror, and the fastest way to put the mirror down is to get you to join. That does not make it harmless. Repeated asking is how boundaries get worn rather than broken, and the reason-demand is the actual move: once you are explaining, you are in a negotiation, and negotiations have outcomes. You are not in a negotiation. The most effective response is also the most boring one, which is the same three words, at the same volume, indefinitely, with no new information added.

Here's your check. The S check: “Am I explaining?” — The moment you start supplying reasons, you have accepted that your no needs approving. It does not.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Some Of Them Know Exactly What You Are. Most of the people doing this are just drunk. A few are not, and the difference shows in what they do with the information once you give it. Someone who learns you are in recovery and keeps pushing is not being socially clumsy — they have been told there is a specific cost to you and have decided it is worth it, and a person who wants somebody impaired has just found the one person here whose impairment would come with built-in silence. This is why the reason is worth keeping to yourself in a crowd. Not shame — information discipline. Your recovery is not a secret; it is also not something to hand to a stranger who has already ignored three noes.

Here's what you do. Repeat the same three words with no escalation and no new content, and move your body — turn a shoulder, go get water, join a different conversation. Broken-record plus physical exit ends it faster than any argument, because there is nothing to push against.

Say this, out loud. “I'm good. — I'm good. — I'm good, mate. Anyway, has anyone seen Priya?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your no does not improve with an explanation attached. “I'm good” is already the strongest version of it.

### 2. “I MADE YOU ONE”
*The Handed Drink / Why This Rule Is Not Paranoia — clinically, drink spiking / non-consensual administration.*

Let's talk about who does this. Anyone holding a cup you did not watch being filled. Usually a friend being kind. Occasionally not — and the second category is why the rule has to apply to the first, because you cannot tell them apart by looking.

Here's how it plays out. Someone hands you a lemonade. They are being nice, they know you do not drink, they went and got it specially. Refusing it feels ungrateful and slightly insulting and like you are accusing a friend of something. It also costs nothing, and the alternative — a drink you cannot vouch for, in a body with no tolerance, in front of people who will later say you seemed fine — costs a great deal.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- A drink arriving already poured, that you did not see made
- Insistence when you set it down or ask for a sealed one
- Attention to whether and how much you are drinking it
- “It's only soft” as an argument for skipping the rule
- Someone treating the rule as an accusation rather than a policy
- Any drink left unattended, including yours, including for a minute

Here's what's really going on. The most common drink adulterant is alcohol added to something someone believes is soft, which matters enormously here: for a sober or in-recovery person that is not a lesser version of spiking, it is the specific harm. Zero tolerance means a small dose lands hard, and the aftermath is not only physical — it can register internally as a relapse, with all the shame that carries, even though nothing about it was chosen. The rule is the same one this whole book gives everyone: you pour it, you watch it poured, or you carry it sealed. The difference is that for you the failure mode includes losing your sense of your own recovery.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Did I watch this being made, or open it myself?” — If not, it is not a drink, it is an unknown.

Here's what's underneath all of it. When It Registers As A Relapse. Here is the part worth having read in advance, because it matters in the hour it happens: if you are dosed without knowing, that is not a relapse. It is an assault. Your body may not distinguish, your recovery community's language may not distinguish, and at four in the morning you will absolutely not distinguish — which is exactly why a person who wanted you compliant and quiet would choose this method. The shame does the silencing for them. Decide now, while it is hypothetical: if it happens, the first call is to somebody in your recovery network and the second is to medical, and neither of those calls is a confession. Say it out loud once so the sober version of you has already made the ruling.

Here's the play. Carry your own, sealed, and open it yourself. Decline anything handed to you already poured, cheerfully and as a blanket policy rather than as a judgment of the person offering. If you set a drink down, it is finished.

Here are the words. “Ah, cheers — I've got a weird rule where I only drink stuff I've opened myself, nothing personal. I'll grab one with you though.”

Remember this. Being dosed is not drinking. Decide that now, in daylight, so you are not deciding it at four in the morning.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Being assigned the care role, the FOMO squeeze, the hour the night turns.*

### 3. “YOU'RE THE ONE WHO REMEMBERS”
*The Designated Adult / Care Assigned Rather Than Chosen — clinically, role assignment / caregiver burden.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The whole group, gradually and without a conversation. Because you are the one who is sober, you become the one with the keys, the map, the phone battery, the plan, the medical decisions, and everybody's emergency.

Here's the scene. It is two in the morning and you are walking a stranger to medical because you are the only one upright enough to do it. This is the third night running. You came here for the music and you have seen about forty minutes of it, and somebody said earlier, as a compliment, that it is so good having you around because you always know what to do.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- You hold the keys, the cash, the phones, the plan — by default, never by agreement
- Your sobriety is described as a resource the group has
- You have not seen the sets you came for
- Other people's medical decisions keep landing on you
- Nobody has asked whether you want this role
- Resenting it makes you feel like a bad friend, so you do not mention it

Here's what's actually happening. Care work assigned by default rather than agreement is a well-documented route to burnout, and the festival version is unusually total: you are the group's driver, medic, treasurer, and institutional memory simultaneously, at exactly the time of year you came to enjoy yourself. It matters more than it sounds. Resentment plus exhaustion plus not having had any of the thing you came for is a genuinely recognized relapse-risk combination, and it builds quietly because the role arrives as a compliment. Choosing to look after your people is one of the good things about being the sober one. Being silently conscripted is not the same act.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The B check: “Did I agree to this role, or was it assigned to me because I'm the one who'll remember?” — Those produce the same behaviour and completely different costs.

Here's the dark reality. Resentment Is A Relapse Risk, Not A Character Flaw. The dangerous sequence is specific and it is worth being able to see coming: you do the caretaking, you miss the festival, you start resenting people you love, you feel guilty about the resentment because they are your friends and you chose this, and the guilt stops you saying any of it out loud. Depleted, resentful, isolated inside a group, and unable to name it — that is the state, and in recovery terms it is a familiar and serious one. Nobody engineered it. It assembled itself out of everyone's good intentions and the fact that you are reliable. The fix is a conversation on Tuesday, not heroism on Saturday.

Here's your move. Rotate it explicitly before the event — a named driver, a named phone-holder, a named person on duty each night, sober or not. Then take the sets you came for and say so in advance so it is a plan rather than an abandonment.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm not on duty tonight — I'm watching the whole set, phone on if it's an emergency. Who's got the keys? Let's actually rota this.”

And here's the truth. Being sober makes you capable of holding everyone. It does not make you responsible for it. Those are different sentences.

### 4. “THERE WAS SOMETHING IN THAT”
*Accidental Exposure / The Brownie, The Vape, The Shared Bottle — clinically, unintentional ingestion / involuntary exposure.*

Here's who this is. Nobody hostile. A camp full of people who forgot, or who genuinely did not consider that the tray of brownies needed a label, or that the vape going round has more in it than nicotine.

Picture this. You ate one thing from the communal table because you were starving and it was food. Forty minutes later you know. You are sitting in a camp chair doing arithmetic about whether this counts, whether you have to tell anyone, whether your sponsor will think you engineered it, and whether your sobriety date has just moved — and none of that arithmetic is helping with the part where you are currently high and did not agree to be.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Unlabelled food on a communal table
- A vape, joint or bottle passed round with no statement of contents
- Nobody in the camp thinks to mention it because everybody else knows
- A drink topped up rather than handed over
- You are the only sober person and the camp has not adjusted anything
- The first you hear of it is somebody laughing about it afterwards

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Accidental ingestion at festivals is common and it is a labelling failure rather than an attack — which does not make it less serious for you. Edibles are the usual route because they are food-shaped and the onset is delayed enough that you have eaten it before anyone could have told you. The practical harm is real — being involuntarily altered removes your capacity in an environment where you are frequently the person others are relying on — and the internal harm can be larger, because it lands as a relapse in a body that cannot tell the difference.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The O check: “Do I know what is in this, from the person who made it?” — Communal food counts. This is the rule the book gives everybody, applied to a plate.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. It Is Not A Relapse, And Decide That Now. Settle this in advance, in daylight, because at the time you will not be able to. Being dosed without agreeing to it is an exposure, not a use — there was no decision, so there is nothing to have relapsed from. Recovery frameworks vary in language but not in substance on this, and every sponsor you could call has fielded it. The danger is not the substance, it is the forty-eight hours afterwards, when shame says you are a fraud, that you must have known, that you cannot tell anyone — and that is the state that turns an accident into an actual return to use. Ring your person that night, not when you feel ready.

Here's what you do. Eat from your own supply at camp and ask about anything communal before it reaches your hand. Ask the camp once, openly, on Thursday to label things. If it happens: get somewhere safe with a person, hydrate, ride it out, and call your recovery contact the same day.

Say this, out loud. “Quick one before we settle in — I'm sober, so can we label anything with stuff in it? Doesn't have to be a whole thing, just a note on the tray.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You did not decide, so you did not relapse. Call your person the same night, before shame writes a different version.

### 5. “IT'S COMING ROUND AGAIN”
*The Rotation / Declining The Same Thing Forty Times — clinically, repeated refusal fatigue / social ritual pressure.*

Let's talk about who does this. The circle itself. The joint, the bottle, the balloon, the vape — a physical object moving clockwise, arriving at you every few minutes, requiring a small public decision each time, for six hours.

Here's how it plays out. It is not one refusal, it is the fortieth, and by the fortieth you have stopped saying anything and started doing the small hand movement, and somebody has noticed and said something kind about it which made it worse, and you are now thinking about leaving a fire you were enjoying because the geometry of the circle has made your sobriety the most visible thing about you all night.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- The object physically arrives at you on a loop
- Each pass is a small public moment you have to handle
- Somebody kindly announces your sobriety on your behalf
- The circle re-forms around you in a way that makes leaving obvious
- You are exhausted by evening and cannot say why
- You have started avoiding fires you actually like

Here's what's really going on. Refusal fatigue is real and underrated: the cost of any single no is negligible and the cost of forty is significant, particularly when each one happens in front of people and requires a micro-decision about tone. It is also the mechanism by which sober people quietly stop going to the parts of a festival they enjoy most, which produces exactly the isolation that everything else in this guide warns about. The circle is not doing anything to you. The geometry is doing it, and geometry can be changed.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The B check: “Am I avoiding somewhere I like because of the effort of being there?” — That is the cost showing up, and it is fixable without leaving.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Do Not Let It Push You Out. The outcome to prevent is not any single awkward pass, it is the slow withdrawal — skipping the fire, skipping the camp, spending the weekend at the edges, and ending up alone at a festival, which is the precondition for every pattern in this guide. That withdrawal is invisible while it happens because each individual decision is reasonable. If you find yourself three nights in and mostly on your own, that is not you being antisocial; it is forty small refusals adding up. Fix the mechanics rather than removing yourself: sit at the end of the arc, hold your own cup, and let one friend know to pass it over you.

Here's the play. Change the geometry — sit at the end of the rotation rather than mid-circle, keep both hands busy with your own drink, and ask one friend to skip you so the object never arrives. Pre-empt once, lightly, at the start of the night, so nobody announces it for you later.

Here are the words. “Pass it over me all night, saves us both forty conversations. — [holding up your cup] I'm sorted, cheers.”

Remember this. Forty small refusals cost more than one big one. Move your chair rather than leaving the fire.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Engineered relapse as leverage, being outed, the shame spiral.*

### 6. “NOBODY NEEDS TO KNOW ABOUT THIS”
*The Engineered Slip / Relapse As Leverage — clinically, induced relapse / shame-based silencing.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Someone who learned what your sobriety means to you and filed it. Manufactures the slip, or simply waits for one, and then becomes the only person who knows about it — which is the actual objective.

Here's the scene. It happened. However it happened, it happened, and he was there, and now he is being extraordinarily kind about it. He says it stays between you. He says he understands, no judgment, everyone slips. He says it about four times over the next two days, in a way that is comforting the first time and something else by the fourth.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Present for the slip, and unusually relaxed about it
- Immediately frames it as a secret the two of you now share
- Repeats the reassurance more often than reassurance requires
- Discourages you from telling your sponsor, your partner, your people
- Your recovery network is described as judgmental or as not understanding festivals
- Small asks start arriving, and declining them feels newly complicated

Here's what's actually happening. A secret held by one other person is leverage whether or not anyone ever names it, and shame is the most reliable silencer there is because the person being controlled does the work themselves. This is why isolation from your recovery network is the tell to watch rather than any explicit threat — the threat is never made, it does not need to be. Coercive control research finds this shape consistently: cut the person off from the relationships that would give them a competing account of themselves, then be the only one who knows the worst thing. What makes recovery a target is simply that the worst thing is already pre-loaded and everyone knows how much it costs you.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The R check: “Is there something about me only one person here knows?” — That is not intimacy. That is a holding.

Here's the dark reality. Disclosure Is How You Disarm It. The single move that ends this is the one that feels impossible: tell someone else. The leverage exists only while the information is scarce, and it evaporates the moment a second person knows — not because the slip stops mattering, but because he is no longer the only one holding it. Recovery communities are unusually good at exactly this; a slip disclosed is an ordinary event with a known next step, and everyone in your network has either had one or sat with somebody who did. The person telling you they will keep it between you is not protecting you from judgment. They are protecting the scarcity. Call your sponsor tonight, not when you feel ready — feeling ready is the thing the shame is preventing.

Here's your move. Break the exclusivity immediately: tell one person in your recovery network today, before the story gets any older. Then treat the slip as a practical event with a next step — that is all it is — and put distance between you and whoever wanted to hold it.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm telling my sponsor, so it's not staying between us — that's not how I do this. — [to your person] It happened Saturday. I'm okay. I wanted you to know before it got old.”

And here's the truth. A secret with one keeper is a lever. Hand it to a second person and it stops being one.

### 7. “ISN'T THAT STILL A DRUG”
*The Medication Auditor / Policing Your Prescription — clinically, recovery purity policing / medication stigma.*

Here's who this is. Somebody in your own corner — a recovery acquaintance, a camp mate who is proud of you, occasionally someone with a strong opinion about a programme — who has appointed themselves the judge of whether your sobriety counts.

Picture this. You take your prescribed medication in front of the camp, as you do every day, and somebody asks what it is. Then asks whether that is allowed. Then says, not unkindly, that they thought you were clean, and you find yourself standing in a field defending a prescription to a person with no medical training and considerable confidence.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Being asked to justify a prescription socially
- Talk of being properly clean, or real sobriety
- Your psychiatric or substitution medication discussed as a loophole
- Pressure to reduce or stop, from someone who is not your prescriber
- You have started taking your meds privately to avoid the conversation
- The same person is relaxed about their own caffeine, nicotine or painkillers

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Medication-assisted treatment and psychiatric prescriptions are medicine, and the evidence for them is strong — but purity narratives inside some recovery cultures treat them as a compromised form of sobriety, and the result is people quietly skipping doses to stay socially legitimate. That is the actual harm here and it is a serious one: interrupted psychiatric medication at a festival, with no sleep and heavy sensory load, is a genuine risk of crisis, and interrupted substitution treatment carries a risk of return to use with reduced tolerance.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The R check: “Has my prescriber said this is fine?” — That is the only opinion in this conversation that has any standing.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Hiding Doses Is How This Hurts You. Watch for the behavioural tell rather than the argument: the moment you start taking your medication where nobody can see, you have accepted that it is shameful, and skipping becomes easy from there. That is the sequence that lands people in a medical tent on Sunday or in a crisis three weeks later, and it starts with one well-meaning question in a field. Your prescriber knows your history, knows what you are taking and knows why. Nobody in that circle does. If somebody keeps pushing after a flat answer, that is not concern about your recovery, it is a person who needs your compliance to feel right about their own framework.

Here's what you do. Answer once, flatly, and do not debate. Take your meds on schedule, in the open, on the same routine you use at home. Tell one person in your camp what they are and what to do if you are unwell, so the information exists somewhere useful.

Say this, out loud. “It's prescribed, my doctor's across it, and I'm not discussing the dose. — Setting an alarm for it, same as at home.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The only person qualified to audit your prescription is the one who wrote it. Take it in the open and on time.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The festival sober plan, drink discipline, the people you find first.*

### 8. “YOUR FESTIVAL SOBER PLAN”
*Twenty Minutes That Hold All Weekend — clinically, relapse prevention planning / preventive structure.*

Let's talk about who does this. For anyone sober, in recovery, California sober, taking a break, or just not drinking this weekend. Made in advance, in a calm room, by a version of you who is not tired, lonely, or three days into a field.

Here's how it plays out. Tuesday, twenty minutes, a notes app. Everything that will be hard on Saturday night is currently something you can solve in one line. On Saturday night it will not be, because the whole difficulty of Saturday night is that it takes away the part of you that makes plans.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Your line, decided now: what you drink, what you never accept, what ends the night
- The hour you leave — an actual time, set before you arrive
- Two people on site who know you are sober and will not make it a thing
- One person off-site who picks up at any hour, tested before you travel
- The sober meetings on site, located Thursday — the Rats, the Phellowship, the sober camp
- What you do if you slip: who you call, in what order. Written down.

Here's what's really going on. Relapse prevention planning is the most evidence-backed piece of self-management in recovery, and its whole logic is pre-commitment — a decision made in a regulated state, handed to a depleted one, so the hard moment only requires following rather than deciding. A festival concentrates every known risk factor into seventy-two hours: sleep loss, crowds, availability, loneliness inside a group, and the specific ache of watching people you love have an experience you have chosen not to have. None of that means you should not go. It means you go with a plan, the way you would go anywhere else that mattered.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “Do I know what time I'm leaving tonight, and who I call if it gets hard?” — Two answers, decided before you arrive.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Write The Slip Plan While You Are Fine. Write the what-if-I-slip page now, while it is theoretical and slightly embarrassing to be writing. The reason is simple and it is the thing that kills people: after a slip, shame closes the exact channels you need, and the plan you did not write is not a plan you will invent at four in the morning while believing you are a fraud. Two names and an order of calls, written down on a Tuesday by someone who was fine, is a message from a version of you that your Saturday self will not be able to argue with. Nobody who writes that page is planning to use it. Everyone who needed it was glad it existed.

Here's the play. Write the six lines. Send the off-site person a message before you travel so they know they are on. Find the sober meeting on Thursday, before you need it — walking in on Saturday when it is hard is a much bigger ask than walking in on Thursday when it is not.

Here are the words. “I'm out by one tonight, that's fixed. — You're my call this weekend if it gets hard, is that alright? I probably won't need it, I just want it arranged.”

Remember this. The plan is not a prediction. It is Tuesday-you leaving instructions for Saturday-you, who will not be able to work it out alone.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*Sober scene inside the scene — where it already exists and how to find it.*

### 9. “THERE ARE MORE OF US THAN YOU THINK”
*The Sober Scene Inside The Scene — clinically, recovery community / peer support access.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The Wharf Rats with the yellow balloons. The Phellowship. The sober camp with the coffee urn. The Zendo volunteers. The ones who have been coming for thirty years and have not had a drink for twenty-five of them.

Here's the scene. You assume you are the only one. You are not remotely the only one — there is a meeting on this site, probably daily, probably signposted with a yellow balloon or a hand-lettered board, and the people at it have been navigating exactly this weekend for longer than you have been alive. Most of them found it the same way, which is late, having assumed the same thing you are assuming.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/sober/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Yellow balloons at the back of the crowd — that is the Wharf Rats, and it is an invitation
- A meeting board, a sober camp, a coffee urn where a bar would be
- Harm reduction volunteers, a lot of whom are sober themselves
- People who clock your soda and say nothing, which is the good sign
