# S.O.U.N.D. — MUSICIANS & TOURING
### Musicians & Touring Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 252 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is S.O.U.N.D.: Musicians & Touring Edition. Your music is the power. Don't let anyone else hold the switch.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **S** — Sign Before You Play. No contract, no soundcheck. Your performance has value. Protect it in writing. Every time.
- **O** — Own Your Masters. Who owns the recordings made of your set? Who owns the footage? Find out before you go on, not after it's posted.
- **U** — Understand The Room. Who's in the green room? Who has access? The most dangerous situations happen in spaces that feel private and protected.
- **N** — No Is A Full Sentence. To the promoter. To the superfan. To the manager. No explanation required. No is complete.
- **D** — Document Everything. Agreements, payments, incidents, communications. Screenshots. Paper trail. Your memory is not evidence.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*Promoter traps, false management, industry gatekeeping.*

### 1. “I CAN MAKE YOU”
*The Promoter Who Books and Controls — clinically, manufactured dependency / future faking.*

Here's who this is. The promoter with the network, the stages, the bookings. Positions themselves as the gatekeeper to your next level. Uses that position to extract below-rate fees, free promotion work, personal access, and silence. The next level stays perpetually 3 months away.

Picture this. "I'm building something with your sound specifically in mind — I need you for three dates this summer and if those go well we're talking main stage next year. We don't need a formal contract yet, this is about building a relationship first." Three shows later: you've covered your own travel, been paid 60% of what was agreed verbally, done promotional content for free, and the main stage conversation has vaporized.

Here are the signs.

- Future opportunity always slightly out of reach — dependent on your continued compliance
- Contract discussions deflected as premature or as signs of distrust
- Payment consistently below what was agreed verbally, framed as temporary
- Any friction triggers implicit threat to the whole relationship and future bookings

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The festival and music industry has a specific power structure that predatory promoters exploit: artists need bookings, bookings require promoter relationships, and challenging a promoter feels like risking your entire access to the scene. The "building something together" frame removes professional clarity while the promoter retains all the structural power. A legitimate promoter makes money when you get paid fairly.

Here's your check. The S check: “Is there a signed contract — or just a verbal promise and a sense that questioning it risks...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Minimum Performance Contract. Every booking needs in writing: performance fee, payment timeline (50% deposit before, 50% at show is standard), cancellation terms, accommodation and travel responsibilities, set time and length, technical rider requirements, who owns live recordings or footage, and promotional obligations on both sides. American Federation of Musicians (afm.org) has standard contract templates. Music attorneys offer one-time consultations. A legitimate promoter will sign. The ones who won't are giving you your answer.

Here's what you do. No contract, no soundcheck. This is a full sentence and a complete policy.

Say this, out loud. “I'm excited about these dates — let's get the agreement in writing so we're both protected. I can send my standard rider tonight.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Any promoter who won't put it in writing is showing you what the deal actually is.

### 2. “LET ME MANAGE YOU”
*Predatory Management / The Isolation Manager — clinically, access control / isolation + financial exploitation.*

Let's talk about who does this. The manager who positions themselves between you and every other industry relationship. "Let me handle this" becomes "let me control this" becomes "I am the only person who understands your vision." By the time you see it, most of your independent contacts are gone.

Here's how it plays out. She approached you after a set that went off. Clearly connected. "I think you're three months away from something major and I want to be the person who gets you there." No management contract yet. A year later: she takes 25% of everything including merch you handle yourself, discourages direct relationships with bookers ("let me handle it, it's cleaner"), and when you question any of it the conversation turns to everything she's done for you.

Watch for these.

- Discourages direct relationships with bookers, labels, promoters — "let me handle it"
- Commission structure expands beyond agreed terms without renegotiation
- Questions about finances or terms received as betrayal or ingratitude
- Industry contacts you had before have been quietly replaced by her relationships

Here's what's really going on. Predatory management isolates artists from their industry network under the cover of professionalism. When your entire industry network runs through one person, that person has complete leverage. Standard management commission is 15-20% of gross income from work THEY source — not your merch, not your pre-existing relationships. Know that standard before any conversation.

Ask yourself this. The N check: “Can I talk directly to my booker or label — or does everything go through them first?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. Management Contract Basics. A legitimate management contract specifies: commission rate and what it applies to, term length (1–2 years standard — not open-ended), sunset clause (they earn commission on deals they originated after the contract ends, for a defined period), and termination conditions for both parties. No management contract should give them power of attorney or control over your bank accounts. California Talent Agencies Act and similar state laws regulate management. Music attorney consultation before signing — non-negotiable.

Here's the play. Maintain direct relationships with at least 3 industry contacts independently of any manager. Always.

Here are the words. “"I appreciate you handling things — I also keep direct relationships with my bookers. That's just how I work."”

Remember this. A manager who needs to be your only industry contact needs to be your former manager.

### 3. “THE FESTIVAL WANTS YOU”
*The Bait and Switch Booking — clinically, bait and switch / manufactured dependency.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The booking that changes significantly between offer and show — stage size, set time, payment, production support — revealed after you've committed flights, accommodation, and your promotional energy to announcing the show.

Here's the scene. "We're really excited to have you — you'll be on the Garden Stage, Saturday evening set, solid slot." You announce it, post it, your fans make plans. Two weeks before the show: "there have been some lineup adjustments — we've moved you to the Morning Stage, Sunday 11am." The ticket buyers for your slot have already made travel plans. Pulling out now costs you more than accepting the downgrade.

Here's what to watch for.

- Details confirmed verbally don't match the written confirmation when it arrives
- "Adjustments" happen after you've publicly announced and promoted the show
- Changes arrive late enough that backing out carries significant cost
- Each change is framed as minor — the cumulative impact is significant

Here's what's actually happening. Bait and switch bookings work by getting your public commitment before you have the details in writing. Once you've announced a show to your audience, backing out has real social and professional cost — which the promoter counts on. The protection is simple: nothing goes on your social media until you have the booking confirmation in writing, with all material details (stage, set time, date, fee) confirmed. No exceptions.

Run the check. The S check: “Have I confirmed every material detail of this booking in writing before I announce...

Here's the dark reality. Announce Only After Written Confirmation. The sequence for any festival booking: (1) Verbal offer received. (2) Written confirmation requested — stage, set time, date, fee, production details. (3) Contract signed. (4) Deposit received. (5) Announcement made. Skipping any of these steps removes your leverage. Promoters who push you to announce before paperwork is signed are using your audience as collateral. Your fans are excited to see you — that excitement belongs to you, not to a promoter who hasn't put anything in writing yet.

Here's your move. Nothing gets announced until you have written confirmation of every material detail.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'd love to announce this — can you send me the written confirmation with stage, set time, and fee first?”

And here's the truth. Your audience's excitement is yours. Don't let it become someone else's leverage.

### 4. “AFTER PARTY AT MINE”
*Green Room Access as Grooming / Post-Show Vulnerability — clinically, access exploitation / post-performance vulnerability.*

Here's who this is. The post-show window is one of the highest-risk periods for performing artists. Adrenaline crash, endorphin flood, crowds wanting proximity — and a specific category of person who knows exactly how to exploit that window.

Picture this. You just came off a set that felt transcendent. Everyone backstage is warm and electric. Someone from the crowd has somehow gotten backstage — through who, you're not sure. They're beautiful and clearly want to be near you and the attention feels like an extension of the performance high. Two hours later, in a much quieter context with a much clearer head, you're trying to reconstruct how the decisions of the last two hours happened.

Here are the signs.

- People in backstage spaces you don't know how they got there
- The post-show adrenaline state is specifically targeted — decisions here are impaired
- Someone who zeroes in on you immediately post-performance before you've landed
- An entourage that appears after every show and gradually never leaves

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The post-performance state is a genuine altered state — high adrenaline, endorphin flood, identity in flux between performer-self and regular-self. Predatory fans and industry people know this window intimately and specifically target it. As the performer you're also in the position of power in that space — which means your responsibility is higher, not lower. This page is simultaneously about protecting yourself AND about not using the post-show window to cover your own behavior.

Here's your check. The U check: “Who is actually in this space — do I know how they got here and what they want?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Green Room Protocol — Yours To Set. As the artist, you set the terms of your backstage space. Legitimate protocols: explicit guest list with named individuals only (not "plus whoever"), a trusted person who manages access, a minimum 30-minute closed cooldown after you come off before socializing, and a clear policy communicated to promoters before the show about who can be back there. Your rider can include backstage access requirements. Use it.

Here's what you do. Land first. Decompress with your core people. Then decide who you want in your space from a clearer head.

Say this, out loud. “[To your team before the show]: "30-minute closed cooldown after we come off — then we open the room. Can you hold that?"”

Here's the truth underneath it. The post-show high is real and beautiful. It is also the window people have been waiting all night to exploit.

### 5. “THE SUPERFAN SITUATION”
*When Admiration Becomes Stalking — clinically, parasocial fixation / stalking behavior.*

Let's talk about who does this. Most superfans are genuinely loving people your music has meant something real to. Some are not. The ones who aren't operate with a sense of relationship and entitlement that your actual relationship with them does not support.

Here's how it plays out. She's been at six of your shows in three cities. Knows your setlist changes before you announce them. Has your tour schedule memorized. Shows up at the venue during soundcheck. DMs you daily — long, personal, detailed. When you don't respond she escalates. At the last show she got backstage and stood close in a way that felt like pressure. Your team dismisses it: "that's just a dedicated fan." Your body is saying something different.

Watch for these.

- Attendance at multiple shows in multiple cities without your invitation
- Knowledge of non-public information: hotel, soundcheck time, private schedule
- Access to backstage or personal spaces that should have been controlled
- Communication that escalates when you don't respond or engage

Here's what's really going on. Parasocial relationships — where a fan experiences genuine emotional intimacy with an artist who doesn't know them — are a normal feature of music fandom. They become dangerous when the fan can't hold the asymmetry and begins operating as though the relationship is mutual. The music industry often fails artists here by treating obvious stalking as enthusiasm. Your discomfort is data. Your body's signal about someone is data.

Ask yourself this. The D check: “Have I documented this person's behavior with dates, locations, and screenshots — sol...

Here's what's underneath all of it. Documenting And Reporting Stalking. Documentation: screenshot every message, note dates and locations of every appearance, keep a written log. When to report: when they appear at non-public locations, when they attempt physical contact you haven't invited, when communication escalates after you stop responding, when they contact people in your personal life. Report to: venue security (who can issue trespass notices), local police (a report creates a record even if no immediate action), Stalking Prevention Awareness and Resource Center (stalkingawareness.org). Report early rather than late.

Here's the play. Trust your body's signal. Document. Tell your team. Report early.

Here are the words. “[To your tour manager]: "I need you to document this person's appearances and pull their backstage access. I want a record."”

Remember this. "Dedicated fan" is not a category that overrides your safety. Your discomfort is data.

### 6. “ROAD ISOLATION”
*How Touring Disconnects You From Everyone Who Knows You — clinically, isolation / dependency on road crew.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Touring creates a specific kind of isolation — from your home community, your support network, and the people who knew you before the music. That isolation makes you more dependent on the people immediately around you, which creates leverage for anyone on tour with you who wants to exploit it.

Here's the scene. You're four weeks into a run and you haven't had a real conversation with anyone from home in two weeks. The tour manager controls the schedule, the transport, the accommodation, and by extension a lot of how your days feel. The sound engineer has been making comments about your decisions. Your bandmate has a habit that's starting to affect the shows and nobody will address it. You feel completely dependent on the very people who are causing friction.

Here's what to watch for.

- Your home support network feels increasingly remote and unreachable
- One person on tour controls multiple aspects of your daily logistics and wellbeing
- Decisions about your career are being made without your input during the run
- You're too exhausted and too far from home to push back on anything

Here's what's actually happening. Road isolation is structural — touring literally removes you from your support network and puts you in a temporary closed system where the people with you have enormous power over your daily experience. This is not inherently predatory but it creates conditions that are easily exploited. Maintaining active connection to your home network, having at least one person on tour who is genuinely in your corner, and preserving your own decision-making authority are the protections.

Run the check. The U check: “When did I last have a real conversation with someone who knew me before the music?”

Here's the dark reality. Maintaining Connection On The Road. Practical steps for staying connected during extended tours: (1) Weekly scheduled call with your most trusted person at home — protected in the schedule. (2) At least one person on tour who is genuinely your person, not the promoter's or label's. (3) Keep your own record of all agreements, all financial terms, all incidents — don't rely on tour management's records as your only documentation. (4) If something is happening on tour that feels wrong: name it to a trusted person at home who can help you think through it with outside perspective.

Here's your move. Maintain active connection to your home network throughout the tour. Schedule it.

Here's exactly what to say. “[To your most trusted person at home]: "Can we do a weekly call on Sundays for the duration of this run? I need someone external to check in with."”

And here's the truth. The road is a closed system. Keep one door open to the outside.

### 7. “WE OWN THE RECORDINGS”
*Live Recording Rights / Who Owns Your Festival Set — clinically, intellectual property exploitation / rights grab.*

Here's who this is. Every festival set you play is potentially being recorded — audio, video, professional and amateur. Who owns those recordings is determined by what's in writing before you play. If nothing is in writing: probably not you.

Picture this. Your set went viral — 400,000 views on the festival's YouTube channel. The recording is incredible. Fans are using it everywhere. The festival is now licensing it to a streaming platform for a compilation. You receive nothing because when you signed the booking confirmation — buried in the terms — you assigned all recording rights to the festival for all formats in perpetuity.

Here are the signs.

- Booking confirmation or contract contains a recording rights clause you didn't negotiate
- "Standard terms" applied to recording rights without specific discussion
- Festival recording professionally without having discussed rights with you
- Live recordings appear on streaming platforms or in commercial contexts without your knowledge

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Live recording rights are one of the most commonly overlooked and most valuable assets in a musician's career. Festival organizers routinely include sweeping rights grabs in standard booking paperwork. Your performance is a copyrightable work. Your likeness in those recordings is yours. A simple rider clause reserves your recording rights or requires negotiation. Without it, you're playing the show and handing them a product.

Here's your check. The O check: “Who owns the recordings being made of me tonight — and is that in writing before I go...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Recording Rights Rider Language. Add to your standard rider: "Artist retains all rights to audio and video recordings of the performance. Any recording by Presenter requires written agreement specifying format, distribution, compensation, and Artist approval rights prior to the event." For streaming: separate sync licensing fees apply. ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC collect performance royalties — make sure your set is registered. SoundExchange collects digital performance royalties. If a festival has a streaming deal, your performance may generate royalties you're entitled to but not receiving.

Here's what you do. Add recording rights language to your rider. Always. Non-negotiable.

Say this, out loud. “Before I confirm — I need to add my standard recording rights clause to this agreement. I can send the language tonight.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your performance is a product. Know who owns it before you create it.

### 8. “WHATEVER YOU NEED, IT'S SORTED”
*The Promoter Who Supplies / Hospitality As A Hook — clinically, substance provision as leverage / dependency cultivation.*

Let's talk about who does this. The promoter, the tour manager, or the local fixer whose hospitality is unusually comprehensive and who never seems to want paying for that part of it.

Here's how it plays out. The rider is fine and there is something else on the table that was not on it, and he says it is sorted, and this happens at every one of his shows. By the fourth booking you are aware that you look forward to his shows for a reason that is not the audience, and that the conversation about the fee has become a conversation you are less inclined to have.

Watch for these.

- Substances provided as hospitality, routinely and unasked
- Never itemized, never charged, never discussed
- Availability increases around contract or payment conversations
- Supply is tied to continuing to work with this person specifically
- Your consumption has escalated in a way that tracks the touring schedule
- You have stopped pushing on money and cannot say exactly when that started

Here's what's really going on. Supply is one of the oldest forms of leverage in this industry and it works without a single explicit exchange being proposed. It creates reciprocal debt, it degrades judgement during exactly the conversations where your judgement matters commercially, and it establishes a dependency that is tied to one relationship. It also builds an accumulating hold: somebody who has supplied you for two years knows things about your consumption that they can allude to, and that hold does not require any intention at the time to be usable later.

Ask yourself this. The N check: “Would I push as hard on this contract if the hospitality stopped?” — If the answer is yes, the hospitality is doing business.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Supply Is A Record Of You. The part that arrives years later is the knowledge. A person who has supplied you routinely has a detailed, private account of your use, and at the point where you want to leave, renegotiate, or raise something about their conduct, that account exists and both of you know it. Nothing has to be said explicitly; the industry is full of artists who simply found it easier not to push. Keep your own supply if you use, decline provided substances as a flat policy rather than a judgement, and keep the money conversation in writing and in daylight where the hospitality cannot reach it.

Here's the play. Make it a standing policy and say it early so it is not about anyone: your rider is your rider, and you do not take anything else from promoters. Do contract and money conversations by email, sober, before the show rather than after it.

Here are the words. “Appreciate it, I keep it to what's on the rider — blanket rule for me, nothing personal. — Let's do the fee by email in the morning rather than tonight.”

Remember this. Hospitality that is never itemized is not hospitality. It is an account being opened in your name.

### 9. “THE CHECK'S COMING”
*Payment Delays / Revenue Skimming — clinically, financial exploitation / payment manipulation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Getting paid what you were promised, on the timeline agreed, is the baseline. It is also one of the most commonly violated terms in festival and live performance. Your leverage decreases dramatically after you've performed.

Here's the scene. "The accounting is still getting sorted — it'll be in your account by end of month." End of month: "we had some unexpected costs with the event, we're going to need to adjust the final payment." Or: the merch sales from your table were handled by their team and the reconciliation is $800 short of what you counted. Or: your 50% deposit never arrived but your show is tomorrow and the flights are booked.

Here's what to watch for.

- Deposit due before show hasn't arrived and you're too committed to pull out
- Final payment delayed past agreed date without prior communication
- Merch or door split reconciliation doesn't match your own count
- "Unexpected costs" appear after the show as reasons for reduced payment

Here's what's actually happening. Cash flow manipulation benefits the promoter while your leverage decreases once you've performed. That's why deposits exist. Get them before you travel. Count your own merch. Send a payment reminder email with terms on Day 1 after the show. Paper trail everything. Most non-payers rely on artists being too tired or too non-confrontational to pursue it.

Run the check. The D check: “Do I have documentation of everything agreed financially — so I have something to...

Here's the dark reality. When They Don't Pay — Your Options. Step 1: written demand email with contract attached, specific payment deadline (7-14 days). Step 2: small claims court handles disputes up to $10,000-$25,000 depending on state — filing fee usually under $100. Step 3: music attorney demand letter ($150-300) frequently resolves it. Step 4: American Federation of Musicians (afm.org) has a Claims & Collections department. Step 5: publicizing non-payment in musician communities (Indie on the Move, local music forums) is legal and effective. Document everything before going public.

Here's your move. 50% deposit before you travel. Count your own merch. Send payment follow-up in writing the morning after.

Here's exactly what to say. “Per our agreement — the remaining balance of [X] is due within 7 days of the show. Please confirm receipt of this.”

And here's the truth. You performed. You get paid what was agreed. This is completely enforceable.

### 10. “YOUR REPUTATION OR YOUR COMPLIANCE”
*Reputation Threats / Blacklisting — clinically, coercion / reputation destruction.*

Here's who this is. The implicit or explicit threat that pushing back on a promoter, a manager, or an industry figure will result in being blacklisted, badmouthed, or frozen out of the circuit. One of the most effective control mechanisms in the music industry.

Picture this. You stopped working with the promoter who wasn't paying you. Two months later you start hearing through the circuit that you're "difficult to work with" and "unreliable." Your booking inquiries from other promoters in that network have slowed. You didn't do anything wrong. But the narrative is already in circulation and you don't know how to counter something you're not supposed to know is being said.

Here are the signs.

- Implicit or explicit threats to your reputation when you exercise independence
- Negative characterizations begin circulating after you end a bad deal
- Professional contacts who were previously warm become suddenly distant
- The person spreading the narrative directly benefits from your compliance

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Reputation threats work because the music and festival circuit is small, relationship-driven, and operates heavily on word of mouth. The goal is to make the cost of pushing back higher than the cost of compliance. It often works. The counter is: document everything before any dispute escalates, build independent relationships across the industry, and proactively communicate your version of events to people who matter to you. Being blacklisted by predatory promoters is often a feature, not a bug.

Here's your check. The D check: “Have I documented the facts of this situation so I have something to point to if my...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. When Your Reputation Is Being Attacked. (1) Document: exactly what is being said, where, by whom, and when. (2) Communicate directly with your most important professional relationships — not to spin but to share facts. "I wanted to reach out directly because I understand there may be things being said about me — here's what actually happened." (3) Let your work speak. Predatory promoters lose credibility over time as more artists share experiences. (4) Musician-specific reputation defense: being known as someone who stands up for fair pay makes you MORE attractive to legitimate promoters.

Here's what you do. Document the dispute. Communicate directly with people who matter to you. Let your work speak.

Say this, out loud. “[To a trusted industry contact]: "I'm reaching out directly because I understand there may be things being said — I wanted you to have the facts."”

Here's the truth underneath it. Being blacklisted by predatory promoters is often a feature, not a bug. Your real advocates will know the difference.

### 11. “THE RINGING STOPS EVENTUALLY”
*Hearing Damage / The Injury That Ends The Career Quietly — clinically, noise-induced hearing loss / tinnitus.*

Let's talk about who does this. The job itself. Monitors, the pit, load-in, the bus, three hundred nights a year, and a culture where asking for in-ears or wearing plugs still occasionally reads as not being committed.

Here's how it plays out. The ringing after a show used to last an hour and now it lasts until the afternoon. You have started asking people to repeat themselves in bars. You mix your own tracks slightly brighter than you used to, which is your ears telling you something you are choosing to hear as a preference.

Watch for these.

- Tinnitus that persists longer after each run
- Struggling with speech in noisy rooms
- Turning monitors up across a tour rather than down
- No custom moulded plugs, or a pair you have stopped using
- Ear fatigue changing the mixing decisions you make
- The idea that plugs would ruin the experience, which is the belief that costs the most

Here's what's really going on. Noise-induced hearing loss and tinnitus are the defining occupational injuries of this trade, they are cumulative, and they are permanent — there is no treatment that restores what is gone. The specific cruelty for musicians is that the damaged faculty is the instrument: the loss is not merely a disability, it degrades your ability to do the work, and it does so gradually enough that you will adjust to it rather than notice it. Custom moulded musicians' plugs attenuate evenly rather than muffling, which is the objection most people have and the one that is not true.

Ask yourself this. The D check: “How long does the ringing last now compared with a year ago?” — Write it down. Trend is the measurement that matters.

Here's what's underneath all of it. It Does Not Come Back. There is no recovery arc here and that is the whole point of the page. Hair cells do not regenerate, tinnitus that becomes permanent is permanent, and the endpoint for a significant number of people in this industry is a career ended by an injury that was completely preventable with a piece of equipment costing less than a single flight case. It is also the injury least likely to be taken seriously in the moment, because it does not hurt and there is nothing to see. Get a baseline audiogram now, get moulded plugs, use them at load-in and in the pit as well as on stage, and treat the trend as data rather than as ageing.

Here's the play. Get custom moulded musicians' plugs with interchangeable filters, and a baseline audiogram you repeat annually. Wear protection at load-in, soundcheck and in the crowd, not just on stage. Push for in-ears in your rider. Give your ears two quiet days after a run.

Here are the words. “In-ears are on my rider and they're not a nice-to-have — I'm protecting the thing I work with. — I've got moulded plugs in, I can hear you fine, they're flat.”

Remember this. Your ears are the instrument and they do not heal. The plugs cost less than one flight case.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Contract manipulation, revenue skimming, reputation threats.*

### 12. “THE BAND MEMBER WHO TAKES”
*Internal Financial Exploitation / Band Dynamics — clinically, financial exploitation / internal power abuse.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The financial and power dynamics within a band or creative partnership are one of the most commonly neglected protection areas for musicians. Exploitation by a bandmate or creative partner is just as real as exploitation by a promoter — and harder to name because the relationship is closer.

Here's the scene. You write most of the songs. You book most of the shows. You handle most of the promotion. The split has always been equal. You never thought to question it — you're a band, you're family. Then a manager offers you a solo project and you realize that without you the band doesn't function, and with the band you're sharing everything you generate equally with people who generate a fraction of what you do.

Here's what to watch for.

- Contributions to the band's output and income are significantly unequal but compensation isn't
- One member controls band finances, social media, or industry relationships
- Leaving the band feels like losing ownership of work you created
- Creative credit doesn't reflect actual creative contribution

Here's what's actually happening. Band agreements are one of the most neglected legal documents in independent music. Who owns the name? Who owns the masters? What's the songwriting split? What happens when someone leaves? Who controls social media? Without answers in writing, whoever controls the logistics controls the band — and the person who does the most work often has the least formal protection. This isn't just about money. It's about who owns your creative legacy.

Run the check. The O check: “Do I know what I actually own in this creative partnership — in writing?”

Here's the dark reality. Band Agreement Basics. A band agreement should address: (1) who owns the band name and what happens to it if someone leaves. (2) Songwriting and publishing splits — and whether they're per-song or blanket. (3) Who owns the master recordings. (4) Revenue split for touring vs recording vs publishing vs merch. (5) Decision-making process — majority vote, unanimous, or designated authority. (6) Exit terms — what a departing member takes with them. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts has band agreement templates. A music attorney review costs a fraction of what disputes cost later.

Here's your move. Get your band agreement in writing before you have a dispute about what you each own.

Here's exactly what to say. “I think we should get our band agreement written down so we're all protected — I can get a template and we can go through it together.”

And here's the truth. The band feels like family until there's money involved. The paperwork is how you protect the family.

### 13. “YOUR STANDARD RIDER”
*What to Include, How to Use It — clinically, professional sovereignty / contract protection.*

Here's who this is. Your rider is not just technical requirements. It is the document that defines your professional terms, protects your rights, and sets the expectations for every engagement before you arrive. Most musicians don't use it fully. This page changes that.

Picture this. You've been showing up to gigs and figuring things out on arrival — the recording situation, the merch arrangement, the accommodation, the payment timing. Each thing individually seems manageable to negotiate in person. Collectively, negotiating all of these while you're also trying to prepare for a performance means you're always behind and always slightly at a disadvantage. Your rider is supposed to solve this before you arrive.

Here are the signs.

- Technical requirements: sound system specs, monitoring, load-in time, soundcheck time
- Financial terms: fee, payment method and timing, deposit requirements and deadline
- Recording and broadcast: explicit statement that Artist retains all recording rights
- Accommodation and hospitality: what's provided, what's not, quality standards

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A comprehensive rider serves two functions: it communicates your professional requirements AND it creates a paper trail of what was agreed. When things don't match the rider at the venue, you have documentation of what was promised. When recording happens without your agreement, you have documentation of what your rights are. The rider is not a diva document — it's a professional standard that protects both parties.

Here's your check. The S check: “Does my rider include recording rights language, payment terms, and all my technical...

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What A Complete Rider Covers. Technical rider: stage plot, input list, monitoring requirements, backline requirements, load-in/soundcheck/show times. Business rider: fee and payment terms, deposit amount and deadline, cancellation policy, accommodation and transportation specs, press photo and bio requirements. Rights rider: recording rights clause (you retain all), broadcast rights, how your name can be used in promotion. Hospitality rider: green room requirements, food and beverage, guest list. Template riders are available from AFM (afm.org) and from music attorney blogs. Customize and use yours consistently.

Here's what you do. Send your rider with every booking confirmation. Update it annually. Use it as your baseline.

Say this, out loud. “I'll send my rider along with the signed contract — let me know if anything needs discussion before the show.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The rider is not arrogance. It is professionalism. It protects you and it protects the promoter.

### 14. “THE VAN GOT DONE”
*Gear Theft On The Road / The Night The Tour Ends — clinically, equipment theft / operational continuity failure.*

Let's talk about who does this. Organized and specific. People who know what a band's van looks like, which hotels touring acts use, and that a vehicle packed after a show and parked overnight contains everything the act owns.

Here's how it plays out. You loaded out at two, drove forty minutes, parked outside the travel lodge like always, and in the morning the side door is open and the van is empty. Instruments, laptops with the stems, merch, the lot. You have a show tomorrow, the insurance certificate is in a folder somewhere, and nobody wrote down a single serial number.

Watch for these.

- A packed vehicle left overnight in an unsecured car park
- Band or crew branding on the van, which advertises the contents
- No serial numbers, no photographs, no itemized inventory
- Insurance that does not cover unattended vehicles overnight — most do not
- Backups of session files kept only on the stolen laptops
- Predictable routine: same venues, same hotels, same parking

Here's what's really going on. Van theft is a specialist activity aimed at touring musicians precisely because the value density is extraordinary and the routine is predictable. The part most bands get wrong is the insurance: a great many policies exclude theft from an unattended vehicle overnight, which means the cover people believe they have does not apply to the single most likely way they will lose everything. The second thing is documentation — without serials and photographs a claim is slow and a police recovery is effectively impossible even when the gear surfaces.

Ask yourself this. The D check: “Could I list every serial number and produce a photograph of each item today?” — If not, you do not have a claim, you have a list of regrets.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Read The Overnight Exclusion Before The Tour. Do this one thing this week: find the clause about unattended vehicles. Bands discover it at the worst possible moment — after the theft, mid-tour, with dates they cannot play and a claim that will be declined. Alongside it, keep the session files somewhere that is not in the van: a cloud backup of stems and masters costs almost nothing and is the only part of the loss that is genuinely irreplaceable, because instruments can be re-bought and a year of unreleased recordings cannot. Everything else on this page is inconvenience; that part is the career.

Here's the play. Photograph and serial-number everything into a shared document before you leave. Unload the critical items into the accommodation every night — tedious, and it is the whole prevention. Use an unbranded van, vary parking, fit a secondary lock and a tracker. Check the overnight exclusion and back up your files off the vehicle.

Here are the words. “Small gear and laptops come in with us every night, no exceptions — it's twenty minutes and it's the difference between a bad night and no tour. — Can you send me the policy wording for unattended vehicles?”

Remember this. Twenty minutes of carrying flight cases into a hotel is the cheapest insurance on the road, and the only one with no exclusions.

### 15. “BUYING AND SELLING SAFELY ON THE CIRCUIT”
*Legal Risk, Undercovers, and Festival Transactions — clinically, legal risk awareness / harm reduction.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Musicians and touring artists face specific legal risks at festivals around buying, selling, and the substance culture of the touring life. This page is practical harm reduction for people who live on the circuit.

Here's the scene. After the show, in the green room, someone offers you something. You're still running on the set, the green room feels safe and private, and the person seems completely trustworthy — they've been on tour with you for weeks. The substances have been tested this run, you know what you're getting. Two hours later you're in a situation that's more complicated than the green room felt.

Here's what to watch for.

- Green room transactions feel safer because they're "backstage" — they're not legally safer
- Someone you trust on tour can still put you in a position of legal exposure
- Substances offered in the context of a close working relationship still carry risk
- The touring lifestyle normalizes substance use in ways that can gradually erode risk-assessment

Here's what's actually happening. Artists on tour are subject to the same drug laws as everyone else regardless of backstage location. Green room status does not create legal protection. The touring lifestyle creates normalization of substance use that can gradually erode your assessment of what's risky. Know what you're taking (test it), Know your legal exposure, and be aware that the people around you on tour have their own legal exposure to consider — which may or may not align with your interests.

Run the check. The U check: “Do I understand who is actually in this space and what the legal implications of this...

Here's the dark reality. If You're Detained At A Festival Or Venue. Know this before you need it: (1) You have the right to remain silent — use it. "I'd like to speak with a lawyer." (2) Your manager or label does not automatically have the right to make legal decisions for you — that's your call. (3) Your tour rider and performance contract are your documents — you have the right to a copy of any contract you've signed. ### 16. “THE STAGE IS NOT A CONSENT BYPASS”
*Fame, Access, and Your Responsibility With Both — clinically, power differential awareness / consent education.*

Here's who this is. This page is for every musician and touring artist regardless of level. The stage gives you power — social, sexual, financial, reputational. Power requires responsibility. This is the one nobody puts in the welcome packet.

Picture this. You're coming off a strong set and you're still buzzing. Someone backstage is clearly interested — has been sending signals all night. They're consenting adults, everything feels mutual. Here's what's also true: you just performed to thousands of people, you have a team and access they don't have, you are in your element and they are in yours — and that power differential is real whether you acknowledge it or not.

Here are the signs.

- The post-show high and stage power can distort your read of consent signals
- Someone in your green room may feel unable to say no to you — that's real whether you intend it or not
- Your access to substances and role as host creates a power dynamic you're responsible for
- Fame makes people want to be chosen by you — that want is not the same as uninhibited consent

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The music industry has a long and documented history of artists using stage power and access to override or bypass consent. This is not ancient history — it is ongoing. You don't have to be a predator to contribute to this dynamic. The post-show adrenaline state impairs your judgment. The power you hold in that space is real even when you're not wielding it deliberately. The ask is simple: slow down, check in with words, receive the answer, and respect it completely.

Here's your check. The N check: “'No' from someone in my space is a full sentence. It requires nothing further from me.”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. If You've Crossed A Line. If you've done something — in an altered state, in the post-show window, under pressure, or deliberately — that you know wasn't okay: the accountable path is not silence. Reaching out to acknowledge what happened, through a trusted intermediary if direct contact might cause more harm, is the beginning of actual accountability. RAINN has resources for people who have caused harm (rainn.org). The music community is small. The reckoning always comes. Being ahead of it with honesty is better than being caught by it.

Here's what you do. Ask with words. Receive the answer. Respect it completely. This is the whole job.

Say this, out loud. “"I'm feeling this — is this okay with you?" Then honor whatever comes next. Fully.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The artists who build lasting careers are the ones their community can actually trust. Be that artist.

### 17. “WHAT GOOD LEADERSHIP SOUNDS LIKE”
*Using Your Platform for Actual Safety — clinically, platform responsibility / bystander activation.*

Let's talk about who does this. If you have a stage, you have a microphone. If you have a microphone, you have an opportunity to make the space safer for everyone in it. This page is about using that opportunity.

Here's how it plays out. You're two songs into your set and you can feel the energy of the crowd — fully in it, totally open. You have their complete attention. A lot can happen in that crowd tonight. Some of it will be beautiful. Some of it won't be. You have an actual ability to influence which way it goes. The question is whether you choose to use it.

Watch for these.

- Stating consent expectations from the stage normalizes them in the crowd
- Pointing to Harm Reduction resources from the stage reduces the stigma of using them
- Calling out behavior you see from the stage carries enormous weight
- Creating moments of grounding and community check-in is part of what great performers do

Here's what's really going on. Artists with stage platforms have a specific ability to shape the culture of a crowd that most people in that crowd don't have. Stating consent expectations, pointing to safety resources, calling out behavior you see — these are not political statements. They are basic community care that is within your power to provide. The artists who do this are often the ones whose shows feel different — where people feel safer to be fully present.

Ask yourself this. The N check: “What does my platform allow me to contribute to the safety of this community right now?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. From Stage — What To Actually Say. Simple, real, effective stage interventions: "Before we go any further — look around you. Here's the play. Use your platform. Even one sentence from the stage can change someone's night.

### 18. “THE MENTAL HEALTH REALITY OF TOURING”
*What Nobody Talks About and What Actually Helps — clinically, occupational mental health / burnout prevention.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Touring is one of the most mentally demanding sustained experiences a person can undertake. The highs are extraordinary. The lows are real and they're often invisible — because the expectation is that you're living the dream and therefore shouldn't be struggling.

Here's the scene. You're three weeks into a six-week run. The shows have been incredible. The audiences have been extraordinary. You're also sleeping in different places every two days, eating inconsistently, performing at high emotional output nightly, and haven't had a genuine conversation with someone who isn't on tour with you in three weeks. You feel both full and completely empty. You don't know how to explain that to anyone.

Here's what to watch for.

- Simultaneous emotional fullness from performance and depletion from everything else
- No stable environment, schedule, or routine for weeks or months at a time
- High public performance combined with very little genuine private connection
- The expectation that "living the dream" means you shouldn't have struggles

Here's what's actually happening. Touring mental health is a serious and underaddressed issue in the music industry. The combination of sleep disruption, dietary inconsistency, emotional performance demands, social isolation, and substance exposure creates conditions that are genuinely hard on mental health — regardless of how much you love the music. Acknowledging this is not weakness. It is professional self-care. The musicians who sustain long careers are the ones who take their mental health as seriously as their technical craft.

Run the check. The N check: “What does my actual wellbeing need right now — not what does the tour schedule need...

Here's the dark reality. Mental Health Resources For Musicians. MusiCares (grammy.com/musicares): free mental health referrals and financial assistance for music professionals. Sweet Relief Musicians Fund (sweetrelief.org): financial assistance for touring musicians in crisis. Musicians Foundation (musiciansfoundation.org): emergency assistance grants. Backline (backline.care): free mental health and wellness resources specifically for music industry professionals. 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, 24/7. The music industry is increasingly building mental health infrastructure — use it. Reaching out is not a career-ending move. Burning out silently is.

Here's your move. Treat your mental health with the same professionalism you bring to your craft.

Here's exactly what to say. “[To your most trusted person]: "I'm struggling on this run and I need to say that out loud to someone who isn't on tour with me."”

And here's the truth. You can love the music AND acknowledge that the life is hard. These are not opposites.
