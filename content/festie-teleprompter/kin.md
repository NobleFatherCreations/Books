This is K.I.N.: Family Camping & Parents Edition. You brought your kids to the best place you know. This guide is the boring infrastructure that keeps it that way.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **K** — Know Where They Are, By Name. Not “with the camp”. A named adult who has agreed, out loud, that they have your child right now and until when.
- **I** — Identify Them If They Are Lost. Wristband with your number, a photo taken each morning in that day's clothes, and a meeting point they can say back to you.
- **N** — No Adult Needs Private Time With Your Kid. Not to show them something, not for a lesson, not to be kind. The rule is simple because simple rules survive a friendly, trusted, well-liked exception.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*The helpful adult, gifts and special attention, the camp everyone trusts.*

### 1. “HE'S SO GOOD WITH KIDS”
*The Beloved Camp Uncle / Access Through Trust — clinically, child grooming / institutional trust exploitation.*

Here's who this is. Genuinely great with children, which is the difficult part of this page. Everybody's favourite. Has been coming for years, runs the games, remembers the kids' names between festivals, and is the first person anyone would defend.

Picture this. Your daughter adores him. He does the face paint and the treasure hunt and he is the reason the family field is fun. He has started giving her small presents — a bracelet, a patch for her jacket — and asking for her specifically, and suggesting he take her to the workshop tent because you look tired, and the sentence forming in your head is that you would be insulting a good man to say no.

Here are the signs.

- Seeks time alone with a specific child, always with a good reason
- Gives gifts or attention to one child rather than to all of them
- Establishes secrets — small, harmless ones, framed as a shared joke
- Prefers the company of children to that of adults at an adult event
- Pushes past a soft no with warmth rather than pressure
- Universally liked, which means raising anything has a social cost

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Child sexual abuse is overwhelmingly committed by somebody the child and the family already know and trust, and the grooming literature describes a consistent sequence: access, then a special relationship with one child, then small secrets, then isolation. Every step looks like kindness and most of them, performed by most people, are kindness. That is exactly why safeguarding policy in every organization that works with children is built on rules about situations rather than judgments about people — because judging individuals is precisely what the pattern defeats, and because the people who are wrongly suspected and the people who are correctly suspected look identical from outside.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The N check: “Is an adult asking for time alone with my child?” — The answer is no, every time, regardless of who is asking. The rule is the protection. Assessing the person is not.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Rule Exists So You Never Have To Judge. This is the hardest page in this book to write and to read, and the reason is that the overwhelming majority of beloved camp uncles are exactly what they appear to be — generous adults who like kids and make festivals magical. A blanket rule is not an accusation against any of them. It is a rule precisely because no parent can reliably tell, because the ones who cannot be told apart are the whole problem, and because a good man will not mind. That last part is the practical test: a genuinely safe adult hears “we don't do one-on-one” and says fine, of course, come along too. Only one kind of person argues with a safeguarding rule, and the argument is the finding.

Here's what you do. Make it a policy rather than a judgment, and say it out loud to the whole camp at the start so it is never about one person: no one-on-one, activities happen with two adults or in view. Then say yes to everything that meets the rule — the face paint, the treasure hunt, all of it.

Say this, out loud. “We do a two-adult rule for the kids, nothing personal to anyone — so yes to the workshop, and I'll walk over with you both.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Good adults are not offended by safeguarding rules. The rule protects them too, and the person who argues has told you something.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Diffused supervision, your own capacity, the festival's actual hazards.*

### 2. “I THOUGHT HE WAS WITH YOU”
*Diffused Supervision / The Camp Everybody Assumed — clinically, responsibility diffusion / supervision gap.*

Let's talk about who does this. Not a person — a structure. Eleven adults, four children, a shared camp, an unspoken sense that somebody is always around, and no moment at which anybody specifically agreed to be watching.

Here's how it plays out. It is nine in the evening and somebody asks where Theo is and there is a pause, and in that pause four adults each realize they had assumed one of the others had him. He is almost certainly thirty metres away at the other fire. He almost certainly is. The forty seconds before you find him are the longest forty seconds of the year and nothing in the camp's arrangements was designed to prevent them.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Supervision described as “the camp” rather than a named adult
- No agreed handover — children move between adults without a word said
- Nobody knows how many adults are currently drinking or dosed
- No headcount, and no agreed time for one
- Older children tasked with younger ones without that being decided
- “Someone's always around” said as though it were a system

Here's what's really going on. Diffusion of responsibility is one of the most robust findings in social psychology: the more people who could act, the less likely any given person is to act, and the more each assumes somebody else has. Applied to child supervision in a camp it produces a specific failure — not neglect, but a group of attentive adults each accurately believing the child is covered. Festivals add the aggravating factors: no fences, thousands of strangers, darkness, noise that swallows a shout, and a proportion of the adults present with reduced capacity. The fix is not more vigilance. It is a named person and a spoken handover, which converts an assumption into an agreement.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The K check: “Can I name the adult who has my child right now, and did they say yes out loud?” — If the answer is a camp rather than a name, nobody has them.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Other Half Is Your Own Capacity. The part that is harder to say: the handover rule has to include you. A festival is one of the few places where a parent may be genuinely impaired — drinking, dosed, or simply forty hours short of sleep — and the moment where you most need to hand over is the moment you are least able to assess that you should. So the decision gets made in advance, sober, like a designated driver: who is on duty tonight, who is not drinking, and what the signal is for I am done, take over. Agreeing that on Thursday costs a five-minute conversation. Not agreeing it is how a child ends up nominally supervised by four adults, none of whom is currently able to drive.

Here's the play. Run a nightly rota decided in advance: a named on-duty adult each evening who stays under, and a spoken handover every time a child changes hands — “you have Theo until eight”, answered out loud. Headcount at every set change.

Here are the words. “You've got Theo till eight — say it back to me. — I'm on duty tonight, so I'm not drinking; Mo's on tomorrow.”

Remember this. “I thought he was with you” is what four attentive adults say. A name and a time out loud is the only thing that prevents it.

### 3. “THE TRAY ON THE TABLE”
*Substances Within Reach Of A Child — clinically, pediatric ingestion risk / environmental safeguarding.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Your camp, and the eleven adults in it who are all lovely and none of whom is thinking about a four-year-old's sight lines.

Here's the scene. There is a tin on the low table with something in it, and a half-drunk cider at child height, and a bag on a chair. Your daughter is four and everything at that height is, to her, food. She has been putting things in her mouth for four years and nobody in this camp has had to think about that for a decade.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Edibles, powders or pills at or below adult-knee height
- Unlabelled food on a shared table in a camp with kids in it
- Half-finished drinks left within reach
- Bags left open on chairs and in tent doorways
- The camp has never explicitly discussed having children present
- “She won't touch it” offered as the safety measure

Here's what's actually happening. Pediatric cannabis ingestion has risen sharply everywhere edibles have become common, and the reason is entirely mechanical: edibles look like sweets, a child's dose is a fraction of an adult's, and onset is delayed so the first sign is a small child who is unexpectedly unwell an hour later. At a festival the risk multiplies — more substances, more adults, more surfaces, and a medical tent rather than an emergency department. This is not a page about whether adults in your camp should use anything. It is a page about height.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The K check: “If I crouch to my child's eye level right now, what can I reach?” — Do it literally. It is a different camp from down there.

Here's the dark reality. Tell The Medics What They Might Have Taken. The failure that turns this serious is not the ingestion, it is the hour afterwards. A small child who has eaten an edible presents as unusually drowsy and unsteady, and a parent who does not know what happened reports a child who seems sleepy — which is a completely different clinical picture from a possible ingestion, and gets a completely different response. So the instruction is blunt: if there is any possibility, say so immediately and say exactly what it might have been. Nobody at that medical tent is interested in getting anybody in trouble and they will tell you so, and the treatment depends entirely on knowing. Guessing wrong costs hours, and hours matter at that body weight.

Here's your move. Run a waist-height rule as camp policy on day one — nothing above the ground and below the waist, everything in a closed bag in a tent. Label communal food. Do the crouch test yourself each morning, because camps drift by Saturday.

Here's exactly what to say. “Kids in camp this year, so can we keep everything above waist height and label the food? Not a judgement thing, just height. — [at medical] She may have eaten an edible, about an hour ago, I don't know how much.”

And here's the truth. Your camp is safe at adult eye level. Crouch down to three feet and look again.

### 4. “THEY'RE FIFTEEN, NOT FIVE”
*The Teenager At A Festival / A Completely Different Job — clinically, adolescent supervision / autonomy and risk calibration.*

Here's who this is. Your fourteen-to-seventeen-year-old, who is too old for the kids' field, too young for everything else, and now navigating a site full of adults, substances and sexual attention with a child's experience and an adult's height.

Picture this. They want to go and watch a band with the two kids from the next camp and be back later, and that is a completely reasonable thing for a fifteen-year-old to want. It is also a fifteen-year-old, at night, in a crowd of adults, where nobody checks anybody's age for anything, and where a nineteen-year-old and a twenty-six-year-old will both read them as older than they are.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Adults who take an unusual interest in your teenager specifically
- An older new friend who appears and is vague about their age
- Substances offered to them because they read as older
- They have no meeting point, no charged phone and no agreed check-in
- They will not tell you if something goes wrong, because of what they were doing
- You have not said the one sentence that makes telling you possible

Here's the mechanism underneath it. A teenager needs a different regime from both a child and an adult — real independence, because refusing it produces a teenager who lies, and real structure, because the environment contains things they have no experience of. The single highest-value item is not a rule, it is a disclosure guarantee. Adolescents decide whether to tell a parent about a bad situation almost entirely on their prediction of the reaction, so a teenager who believes they will be punished for being where they were will handle it alone, at night, at a festival. That is the outcome to prevent.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The K check: “Do they have a check-in time, a meeting point, and a promise from me that ringing costs them nothing?” — Three things, agreed before they go anywhere.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Sentence That Actually Protects Them. Say this to them plainly, once, before the weekend: if anything goes wrong, whatever you were doing, ring me and I will not be angry and we will sort it out afterwards. Then keep it the first time it is used, over something small, because that is the test and they will run it. The alternative is a fifteen-year-old who has taken something, or is with somebody who will not let them leave, running the calculation about how much trouble they are in, and deciding to manage it themselves. Every safeguarding professional will tell you the same thing: the child who tells you is the one who thinks telling you is survivable.

Here's what you do. Give real autonomy with real structure — agreed zones, an hourly-ish check-in, a charged phone and a power bank, a meeting point, and the names of the friends they are with. Say the unconditional sentence and mean it. Tell them the two adult-world things nobody else will: watch your own drink, and an adult being very interested in you is a reason to leave.

Say this, out loud. “Here's the deal: go, have a great time, text me at ten and midnight. And whatever happens, whatever you were doing, ring me — I won't be angry, I'll just come. That's a promise, not a trap.”

Here's the truth underneath it. You cannot supervise a fifteen-year-old at a festival. You can be the person they are not afraid to ring, and that is worth more.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The lost-child kit, the handover rule, the family-field brief.*

### 5. “THE LOST-KID KIT”
*Ten Minutes On Thursday That You Will Never Regret — clinically, child safety preparation / lost child protocol.*

Let's talk about who does this. For everyone in the family field, every year, including the veterans who have done twelve of these and especially the ones who think their kid is too sensible to get lost.

Here's how it plays out. The first ninety seconds of a missing child are the ones that matter, and they are ninety seconds in which you will be useless — heart rate through the roof, unable to remember what they were wearing, phone at four percent. Everything on this page exists so that a calm Thursday version of you has already done the thinking.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Wristband with your mobile number, written fresh each morning
- A photo of each child taken every morning, in that day's actual clothes
- A meeting point they can say back to you, chosen and physically visited
- The phrase: find a person with a radio, or a stall, or a mum with kids
- Ear defenders, sun hat, water — the boring three that prevent most of it
- Your phone charged, and a second adult's number on the band too

Here's what's really going on. Lost children at festivals are common and almost always resolve in minutes, and the reason they resolve is infrastructure: wristbands with numbers, a welfare tent that does this constantly, and staff who are trained for it. The daily photo is the single highest-value item and the one almost nobody does — a described child is hard to search for and a photographed child in today's clothes is easy. Teaching who to approach matters as much as teaching not to wander: children told only about stranger danger will avoid the very adults most likely to help them, so name the categories instead — radio, stall, parent with kids.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The I check: “Wristband on, photo taken today, meeting point they can repeat?” — Three things, every morning, before anything else.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Ninety Seconds, And What To Do In Them. Have the protocol memorized because you will not be able to reason it out. One adult stays exactly where the child was last seen and does not move — children double back, and a moving parent and a moving child can search past each other for an hour. A second adult goes straight to welfare or the nearest radio and reports it immediately; there is no waiting period and nobody will think you are overreacting, because they do this several times a day. Show the photo. Give the wristband number so it can be called. Everyone else checks the last three places the child liked, not the whole site. Ninety seconds of the right actions beats twenty minutes of frantic ones.

Here's the play. Do the three every morning — band, photo, meeting point — and walk the child to the welfare tent on Thursday so they have physically seen where it is. Agree the stay-put rule with the other adults now, before it is needed.

Here are the words. “If you can't find me: stay where you are and find someone with a radio, or a stall, or a mum with kids. Show them your wristband. Tell me the plan back?”

Remember this. Ten minutes on Thursday buys you the only ninety seconds that will ever matter. Do the photo — it is the one everybody skips.

### 6. “EARS, SUN AND THE LONG DAY”
*The Three Physical Things That Actually Hurt Children Here — clinically, pediatric environmental protection / noise-induced hearing loss.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For every adult with a child at a festival. Nothing on this page involves another person. All of it is the environment, and all of it is preventable with items that cost less than a round of drinks.

Here's the scene. The three things that send children to a festival medical tent are almost never dramatic: overheating, exhaustion, and the slow damage of a hundred decibels going into ears that are much more vulnerable than yours. None of it looks like an emergency while it is happening, which is precisely the problem.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Ear defenders: not optional, and not just at the main stage
- Shade, hat, high-factor sun cream reapplied — they will not ask
- Water pushed on a schedule, because children under-report thirst
- A real nap or quiet hour every day, in shade, with defenders off
- Their own bag: snack, water, defenders, hat, a photo of you
- Watch for the quiet child — that is the sign, not the crying one

Here's what's actually happening. Children's hearing is more vulnerable than adults' and noise damage is cumulative, permanent, and completely painless at the time, which is why nobody acts on it. Festival sound levels routinely sit where hearing protection is required in an occupational setting, and a child does not have to be at the rail to be exposed — a campsite within earshot of a stage is enough over three days. Heat and exhaustion are the other two, and both present quietly: a child who is overheating or depleted goes still and compliant rather than loud, which reads to a busy adult as a child who is finally settling down.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The I check: “Defenders on, hat on, water in the last hour, and when was the last quiet hour?” — Four things, on a loop, all weekend.

Here's the dark reality. The Quiet Child Is The Warning. Know the direction this goes, because it is the opposite of what instinct says. A distressed child makes noise. A child who is overheating, dehydrated or severely depleted becomes quiet, floppy and easy — and in a loud, exhausting, overstimulating environment that reads as a child who has finally calmed down, so the adults relax at exactly the wrong moment. If a child who has been lively all day goes unusually still, stops complaining, or is hard to rouse, that is a medical tent visit now rather than a nap. This is also why the quiet hour is scheduled rather than offered: children will not opt out of a festival on their own, and they do not know when they have had enough.

Here's your move. Defenders from the moment you are on site, not just for the headliner. Shade and water on a timer rather than on request. A scheduled quiet hour every afternoon in the tent with the defenders off. Their own small bag with your number and a photo of you in it.

Here's exactly what to say. “Defenders on before we go anywhere near a stage — every time, that's the rule. — Quiet hour, back to the tent, we'll come out again at four.”

And here's the truth. Hearing damage does not hurt while it happens and it does not come back. Defenders cost fifteen pounds and last their whole life.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*Real village versus nobody actually watching.*

### 7. “IT TAKES A VILLAGE, NAMED”
*A Real Village Versus An Assumed One — clinically, authentic collective care vs diffused assumption.*

Here's who this is. The family field at its best, which is genuinely one of the great things this culture has built — kids running semi-feral in the safest possible way, raised for a weekend by twenty adults who all know them.

Picture this. The real version is unmistakable once you have seen it. Adults who know which kid belongs to whom. Somebody bringing your child back with a comment about the face paint. A camp where the rota is on the whiteboard and nobody thinks that is uptight. Kids who can name four adults they are allowed to go to, because somebody sat down and taught them the four.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Real: adults are named, rotas exist, handovers are spoken
- Real: children can name the adults they may go to
- Real: someone is always sober and everyone knows who
- Real: the two-adult rule is stated openly and nobody is offended
- Assumed: “someone's always around”, and no one is specifically
- Assumed: raising a safeguarding point would make you the awkward one

Here's the mechanism underneath it. The village is real and it works, and it works because of structure rather than in spite of it. The camps where children are genuinely safest are the ones that look, from outside, slightly over-organized: whiteboard, rota, named on-duty adult, an out-loud rule about one-on-one. That structure is what lets the freedom be real — kids roaming the family field all weekend, which is the thing you came for. The unstructured version produces the opposite: adults who feel responsible in general and therefore nobody responsible in particular, and a culture where the person who suggests a rota is the problem.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/kin/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The K check: “Can every adult here name tonight's on-duty person, and can every kid name four adults they can go to?” — Two questions, asked on Thursday.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Be The One Who Says It First. Somebody has to say the rules out loud, and in a warm camp of old friends that feels enormously awkward — like importing suspicion into a place built on trust. Say them anyway, and say them on day one, to everyone, before anything has happened, because a rule introduced as general policy costs nothing and a rule introduced after a specific worry is an accusation. You will find that most of the camp is relieved. Parents are usually all quietly thinking it and each assuming they would be the uptight one, which is the same diffusion problem as the supervision gap, applied to speaking up.

Here's what you do. Call the five-minute family brief on Thursday: rota, on-duty adult, two-adult rule, meeting point, and the four adults each child may go to. Put it on a whiteboard. Then let them run.

Say this, out loud. “Quick family-camp thing before we all settle in — rota's on the board, on-duty's not drinking, no one-on-one with the kids, and let's make sure every kid can name four grown-ups they can go to.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The village is real, and it is made of names. A village where nobody is named is just a field with your child in it.
