# H.O.M.E. — THE PEOPLE AT HOME
### For The People At Home Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 263 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is H.O.M.E.: For The People At Home Edition. Somebody you love is going, and you are not. This one is for you — short, honest, and not what you are expecting.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **H** — How You React Is The Whole Thing. Everything else on this page is downstream of one question: if it went wrong at 4am, would you be the call they make or the one they avoid?
- **O** — One Useful Ask, Not Twenty Worried Ones. Ask who they are going with and where they are staying. That is it. Twenty questions gets you a closed door and no information at all.
- **M** — Myths Cost You Credibility. If what you say does not match what they see, they stop believing the parts that are true. Accuracy is not permissiveness, it is how you stay worth listening to.
- **E** — Emergency Means You Act, Not Panic. If a real call comes, there are three things to do and none of them is a lecture. Know them before the phone rings.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*What the internet told you, and why most of it is wrong.*

### 1. “WHAT YOU READ ABOUT THESE PLACES”
*The Fear You Were Sold / Why Accuracy Is On Your Side — clinically, risk miscalibration / credibility cost of inaccuracy.*

Here's who this is. You, at eleven at night, four articles deep, having typed a question into a search bar that only ever returns the worst weekend anybody ever had anywhere.

Picture this. You have read about the deaths. You have read the thread. You have a picture in your head assembled entirely from the events that were unusual enough to be written about, and you are about to have a conversation with somebody you love, using that picture as your evidence, and you cannot understand why they are getting defensive.

Here are the signs.

- Everything you know came from coverage of things that went wrong
- You have not asked them a single question you did not already have an answer to
- Your sentences start with statistics you cannot source
- You are describing their friends, whom you have not met, as a risk
- The conversation always ends the same way, and it ends early
- You know less about this trip than you did about the last one

Here's the mechanism underneath it. News coverage selects for the rare and the terrible — that is what makes something news — so an hour of searching builds a picture in which the exception is the rule. The person you are talking to has been to four of these and had four ordinary, joyful weekends, so when you describe a place they do not recognize, the thing they learn is not that festivals are dangerous. It is that you do not know what you are talking about, and that everything else you say on the subject can be discounted. That is the actual cost, and it is not abstract: it is why they will not ring you.

Here's your check. The M check: “Does what I am about to say match what they will actually see this weekend?” — If not, you are spending credibility you will need later.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Silence You Are Building. Here is the thing nobody tells parents and partners. The research on this is consistent and it is the opposite of intuitive: young people decide who to tell about a bad situation almost entirely on their prediction of the reaction, not on the severity of what happened. Every exaggeration, every I-told-you-so, every conversation that ended in a lecture is data they are using to make that prediction. So an approach built on warning them can produce exactly the outcome it was designed to prevent — a person in real trouble at four in the morning, running down the list of people who will not make it worse, and not finding you on it. That is what is actually at stake in how you handle the week before.

Here's what you do. Stop researching and start asking. Say plainly that you do not know much about this and would like to, and then let them tell you — people explain their world at length to anyone who is genuinely curious rather than building a case.

Say this, out loud. “I've been reading stuff that's probably rubbish and I'd rather hear it from you. What's it actually like? What's the bit you look forward to?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Accuracy is not approval. It is the only way to stay somebody whose opinion still counts for anything.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The conversation before they go, and the one ask that works.*

### 2. “THE CONVERSATION BEFORE THEY GO”
*Two Questions, One Sentence, Four Minutes — clinically, protective communication / disclosure enablement.*

Let's talk about who does this. For the parent, partner, sibling or friend who has one conversation available and wants to spend it well rather than spend it all.

Here's how it plays out. You have maybe four minutes of genuine attention before this becomes a thing you do every year that they endure. Four minutes is plenty. What follows is more protective than any amount of worrying, and it is almost entirely about what you do not say.

Watch for these.

- Ask who they are going with — names, not “mates”
- Ask where they are staying and how they are getting home
- Say the one sentence about calling you, and then stop talking
- Do not attach conditions, consequences, or a lecture to that sentence
- Do not ask about substances unless you can hear the answer without reacting
- End it early, warmly, and let them go and have a good time

Here's what's really going on. Two questions get you the information that actually matters — who is around them and how they get out — and both are answerable without anybody having to confess to anything. The one sentence is the load-bearing part, and its power comes entirely from being unconditional: the moment it has a clause attached, it stops being a promise and becomes a negotiation, and negotiations get remembered at four in the morning. Say it once, plainly, and do not garnish it. The restraint is the technique.

Ask yourself this. The O check: “Have I asked two questions, or started listing things?” — Two questions and a sentence. Then stop.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Say It Once, Exactly, With No Clause. The sentence is: if anything goes wrong, at any hour, for any reason, ring me — and I will not be angry and I will not say I told you so, I will just come and get you. That is the whole sentence and every word of it is doing work. At any hour, because they will otherwise wait until morning. For any reason, because they will otherwise decide theirs does not qualify. I will not be angry, because that is the specific prediction they are running. And you have to mean it, because you will be tested on it once, probably over something small, and how you handle that one determines whether the sentence is real for the next ten years.

Here's the play. Ask the two questions. Say the sentence once, plainly, with nothing attached. Then change the subject and let them pack. Four minutes.

Here are the words. “Who are you going with? And where are you staying — how are you getting back? — Right. Last thing: if anything goes wrong, any hour, any reason, ring me. I won't be angry and I won't say I told you so, I'll just come and get you. That's it. Have an amazing time.”

Remember this. The most protective thing available to you is a promise with no conditions on it, said once and kept the first time it is used.

### 3. “I THINK THEY'RE TAKING SOMETHING”
*The Substance Conversation That Does Not Close The Door — clinically, harm reduction communication / disclosure preservation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. You, holding a worry you have not said out loud, about to have the conversation that will determine whether you hear anything true from them for the next ten years.

Here's the scene. You are fairly sure. You have one conversation available before they go and two options for how to use it, and one of them ends with them telling you less forever. Everything in you wants to say do not take anything, and that sentence has a known effect, and the effect is not the one you want.

Here's what to watch for.

- You are planning to lead with a prohibition
- You have facts from headlines rather than from harm reduction sources
- You want a promise, and a promise will be given to end the conversation
- You have not said the unconditional sentence yet
- You do not know what testing kits are, or that they exist
- You would rather not know, and they can tell

Here's what's actually happening. Abstinence-only messaging performs poorly at changing behaviour and performs actively badly at maintaining disclosure — a young person who believes you cannot hear it will simply stop telling you things, and you lose the visibility that was your only real protection. The evidence favours the opposite approach: accurate information, no lecture, and an explicit guarantee that calling you costs them nothing. This is the same conversation as the one earlier in this guide, applied to the specific thing you are most afraid of.

Run the check. The M check: “Am I trying to get a promise, or trying to stay somebody they can tell the truth to?” — You can have one of those.

Here's the dark reality. The Three Facts That Actually Save Lives. If you say nothing else, say these. One: never take something from someone you do not know, and never take something that has not been tested — testing kits are legal and available in most places, and the Bunk Police and DanceSafe exist for exactly this. Two: never mix, especially not with alcohol, and especially not when somebody says it is fine. Three, and this is the one that saves lives: if somebody is in trouble, call for help immediately and tell the medics exactly what was taken, because Good Samaritan protections exist in most places and nobody at a festival medical tent is interested in getting anybody arrested — the deaths happen when people wait because they are frightened of getting in trouble. A young person who knows those three things and knows they can ring you is in a dramatically better position than one who promised you they would not.

Here's your move. Lead with the guarantee, not the prohibition. Give the three facts plainly and without moralizing. Say that you would rather know and be able to help than be protected from the truth. Then stop talking and let them decide what to tell you.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'd rather you didn't, and I'm not going to pretend that's the end of it. So: don't take anything you haven't tested or that came from someone you don't know, never mix it with drink, and if anyone's in trouble get the medics straight away and tell them exactly what was taken — nobody gets in trouble for that, and waiting is what kills people. And whatever happens, ring me. I won't be angry.”

And here's the truth. You can have a promise or you can have the truth for the next ten years. The promise is worth nothing and it costs you the other one.

## Emergency — what to do when it's already happening

*If the call comes: what to do in the first ten minutes.*

### 4. “IF THE CALL COMES”
*The First Ten Minutes — clinically, crisis response / immediate support protocol.*

Here's who this is. You, woken at four in the morning, hearing something in their voice before you have understood a single word.

Picture this. The call you hoped would not come has come. Everything you do in the next ten minutes either makes you someone they can use or someone they regret ringing, and you will be operating on no sleep and pure adrenaline. This is why it is written down.

Here are the signs.

- First sentence: are you safe right now, and where exactly are you
- Second: is anyone with you, and can you get to welfare or medical
- No questions about what they took, what they were wearing, or whose fault it is
- Do not say you told them so, at any point, for any reason, ever
- Write down what they tell you — location, names, time — while they say it
- Stay on the line until they are with a person who can help

Here's the mechanism underneath it. In the first ten minutes there are exactly three useful things: establish safety, establish location, and get them to someone physically present who can help. Everything else — the explanation, the details, the feelings, the question of how this happened — belongs to a later conversation and will be available then. The reason to know this in advance is that your own fear will be enormous and will present itself as an urgent need for information, and answering that need is the single most common way these calls go wrong.

Here's your check. The E check: “Am I getting them help, or getting myself information?” — Both feel identical at four in the morning. Only one of them is useful.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What Not To Say, And The Thing To Know. Never, in that call, ask what they took or what they were wearing or why they went off alone. Not because it is rude — because it tells them this was their fault, and that judgment will shut down the disclosure mid-sentence and quite possibly permanently. Two practical things worth knowing before you ever need them. Every festival has a welfare or medical tent that operates day and night, and it is staffed by people who do this constantly and without judgment; getting your person to that tent is more useful than any advice you can give down a phone. And if it was an assault: in most places a forensic examination can be done without making a police report, the window is measured in days not hours, and the decision about police can be made later. That single fact is one most people do not know, and knowing it removes the choice that stops people getting care.

Here's what you do. Safety, location, get them to a human. Write down what they say as they say it. Then — and only then — ask what they need from you, and do that rather than what you think they need.

Say this, out loud. “Are you safe right now? Where are you exactly? Is anyone with you? — Okay. I want you to walk to the welfare tent and stay on the phone with me while you do it. You've done the right thing ringing me. We'll sort everything else out after.”

Here's the truth underneath it. They rang you. Whatever else happens tonight, that means you got the week before right. Do not spend it in the first ten minutes.

### 5. “I CAN'T REACH THEM”
*Twenty-Six Hours Of Silence / What To Do And When — clinically, contact loss / escalation thresholds for families.*

Let's talk about who does this. You, at home, watching an unanswered message go from normal to not normal, with no idea where the line is.

Here's how it plays out. They have not replied since Friday afternoon. It is now Saturday evening. You know the phone probably died, because everyone's phone dies, and you also cannot stop refreshing, and you have no idea whether calling the festival makes you a reasonable parent or a ridiculous one.

Watch for these.

- Silence longer than they said it would be
- Their last message was odd, short, or unlike them
- You do not know who they are with or where they are camped
- You have been calling repeatedly, which achieves nothing and floods the line
- You are about to post publicly, which you should not do yet
- You do not know the festival has a welfare team that does exactly this

Here's what's really going on. Silence at a festival is overwhelmingly a dead battery and no signal — mobile networks at large events are saturated, charging queues are hours long, and messages sent from a field frequently never arrive. That is the likeliest explanation and it is worth holding onto. It is also true that every large festival has a welfare team whose job includes locating people and passing on messages, they are contactable, and they will not think you are overreacting. Most families do not know that team exists, so they oscillate between doing nothing and calling the police.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “Have I contacted the festival's welfare team?” — That is the step between waiting and panicking, and almost nobody knows it exists.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Ladder, So You Are Not Choosing Between Nothing And Everything. Work it in order rather than jumping. One: message the friends they went with, which is why you asked for names before they left — this resolves it most of the time within an hour. Two: call the festival's information or welfare line, give the name, camp area and a description, and ask them to pass a message; they do this many times a day. Three: if there is a genuine reason for concern — a worrying last message, a known health condition, a specific threat — say so to welfare, because that changes what they do. Four: police, and there is no waiting period for a missing person, that is a myth. What not to do is call them forty times, because it drains the battery you are hoping stays alive, and do not post publicly early, because it is very hard to undo and they are almost certainly asleep in a tent.

Here's the play. Message their friends first. Then ring the event's welfare or information line and ask them to pass a message. Send one text, not thirty, saying you are fine and just want a thumbs-up when they see it. Escalate to police if there is a specific reason, without waiting.

Here are the words. “Hi — my daughter's at your event, camped in the yellow field, I've not heard from her since Friday and her phone's likely dead. Could someone pass her a message to text home? — [text] No panic at all, just send me a thumbs-up when you see this. Have a brilliant time.”

Remember this. It is almost always a dead battery. Ring welfare, send one message, and do not call forty times — you are draining the phone you are waiting on.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*What this place actually is, from someone who knows.*

### 6. “WHAT THIS PLACE ACTUALLY IS”
*The Part The Coverage Never Gets To — clinically, contextual understanding / accurate risk framing.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For you, at the end, from people who have spent their lives in this world and have written an entire book about its dangers — which is exactly why this page is worth trusting.

Here's the scene. Everything else in this book is about what can go wrong, because that is what a safety guide is for. Read as a whole it gives a false impression, and it would be dishonest to hand you a hundred pages of predators and let you think that is the place your person is going to.

Here's what to watch for.

- Strangers walk each other to medical tents at three in the morning, constantly
- People search fields all night for someone they have never met
- There are volunteers whose entire role is sitting with frightened people
- Someone will give your person water, sunscreen, a charger, a lift, for nothing
- The vast majority of people come home tired, sunburnt and happy
- They will talk about it for a year, and you will get bored of hearing about it

Here's what's actually happening. The reason this scene is worth protecting is that it is genuinely unusual: a place where radical acceptance is the default, where people look after strangers as a matter of course, and where somebody can be more themselves for seventy-two hours than they manage anywhere else all year. The patterns in this book exist because predators seek out open-hearted places, and open-heartedness is the thing worth defending rather than the mistake. Your person is not naive for loving it. They are going somewhere that gives them something the default world does not, and the overwhelming likelihood is that they will come home with a sunburn and too many stories.

Run the check. The H check: “Am I treating this as a threat to survive, or as a thing they love?” — They can tell which one you think it is, and it determines everything.

Here's the dark reality. Why You Were Given This. Somebody handed you this guide. That was not an accident and it was not an accusation — it means they want you inside this part of their life rather than outside it, and they were worried enough about your reaction to give you a manual first. That is worth sitting with. The single most protective thing in this entire book is not a tactic or a check or a script; it is a young person who is certain, without having to think about it, that there is somebody at home who will pick up at four in the morning and not make it worse. You can be that by Friday. Nothing else on this page matters as much.

Here's your move. Text them on the Saturday and ask what the best set was. Do not ask if they are okay — ask what was good. Then, when they get back, let them tell you about it at length and be genuinely interested, because that is what keeps the channel open until the year they need it.

Here's exactly what to say. “What's been the best thing so far? — [when they're home] Right, tell me everything. Start with the music.”

And here's the truth. They are going somewhere that makes them more themselves. Be the person they can come home and tell.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*How the situation is shaped once you are in it.*

### 7. “WHEN IT'S YOUR PARTNER, NOT YOUR KID”
*Worry, Jealousy, And The Line Between Them — clinically, relational anxiety / coercive control risk in monitoring.*

Here's who this is. You, at home, while somebody you love is somewhere that is uninhibited, physically affectionate, full of strangers, and entirely outside your view for four days.

Picture this. You said have a great time and you meant it. It is Saturday night and you have checked their location three times, and you have looked at who is posting from that field, and you have drafted a message that is technically about whether they are okay and is actually about something else, and you are aware that you are doing it.

Here are the signs.

- Checking location sharing repeatedly rather than once
- Messages framed as concern that are asking a different question
- Wanting proof of who they are with or what they are doing
- Setting expectations for contact frequency that are about reassurance
- Feeling relief when they answer and worse forty minutes later
- You would not be comfortable if they described your behaviour to a friend

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Distance plus a scene with a reputation plus no information is an excellent generator of anxiety, and anxiety is not the same thing as a problem in the relationship — but the behaviours it produces are the same behaviours that constitute monitoring, and that is worth being honest about while it is still small. Frequent location checking, demands for contact, and requests for proof are on a continuum whose far end is coercive control, and nobody arrives at the far end on purpose. They get there in small, reasonable-feeling steps, each one justified by worry.

Here's your check. The O check: “Am I asking for information, or for reassurance?” — Both are human. Only one of them is fair to send.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Say The Real Thing, Or Say Nothing. The honest version is better than the disguised one in every case. “I'm feeling insecure and I don't need you to fix it, I just wanted to say it” is a sentence that costs you something and does no damage. “Are you okay? Who's there? Why haven't you replied?” is a sentence that looks like care and functions as surveillance, and they will feel the difference even if they cannot name it — and what it produces is a partner who starts managing your anxiety from a field, which is the beginning of them telling you less rather than more. If the checking is compulsive, the work is yours and it is not about this weekend. Deal with it at home, not through their phone.

Here's what you do. Agree the contact plan before they go — one message a day, no expectation of a reply within any window — so neither of you is improvising. Then put the phone down and do something. If you are checking location repeatedly, turn the sharing off for the weekend; it is feeding the thing it is supposed to settle.

Say this, out loud. “One message a day, whenever suits you, and no pressure to reply — I'll assume you're having a brilliant time. — I'm feeling a bit insecure this weekend. That's mine, not yours. Just wanted to say it rather than send you weird texts.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Anxiety and monitoring feel identical from the inside and completely different from the other end. Say the real sentence instead.
