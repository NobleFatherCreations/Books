### 1. “BRO YOU'RE ONE OF US NOW”
*False Brotherhood / Instant Tribe — clinically, forced teaming / manufactured loyalty.*

Here's who this is. The crew that adopts you on Day 1. Full send — sharing food, substances, camp space, inside jokes. It feels like the tribe you've always wanted. By Day 2 there's an unspoken expectation that loyalty runs both ways. By Day 3 that expectation has teeth.

Picture this. You rolled in solo and by Friday night you're deep in a crew that feels like family. They've got a sick camp, good connects, everyone seems solid. Saturday they ask you to hold something for them while they go through a checkpoint — "just for a minute, we're family now." The ask feels small. The crew feels real. Saying no feels like betraying the whole weekend. You take it.

Here are the signs.

- Full adoption happens within hours — you're "family" before you've slept
- The belonging feels disproportionately intense for how little time has passed
- Small asks escalate: hold this, cover this, don't mention this to anyone
- Saying no to any ask feels like it will cost you the whole group

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Forced teaming is the rapid manufacture of "we" — creating shared identity and loyalty before it's been earned. Festival culture is exploited here because genuine fast connection IS a real thing that happens. Predatory crews exploit that norm. Real brothers don't ask you to hold their drugs through security two days after meeting you. Real loyalty doesn't come with immediate debt.

Here's your check. The S check: “When did I last actually talk to my own people — not this crew?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Drug Mule Setup — This Is Real. A specific predatory pattern: a crew befriends solo male attendees over 24-48 hours, builds genuine-feeling rapport, then asks them to transport substances through a checkpoint or off-site. Sometimes they know it's a setup. Sometimes they genuinely think you're family. Either way — you're the one holding it. You're the one who gets caught. Real friends with substances don't need your body to transport them. Ever. "I'm good" is a complete sentence that costs you nothing that was real.

Here's what you do. Enjoy the crew. Don't carry anything for people you've known less than a weekend. Full stop.

Say this, out loud. “I'm not gonna hold anything for anyone — not my thing. Still down to hang though.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Real brothers don't put you at legal risk two days after meeting you. That's not family.

### 3. “TRUST ME, THIS IS LEGIT”
*Financial Scams / The Opportunity That Isn't — clinically, scarcity urgency / credential costuming.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The guy with the deal. The connection. The thing that sounds almost too good but he's vouched for by someone in the crew and the window is closing fast. Festival environments are full of real opportunities AND people who know how to dress a scam in festival clothing.

Here's the scene. "I'm only doing this for three people and I've got two already. You in? It's $400 now and you'll clear $1200 by next weekend. My guy is totally solid." You've known this person for 36 hours. His camp looks legitimate. He name-drops someone you met at the fire. The window is closing. You Venmo him. Monday: number disconnected, Venmo non-refundable.

Here's what to watch for.

- Decision needs to happen RIGHT NOW — closing window, tonight only
- Legitimacy established through other new festival contacts you can't verify
- Numbers are just specific enough to feel real
- Payment method is non-refundable: Venmo, Cash App, crypto always

Here's what's actually happening. Festival scams exploit time compression (you feel like you know people better than you do), social proof (vouching from people you also just met), and altered states (your financial risk-assessment is impaired). The tell is always urgency — real opportunities don't evaporate if you take 24 hours to verify them. Real investors don't need your $400 tonight from someone they met at a fire pit.

Run the check. The A check: “Who actually has access to my financial decisions right now, and what do I actually know about where this money is going?” — if you can’t answer the second half, you don’t have an investment, you have a story.

Here's the dark reality. Ticket Wristband Scams At The Gate. Fake wristbands, counterfeit tickets, and "I have an extra" scams concentrate at festival gates. Real tells: barcodes that scan once and deactivate (seller walks away before you scan), wristbands with seam inconsistencies, sellers who approach you rather than you finding them, prices too good for a sold-out event. Official box office or authorized resellers only. Verify the wristband goes on YOUR wrist and scans before any money moves.

Here's your move. Any opportunity that requires a fast yes deserves a slow no. Real things survive 24 hours of verification.

Here's exactly what to say. “That sounds interesting — let me sleep on it and find you tomorrow if I'm in.”

And here's the truth. If it evaporates the moment you slow down, it was never real.

### 4. “DON'T BE SOFT, BRO”

But the crew is still going and the implication is clear — tapping out means you're not built for this. You take it. By 7am you're in a situation that wouldn't have happened if you'd listened to yourself five hours ago.

Here are the signs.

- Your limit is framed as a character flaw — "soft," "scared," "not a real raver"
- The group's pace is the only acceptable pace — individual limits are embarrassing
- Shame is the enforcement mechanism: it hangs in the air, rarely said directly
- You override genuine body signals to avoid social cost

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Peer coercion in male spaces rarely looks like obvious pressure. It's ambient — the raised eyebrow, the "seriously?", the way the group keeps moving while you hesitate. Men are socialized to read ambient disapproval as a threat to status and respond with compliance. At a festival on substances, that socialization gets amplified. Your limits are not a personality flaw. The ones who respect your "I'm good" are the real crew.

Here's your check. The B check: “What is my body actually saying right now — not what does the group expect me to say?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Body Count Of Not Wanting To Look Soft. Men die of this. Not metaphorically. The overdose statistics in this scene skew male, and a meaningful share of them are men who took a second dose they did not want, or did not mention they had already taken something, or waited too long to say they felt wrong — because every one of those admissions reads as weakness in a circle that has been grading each other on hardness all weekend. They are running a script they also did not write. You are allowed to be the one who breaks it: “I’m good”, said flatly, twice, ends it. The man who keeps pushing after the second one is telling you something about himself, not about you.

Here's what you do. Your "I'm good" is a complete sentence. The crew that respects it is your actual crew.

Say this, out loud. “"I'm tapped — I'm gonna wind down. You lot carry on, I'll catch you at sunrise."”

Here's the truth underneath it. The ones who make you feel weak for having a limit are not your brothers. They're just louder than your gut.

### 5. “THE PLUG SITUATION”

- His emotional temperature changes when you try to spend time elsewhere

Here's what's really going on. Substance gatekeeping manufactures dependency by making one person the gatekeeper to your altered state. Ask yourself this. The A check: “Who controls my access to substances this weekend — and what is that actually costing me?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. When The Supply Is The Setup. In some cases the substance access IS the trap — you're being kept compliant, isolated, or in debt through the supply. Signs this is happening: you feel like you owe him beyond what any substance cost, he uses access as a bargaining chip in disagreements, leaving his orbit feels dangerous rather than just inconvenient. Remember this. Your altered state should never be owned by someone who wants something from you.

### 6. “HOT AND COLD ALL WEEKEND”
*Intermittent Reinforcement / The Pull-Push Cycle — clinically, intermittent reinforcement / manufactured attachment.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The person — could be a romantic interest, could be a crew lead, could be a promoter — who alternates warm and cold in ways that leave you perpetually trying to get back to the warm phase.

Here's the scene. Friday she was electric — connected, present, making you feel like the only person at the festival. Saturday she was barely there, cold, short. Saturday night she reappeared like nothing had happened, warm and magnetic again. You felt such relief to have her back you didn't ask about Saturday. Now it's Sunday and you've spent the whole weekend managing her temperature rather than enjoying the festival.

Here's what to watch for.

- Intense warmth followed by unexplained withdrawal, then warmth again
- You feel relief — not just happiness — when the warm phase returns
- You're adjusting your behavior to avoid triggering the cold phase
- The cold makes the warm feel more valuable than it actually is

Here's what's actually happening. Intermittent reinforcement is the most powerful conditioning mechanism in human psychology. Random reward creates compulsive seeking — it's the slot machine principle. When warmth is unpredictable you crave it more than if it were consistent. This applies whether the hot-cold is romantic, social, or professional. The cold isn't distance — it's the tool. The warmth isn't the reward — it's the hook being set.

Run the check. The B check: “Am I feeling relief or genuine happiness when she's warm? Those are different things.”

Here's the dark reality. This Is Not Just A Romance Thing. Men experience this pattern in crew dynamics too — the camp lead who blows cold when you question anything and warm when you comply, the promoter who withdraws opportunity when you push back. Hot-cold cycling as a control mechanism works in any relationship where one person has leverage over something you want. Name the pattern. That's the first step to not being run by it.

Here's your move. Notice whether you feel relief or joy when the warmth returns. Relief is not connection. Relief is a warning.

Here's exactly what to say. “[To yourself]: "I deserve warmth that doesn't come with cold attached to manage me."”

And here's the truth. Consistency is not boring. Unpredictability is not chemistry — it's a mechanism.

### 8. “WE NEED SOMEONE BIG”
*The Recruit / Being Used As Muscle — clinically, instrumental recruitment / liability transfer.*

Let's talk about who does this. The camp lead, the promoter, or the guy with the arrangement, who has looked at you and seen an asset rather than a person. Flattering about it. Specific about what he needs and vague about what it involves.

Then it is someone to have a word with a bloke who has been a problem. Watch for these.

- Recruited for your size or presence rather than for judgement or skill
- The task keeps growing and was never described in full
- No role, no training, no authority, and no radio
- You would be the one physically present if it goes wrong
- He is not coming, or is coming and standing behind you
- Nobody has mentioned who is liable if somebody gets hurt

Here's what's really going on. Untrained physical intervention is the fastest route to a criminal charge at a festival, and men get recruited into it constantly because presence reads as competence. The person asking transfers every risk to you — legal, physical and reputational — while keeping the authority and the benefit. Real security is trained, licensed, insured and radio-connected for exactly these reasons. Being big is not a qualification, it is an appearance, and it is the thing that makes you the most useful available person to put between someone else and a problem.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “What exactly am I being asked to do, and who is responsible if it goes wrong?” — If either answer is fuzzy, that fuzziness is the arrangement.

Here's what's underneath all of it. You Are The One In The Footage. Picture the aftermath rather than the moment. There is phone footage, and it starts thirty seconds in, and in every frame of it the large man doing something physical is you. The person who asked is not in shot. He was not there, he did not instruct anything, and his account afterwards will be that you took it upon yourself. This is precisely how men end up arrested at festivals for other people's disputes, and it is why the answer is not a braver version of yes. Getting a trained person is not cowardice; it is the only version of this that ends with you not being the defendant.

Here's the play. Decline the physical role and supply the actual one: get security or the duty manager, be a visible witness, film from a distance, stay with the person who is at risk. Presence without contact is genuinely useful and is not a criminal offence.

Here are the words. “I'm not putting hands on anyone — that's a job for someone licensed. I'll come and stand there, and I'll get a radio, but I'm not the muscle.”

Remember this. Being the biggest bloke available is not a job description. It is just who they will name afterwards.

### 9. “THE PROMOTER'S GOT A DEAL FOR YOU”
*Promoter Exploitation / Access Used as Control — clinically, manufactured dependency / access control.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The promoter or "connector" who offers access — backstage, bookings, exposure — in exchange for labor, money, or loyalty that goes one direction. Common target: male artists, DJs, and performers hungry for the next level.

Here's the scene. He can get you on a bigger stage. He knows the right people. He wants to manage you — no contract yet, "let's feel this out." The bigger stage keeps being three months away.

Here's what to watch for.

- Handshake deals with no contract — "we're family, we don't need paperwork"
- Money and labor flow toward him; opportunity stays perpetually in the future
- Raises the stakes of questioning him — "after everything I've done for you"
- Isolates you from other industry contacts — "those people don't understand your vision"

Here's what's actually happening. Access is currency in the music and festival world and predatory promoters know exactly how to use it. The pattern: create genuine-feeling opportunity, establish dependency before paperwork, extract labor while the opportunity stays always ahead. The "family" framing removes the business structure that would protect you. Contracts are not distrust — they're what makes relationships honest.

Run the check. The A check: “Who controls my access to the next level — and what is that actually costing me?”

Here's the dark reality. Get It In Writing — Every Time. Every booking, management agreement, revenue share — in writing, signed, before you show up. A basic performance contract covers: fee, payment timeline, cancellation terms, what you're responsible for. Music attorneys offer one-time consultations. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts offers free/low-cost help. A legitimate promoter will have no problem signing. The ones who resist paperwork are the ones the paperwork was designed to protect you from.

Here's your move. Opportunity is real. Opportunity without a contract is a liability. Get it in writing or keep walking.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm interested — let's put the basics in writing so we're both protected. I can send you a simple agreement tonight.”

And here's the truth. Any promoter who won't sign a contract is telling you what the deal actually is.

### 10. “EVERYONE SAW WHAT YOU DID”
*Reputation Threats / Social Smear Campaigns — clinically, smear campaign / reputation destruction.*

Here's who this is. The person who threatens or executes damage to your reputation on the festival circuit when you push back, question them, or simply stop complying. Common in promoter relationships, crew dynamics, and romantic situations gone sour.

- Negative characterizations of you begin circulating after you exercise independence
- Your professional contacts become suddenly distant without clear reason
- The people spreading the narrative are the same people you had a dispute with

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Reputation threats are a control mechanism that works specifically because the festival circuit is small and relationship-driven. The goal is to make the cost of pushing back higher than the cost of compliance. It often works. Here's your check. The S check: “Have I documented this situation in writing so I have something to point to?”

If payments are owed: send a written demand via email with terms attached — this creates a legal paper trail. If reputation damage is happening: keep records of what's being said, where, and when. Defamation (false statements presented as fact that damage your reputation) is actionable. Most predatory promoters rely on targets being too embarrassed or too tired to document. Document anyway.

Here's what you do. Build independent circuit relationships. Document everything. Your reputation is built over time — one smear campaign doesn't end it.

Say this, out loud. “"I'm going to communicate directly with the people who matter to me about what actually happened."”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your reputation is yours. It was built by your actual work. One person's narrative doesn't own it.

### 12. “ARE YOU GOING TO DO SOMETHING ABOUT IT”
*The Goad / Being Walked Into A Fight — clinically, provocation / escalation engineering.*

And here's the truth. He is offering you the chance to lose everything in front of an audience. Declining an invitation is not losing a fight.

### 13. “YOU OWE THE CAMP”

Week 3: managing other members. Month 3: you're running operations for the camp and still paying your entry fee and you're not sure exactly how you got here, but leaving now feels like abandoning something you've built.

Here are the signs.

- Contribution expectations vague at start and expand with each event
- Access to camp infrastructure tied to your continued compliance
- "After everything this camp has given you" appears when you express. a limit
- Camp leadership is not accountable in the same ways regular members are

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Camp debt works the same way gifter debt does — at a larger scale with a group enforcement mechanism. The contributions feel small individually but accumulate. The belonging feels real and the infrastructure is genuinely valuable. The tell is whether the arrangement is reciprocal and defined. Real community has terms both parties can articulate. When your contributions keep expanding and the camp's contributions stay stable — you're working, not belonging.

Here's your check. The S check: “When did I last actually talk to people outside this camp — my own people?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Calculate Your Actual Camp Cost. Write this down: (1) Entry fees and membership costs, (2) Hours of your labor at your hourly rate, (3) Cash contributions to camp expenses, (4) Value of any goods or services you provided. Compare to: what this event would cost you as an independent attendee with a solo ticket and your own setup. If the camp costs you more in total than going independently — you are paying for the privilege of working for them. Name that clearly before committing to another season.

Here's what you do. Define the terms before you commit. Expanding terms after commitment is pressure, not negotiation.

Say this, out loud. “I need to understand what's expected specifically before I commit to another season.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Community costs should be mutual, defined, and agreed to in advance. Everything else is just uncompensated labor.

### 14. “BUYING AND SELLING SAFELY”
*Legal, Otherwise, and How to Spot Undercovers — clinically, legal risk awareness / undercover operations.*

Let's talk about who does this. Transactions at festivals — substances, goods, services, tickets — carry real legal risk. Undercover officers work festivals and look exactly like attendees. This page is about navigating that reality with eyes open.

Here's how it plays out. He's in full kandi, wristbands up both arms, talking knowledgeably about the lineup, vibing completely with the crowd. He finds you and opens a conversation about what you're looking for. Everything about him reads as a fellow festie. He seems genuinely connected. He asks for your number. He says to meet him near the back of the camping area in twenty minutes. Something about the sequence feels slightly scripted.

Watch for these.

- They approach YOU to buy or sell — not the other way around
- Unusual interest in quantities, prices, or whether you know "anyone else who's looking"
- Wants to meet somewhere private or away from the crowd to transact
- Pushes for a quick decision or seems vaguely impatient with your hesitation

Here's what's really going on. Undercover officers at festivals are specifically trained to blend in and are often very good at it. The behavioral tells are in the transaction pattern itself, not the appearance: they initiate, they guide you toward isolation, they want specificity about quantities, and they push pace. You are never obligated to complete any transaction. "Actually I'm good" ends it without incident. Never follow anyone to an isolated location for any transaction regardless of what's being offered.

Ask yourself this. The A check: “Who has access to my decisions in this transaction — and do I know enough about this person to be doing this at all?” — if the answer is no, the deal isn’t worth what it’s about to cost you.

Here's what's underneath all of it. If You're Approached By Police Or Arrested. Know this before the festival: (1) You have the right to remain silent — use it. "I'd like to speak with a lawyer" is the only thing you say. (2) You can ask if you're being detained or are free to go — if free to go, go. (3) Do not consent to a search — you can decline without it being suspicious. (4) Write down badge numbers and officer names as soon as possible. (5) Contact a lawyer or the National Lawyers Guild (nlg.org) which has observer programs at many festivals. Good Samaritan laws protect people seeking overdose help in most states — use them.

Here's the play. "Actually I'm good" ends any transaction. Walk toward people. Never go somewhere isolated for any deal.

Here are the words. “"Actually I'm good" — then walk. No elaboration. No explanation. Just go.”

Remember this. The festival is not worth your freedom. No transaction is worth your freedom.

### 15. “THE EXIT IS ALWAYS YOURS”
*Getting Out of Any Situation Without Drama — clinically, exit planning / sunk cost interruption.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Men are socialized to stay in uncomfortable situations because leaving reads as weakness. It's not. Knowing when and how to exit — a crew, a deal, a tent, a transaction — is one of the most important skills you'll use this weekend.

Here's the scene. You're in a situation that feels wrong and the voice in your head is running through reasons to stay: it'd be rude to leave, you don't want to make it weird, maybe you're just being paranoid, the crew will think you can't handle it. None of those reasons are about your safety. All of them are about managing other people's opinions of you. Those are not the same thing.

Here's what to watch for.

- "I need to find my camp" works everywhere and requires zero explanation
- Code word system with your crew before the festival: one text = come get me
- Festival Rangers are there for uncomfortable situations — not just medical emergencies
- You don't have to explain yourself. You don't have to make it clean. You can just go.

Here's what's actually happening. The difficulty of leaving is almost always manufactured — by social pressure, by sunk cost thinking, by not wanting to be the one who breaks the vibe. Men specifically are socialized to read the desire to leave as cowardice rather than wisdom. It is wisdom. Your body knows when something is off before your brain has fully processed it. The moment leaving feels complicated is the moment leaving becomes most important.

Run the check. The S check: “When did I last actually check in with my squad — my actual people, not this new crew?”

Here's the dark reality. Safety Infrastructure Before You Arrive. Before gates open: set a daily check-in time with at least one friend outside whatever crew you end up in. Pick a meeting spot your whole group knows. Establish a code word. Write two emergency numbers in pen on your arm. Download an offline festival map. Know where the medical tent and Harm Reduction tent are. Know where the Ranger station is. These feel like overkill until the one time they're not.

Here's your move. Stand up. Say "I'm out." Walk. Managing their feelings about your departure is not your job.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I need to bounce — catch you later." Then go. No elaboration needed.”

And here's the truth. Knowing when to leave and actually leaving is a skill. Practice it.

*When You're the One With Access or Status — clinically, power differential awareness / personal accountability.*

Let's talk about who does this. If you're a camp lead, a promoter, a performer, a veteran with a big network, or simply the one who controls the substances, the ride, or the access — you have power. This page is about what you do with it.

- Silence from someone in your camp or crew is not consent — it may be fear

Ask yourself this. The B check: “Are the people in my orbit genuinely free to say no to me — and do they know that?”

Check in with people who seem quiet or withdrawn rather than assuming they're fine. ### 18. “SEE SOMETHING, DO SOMETHING”
*Bystander Activation for Men — clinically, active bystander intervention.*

Here's who you're dealing with. At some point this weekend you will see something that doesn't look right. A woman in a situation she didn't choose. A person too out of it to be alone. A transaction that has setup written all over it. What you do in that moment matters.

Here's the scene. You're walking between stages and you see a woman sitting with a guy who seems agitated. Her body language is closed. He's leaning into her space. You don't want to assume. You don't want to make it weird. You're not sure it's anything. But something caught. This is the moment. This is the one that either you act on or you walk past. Those are the only two options.

Here's what to watch for.

- THE DIRECT APPROACH: walk over like you know her — "Hey! There you are! Come on, everyone's looking for you"
- THE RESOURCE APPROACH: find a Ranger or Harm Reduction worker, describe what and where
- THE BUDDY APPROACH: position yourself nearby and make conversation with both until the dynamic shifts
- You do NOT need to confront anyone — that is not required and not always safe

Here's what's actually happening. Men specifically have a role in bystander intervention that's often underused. A man appearing who "knows" the woman in a tense situation changes the social calculus immediately — it creates an out without confrontation. You don't have to be the hero. You have to be the person who showed up. The most effective interventions are the quiet ones. Walk over. Be a familiar face. Give someone an exit.

Run the check. The S check: “Is someone in my squad or nearby in a situation that doesn’t look right? What would I want someone to do if it were me?” — do that.

Here's the dark reality. When A Friend Discloses Something Happened To Them. Men rarely know what to do when a friend discloses assault or harassment. Here's what actually helps: listen without judgment, believe them without interrogating the details, ask what they need rather than assuming, don't push them toward reporting if they're not ready. What doesn't help: "are you sure that's what happened," "what were you doing there," immediately offering to "handle it" in ways that remove their agency. Their experience is theirs to process on their timeline. Your job is to show up and stay.

Here's your move. See something, do something. You don't have to be the hero. You just have to show up.

Here's exactly what to say. “[Natural, easy]: "Hey! There you are! Come on, everyone's at the stage." Then walk with her.”

And here's the truth. The community is only as safe as what we each do in the moment we see something.

### 19. “IF SOMEONE'S NOT OKAY”
*Mental Health & Crisis Support at Festivals — clinically, crisis intervention / peer support.*

Here's who this is. Sometimes the festival brings up things that were already there. Sometimes the substances take someone somewhere dark. Sometimes it's exhaustion and overwhelm and feeling completely alone in a crowd of thousands. This page is for knowing what to do in those moments.

Picture this. Your campmate has been in his tent since Saturday afternoon. He came out once, looked off, said he was fine, went back in. It's Sunday morning. You've knocked twice. He said he's okay but something in his voice sounds wrong. You don't want to be dramatic. You also know him and this isn't normal. The moment you're sitting with right now — do you go back and check on him again, or do you leave it?

Here are the signs.

- Withdrawal from the festival when they came to engage — especially sudden
- Saying they're fine in a way that doesn't match their voice or energy
- Not eating, not sleeping, not coming out — for hours longer than exhaustion explains
- Saying things that sound like goodbye or that they shouldn't have come

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Festival environments can precipitate real mental health crises — substance effects, sleep deprivation, emotional openness that surfaces difficult things, and the isolation that can exist inside a crowd. Men are specifically at risk of not receiving support because "I'm fine" is the expected answer and people accept it. If your gut says something is wrong, knock again. Ask directly. "Are you actually okay?" delivered with real eye contact is a different question than "you okay?"

Here's your check. The B check: “What is my gut actually saying about this situation — and am I acting on it?”

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Crisis Resources At Festivals. Zendo Project (zendoproject.org) specializes in difficult psychedelic experiences and is present at many festivals — they can help with everything from bad trips to deeper crisis. Festival medical tents handle mental health crises — this is not just for physical injuries. Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741 — works where voice calls feel too hard. 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, available 24/7. If someone is in immediate danger, get festival medical or call 911. Staying with someone in crisis until help arrives is the most important thing you can do.

Here's what you do. Knock again. Ask directly. Stay with them. Get help if needed. That's the whole protocol.

Say this, out loud. “Hey — I'm asking for real. Are you actually okay? Because I'm not going anywhere until I know.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Checking on your people is not being dramatic. It's being a real friend.
