This is B.U.I.L.D.: Build Crew & Installation Edition. You are on site two weeks before anyone else and you leave after the lights go. This guide is for the part of the festival nobody photographs.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **B** — Barter Is Still A Contract. Wristband, food, a bunk, a credit — if you are working for it, it has terms. Get them in writing before the first shift.
- **U** — Unsafe Is A Full Stop. You are allowed to refuse a task. Fatigue, missing PPE, bad rigging, no banksman. The site's schedule is not your liability.
- **I** — Isolation Is The Risk Multiplier. Half-empty site, night shift, no medical yet, no phone signal. Everything in this book gets more dangerous when nobody is around.
- **L** — Log It While It Is Fresh. Hours, incidents, changes to the deal, who said what. Build runs on memory and goodwill, and both get rewritten at settle-up.
- **D** — Danger Of The Ninety-Sixth Hour. Most build injuries happen late in a long stretch to experienced people. Tiredness is the hazard, not inexperience.

Let's get into it.

## Capture — how access and trust get established

*Recruitment by passion, the deal that changes, crew as family.*

### 1. “WE'RE NOT A COMPANY, WE'RE A FAMILY”
*The Crew That Pays In Belonging — clinically, familial framing / labor obligation substitution.*

Here's who this is. Warm, genuinely bonded, and absolutely running on unpaid hours. Recruits on love of the work. Uses family language exactly where a normal employer would use a contract, and only ever in the direction of asking.

Picture this. The build crew is the best group of people you have ever worked with and the first four days are the happiest you have been in a year. On day five you realize nobody has mentioned money since the first conversation, the sixteen-hour days have become the schedule rather than the exception, and when you ask about the rate the lead says, kindly, that this is not really that kind of thing, that everybody here is doing it for the same reason, and that the family looks after each other.

Here are the signs.

- Family language appears exactly where terms should be
- No written agreement, and asking for one reads as a trust problem
- Hours expand without the deal changing
- Asking about money is treated as a character flaw rather than a question
- Looking after each other is invoked when asking, never when paying
- Everyone senior has a real job title in the outside world

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Familial framing is the most efficient way to extract labor, because it converts a commercial question into a loyalty question — and once that conversion has happened, asking about your rate makes you the person who does not love the thing, in front of people whose respect you now depend on. Build crews are especially exposed because the work is genuinely joyful, the bonds are genuinely real, and the culture genuinely is one of mutual aid. All of that is true and none of it is a contract. The test is directional: notice whether family gets invoked when something is being asked of you, or also when something is owed to you.

Here's your check. The B check: “If I could not work another shift from tomorrow, what would I actually be owed — in writing?” — If the answer is a feeling, you do not have a deal.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Family Is Load-Bearing In One Direction. Watch what happens at the edges, because that is where the frame reveals itself. Somebody gets injured on a family crew and discovers there is no insurance, no sick pay, and no employer, because they were never an employee — they were family, and family has no legal standing. Somebody raises a concern about a senior member and discovers the family closes, because a family protects its own and you are the newest one. That is the same mechanism the cult chapter of this scene's literature describes, arriving in work boots. It does not mean the warmth is fake. It means warmth is not a substitute for terms, and the crews that have both are the ones worth staying with.

Here's what you do. Get the deal in a message before the first shift — hours, what you get, what happens if you are injured, who pays for the ferry home. A text is a written agreement. Then keep working and enjoy the crew, which is genuinely one of the best things in this scene.

Say this, out loud. “Love it here, in for the full build. Can you drop me a quick message with the hours and what the deal covers — mostly so I know where I stand if I get hurt.”

Here's the truth underneath it. A real family will write it down without being wounded about it. Only the extractive version treats the question as an insult.

## Condition — how the situation gets shaped once you're in it

*Fatigue, unsafe tasks, night-shift isolation, PPE that never arrives.*

### 2. “THE NINETY-SIXTH HOUR”
*Fatigue As The Actual Hazard — clinically, cumulative fatigue / impairment-equivalent performance.*

Let's talk about who does this. Nobody. This one is the schedule. Day nine of a build, fourth consecutive sixteen-hour day, everybody running on catering coffee and the fact that the gates open Thursday whatever happens.

Here's how it plays out. You have been awake for nineteen hours and you are about to make a cut you have made a thousand times. The thing is you have made it a thousand times, which is why you are doing it now instead of stopping, and which is also why your hands are doing it while your attention is somewhere else. Everyone around you is in the same state and nobody is going to be the one who says the crew should stand down, because the schedule is the schedule.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- Consecutive long shifts with no rostered rest
- Experienced people making basic errors and laughing about it
- Microsleeps, dropped tools, losing the thread mid-sentence
- Safety-critical work scheduled at the end of shifts rather than the start
- Nobody empowered to stop the job without it being a confrontation
- The build is behind and that has quietly become everyone's justification

Here's what's really going on. Sustained sleep restriction degrades reaction time, judgment, and risk assessment measurably, and past roughly seventeen to nineteen hours awake the impairment is comparable to being over the legal driving limit — with the extra problem that self-assessment degrades alongside performance, so the more impaired you are the more confident you feel. Occupational injury data across construction and events points the same way: serious incidents cluster late in long stretches, among experienced workers, on routine tasks. The hazard is not the dangerous job you were nervous about. It is the easy one you have done a thousand times, at hour nineteen.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The D check: “How long have I been awake, and is this task one I would let a drunk person do?” — If you would not, the honest answer is that the site currently has one.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Competence Is Why It Gets You. The cruel part is that skill is the risk factor here, not the protection. Novices are slow and frightened, which keeps their attention on the task. Experienced hands run the cut on autopilot, which is exactly the faculty fatigue takes first, and it is why the amputations and the falls in this industry belong overwhelmingly to people who had done that job for years. There is a second layer specific to build: the incident happens on a site with no medical yet, no public, thin signal, and everyone else also exhausted. The margin you are relying on is not there. Stopping is not weakness on a build crew — it is the only intervention with any evidence behind it.

Here's the play. Name it as a crew rather than as an individual, because the social cost is what keeps it running: agree a hard stop hour for safety-critical work, and agree that anyone can call fatigue on anyone without argument. Schedule the height work, the plant, and the blades for the start of a shift.

Here are the words. “I'm nineteen hours in — I'm not cutting tonight, I'll do it first thing and it'll be better work. Anyone else past it should say now, nobody's keeping score.”

Remember this. The most experienced person on site is the one most likely to be hurt at hour ninety-six. That is not an insult. It is the data.

### 3. “IT'S JUST YOU OUT THERE TONIGHT”
*The Half-Built Site / Isolation Before The Festival Exists — clinically, environmental isolation / absent safety infrastructure.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The site itself, during load-in. Dark, enormous, mostly empty, with the medical tent not up yet, security not started, no public, patchy signal, and people you have never met driving plant around in the dark.

Here's the scene. You are finishing a run of cable at the far end of a field at eleven at night. There is one work light. The nearest other human is about four hundred metres away and does not know exactly where you are. Your phone has one bar. Every single protection this festival will have by Friday — rangers, medical, welfare, crowds of witnesses — does not exist yet, and will not for six days.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Lone working at night with nobody holding your location
- Medical, welfare, and security not yet operational on site
- Plant moving in the dark without a banksman
- No radio, or a radio on a channel nobody is monitoring
- Accommodation shared with people who have not been vetted at all
- No headcount — nobody would notice by morning

Here's what's actually happening. Everything else in this book assumes a populated festival: witnesses, a medical tent you can walk to, rangers, other people's phones. Build phase has none of that, and it is also when the workforce is smallest, least known to each other, and most exhausted. Two separate risks stack in that gap — the industrial one, where an ordinary injury becomes serious because nobody finds you for two hours, and the interpersonal one, where every pattern in this book operates without any of the infrastructure that usually constrains it. Crew accommodation in particular is often a field of strangers with no vetting and no lock, which is not a comment on crew, it is a description of the conditions.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The I check: “Does one named person know where I am and when I am due back?” — Not the crew. A person, with a time.

Here's the dark reality. The Six Days Before Anyone Is Watching. Say the plain version: the safest time to harm someone at a festival is before it is a festival. No public, no cameras, no rangers, no welfare tent, a small pool of people who all outrank you in tenure, and a culture that will close ranks around the build because the build is behind. The people this lands on hardest are new crew, and especially young or lone women and anyone who needed this gig — because raising anything means losing the accommodation you are currently sleeping in, on a site you cannot leave without a lift. That is the structural problem, and it is why the buddy rule is not fussiness. Two people, a stated return time, and one person off-site who expects to hear from you.

Here's your move. Buddy anything at height, with plant, with blades, or after dark — no exceptions, arranged as a crew norm rather than a personal request. Put a location and a return time to one named person every night. Know where the first-aider is, and where the vehicle keys live.

Here's exactly what to say. “Buddy rule after dark, I'm not doing the far field on my own. — Back by half eleven, if I'm not, come find me, I'm at the north cable run.”

And here's the truth. The festival's safety net goes up on Wednesday. You have been on site since the Thursday before. Build your own until then.

### 4. “THE DRIVE HOME”
*The Most Dangerous Hour Of The Whole Build — clinically, fatigue-impaired driving / post-event collapse.*

Here's who this is. You, on the Monday, after a three-week build and a two-day strike, with a van full of kit and four hours of motorway between you and a bed.

Picture this. Everything is loaded. The site is a field again. You have slept eleven hours in the last three days and you are going to drive home now because that is what everybody does and because the alternative is another night in a field. You will be fine for the first hour. The one that gets people is the third.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Driving at the end of a long build or a full strike
- Fewer than five hours' sleep in each of the preceding nights
- Driving in the window your body expects to be asleep
- Loaded van, unfamiliar route, motorway
- The whole crew leaves at once, so nobody is checking on anybody
- “I'll stop if I get tired”, which is the belief that does the damage

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Fatigue is a leading cause of fatal road collisions and the risk profile matches this exact moment: sustained sleep debt, monotonous roads, and driving at a time of day when the body is trying to shut down. The specific danger is the microsleep — a few seconds of sleep with the eyes open, which the driver does not experience as having happened, which is why “I'll know when I'm too tired” is false. At seventy miles an hour, four seconds is a hundred and twenty metres of unattended vehicle.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The D check: “How many hours have I slept in the last three nights?” — If it is under fifteen, you are not driving tonight. That is arithmetic, not opinion.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Crews Lose People On The Monday. This is the one that actually kills build crew, and it happens after the dangerous work is finished, which is exactly why nobody plans for it. The whole site has spent weeks being careful about heights and plant and blades, and then everybody gets in a vehicle in the worst physiological state of the entire job and drives home alone. Budget the extra night the way you budget fuel — sleep on site and leave Tuesday morning, or put the money for a room in the quote. If you are running the build, build it into the schedule and say out loud that nobody drives on Monday, because a crew will not do it individually if the culture expects the Monday drive.

Here's what you do. Sleep before you drive — one more night on site is cheaper than any alternative. Share the driving and stop every two hours regardless of how you feel. Caffeine buys twenty minutes, not four hours. Text somebody when you arrive so a missing message means something.

Say this, out loud. “I'm not driving tonight, I've had about four hours a night all week — I'll go first thing. — Crew rule this year: nobody drives on the Monday. Put the extra night in the budget.”

Here's the truth underneath it. The build did not hurt you. The drive home is the part that kills people, and it happens after you think the job is over.

### 5. “STRIKE IS WHEN IT ALL GOES WRONG”
*Teardown / The Phase Nobody Briefs — clinically, post-event risk concentration / governance collapse.*

Let's talk about who does this. The last forty-eight hours, when the audience has gone, the production office has half packed, most of the supervisors have left, and whoever is still on site is dismantling in the dark with the fewest people and the least oversight of the entire job.

Here's how it plays out. Everyone is exhausted, the structures are coming down in reverse order by people who did not put them up, half the crew are new faces brought in for the teardown, and the person who would have said no to something is already on a train. There is no briefing for strike. There never is.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- No strike briefing, though there was a build one
- Structures dismantled by people who did not erect them
- Supervisors and safety leads already departed
- New casual crew with unverified experience
- Everyone at their most fatigued, working at speed to finish
- Payment and paperwork also leaving the site, with you unpaid

Here's what's really going on. Teardown concentrates every risk factor in this guide into the shortest window: maximum fatigue, minimum supervision, unfamiliar crews, and a strong collective incentive to be finished. Dismantling is more dangerous than erecting, because loads shift unpredictably when a structure comes apart and because the people taking it down often do not know how it went together. It is also when the money leaves — the production company is closing the site down, and a worker who is owed and has not been paid is now chasing an organization that has physically gone.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The U check: “Who is supervising strike, and who am I handing this structure to?” — If nobody can answer, that is the finding, on day one of teardown.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Money Leaves With The Site. Two things walk off during strike and both matter. The first is competence: the people who know how each structure was built leave before it comes down, so ask for a handover from whoever erected it and do not guess a load path. The second is your wage. Payment at settle-up is bad enough; payment at settle-up during a teardown, when the office is packing and the crew is dispersing, is how people go home unpaid with no one left to ask. Get your hours signed before strike begins, not after, while there is still somebody on site whose job it is to sign them.

Here's the play. Insist on a strike briefing, even a five-minute one. Get a handover from whoever built a structure before you take it down. Get your hours signed and confirmed before teardown starts. Keep the buddy rule and the fatigue rule harder in strike than in build.

Here are the words. “Before we start pulling this down — who put it up, and can they walk me through it? — Can you sign off my hours today rather than Monday, while there's still an office?”

Remember this. Strike has the most fatigue, the fewest supervisors and the least oversight of the whole job, and nobody briefs it. Ask for the briefing.

## Control — how leverage is held and used

*Wages withheld, accommodation as leverage, blacklisting.*

### 6. “SETTLE UP AFTER THE EVENT”
*The Deal That Shrinks At The End / Payment As Leverage — clinically, wage withholding / retrospective renegotiation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The production manager with a spreadsheet and a cash-flow problem. Pleasant all build. Becomes hard to find on the Monday, and when found, remembers the arrangement differently and has the advantage of being the one who writes it down.

Here's the scene. The agreement was a day rate and expenses. It is Monday, the site is half struck, and the number he says now is lower — because the days you did were partly travel, because the accommodation counts against it, because the original figure was always a best case. You have no contract. You have a fortnight of sixteen-hour days, a van you paid the diesel for, and no leverage at all, because the work is already done.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Terms agreed verbally and never confirmed in writing
- Payment scheduled entirely after the work is complete
- Deductions appearing at settle-up that were never mentioned
- Your accommodation or transport home is in their gift
- Other crew have had the same conversation and did not compare notes
- The implication that complaining costs you next season

Here's what's actually happening. All the leverage in a build arrangement sits at the end, which is why unscrupulous production will schedule payment there and why the honest ones pay staged. Once your labor is spent it cannot be withdrawn, and the threat that does the real work is rarely stated: next year's booking. In a scene where work comes by reputation and relationship, being known as difficult about money is a genuine cost, and everybody doing this knows you have done that arithmetic. It is the same structure as the vendor non-payment page in M.A.R.K.E.T., with one addition — they may also control whether you have a bed tonight and a way home tomorrow.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Run the check. The L check: “Do I have the agreed terms in writing, and a dated log of hours I actually worked?” — Memory loses to a spreadsheet, every time.

Here's the dark reality. Next Year Is The Threat Nobody Says. The unspoken sentence doing all the work is that there is always next season, and you want to be on it. That is what keeps crews quiet about underpayment for years at a time, and it is why the problem is almost never one bad payer — it is a whole circuit where everybody has been shorted and nobody compared figures, because comparing figures is exactly what the threat prevents. The single most effective move available is the one that costs nothing and feels the most awkward: ask two other crew what they were paid. Wage secrecy is the mechanism. Breaking it is the counter, and crews that talk openly about rates do not have this problem.

Here's your move. Get the terms in a message before shift one and stage the payment — something up front, something mid-build. Log hours daily in a dated note with photographs of the work. Keep your own route home so your exit is never in their gift. Afterwards: a dated written demand, politely, with the log attached, beats a phone call every time.

Here's exactly what to say. “Before I start — can you confirm the rate, the days, and what's covered, just in a text? And I'd want a third up front. Standard for me, nothing personal.”

And here's the truth. Your labor is only leverage before you spend it. Write the terms down while you still have some.

### 7. “YEAH, I'VE DRIVEN ONE OF THESE”
*Faked Competence / The Ticket Nobody Checked — clinically, unverified qualification / plant operation risk.*

Here's who this is. Somebody who needs the work and has said yes to a question they should have said no to — usually not out of recklessness, but because admitting it on a site where everyone else seems to know what they are doing feels impossible.

Picture this. He is on the telehandler moving a two-tonne load over a walkway and his movements are not right and everybody can see it and nobody is saying anything, because saying something means calling a man a liar in front of the crew. You are standing in the area he is swinging over.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here are the signs.

- Nobody has asked to see a ticket, licence or certification
- Hesitant, jerky or improvised operation of plant or lifting equipment
- Vague or deflecting answers about where and when they trained
- No banksman, no exclusion zone, no lift plan
- The crew has noticed and is communicating about it by eye contact
- The site has no record of anybody's qualifications

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Plant, lifting and height work are ticketed for a reason and festival builds are one of the last places where nobody asks to see the ticket. The pressure runs both ways: an operator who overstates their experience is usually somebody who could not afford to lose the gig, and a crew that will not challenge it is protecting a man's dignity at the expense of everyone standing underneath. Neither of those is malice, and the outcome is the same as if it were.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Here's your check. The U check: “Has anybody actually seen this person's ticket?” — Assumed competence is not competence, and you are standing in the swing radius.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Say It To The Supervisor, Not To The Man. The reason this persists is entirely social, so the fix has to be social too. Challenging the operator directly in front of the crew is humiliating and will be resisted, which is why nobody does it and why the situation runs for days. Take it to the supervisor as a question about paperwork rather than about a person — have we got tickets on file for the plant operators — and it becomes an administrative matter with no one accused of anything. And remember the other half: if you are the one who said yes to something you cannot do, saying so now costs you a job, and saying so after costs somebody a life. Nobody on a site has ever been fired for saying they need a hand with a machine they do not know.

Here's what you do. Ask the supervisor for tickets to be on file as a site-wide matter, not about an individual. Stay out of swing and drop zones regardless. Insist on a banksman for every lift. And if it is you: say so, today, to one person.

Say this, out loud. “Have we got plant tickets on file for everyone? Worth having if anything happens. — [if it's you] I've not run one of these before, I said yes when I shouldn't have. Can I get shown properly or put somewhere else?”

Here's the truth underneath it. Nobody wants to call a man a liar in front of the crew. Make it a question about paperwork and it stops being about him.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

*The written deal, the hours log, the refusal script, the buddy rule.*

### 8. “THE BUILD CREW KIT”
*The Five Things That Make A Build Survivable — clinically, preventive infrastructure / worker protection basics.*

Let's talk about who does this. For anyone taking a build gig, at any level, on any crew — including the good ones, because the good ones do all of this already and will respect you for it.

Here's how it plays out. You are packing. What goes in the bag now is what you will have at hour ninety-six on a dark field with no signal and a production office that has gone home. Everything on this page takes ten minutes to arrange before you travel and is impossible to arrange afterwards.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Watch for these.

- The deal in writing: rate, days, what's covered, injury, transport home
- A dated hours log you fill in daily, with photos of the work
- Your own PPE — boots, gloves, hi-vis, glasses, ear protection, head torch
- Your own route home that nobody else controls
- One off-site person with the site address who expects a daily check-in
- The name of the site first-aider and where the trauma kit actually is

Here's what's really going on. Every pattern in this guide is defeated by something arranged in advance rather than negotiated in the moment, and the moment is always the worst time — you are tired, you are isolated, the work is already done, and the other party holds the leverage. Bringing your own PPE is the clearest example: it removes the most common coercive sentence on any site, which is that there is not a spare and the job still needs doing. Owning your route home removes the second. These are not signs of distrust. Experienced crew arrive with all of it, and the crews worth working for notice and think better of you.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the spoken line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want the runtime down.

Ask yourself this. The U check: “Can I refuse a task right now without losing my bed or my lift?” — If not, fix that before you need to refuse anything.

Here's what's underneath all of it. The Kit Is What Makes No Possible. Every item on that list exists to make one sentence survivable: no, I am not doing that. Your own boots mean nobody can tell you the job goes ahead in trainers. Your own transport means refusing does not strand you. The written deal means walking away does not cost you a fortnight's pay. The check-in means somebody notices. Refusal is not a matter of courage on a build site, it is a matter of what refusing costs — and everything on that list is there to lower the price until it is something you can actually afford to say.

Here's the play. Pack the kit, send the deal message, name your off-site person, and find the first-aider and the trauma kit in your first hour on site. Then do the work, which is some of the best work there is.

Here are the words. “Got my own PPE and my own van, so I'm sorted. Who's the first-aider this build, and where's the trauma kit living?”

Remember this. Every bit of that kit buys you the same thing: the ability to say no and still have a bed, a lift, and a wage.

## Community — the real version versus the counterfeit

*Real crew solidarity versus a family that only takes.*

### 9. “THE CREW THAT ACTUALLY HOLDS”
*Real Solidarity Versus A Family That Only Takes — clinically, authentic crew culture vs extractive belonging.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The real thing is unglamorous and specific: people who tell each other their rates, who cover a shift without a ledger, who stop the job when somebody is unfit, and who back the newest person over the most senior one when the newest person is right.

Here's the scene. Two crews. On the first, a lad two days in says he is not comfortable going up in that wind, and the lead says fine, get the ground kit, and nothing else is ever said about it. On the second, the same sentence produces a silence, and then a joke, and then somebody else goes up instead — and everybody learns what happens if you say it.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/build/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with that scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want the runtime down.

Here's what to watch for.

- Real: refusing an unsafe task costs nothing, visibly, in public
- Real: people talk openly about rates and compare them
- Real: the newest person is listened to when they flag something
- Extractive: family gets invoked only when something is being asked
