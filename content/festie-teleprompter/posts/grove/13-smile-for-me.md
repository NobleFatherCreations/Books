# “SMILE FOR ME”
*Photography as Predation — clinically, boundary violation / digital coercion + stalking.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The Photographer With Access. Could be credentialed, could be a guy with a phone. Uses the camera to create proximity, extract intimacy, and build a collection you may not know exists.

Here's the scene. He took a gorgeous shot of you dancing Saturday and showed you on his screen — genuinely beautiful. Said he'd send it. Asked to get a few more, closer, "just your energy." Later your friend shows you his Instagram stories. Photos of you that you don't remember being taken. One from behind. One that feels intimate in a way you can't fully articulate. Everyone says he's a great photographer. You feel like you're being dramatic. You're not.

Watch for these signs.

- Photos taken without asking, normalized as "just capturing the moment"
- Escalation from group shots to solo shots to closer, more intimate framing
- Promised photos create a reason to stay in contact and find you after the festival
- Images appear on social that you didn't know were being taken of you

Here's what's actually happening. The camera creates three manipulation vectors: proximity (he needs to get close), intimacy (he frames you personally), and post-festival leverage (he has your image and a reason to contact you). In festival culture photography is celebrated and normalized — which makes predatory documentation easy to disguise as art. Your image is yours in every context. "Everyone was being photographed" is not consent. A camera pointed at your body without your knowledge is a violation regardless of cultural context.

Run the check. The E check: “Can I freely ask him to delete photos I'm uncomfortable with? What happens when I do?”

Here's the dark reality. The Post-Festival Digital Predator. Festival photography is often setup for what happens after: unsolicited DMs, location tagging your images, sharing photos you didn't consent to, or using intimate images as leverage. Before leaving any photographer's presence: "Can you show me everything you took of me?" You have the right to ask for deletions on the spot while you watch. If they post images of you without permission after — that's reportable on every platform. Screenshot before reporting. Document everything.

Here's your move. Your image is yours. You can ask anyone, anytime, to delete a photo of you — no explanation required.

Here's exactly what to say. “Hey — I'm not comfortable with that one. Can you delete it right now while I watch?”

And here's the truth. Consent for one photo is not consent for all photos forever in all contexts. Each image needs a yes.
