# “HE'S BEEN IN OUR GROUP FOR YEARS”
*The Insider / The Predator Who Is Already Vouched For — clinically, in-group perpetrator / pre-established trust exploitation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Not a stranger. Your friend's partner, the camp regular, the one who has been coming since before you did. Vouched for by people you trust, which is the entire advantage, and which is why he does not have to run any of the approaches in the earlier pages of this guide.

Here's the scene. It is not a stranger at the rail. It is the guy who has been in this group for six years, who everyone loves, who your best friend introduced you to — and who was in your tent doorway at four in the morning saying he just wanted to check you got back okay, standing slightly too close, for slightly too long, in a way you will spend the next three weeks trying to describe to yourself.

Watch for these signs.

- Contact happens at the edges — late, in transit, in the tent, when you are alone
- Individually deniable each time, and there is always a reason
- He is closer to your friends than you are, and you both know it
- Other women in the group have gone slightly quiet about him
- Your account would cost the group more than his would
- Nothing he has done would sound like anything if you said it out loud

Here's what's actually happening. Most sexual harm is committed by somebody the person already knows, and festival crews are exactly the trust structure that makes it work — years of shared history, a group identity, and a strong collective incentive not to have this be true. The insider does not need charm or a technique, because vouching has already done the work that an approach would otherwise have to do. What he has instead is the thing a stranger never gets: repeated, unremarkable proximity, and a group that will find it easier to reinterpret your discomfort than to lose him.

Run the check. The G check: “Am I explaining away something I would flag instantly in a stranger?” — Do the substitution deliberately. If a man you met yesterday had done it, you would already know.

Here's the dark reality. The Group Is The Reason It Keeps Working. Here is the part that makes this the hardest page in the guide. When someone finally says something, groups usually do not split over whether it happened — they split over what it costs, and the person who spoke is generally the one who pays. Everybody can feel that in advance, which is why it takes years, and why when it finally comes out three other women say quietly that he did the same to them. If you are at the start of that: you do not have to accuse anybody of anything. Say the specific thing, once, to one woman in the group, factually. The overwhelming likelihood is that she has been waiting for somebody else to speak first, and two accounts change everything that one account cannot.

Here's your move. Write it down with the date while it is fresh, in plain factual language with no adjectives. Then tell one woman in the group, privately, as a fact rather than a verdict. Stop being alone with him — no lifts, no late walks, no tent doorways — without announcing a policy.

Here's exactly what to say. “I want to tell you something and I'm not asking you to do anything with it. On Saturday at about four, Callum did [the specific thing]. I'm writing it down. Has anything like that come up for you?”

And here's the truth. Vouched for is not the same as safe. It only means nobody has said it yet.
