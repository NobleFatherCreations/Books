# “WHERE THAT PHOTO LANDS”
*What You Owe The People In Your Frames — clinically, publication ethics / downstream harm awareness.*

Here's who you're dealing with. You, at home on the Tuesday, with two thousand frames and a deadline and no one watching what you select.

Here's the scene. There is a frame in there of somebody mid-breakdown at the edge of a crowd, and it is the best photograph you took all weekend. It says something true about the whole event. Nobody would ever know who they are except everyone who knows them, which at a festival of this size is probably four hundred people.

Watch for these signs.

- Would this person be fine with their employer, family or ex seeing this
- Are they identifiable to people who know them, even if not to strangers
- Are they in a medical, harm-reduction, welfare or crisis context
- Is anybody in the frame a minor, and is anybody outed by it
- Would you be comfortable sending it to them before you post it
- Is this frame about the festival, or about them specifically

Here's what's actually happening. Selection is where the ethics actually live — the shutter is a reflex, the edit is a decision made with time and without pressure, and that is the moment you are accountable for. Festival crowds contain people who are not out, people whose employers do not know they are here, people in custody disputes, people in recovery, people who are somewhere they told someone else they were not. None of that is visible in the frame and all of it is downstream of your upload. You cannot check for every case, which is exactly why the general rules exist: no crisis contexts, no minors without a parent's yes, no identifiable faces in compromising states, and send it to them first when you are unsure.

Run the check. The E check: “Am I willing to send this to the person in it before it goes up?” — If not, that reluctance is the answer, and you already know what it means.

Here's the dark reality. The Best Frame Is Usually The One To Kill. The photographs that do the most damage are almost never the careless ones — they are the excellent ones, because those are the ones that travel. A brilliant frame of somebody at their worst gets shared precisely as far as it is good, and the person in it spends a year meeting it. If you shoot festivals for long enough you will take a frame that is genuinely the best work of your career and should never be published, and what you do with that frame is the whole of your professional character. Nobody will ever know you killed it. That is rather the point.

Here's your move. Make the edit a separate session with the rules in front of you, not a scroll at 1am. When a frame is strong and you are unsure, send it to the subject and let them decide — most say yes, and the ones who say no are the ones that would have been the problem.

Here's exactly what to say. “Hey — took this of you on Saturday, think it's a great shot. Only posting it if you're happy, no hard feelings either way.”

And here's the truth. Your best photograph is not automatically a photograph you are allowed to publish. The edit is where you find out what kind of photographer you are.
