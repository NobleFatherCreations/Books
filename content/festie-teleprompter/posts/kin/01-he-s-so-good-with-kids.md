# “HE'S SO GOOD WITH KIDS”
*The Beloved Camp Uncle / Access Through Trust — clinically, child grooming / institutional trust exploitation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Genuinely great with children, which is the difficult part of this page. Everybody's favourite. Has been coming for years, runs the games, remembers the kids' names between festivals, and is the first person anyone would defend.

Here's the scene. Your daughter adores him. He does the face paint and the treasure hunt and he is the reason the family field is fun. He has started giving her small presents — a bracelet, a patch for her jacket — and asking for her specifically, and suggesting he take her to the workshop tent because you look tired, and the sentence forming in your head is that you would be insulting a good man to say no.

Watch for these signs.

- Seeks time alone with a specific child, always with a good reason
- Gives gifts or attention to one child rather than to all of them
- Establishes secrets — small, harmless ones, framed as a shared joke
- Prefers the company of children to that of adults at an adult event
- Pushes past a soft no with warmth rather than pressure
- Universally liked, which means raising anything has a social cost

Here's what's actually happening. Child sexual abuse is overwhelmingly committed by somebody the child and the family already know and trust, and the grooming literature describes a consistent sequence: access, then a special relationship with one child, then small secrets, then isolation. Every step looks like kindness and most of them, performed by most people, are kindness. That is exactly why safeguarding policy in every organization that works with children is built on rules about situations rather than judgments about people — because judging individuals is precisely what the pattern defeats, and because the people who are wrongly suspected and the people who are correctly suspected look identical from outside.

Run the check. The N check: “Is an adult asking for time alone with my child?” — The answer is no, every time, regardless of who is asking. The rule is the protection. Assessing the person is not.

Here's the dark reality. The Rule Exists So You Never Have To Judge. This is the hardest page in this book to write and to read, and the reason is that the overwhelming majority of beloved camp uncles are exactly what they appear to be — generous adults who like kids and make festivals magical. A blanket rule is not an accusation against any of them. It is a rule precisely because no parent can reliably tell, because the ones who cannot be told apart are the whole problem, and because a good man will not mind. That last part is the practical test: a genuinely safe adult hears “we don't do one-on-one” and says fine, of course, come along too. Only one kind of person argues with a safeguarding rule, and the argument is the finding.

Here's your move. Make it a policy rather than a judgment, and say it out loud to the whole camp at the start so it is never about one person: no one-on-one, activities happen with two adults or in view. Then say yes to everything that meets the rule — the face paint, the treasure hunt, all of it.

Here's exactly what to say. “We do a two-adult rule for the kids, nothing personal to anyone — so yes to the workshop, and I'll walk over with you both.”

And here's the truth. Good adults are not offended by safeguarding rules. The rule protects them too, and the person who argues has told you something.
