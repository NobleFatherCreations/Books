# “IT TAKES A VILLAGE, NAMED”
*A Real Village Versus An Assumed One — clinically, authentic collective care vs diffused assumption.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The family field at its best, which is genuinely one of the great things this culture has built — kids running semi-feral in the safest possible way, raised for a weekend by twenty adults who all know them.

Here's the scene. The real version is unmistakable once you have seen it. Adults who know which kid belongs to whom. Somebody bringing your child back with a comment about the face paint. A camp where the rota is on the whiteboard and nobody thinks that is uptight. Kids who can name four adults they are allowed to go to, because somebody sat down and taught them the four.

Watch for these signs.

- Real: adults are named, rotas exist, handovers are spoken
- Real: children can name the adults they may go to
- Real: someone is always sober and everyone knows who
- Real: the two-adult rule is stated openly and nobody is offended
- Assumed: “someone's always around”, and no one is specifically
- Assumed: raising a safeguarding point would make you the awkward one

Here's what's actually happening. The village is real and it works, and it works because of structure rather than in spite of it. The camps where children are genuinely safest are the ones that look, from outside, slightly over-organized: whiteboard, rota, named on-duty adult, an out-loud rule about one-on-one. That structure is what lets the freedom be real — kids roaming the family field all weekend, which is the thing you came for. The unstructured version produces the opposite: adults who feel responsible in general and therefore nobody responsible in particular, and a culture where the person who suggests a rota is the problem.

Run the check. The K check: “Can every adult here name tonight's on-duty person, and can every kid name four adults they can go to?” — Two questions, asked on Thursday.

Here's the dark reality. Be The One Who Says It First. Somebody has to say the rules out loud, and in a warm camp of old friends that feels enormously awkward — like importing suspicion into a place built on trust. Say them anyway, and say them on day one, to everyone, before anything has happened, because a rule introduced as general policy costs nothing and a rule introduced after a specific worry is an accusation. You will find that most of the camp is relieved. Parents are usually all quietly thinking it and each assuming they would be the uptight one, which is the same diffusion problem as the supervision gap, applied to speaking up.

Here's your move. Call the five-minute family brief on Thursday: rota, on-duty adult, two-adult rule, meeting point, and the four adults each child may go to. Put it on a whiteboard. Then let them run.

Here's exactly what to say. “Quick family-camp thing before we all settle in — rota's on the board, on-duty's not drinking, no one-on-one with the kids, and let's make sure every kid can name four grown-ups they can go to.”

And here's the truth. The village is real, and it is made of names. A village where nobody is named is just a field with your child in it.
