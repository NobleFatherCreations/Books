# “THERE WAS SOMETHING IN THAT”
*Accidental Exposure / The Brownie, The Vape, The Shared Bottle — clinically, unintentional ingestion / involuntary exposure.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Nobody hostile. A camp full of people who forgot, or who genuinely did not consider that the tray of brownies needed a label, or that the vape going round has more in it than nicotine.

Here's the scene. You ate one thing from the communal table because you were starving and it was food. Forty minutes later you know. You are sitting in a camp chair doing arithmetic about whether this counts, whether you have to tell anyone, whether your sponsor will think you engineered it, and whether your sobriety date has just moved — and none of that arithmetic is helping with the part where you are currently high and did not agree to be.

Watch for these signs.

- Unlabelled food on a communal table
- A vape, joint or bottle passed round with no statement of contents
- Nobody in the camp thinks to mention it because everybody else knows
- A drink topped up rather than handed over
- You are the only sober person and the camp has not adjusted anything
- The first you hear of it is somebody laughing about it afterwards

Here's what's actually happening. Accidental ingestion at festivals is common and it is a labelling failure rather than an attack — which does not make it less serious for you. Edibles are the usual route because they are food-shaped and the onset is delayed enough that you have eaten it before anyone could have told you. The practical harm is real — being involuntarily altered removes your capacity in an environment where you are frequently the person others are relying on — and the internal harm can be larger, because it lands as a relapse in a body that cannot tell the difference.

Run the check. The O check: “Do I know what is in this, from the person who made it?” — Communal food counts. This is the rule the book gives everybody, applied to a plate.

Here's the dark reality. It Is Not A Relapse, And Decide That Now. Settle this in advance, in daylight, because at the time you will not be able to. Being dosed without agreeing to it is an exposure, not a use — there was no decision, so there is nothing to have relapsed from. Recovery frameworks vary in language but not in substance on this, and every sponsor you could call has fielded it. The danger is not the substance, it is the forty-eight hours afterwards, when shame says you are a fraud, that you must have known, that you cannot tell anyone — and that is the state that turns an accident into an actual return to use. Ring your person that night, not when you feel ready.

Here's your move. Eat from your own supply at camp and ask about anything communal before it reaches your hand. Ask the camp once, openly, on Thursday to label things. If it happens: get somewhere safe with a person, hydrate, ride it out, and call your recovery contact the same day.

Here's exactly what to say. “Quick one before we settle in — I'm sober, so can we label anything with stuff in it? Doesn't have to be a whole thing, just a note on the tray.”

And here's the truth. You did not decide, so you did not relapse. Call your person the same night, before shame writes a different version.
