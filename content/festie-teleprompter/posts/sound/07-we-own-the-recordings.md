# “WE OWN THE RECORDINGS”
*Live Recording Rights / Who Owns Your Festival Set — clinically, intellectual property exploitation / rights grab.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Every festival set you play is potentially being recorded — audio, video, professional and amateur. Who owns those recordings is determined by what's in writing before you play. If nothing is in writing: probably not you.

Here's the scene. Your set went viral — 400,000 views on the festival's YouTube channel. The recording is incredible. Fans are using it everywhere. The festival is now licensing it to a streaming platform for a compilation. You receive nothing because when you signed the booking confirmation — buried in the terms — you assigned all recording rights to the festival for all formats in perpetuity.

Watch for these signs.

- Booking confirmation or contract contains a recording rights clause you didn't negotiate
- "Standard terms" applied to recording rights without specific discussion
- Festival recording professionally without having discussed rights with you
- Live recordings appear on streaming platforms or in commercial contexts without your knowledge

Here's what's actually happening. Live recording rights are one of the most commonly overlooked and most valuable assets in a musician's career. Festival organizers routinely include sweeping rights grabs in standard booking paperwork. Your performance is a copyrightable work. Your likeness in those recordings is yours. A simple rider clause reserves your recording rights or requires negotiation. Without it, you're playing the show and handing them a product.

Run the check. The O check: “Who owns the recordings being made of me tonight — and is that in writing before I go on stage?” — know who owns the product before you create it, not after.

Here's the dark reality. Recording Rights Rider Language. Add to your standard rider: "Artist retains all rights to audio and video recordings of the performance. Any recording by Presenter requires written agreement specifying format, distribution, compensation, and Artist approval rights prior to the event." For streaming: separate sync licensing fees apply. ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC collect performance royalties — make sure your set is registered. SoundExchange collects digital performance royalties. If a festival has a streaming deal, your performance may generate royalties you're entitled to but not receiving.

Here's your move. Add recording rights language to your rider. Always. Non-negotiable.

Here's exactly what to say. “Before I confirm — I need to add my standard recording rights clause to this agreement. I can send the language tonight.”

And here's the truth. Your performance is a product. Know who owns it before you create it.
