# “IT HAPPENED TO YOU AND YOU HAVE NO WORDS FOR IT”
*The Thing Men Are Not Supposed To Be Able To Have Happen — clinically, male sexual victimization / disclosure barrier.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Nobody. This page is about you, and about the specific reason you have not said anything about the thing that happened on Saturday.

Here's the scene. You were extremely drunk or extremely high or both. You do not have all of it. You have enough of it. And every time you get close to describing it, some part of your head supplies the sentence that men cannot really have this happen, or that you should have wanted it, or that people would laugh — and you put it back down, and it has now been four months.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/bass/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with this scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want this under 3 minutes.

Watch for these signs.

- You have not told anyone, and the reason is how it would sound
- You keep landing on “I should have been able to stop it”
- You are performing fine, loudly, to specific people
- Sleep, drinking, or temper have changed and you have attributed it elsewhere
- You have described it once as a funny story and felt worse afterwards
- You would recognize it instantly as assault if a friend described it to you

Here's what's actually happening. Sexual assault of men is substantially more common than the culture admits and substantially less reported, and the barrier is well documented: the expectation that a man could not be overpowered, the belief that arousal equals consent (it does not — it is a physiological response and it occurs during assaults), and the fear of being disbelieved or mocked. Incapacity is incapacity regardless of gender. If you could not consent, you did not, and nothing about your body's reactions, your size, or your enjoyment of the evening up to that point changes that.

Run the check. The B check: “If a mate described this exact thing to me, what would I call it?” — You already know. Use your own answer.

Here's the dark reality. What The Silence Does Over Time. The untreated course of this is fairly predictable and it is why the page is here rather than in a footnote: undisclosed assault in men shows up later as drinking, as anger, as an inability to be touched, as a relationship that ends without either party understanding why, and in the worst cases as suicidality — and by then it is several years downstream and nobody connects it to a weekend nobody was told about. The single intervention that changes the trajectory is telling one person. Not the group, not a public account, one person: a specialist line, a GP, a therapist, or the one friend you already know would not make it a joke. Male survivors' services exist in every country and they exist because there are many, many of you.

Here's your move. Tell one person this month. Choose a helpline first if choosing a friend feels impossible — it is anonymous, they have heard it, and saying it out loud once breaks the thing that shame relies on. If it was recent, a forensic examination is available to men too, and in most places without a police report.

Here's exactly what to say. “Something happened at a festival and I haven't told anyone and I think I need to say it out loud once. I don't need you to do anything about it.”

And here's the truth. You could not consent, so you did not. That sentence does not have a different version for men.
