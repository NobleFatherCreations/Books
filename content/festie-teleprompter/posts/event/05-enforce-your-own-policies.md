# “ENFORCE YOUR OWN POLICIES”
*Consent Culture That Is Real, Not Decorative — clinically, policy enforcement / selective application.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Many events have consent policies. Fewer events enforce them consistently. The gap between a stated policy and its enforcement is not just a failure of follow-through — it is the active message that the policy is decorative rather than real.

Here's the scene. Your event's consent policy is posted at the entrance. It is in your promotional materials. Your team briefing included it. On Saturday night a security staff member reports a consent violation to the team lead. The team lead, who knows the person being reported, decides to handle it informally. The person stays at the event. By Sunday morning two more people have had experiences with the same individual. The policy did not fail. The enforcement did.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/event/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with this scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want this under 3 minutes.

Watch for these signs.

- Consent violations handled informally when formal processes exist
- Known violators who remain at events because of their connections or contributions
- Different enforcement standards applied based on who the violator is
- Reports that are received and not acted on within a defined timeframe

Here's what's actually happening. Inconsistent enforcement of consent policies does more damage than no policy at all — because it signals to potential violators that the policy is navigable through the right relationships, and to potential reporters that reporting may not be worth the cost. Consistent enforcement regardless of who the violator is — including people who are connected, contributing, or beloved — is the only thing that makes the policy real. The first time you fail to enforce it is the moment you define what the policy actually is.

Run the check. The E check: “Am I enforcing my consent policy consistently — regardless of who the violator is?”

Here's the dark reality. What Real Consent Policy Enforcement Looks Like. Real enforcement means: (1) Every reported consent violation is documented and reviewed by a designated safety lead — not handled informally by whoever is available. (2) The safety lead has actual authority to remove people from the event. (3) Removal decisions are made based on the report, not on the violator's connections or contributions. (4) The person who made the report is updated on what happened. (5) Patterns are tracked — one report about a person is an incident, two is a pattern that changes the response. (6) Post-event review includes analysis of consent-related reports and how they were handled. This is what enforcement looks like. Anything less is decoration.

Here's your move. Enforce your consent policy consistently. The first exception defines what the policy actually is.

Here's exactly what to say. “This person has been reported for a consent violation. Here is our process and here is what happens next regardless of who they are.”

And here's the truth. A consent policy you do not enforce is not a consent policy. It is a liability.
