# “LET'S GET YOU SOMEWHERE QUIETER”
*The Overload Navigator / Sensory Load As A Steering Wheel — clinically, environmental coercion / engineered decision fatigue.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Notices — correctly — that you are struggling with the noise and light, and offers the relief. Sometimes that is kindness and the quiet place is the medical tent. Sometimes the quiet place is his tent, and the route there went past the loudest stage on site for longer than it needed to.

Here's the scene. Four hours in, the bass from the main stage is doing the thing where it stops being sound and becomes a physical pressure in your jaw. Your ability to hold a sentence together is going. He says he knows somewhere quiet. You would agree to almost anything right now to make it stop, and some part of you clocks that, and the part of you that would normally clock it properly is the part that is currently offline.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/access/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with this scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want this under 3 minutes.

Watch for these signs.

- Waits until you are visibly overloaded before making the ask
- The quiet place is private rather than designated — his tent, not the chill dome
- Takes a route that extends the overload rather than shortening it
- Discourages the sensory tools that would fix it without him — loops, defenders, the quiet zone
- Frames leaving as an emergency requiring immediate agreement
- Is unbothered by asking you to make decisions in a state where deciding is hardest

Here's what's actually happening. Decision-making degrades measurably under sensory load, and for autistic and sensory-sensitive people the degradation arrives sooner and steeper. A person who has noticed this has a reliable instrument: they do not need to argue you into anything, they only need to wait, and to ask at the bottom. Nothing about this is exotic — it is the same mechanism as the 3am ask, the twentieth-hour ask, the ask after the long walk. What is specific here is that the pressure is ambient and continuous, the venue supplies it for free, and steering you through it looks exactly like helping.

Run the check. The S check: “Am I agreeing to this, or agreeing to make it stop?” — Those feel identical from the inside. Only one of them is a decision.

Here's the dark reality. Relief Is Not Consent. The cruelty of this one is that it uses your own coping strategy against you. Getting out of overload is the correct instinct — your body is right — and that correctness is what makes the offer so hard to refuse. Afterwards it is also what makes it hard to name: you went willingly, you wanted to go, you were relieved to go. All true. And none of it means you agreed to what happened once the noise stopped. Wanting the noise to end is not consent to whatever is on the other side of the noise. If the quiet place is private and the person offering it is the reason you are deciding badly, the answer is a designated quiet space with staff in it, every time.

Here's your move. Pre-commit while you are regulated: name the quiet places on the map Thursday, put loops or defenders somewhere you can reach one-handed, and agree a signal with one person that means walk me to the chill dome and stay. Pre-committing is the whole technique — you are deciding now for a version of you who will not be able to.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm at capacity, so I'm not deciding anything right now. I'm going to the quiet zone with Sam and I'll find you after.”

And here's the truth. Decisions made to end sensory pain are not decisions. They are escapes. Know your exits before you need them, and make them ones that do not require agreeing to anything.
