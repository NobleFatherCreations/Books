# “THE THIRTY-SECOND PRE-BURN”
*The Check You Run Every Time, Out Loud — clinically, safety protocol / pre-performance checklist.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For every fire, aerial, and prop performer, at every skill level. The veterans who have been hurt are the most rigorous people in any circle, and that is not a coincidence — it is what the injury taught them.

Here's the scene. You are about to go. There is a circle waiting, a running order slipping, and a version of you that wants to skip this because everything looks fine and everything usually is. This page exists because “usually” is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence.

Watch for these signs.

- Fuel: which fuel, how old, where is the depot, is it lidded and upwind
- Tools: wicks sound, no loose hardware, chains and leaders intact
- Body: hair tied, no synthetics, nothing loose, sober enough to be doing this
- Safety: who, do they have duvetyne, can they say what they would do
- Stop: what is the call, who can make it, does everyone know it
- Rig: anchor, rating, second inspection, and a spotter who is watching you

Here's what's actually happening. Checklists work for the same reason in every high-consequence discipline: they take the decision out of the moment and out of the social context. Reading a list is not a judgment call and cannot be read as a comment on the people present, which is precisely what makes it survivable under an impatient circle. Saying it out loud does the second job — it makes the check public, which means the circle adjusts to it rather than you adjusting to the circle, and it quietly gives permission to the newer performer behind you who was not going to ask.

Run the check. The F check: “Did I run all six, this time, for this burn?” — Last burn's check does not cover this one. Fuel moves, safeties change, hardware fails between sets.

Here's the dark reality. The Check Is Also Social Cover. There is a second reason to do it out loud and it is not about fire. A performer who visibly runs their own checks, asks technical questions, and is comfortable being briefly annoying in front of a circle is a conspicuously bad target for every pattern in this guide — the mentor with the private lesson, the rigger who does not like being asked, the person who wants the footage. Not because it makes you unpleasant, but because all of those depend on you being someone who will not make a small public fuss to avoid an awkward moment. The checklist trains exactly that muscle, thirty seconds at a time, on something where you already know you are right.

Here's your move. Run the six out loud, in the same order, every single time, until it is your known habit and the circle expects it. Teach it to the newest person there — that is how it becomes the culture rather than your quirk.

Here's exactly what to say. “Fuel, tools, body, safety, stop call, rig. — Right, what's the stop call tonight?”

And here's the truth. Every burned performer in this scene can tell you exactly which item they skipped. Run all six.
