# “I RIGGED IT MYSELF”
*The Rigger Who Owns The Point / Life Safety As Leverage — clinically, safety-critical dependency / technical gatekeeping.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The one with the hardware, the certification everyone assumes he has, and the only ladder. Rigs for everybody. Decides whose point goes up, when, and how high. Has never been asked to show anything.

Here's the scene. You want to fly and he is the only person here who can hang you. He is warm about it, competent-looking, and slightly dismissive when you ask what the anchor is rated to — he says he has been doing this since before you were spinning. Later he mentions, in the same warm voice, that some people get priority because they are part of the crew and you could be part of the crew.

Watch for these signs.

- One person rigs everything and no second competent person exists on site
- Deflects or jokes when asked about ratings, inspection, or load path
- Rigging priority tracks social closeness rather than the running order
- No written check, no second pair of eyes, no logbook
- Access to flying is described as something you earn from him socially
- Asking technical questions is treated as an insult rather than as normal

Here's what's actually happening. Aerial rigging is life safety — a single point failure is a fall from height — and the entire discipline's professional practice is built on redundancy, documentation, and independent inspection precisely because competence cannot be established by reputation. When a festival has one unsupervised rigger, two things are true at once: the technical risk is unmanaged, and a person now controls something people want badly enough to trade for. The second follows from the first. It is the accessible-platform pattern from the A.C.C.E.S.S. guide, with the stakes raised from a good view to your spine.

Run the check. The W check: “Can this person tell me the load path and the rating, without irritation?” — Competence explains itself. Only insecurity gets offended by the question.

Here's the dark reality. Two Failures Sharing One Cause. An unchecked rigger is both a fall risk and a coercion risk, and they are the same structural fact: nobody is watching, and the thing he controls is something you will trade for. That is how these situations run for years without a complaint — the people who could complain need the point, and the people who could check him were never appointed. If you take one thing from this page: a competent rigger will answer a technical question with relief, because it means somebody else in the room is thinking. The irritation is the signal. It means he has not been asked before, and that is the actual finding — not about him specifically, about the whole arrangement.

Here's your move. Ask the three questions before you ever go up: what is the anchor, what is it rated to, and who checked it besides you. Do not fly on a point nobody but its own rigger has inspected. Find the second competent person on site on arrival — if there is not one, that is your answer about flying this weekend.

Here's exactly what to say. “Before I go up — what's the anchor and the rating, and who's your second pair of eyes on it? I ask everyone, it's not about you.”

And here's the truth. Redundancy is the whole discipline. A rig with one person and no check is not a rig, it is a bet.
