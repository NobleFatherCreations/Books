# “COME BACK TO OURS AFTER, EVERYONE DOES”
*The After-Shift Party And The Person Who Signs Your Hours — clinically, hierarchical social pressure / post-shift coercion.*

Here's who you're dealing with. A supervisor, a team lead, or a long-standing crew member with informal authority — who is not asking you for anything explicit, and who also decides your shifts, your reference, and whether you are asked back.

Here's the scene. The crew party is a real thing and it is where the bonding happens and where next season's roles get informally decided, and the person suggesting you come is the person who writes your hours. Going means drinking with somebody who has power over you, in their camp, at three in the morning. Not going means being the one who does not join in, and both of you know which one is easier.

Watch for these signs.

- Social attendance is where progression and rota decisions actually happen
- The invitation comes from someone in your reporting line
- Declining is noticed and mentioned later
- Alcohol, staff camping, and no HR of any kind
- Other volunteers have had a bad experience nobody wrote down
- There is no reporting route that does not run through this person

Here's what's actually happening. Festival crews have workplace power structures and almost none of the workplace protections — no HR, no written complaints route, no contracts for volunteers, and a supervisor who controls access to next season. The after-shift party is where that power operates informally, and the risk is not the party itself, which is often genuinely one of the best parts of the job. It is that declining has a cost and attending has a different one, and that there is nobody to report to if something happens, because the person you would report is the person you would report to.

Run the check. The O check: “Who could I report this person to, by name?” — Ask on day one. If there is no second name, the hierarchy has no floor.

Here's the dark reality. Find The Second Name Before You Need It. The structural fix is one question asked in your first hour, when it is completely neutral: who handles a concern about a supervisor. Ask it of the volunteer coordinator as a routine induction question and write the answer down. Almost every event has a safeguarding or welfare lead outside the line hierarchy, and almost nobody tells volunteers who they are — which means the information exists and simply is not distributed. Asking on Thursday costs nothing and is forgettable. Asking on Sunday, after something, is an accusation before you have even described it.

Here's your move. Get the out-of-line reporting name on day one. Go to the party if you want to and take a crew mate, or decline warmly and without a reason and let the relationship prove itself. Keep your own notes of shifts and any incident, dated, on your own phone.

Here's exactly what to say. “Who handles it if someone's got a concern about a supervisor — is there someone outside the team? Just like to know where things go. — I'm knackered, I'll catch you tomorrow — have a good one.”

And here's the truth. Ask who the complaint goes to while nothing has happened. On Thursday it is admin; on Sunday it is an accusation.
