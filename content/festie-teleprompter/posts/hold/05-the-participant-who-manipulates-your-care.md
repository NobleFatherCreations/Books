# “THE PARTICIPANT WHO MANIPULATES YOUR CARE”
*When Your Support Role Gets Exploited — clinically, role manipulation / compassion exploitation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The attendee who uses your caregiving role to access resources, information, or proximity that they are not seeking for genuine support needs. Your care is real. Not every person using the space is there for the reasons they present.

Here's the scene. He has been to the Harm Reduction tent four times this weekend. Each time presenting a slightly different version of a support need. He is articulate, charming, and good at presenting in ways that trigger your care response. You have given him supplies, information, quiet space, and two extended conversations. On his fourth visit you notice: he is gathering information about other attendees who have come through, he is asking about staff schedules, and his affect when he thinks he is not being observed is different from his affect when he is.

Watch for these signs.

- Multiple visits with shifting or evolving presentations of their support need
- Interest in other attendees who have used the space or in staff schedules
- Requests that go beyond the scope of your role — information, introductions, personal contact
- Noticeably different presentation when they think they are not being directly observed

Here's what's actually happening. Most people who access Harm Reduction or support spaces at festivals are there for genuine reasons. Some are not. People who want to gather information about vulnerable attendees, access resources beyond what is appropriate, or build proximity to staff will use the care space to do it because the care space is specifically designed to receive people without judgment. Noticing patterns is not being judgmental. It is being a good safeguarder.

Run the check. The O check: “Is the pattern of this person’s engagement consistent with genuine support seeking — or does it track more closely with something else entirely?” — discernment is as much a part of this role as compassion.

Here's the dark reality. What To Do When Something Feels Off. Trust your gut. If something about a repeated visitor feels off — name it to a colleague quietly during a transition. Document: how many visits, what they requested each time, anything that seemed inconsistent. You do not need certainty to document a concern. Documentation is not accusation — it is creating a record in case the pattern becomes clearer later. If the behavior is actively concerning — someone gathering information about other attendees, someone following up with specific attendees after interactions — that goes to your supervisor immediately and in writing.

Here's your move. Trust the pattern. Name it to a colleague. Document it. That is what good safeguarding looks like.

Here's exactly what to say. “I want to flag something I have noticed about a repeated visitor — can we talk privately for a minute?”

And here's the truth. Your care role requires discernment as much as compassion. Both are part of doing it well.
