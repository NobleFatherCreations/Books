# “YOU'RE OFF SHIFT BUT YOU'RE STILL STAFF”
*The Dual Role / Volunteer By Day, Attendee By Night — clinically, role ambiguity / boundary collapse.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Everyone on a volunteer-for-a-ticket arrangement, which is most of the workforce at most festivals — you are staff for eight hours and a punter for sixteen, wearing the same wristband and known to the same people.

Here's the scene. You finished at six. At eleven, in the crowd, somebody recognizes you from the welfare tent and starts telling you about their night, and then somebody else asks you where the medical tent is, and then an actual incident happens four feet away and everyone nearby turns to look at you, and you have had three drinks.

Watch for these signs.

- People bring you work problems when you are off shift
- You are identifiable as staff even out of hi-vis
- You have intervened in something while off duty and impaired
- Your camp is staff camping, so there is no off-site to go to
- Management treats you as available for the whole event
- You cannot say where the shift ended and the weekend started

Here's what's actually happening. The volunteer-for-a-ticket model produces a genuinely ambiguous role and almost no organizations address it. Two specific risks follow. First, liability: intervening while off duty and impaired is not covered by any briefing or insurance, and it is where volunteers get hurt or get someone else hurt. Second, depletion: a person who is never fully off shift does not recover, and the compassion-fatigue pages elsewhere in this guide describe what that turns into by day three. Neither is your fault; both are yours to manage, because the event almost certainly has not.

Run the check. The H check: “Am I on duty right now — and does everyone around me know the answer?” — If it is ambiguous to you, it is ambiguous to them.

Here's the dark reality. Impaired And Intervening Is The Worst Combination. Picture the incident report. A volunteer, off shift, three drinks in, restrained somebody or made a medical call or walked an attendee somewhere — and it went wrong. There is no briefing that covers it, no insurance that applies, and the organization's position afterwards will be that you were not working. This is also the exact scenario where genuinely well-meaning volunteers cause harm, because the instinct to help does not switch off with the shift and the judgement does switch off with the drinks. Make it visible instead: wristband off, lanyard away, and one sentence that hands it to whoever is actually on duty.

Here's your move. Make off-duty physically visible — lanyard off, hi-vis away, and say it out loud when asked. Hand every off-shift request to the on-duty team by radio or in person rather than handling it. Leave staff camping for part of each day if you can, because being permanently among colleagues is what makes the role never end.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm off shift and I've had a drink, so I'm not the right person — but I'll walk you to the welfare tent right now and hand you to whoever's on.”

And here's the truth. A wristband that never comes off is a shift that never ends. Being off duty only counts if the people around you can tell.
