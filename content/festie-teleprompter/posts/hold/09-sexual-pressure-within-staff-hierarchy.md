# “SEXUAL PRESSURE WITHIN STAFF HIERARCHY”
*When the Power Is Above You and Points at You — clinically, sexual coercion / hierarchy abuse.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The supervisor, event director, or senior staff member who uses their position in the staff hierarchy to create sexual pressure on those below them. The power differential makes saying no feel like a professional risk.

Here's the scene. Your supervisor has been warm and supportive all weekend. He has also made several comments that felt personal rather than professional. Saturday night he asks if you want to come back to the staff area after your shift to "debrief." Something about the way he says it makes the invitation feel less professional than the word suggests. You also know he controls your schedule, your access, and your reference if you volunteer here again next year. Saying no feels complicated in a way it should not.

Watch for these signs.

- Comments from a supervisor that feel personal, sexual, or designed to gauge your interest
- Invitations that are framed as professional but feel personally motivated
- The awareness that this person controls something you need — your schedule, your reference, your access
- A feeling that saying no directly would create professional consequences

Here's what's actually happening. Sexual pressure within staff hierarchies is a specific form of workplace sexual harassment regardless of the festival context. The power differential is real: supervisors control schedules, references, access, and the experience of working in that role. That power makes genuine consent complicated and makes declining feel costly. You do not owe a supervisor access to you personally in exchange for a good volunteer experience. The harassment is the problem — not your response to it.

Run the check. The L check: “Have I documented this interaction so I have a record if I choose to report it?”

Here's the dark reality. Reporting Staff Hierarchy Sexual Pressure. Options for reporting: (1) To a supervisor above the person who harassed you — skip the chain if the harasser is your direct supervisor. (2) To the event's designated safeguarding lead if one exists. (3) In writing directly to the event director or organization — this creates a timestamp. (4) After the event: to any professional bodies, associations, or regulatory bodies that oversee the event or the organization. (5) RAINN (1-800-656-4673) supports people who have experienced harassment in workplace contexts including volunteer roles. You do not have to have experienced assault for your experience to be worth reporting.

Here's your move. Document it. Report it through the highest available channel. Your experience in this role should not cost you this.

Here's exactly what to say. “"I am going to keep things professional — I need to head back to my station."”

And here's the truth. Your access to a good volunteer experience should not require tolerating sexual pressure from anyone above you.
