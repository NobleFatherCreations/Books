# “FINDING THE OTHERS”
*Affinity Space, And The Festivals That Get It Right — clinically, affinity community / protective social infrastructure.*

Here's who you're dealing with. The camp with the sound system and the good food that has been coming for eleven years. The crew who clocked you at the gate and nodded. The promoters and collectives who built something deliberately and did the work of making it real.

Here's the scene. Saturday, and you have spent two days being the only one in every frame, doing the low-level arithmetic that costs more energy than anyone who does not do it understands. Then you walk past a camp and three people look up and one of them says come and eat something, and you feel your shoulders drop about two inches, and you had not registered they were up.

Watch for these signs.

- Real: you are not explained, introduced as a demographic, or asked to represent
- Real: you can be ordinary, annoying, or quiet with no change in standing
- Real: people share practical information without ceremony — who is sound, who is not
- Performative: your presence gets mentioned to other people as a credit
- Performative: the event's diversity is discussed with you rather than built
- Performative: you are the only one there and nobody has ever asked why

Here's what's actually happening. Affinity space is protective for reasons that are entirely practical rather than sentimental: it replaces hypervisibility with ordinariness, it supplies witnesses who do not need the situation explained to them, and it is where the informal warnings circulate — which promoter is sound, which crew to avoid, what happened last year. Every mechanism in this guide works better on someone isolated, and the fetishizer, the reacher and the weaponized report all operate most freely on a person with nobody standing near them. This is also the page where the scene deserves credit: there are collectives and festivals doing this properly, and they are findable.

Run the check. The S check: “Have I found my people here, or am I still doing this alone?” — Day one. Not the night it goes wrong.

Here's the dark reality. Shoulders Down Is Data. Worth naming plainly: the constant low-level vigilance has a cost, and because it is continuous it becomes invisible to the person paying it. You will not notice you have been braced all weekend until you stop, and the stopping is what tells you. That fatigue is not fragility and it is not you being difficult — it is the predictable result of running a background process everyone around you has switched off. It also degrades judgment, the same way sensory load and sleep loss do elsewhere in this book, which means finding the people who let you put it down is not a comfort measure. It is a safety measure, and it belongs on day one alongside knowing where medical is.

Here's your move. Find the camp, the crew, or the collective on day one, before you need anything. Ask the regulars what they know — the informal information network is the best safety infrastructure at any festival. And go back to the events that actually built something, because they are the ones that respond to being supported.

Here's exactly what to say. “First time at this one — who's sound round here, and is there anything I should know? — Come find us at the camp with the blue flag, we're cooking Saturday.”

And here's the truth. You came for the music, not to be a subject of interest. Find the people who let you be ordinary, on day one.
