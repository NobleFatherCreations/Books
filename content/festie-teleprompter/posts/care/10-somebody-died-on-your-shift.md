# “SOMEBODY DIED ON YOUR SHIFT”
*The Critical Incident And The Weeks After It — clinically, critical incident stress / post-fatality response.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Nobody did anything wrong. This page is for the hours and the months after the worst night, and it is written because almost nobody in this role is prepared for it and it happens every season somewhere.

Here's the scene. You did everything right. You did CPR, you called it in, the medics came, and it did not matter. It is four in the morning and the tent is still open and there are eleven people outside who need things, and somebody is asking you where the water is.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/care/tells-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with this scenario's tells typed in; you can shorten the next line to "Here's what to watch for" instead of narrating every tell if you want this under 3 minutes.

Watch for these signs.

- You kept working, because the tent was still open
- You are recounting the timeline compulsively, looking for the error
- Nobody senior has formally stood you down
- There is no organizational debrief scheduled, only a gap
- You have not told the people at home, to protect them
- You are fine, loudly, and have started sleeping badly

Here's what's actually happening. A death on shift is a critical incident, and the response to it is well established even though this sector rarely provides it: stand down immediately rather than finishing the shift, a factual operational debrief within twenty-four hours that is separate from any emotional processing, and follow-up over weeks rather than a single conversation on the night. What does not help, and is the standard offering, is an unstructured group conversation at four in the morning with everyone still activated. What helps is sleep, food, being stood down, and a scheduled conversation later with somebody trained.

> 🖼️ **[INSERT IMAGE HERE: `images/care/check-card.png`]** — hold this on screen with the check question and letter typed in; you can shorten the line below to just naming the check instead of reading the full quote if you want this under 3 minutes.

Run the check. The E check: “Am I still working?” — If yes, that is the first thing to change. You are not a resource tonight.

Here's the dark reality. The Review Will Find You Did Not Fail. The thing that hurts people in this role is not the death, it is the replay — the compulsive re-examination of the timeline looking for the two minutes where it could have gone differently. Almost always there is no such moment, and being told that by somebody who knows the clinical picture is enormously more useful than being told it by a friend. Ask for the operational debrief and ask to be walked through what actually happened medically, because unanswered questions are what the replay feeds on. And put the follow-up in the diary for three and six weeks, when everyone has gone home and it gets much louder. Bereaved-by-suicide and critical-incident support services exist for responders and volunteers, not only for family.

Here's your move. Stand down now, not at the end of the shift — hand over and say you are unfit to continue, which is a clinical statement rather than a request. Eat, hydrate, sleep. Ask for a factual operational debrief within a day and book a proper one for three weeks out. Tell one person at home something true.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'm standing down — I'm not fit to be on this tent tonight, someone needs to take over. — I want an operational debrief in the next day, and I want to be walked through what happened clinically.”

And here's the truth. Finishing the shift is not resilience. Standing down is the first piece of clinical judgement you make about yourself.
