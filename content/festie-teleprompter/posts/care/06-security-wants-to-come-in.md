# “SECURITY WANTS TO COME IN”
*Police And Security At The Harm Reduction Tent — clinically, service integrity / third-party interference.*

Here's who you're dealing with. An officer, a security supervisor, or an event manager standing at the entrance of your tent asking a question you should not answer, usually politely, and usually believing they are entitled to.

Here's the scene. There is a young woman in the corner who came in twenty minutes ago in a bad state and is only just settling, and there is now a uniform at the door asking what she took and whether he can have a word. Everybody in the tent has gone quiet and is watching what you do, and what you do in the next ten seconds determines whether anybody uses this tent again this weekend.

Watch for these signs.

- Uniformed presence at or inside the service space
- Requests for names, substances, or details about a participant
- Being asked to identify who is holding, or to point somebody out
- Security using the tent as an observation post
- An event manager asking for information to manage reputation
- Participants stopping at the door, looking in, and walking away

Here's what's actually happening. The entire clinical value of a harm reduction service rests on one thing: people believing they can come in without consequence. That belief is fragile, it is community knowledge rather than individual knowledge, and it is destroyed by a single visible interaction — one person seen speaking to police at the tent door ends the service's usefulness for the rest of the weekend, because the word travels faster than anything you can say to correct it. Protecting the boundary is not obstruction; it is the operational requirement that makes the service work at all.

Run the check. The A check: “What does this look like to the people watching from inside my tent?” — That perception is the service, and it is what you are protecting.

Here's the dark reality. Agree It In Writing Before The Gates Open. The failure is almost always a planning failure rather than a confrontation. Services that get this right have a written agreement with the event and with police before the event opens — where the boundary is, what is and is not shared, and who to escalate to — and services that get it wrong are improvising it at two in the morning with an officer at the door and a frightened participant behind them. Get the memorandum in advance, get the senior officer's name, and brief every volunteer on the single sentence they use so that nobody has to invent one under pressure. Nobody should be working out an organization's information-sharing policy while somebody watches from a beanbag.

Here's your move. Step outside to speak rather than letting the conversation happen in the space. Give one clear sentence, escalate to your coordinator, and do not disclose participant information. Afterwards, tell the room plainly what did and did not happen.

Here's exactly what to say. “I'll come out to you — let's talk out here. We don't share participant information; if you need something formally, my coordinator is the person for that and I'll get them now. — [to the room] Nobody's name was given and nobody's getting spoken to. You're fine here.”

And here's the truth. A single conversation at your door can close the service for the whole weekend. Step outside and protect what the room believes.
