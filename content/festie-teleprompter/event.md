# E.V.E.N.T. — PROMOTERS ACCOUNTABILITY
### Promoters Accountability Edition

*A spoken-word teleprompter script — one scenario at a time, ready to read straight to camera.*

**Quick shoutout before we start:** every scenario in this guide — and 260 more across the other twenty guides — lives free at **www.noblefathercreations.com/festival**.

This is E.V.E.N.T.: Promoters Accountability Edition. You create the conditions. Everything that happens at your event is shaped by your choices.

Here's the framework this whole guide runs on.

- **E** — Environment Is Yours To Set. The physical and social environment of your event is a choice. Safety features, lighting, staffing — all of it is yours.
- **V** — Vendor and Artist Contracts. Written agreements are not distrust. They are the thing that makes every professional relationship honest.
- **E** — Enforce Your Standards. The standards you state and do not enforce are not standards. They are decoration.
- **N** — No Means No From Your Staff. Your staff saying no to unsafe conditions is not insubordination. It is the system working.
- **T** — Transparent Financials. Pay what you agree, on the agreed timeline. Every time. This is the baseline of a business worth working with.

Let's get into it.

## Accountability — what you owe when the power is yours

*Promoter behaviors that cause harm — named directly.*

### 1. “THE BAIT AND SWITCH”
*Promising What You Cannot Deliver — clinically, misrepresentation / fraud.*

Here's who this is. This page is written directly to you. Not about bad promoters. To you. About the specific behaviors that cause harm to artists, vendors, staff, and attendees — and what accountability for those behaviors looks like.

Picture this. You pitched a vendor on a premium spot near the main stage, 2,000 attendees, electricity included. You knew when you pitched it that the numbers were optimistic and the electricity situation was complicated. She paid $800 and planned her whole season around it. She arrived to a spot behind the food trucks and an electricity bill she had not budgeted for. You told yourself it was close enough. It was not close enough. She lost money because of information you provided that was not accurate.

Here are the signs.

- Attendance numbers and traffic projections should be accurate and from the current event context
- Placement commitments belong in writing before payment is collected — not described verbally
- All fees including conditionals like electricity hookups belong in the written agreement
- The vendor cannot make an informed decision based on optimistic projections that serve your pitch

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Misrepresentation in vendor and artist pitches is widespread because the incentive structure rewards it — you get the fee and the performer, and the consequences arrive later and are diffuse. The vendor absorbs the loss. The artist absorbs the gap between what was promised and what was delivered. Your reputation absorbs it slowly. Accurate representation of what your event offers is not just ethical — it is how you build the relationships and reputation that sustain your events long-term.

Here's your check. The V check: “Would I be comfortable if every vendor and artist I have worked with saw this pitch in writing, side by side with what actually happened?” — the reputation you’re building is the accurate one, not the one in your pitch.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. What Honest Representation Looks Like. In your vendor and artist agreements: use attendance numbers from the equivalent prior event, clearly noted as historical. Specify booth placement as a location tier with a map, not a verbal description. List all fees including conditionals before collecting any payment. Include a refund policy that covers material misrepresentation. Make your production details — stage size, sound system, streaming arrangements — specific and accurate. An agreement that accurately represents your event is the foundation of a reputation that lasts. An agreement that oversells it is a debt you will pay in reputation and relationships.

Here's what you do. Represent your event accurately in every agreement. Every time. That is the baseline.

Say this, out loud. “"Here is what our event actually delivered last year and what we are realistically planning for this year."”

Here's the truth underneath it. The reputation you are building is the one that is accurate — not the one in your pitch.

### 2. “PAY WHAT YOU AGREED”
*Financial Accountability to Artists and Vendors — clinically, financial exploitation / contract violation.*

Let's talk about who does this. You agreed to a fee. You agreed to a payment timeline. Those agreements are binding regardless of how the event went, what your costs were, or what complications arose. Pay what you agreed, on the timeline you agreed. This is the entire baseline of a professional reputation.

Here's how it plays out. The event cost more than projected. The attendance was lower than you hoped. The performer you agreed to pay $2,000 is now getting a message that you can only do $1,200 "given how the event went." She had no say in how the event went. She delivered her performance. She incurred the travel costs. She fulfilled her side of the agreement. The event's financial performance is your risk, not hers.

Watch for these.

- Post-event financial "adjustments" are contract violations — not conversations to negotiate
- The event's costs and revenue are your business risk — not a risk that transfers to your artists
- Payment timelines in contracts are enforceable — "end of month" is a legal commitment
- Offering future booking as compensation for current non-payment is not compensation

Here's what's really going on. Late payment and post-event fee adjustments are so normalized in the independent event world that many promoters do not register them as the contract violations they are. They are. When you agree to a fee and a payment timeline, that agreement is binding. The event's financial performance does not change your obligation. Artists and vendors who absorb your losses are subsidizing your events with their own labor and financial risk. That is not a sustainable or ethical business model.

Ask yourself this. The T check: “Am I paying what I agreed, to whom I agreed, on the timeline I agreed — every time?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. What Professional Payment Practice Looks Like. Professional payment practice: (1) Pay deposits on the agreed timeline before the event — not when you get around to it. (2) Reconcile and pay final amounts within the agreed timeframe after the event — 7-14 days is standard. (3) If you genuinely cannot pay on time: communicate proactively before the deadline, not after you have already missed it. (4) Do not offer future booking, exposure, or "a better deal next time" as substitutes for agreed payment. (5) If you had to adjust fees due to genuine crisis: negotiate BEFORE the event, not after. Late adjustment is a unilateral contract change. That is not how agreements work.

Here's the play. Pay what you agreed, on the agreed timeline. Every time. This is the whole standard.

Here are the words. “"The payment will be in your account by [agreed date]. Let me know if you do not receive it by then."”

Remember this. Your financial reputation is built one payment at a time. It is also destroyed one payment at a time.

### 3. “THE REPORT IS ABOUT YOUR HEADLINER”
*When Acting Costs You The Event — clinically, conflict of interest / high-value perpetrator.*

Here's who you're dealing with. You, holding a credible report about the one person on the bill you cannot replace, forty-eight hours out, with six thousand tickets sold on their name.

Here's the scene. It came through welfare last night and it is specific, it is not the first thing you have half-heard about him, and he is on at ten tomorrow. The maths is doing itself in your head before you have finished reading: refunds, the agent, the insurance, next year's booking relationships, and a field full of people who bought tickets for him.

Here's what to watch for.

- The commercial cost of acting is immediately obvious and the cost of not acting is not
- You are looking for reasons the report might be unreliable
- You are thinking about handling it quietly, after the event
- The agent's likely reaction is in your reasoning
- You have heard something before and it did not go anywhere
- Nobody outside your commercial interest is involved in the decision

Here's what's actually happening. This is the decision that defines an event, and the pattern of failure across the industry is consistent: not a promoter who decided to protect a predator, but a promoter who decided to handle it after the weekend, and then after the season, and then not at all. Everything about the moment pushes one way — the cost of acting is concrete and immediate, the cost of not acting is diffuse and deferred — and that asymmetry is the whole trap. The counter is structural: a decision rule written when nothing is at stake, and a person outside the commercial line who makes the call.

Run the check. The E check: “Would I act on this identical report if it were about a volunteer?” — If yes, then the only variable is money, and you already know that is not a reason.

Here's the dark reality. Everything Comes Out Eventually, With Your Name On It. Every event that has been publicly destroyed by this was destroyed by the cover-up rather than by the incident. It surfaces two or three years later, with the timeline, and the sentence that ends the organization is always that they knew and booked him anyway — because that is provable from a welfare log and an email. The decision looks commercial in the moment and reputational in retrospect, and the reputational version is always the larger number. Act on it now, document the decision and the reasoning, and accept the refunds, because the refunds are the cheap option and you will not believe that until it is too late to choose them.

Here's your move. Take the decision out of the commercial line — your safeguarding lead decides, not you. Act on the current event: remove, or supervise and separate with named conditions. Document the report, the decision and the reasoning the same day. Tell the agent factually and in writing. Write the rule into next year's contracts before you need it again.

Here's exactly what to say. “This goes to our safeguarding lead and their decision stands, including if it costs us the headline slot. — [to the agent] We've received a report we're acting on. He's not performing. I'm putting the decision and the reasons in writing to you today.”

And here's the truth. The refund is the cheap option and it will never feel like it. What ends events is not the incident, it is the email showing you knew.

## Responsibility — what you owe the people around you

*What you owe the people who make your event possible.*

### 4. “YOUR STAFF AND VOLUNTEERS”
*What You Owe the People Who Run Your Event — clinically, labor obligation / operational duty of care.*

Here's who this is. Your event runs because people show up to make it run. Staff and volunteers who work your event are owed specific things — clear terms, safe working conditions, basic resources, and the knowledge that raising concerns will be taken seriously rather than cost them their position.

Picture this. Your volunteer briefing says "this is a family" and "we are all in this together." It does not say what the actual shift hours are, what accommodation is provided, what meals are covered, what happens if someone needs to leave early, or how to raise a concern about another staff member. By Saturday your Harm Reduction team is short-staffed, two volunteers have left without explanation, and a concern that was raised Friday morning has not been acted on. The family framing did not substitute for the operational clarity that was needed.

Here are the signs.

- Volunteer agreements should specify hours, accommodation, meals, and exit terms in writing
- Staff should have a clear reporting process for concerns about other staff members
- Basic needs — food, water, breaks, sleep — should be structurally provided not assumed
- Concerns raised by staff should be documented and acted on within a defined timeframe

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Festival promoters who treat staff and volunteers as family while providing no operational clarity are using the emotional frame to avoid the practical obligations. Family framing does not substitute for written agreements. Community language does not provide food and rest. And a culture where concerns are raised but not acted on is a culture where the next concern does not get raised — until something goes seriously wrong.

Here's your check. The N check: “Do my staff have what they need to do their jobs safely — and do they have a clear path to raise a concern without it costing them?” — your event runs on other people’s labor. You owe them clarity, safety, and a path that actually works.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Staff And Volunteer Minimum Standards. For every event: (1) Written volunteer agreements specifying hours, accommodation, meals, and exit terms before the event. (2) Pre-event briefing covering reporting process, code words, daily check-in expectations. (3) Designated staff welfare role — someone whose job includes checking that staff are okay. (4) Documented process for raising concerns about other staff members that does not require going to their direct supervisor. (5) Post-event debrief for staff that includes honest assessment of what could be improved. These are not extras. They are the baseline of running an event with people in it.

Here's what you do. Provide clear written terms, safe conditions, and a functioning reporting process for every person who works your event.

Say this, out loud. “Here is your written volunteer agreement — read it carefully. Here is how to raise concerns. Here is what I am committed to providing.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Your event runs on the labor of other people. You owe them clarity, safety, and the knowledge that concerns will be heard.

### 5. “ENFORCE YOUR OWN POLICIES”
*Consent Culture That Is Real, Not Decorative — clinically, policy enforcement / selective application.*

Let's talk about who does this. Many events have consent policies. Fewer events enforce them consistently. The gap between a stated policy and its enforcement is not just a failure of follow-through — it is the active message that the policy is decorative rather than real.

Here's how it plays out. Your event's consent policy is posted at the entrance. It is in your promotional materials. Your team briefing included it. On Saturday night a security staff member reports a consent violation to the team lead. The team lead, who knows the person being reported, decides to handle it informally. The person stays at the event. By Sunday morning two more people have had experiences with the same individual. The policy did not fail. The enforcement did.

Watch for these.

- Consent violations handled informally when formal processes exist
- Known violators who remain at events because of their connections or contributions
- Different enforcement standards applied based on who the violator is
- Reports that are received and not acted on within a defined timeframe

Here's what's really going on. Inconsistent enforcement of consent policies does more damage than no policy at all — because it signals to potential violators that the policy is navigable through the right relationships, and to potential reporters that reporting may not be worth the cost. Consistent enforcement regardless of who the violator is — including people who are connected, contributing, or beloved — is the only thing that makes the policy real. The first time you fail to enforce it is the moment you define what the policy actually is.

Ask yourself this. The E check: “Am I enforcing my consent policy consistently — regardless of who the violator is?”

Here's what's underneath all of it. What Real Consent Policy Enforcement Looks Like. Real enforcement means: (1) Every reported consent violation is documented and reviewed by a designated safety lead — not handled informally by whoever is available. (2) The safety lead has actual authority to remove people from the event. (3) Removal decisions are made based on the report, not on the violator's connections or contributions. (4) The person who made the report is updated on what happened. (5) Patterns are tracked — one report about a person is an incident, two is a pattern that changes the response. (6) Post-event review includes analysis of consent-related reports and how they were handled. This is what enforcement looks like. Anything less is decoration.

Here's the play. Enforce your consent policy consistently. The first exception defines what the policy actually is.

Here are the words. “This person has been reported for a consent violation. Here is our process and here is what happens next regardless of who they are.”

Remember this. A consent policy you do not enforce is not a consent policy. It is a liability.

### 6. “THE FRONT OF THE CROWD”
*Density, Surges And The Thing That Kills People At Festivals — clinically, crowd dynamics / progressive crowd collapse.*

Here's who you're dealing with. Physics. Not a bad actor, not a decision anybody made — a density figure, a pinch point, and a moment when a crowd stops being people and becomes a fluid.

Here's the scene. It is the headline act, the barrier is full, there is a surge from the back, and somewhere in the middle of it a person goes down and the people around them cannot bend to help because there is no room to bend. Nothing about this looks dangerous from the mixing desk and it is the single most lethal thing that happens at music events.

Here's what to watch for.

- Density above roughly four to five people per square metre
- Pinch points, narrowing barriers, or one exit route into a viewing area
- Crowd movement that propagates as waves rather than as individuals
- No spotters at the barrier and no view from the front looking back
- No agreed show-stop authority, or one nobody will use
- Late-arriving surges toward a single popular act

Here's what's actually happening. Deaths in crowds are almost never trampling, which is the popular misconception — they are compressive asphyxia, where density becomes high enough that people cannot expand their chests, and it is survivable for only a few minutes. It is entirely a design and management problem: density limits, monitoring from a position that can see the front, spotters at the barrier, and crucially a named person with unambiguous authority to stop the show. The single factor that turns a dangerous crowd into a fatal one is hesitation about stopping.

Run the check. The E check: “Who can stop the show, right now, without asking anybody?” — If that person is not named and briefed, you do not have a crowd safety plan.

Here's the dark reality. Somebody Must Be Able To Stop It Without Permission. Every crowd disaster review finds the same failure: people could see it and nobody stopped it, because stopping meant overriding a headline performance and nobody was sure they were allowed. Name the person, tell them in writing that they have the authority, tell the artist and their management in advance that a show-stop may happen and that they are to stop when asked, and rehearse the call. Give your spotters the same authority to escalate. It costs a few minutes of a set and the alternative is the thing that ends people's lives and organizations. Nobody has ever regretted a show-stop.

Here's your move. Set and monitor density limits with a competent crowd safety adviser. Put spotters at the barrier facing the crowd. Name the show-stop authority in writing and brief artists that it exists. Design out pinch points and single-route viewing areas. Rehearse the stop.

Here's exactly what to say. “Show-stop authority tonight is Kerry, and it is not subject to anyone's approval including mine. If she calls it, the band stops mid-song. — [to management] Please brief the artist that a show-stop is possible and what it sounds like.”

And here's the truth. People do not get trampled, they get compressed, and it takes minutes. Name the person who can stop it without asking.

### 7. “YOU HIRED THEM, YOU OWN THEM”
*The Security Contractor Is Your Responsibility — clinically, vicarious accountability / contractor oversight.*

Here's who this is. A security company that you engaged, whose staff you did not select, did not brief on your values, and whose conduct the public will attribute entirely to your event.

Picture this. Two complaints come through about the same door team — a search that was handled badly, and someone spoken to in a way that was not acceptable. They are not your employees. They are wearing lanyards with your logo on them, and to everybody who was there they are your festival.

Here are the signs.

- No briefing given beyond capacity and ejection procedures
- No shared standard on searches, gender, or trans attendees
- Complaints about security route to the security company, not to you
- No mechanism for an attendee to report a guard
- The same complaints recur each year with no record kept
- You have never checked licensing, or spoken to the guards yourself

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Security are the most physically interventionist people on site and usually the least integrated into the event's stated values, because they arrive as a contracted service rather than as part of the team. Attendees do not distinguish: a bad search or an aggressive ejection is remembered as something your festival did. The fix is contractual and procedural — brief them as staff, set the standards in the contract, and provide a complaints route that does not run through the company being complained about.

Here's your check. The N check: “Can an attendee complain about a guard to someone who does not work for the security company?” — If not, you will never hear about it and it will continue.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. Searches Are Where This Actually Goes Wrong. The specific, recurring failure is the search, because it is the one moment where a stranger in a uniform makes physical contact with every single person who enters. Get it in writing in the contract: same-gender searching offered as standard, a stated option for trans and non-binary attendees including a private space and an officer of their choosing, no searching of minors without a guardian present, and a stated protocol for assistance dogs, mobility equipment and medication. None of that is exotic and almost no event specifies it, which is why it is improvised at the gate by whoever is on that morning — and improvisation at a gate is how a person's weekend starts badly and how your festival acquires a reputation.

Here's what you do. Brief security as staff, in person, on your values and your consent policy. Put the search standards, licensing requirements and conduct expectations in the contract. Publish a complaints route that bypasses the contractor. Log every complaint and review it before renewing.

Say this, out loud. “You're briefed with our team, not separately — our consent policy applies to your staff. Same-gender searches offered as standard, private option available, and complaints come to our welfare lead, not to your supervisor.”

Here's the truth underneath it. They wear your logo and the public cannot tell the difference. Brief them like staff, or accept what they do in your name.

## Standards — what you actually own and owe

*Consent, safety infrastructure, and enforcing your own policies.*

### 8. “THE RECORDING RIGHTS QUESTION”
*What You Actually Own From Your Event — clinically, intellectual property / performer rights.*

Let's talk about who does this. Your event produces content — video, audio, photography, social media. The question of what you own and what your artists and performers own is both a legal question and an ethical one. This page is about being honest about both.

Here's how it plays out. Your festival set videos are performing well on your YouTube channel. They are driving discovery for your next event. They are also generating streaming revenue through the platform's music content system. The artists in those videos were paid for their performance. They were not paid for the recording, the distribution, or the streaming revenue. Your standard booking confirmation contained a rights clause they did not negotiate. The content that is building your brand was created by people who are not sharing in its returns.

Watch for these.

- Sweeping recording rights clauses in standard booking confirmation language
- Live recordings generating streaming or licensing revenue without artist participation
- Photography and video of artists used in commercial contexts without their agreement
- Social media content featuring artists used to promote future events without compensation

Here's what's really going on. The gap between what promoters legally own through standard contracts and what they ethically owe the artists who created the content is significant. Artists whose performances are recorded, distributed, and monetized are entitled to participate in that revenue. A recording rights clause buried in a booking confirmation is not the same as a negotiated agreement. The ethical standard is transparency about what you are doing with the content and fair participation for the people who created it.

Ask yourself this. The V check: “Are the artists whose recordings I am using being compensated fairly for the commercial use I am putting that footage to?” — the content that builds your brand was created by artists. Treat their contribution accordingly.

Here's what's underneath all of it. Ethical Recording Rights Practice. Ethical practice for event recordings: (1) Be explicit in booking agreements about what recording will happen and for what purpose. (2) Distinguish between archival recording (no commercial use) and commercial recording (streaming, licensing, promotion) — these are different agreements. (3) Share streaming and licensing revenue with performing artists — this is both the legal standard in many contexts and the ethical one in all contexts. (4) Use artist images and recordings for event promotion only with their explicit agreement. (5) When in doubt: ask before you use. The conversation is easier before the content is published than after.

Here's the play. Be transparent about what you record, how you use it, and share revenue with the people who created it.

Here are the words. “Here is what we record at our events and how we use it. Here is how we share revenue with performing artists.”

Remember this. The content that builds your brand was created by artists. Treat their contribution accordingly.

## Tools — practical structures, scripts and kit

### 9. “THE PROMOTER'S ACTUAL CHECKLIST”
*The Documents And Structures That Make An Event Safe — clinically, operational infrastructure / duty of care implementation.*

Here's who you're dealing with. For anyone putting on an event, from a four-hundred-capacity warehouse to a field with three stages. This page is the difference between believing your event is safe and being able to show it.

Here's the scene. Everything here is arranged in the planning months, when it is cheap and nobody is under pressure. None of it can be improvised on the Saturday, which is when you will find out whether you did it — and the finding out is always somebody's worst night.

Here's what to watch for.

- A written code of conduct, published, with named consequences
- A reporting route that does not run through the person most likely to be reported
- Two named safeguarding leads, on the site map, contactable by radio and in person
- Written contracts with artists, vendors and crew — rates, terms, and payment dates
- Staff and volunteer briefing that covers reporting, not just wristbands and bins
- An incident log kept properly, and read afterwards rather than filed

Here's what's actually happening. The gap between an event that says it takes safety seriously and one that does is almost entirely paperwork done in advance — and the single most load-bearing item is a reporting route that bypasses seniority. Most festival safeguarding failures are not the absence of a policy; they are a report that went to somebody with a relationship to the person being reported. Naming two independent leads and publishing how to reach them is cheap, and it is the thing that determines whether you find out about a problem in year one or in year four when it reaches social media.

Run the check. The E check: “Can somebody report a senior person at my event without that report reaching them first?” — If not, you do not have a reporting process, you have a filter.

Here's the dark reality. You Will Be Judged On The First One. Every event that has come apart publicly over this has come apart the same way: a first report that was handled informally, by someone close to the subject, with no record kept — and then a second and a third, and by the time it is undeniable the story is no longer about the individual, it is about an organization that knew. The incident log is what makes the difference, and not because it protects you legally, though it does. It is because a pattern is invisible without one: three separate reports across three years, each individually ambiguous, are obviously a pattern on one page and obviously nothing at all in three people's memories. Keep the log. Read it before every event.

Here's your move. Publish the code of conduct and the reporting route before tickets go on sale. Name two safeguarding leads and put them on the site map. Get every artist, vendor and crew agreement in writing with payment dates. Keep the incident log and actually read it back each year.

Here's exactly what to say. “Reporting goes to Priya or Mo directly, not through me and not through the stage managers — that's published, and it's on the map. If it involves me, it goes to them and I'm not in that conversation.”

And here's the truth. Safety at an event is not a value, it is a set of documents and two names. You either did it in March or you did not.

## Legacy — the toolkit that outlasts one event

*The event you are known for is the one you actually ran.*

### 10. “THE ETHICAL PROMOTER TOOLKIT”
*Practical Standards for Honest Event Production — clinically, professional ethics / operational standards.*

Here's who this is. This page is the practical one. Specific practices and standards that distinguish events built on ethical foundations from ones built on the exploitation of artists, vendors, staff, and attendees.

Picture this. The promoters you respect — the ones whose names on a lineup or a market mean something — have all built reputations over time through the same things: they pay what they agreed. They represent their events accurately. They enforce their own standards regardless of who is involved. Their staff know what to expect and have somewhere to go with concerns. Their artists are glad to come back. None of this is complicated. All of it is a choice.

Here are the signs.

- Written agreements with all artists, vendors, and staff before any money changes hands
- Accurate representation of attendance, placement, and production in all pitches and agreements
- Clear, functional reporting processes for safety concerns with named responsible parties
- Post-event financial reconciliation completed and communicated within agreed timelines

Here's the mechanism underneath it. Ethical event production is not a higher bar than most promoters can reach. It is a set of consistent practices that most promoters know they should follow and some do not. The ones who do build reputations that compound over time — artists want to play their events, vendors want placement at their markets, staff want to work their shows. The ones who do not build reputations that also compound — and the circuit has a very long memory.

Here's your check. The T check: “Would the artists, vendors, and staff who worked my last event describe it the way I just did?” — ethical production isn’t complicated. It’s a set of choices made consistently. Make them.

Here's the part that's hard to hear. The Ethical Promoter Checklist. Before every event: (1) All agreements in writing and signed before any performance or payment. (2) Attendance and placement representations accurate and documented. (3) All fees including conditionals listed in written agreements. (4) Staff and volunteer agreements specify hours, accommodation, meals, and reporting process. (5) Safety infrastructure plan in place and staffed. (6) Consent policy in writing with enforcement process named. After every event: (7) Financial reconciliation within agreed timeline. (8) Feedback collected from artists, vendors, and staff. (9) Post-event safety review including analysis of any incidents and their handling. (10) Changes made based on what the review reveals.

Here's what you do. Run the checklist before and after every event. The reputation you build is the one that is accurate.

Say this, out loud. “Here is our event agreement. Here is our safety plan. Here is how to raise concerns. Here is when payment will arrive.”

Here's the truth underneath it. Ethical event production is not complicated. It is a set of choices you make consistently. Make them.
