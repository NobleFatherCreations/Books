The short version

  1. The new world is public wayfinding: the sign system of a metro or an airport. It is the most widely read design language there is, and it was built for people who are stressed, hurried and reading at a glance.
  2. The Decoder's data really is a network. The 10 groups become 10 lines and the 310 tactics become stations. The 679 links between related tactics in different groups become interchanges, and the 109 documented sequences become routes. Unlike the star coordinates, every position on the new map means something true.
  3. Entries become answer-first. Screen one is the station sign, the plain answer and two things it sounds like, with safety above everything on the 105 sensitive entries. The other parts follow as four numbered steps with long lists folded. The full read drops from 16.5 phone screens to about 5.
  4. One navigation model on every screen size: four doors (Help, Search, Map, Saved) in the same order on every device, at the bottom of a phone and in a side column on a computer.
  5. Private by default, as you chose. Nothing is stored until the reader turns on "Save on this device".
  6. Calm by construction. Nothing moves on its own. Five short movements are left, each one answering a tap: grain, meteors, pointer light, magnetism and blur reveals are gone, and idle CPU drops to zero.
  7. Type you can read when you are shaken. Sentence-case road-sign lettering (Overpass, from the US Highway Gothic tradition) for signs, and Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, designed for low-vision readers, for reading.
  8. Faster on a cheap phone: only a 150 KB index is parsed at start and each entry is parsed when opened. The target is the headline on screen in 1.2 s on a throttled phone (3.4 s today) and the longest task under 150 ms (836 ms today).
  9. Cleaner to maintain: one token-based stylesheet with no override layers and no !important, plus a real regression suite in the repo.
  10. The single biggest change: a reader who does not know the word for what is happening is now one big, obvious tap from help. The two questions show why each match was suggested.

Three directions, compared

To avoid the obvious answers, I started from the reader's own world, not the topic. The topic invites two predictable sites: the soft pastel "support resource" page, and its opposite, the dark cinematic experience (which v14 already is). Both are off the list. I drew seven visual systems from things this reader already knows by heart (a field guide, a first-aid manual, a phrasebook, a patient leaflet, public signage, a library catalogue, a diary). The design skill then assigned which one to build and dealt alternatives from a catalogue of complete design systems, so the choice was not just my habit. I weighed each one on two questions: would this reader recognise and trust it, and does it make the product clearer?

A · Wayfinding

Chosen

World: the sign system of a metro and an airport. Night-black sign panels, enamel-white lettering, ten line colours each with a letter, ISO safety amber for danger and emergency-exit green for leaving. Heavy sentence-case sign lettering over a hyperlegible reading face. Rectangular sign blades, round line bullets.

Structure: groups are lines, tactics are stations, related tactics in other groups are interchanges, and documented sequences are routes. Home is the concourse with three big directional signs. An entry is a station: its name sign, a strip map showing where you are on the line, and "Change here for" the look-alikes.

Signature moment: tap a result and its line colour sweeps across as the row becomes the station sign. A real shared-element transition, 280 ms, only on tap.

For this reader: it is instantly familiar, and it was designed for exactly their state: stressed, hurried, in a strange place. It answers "where am I, where can I go, how do I get out". On a shared screen it looks like a utility.

Risk: a journey metaphor could suggest that abuse is a track with a fixed next stop. The fix is in the copy: routes are "patterns that often appear together", never "next stop". There are no trains, no cartoons and no "destination".

B · The Tabbed Manual

Alternate

World: a thick reference manual lying open at a coloured divider. Each of the ten groups is a full-strength coloured board, and the text sits on a milky acetate leaf hinged over it. A stepped tab rail down the edge shows all ten divisions, each tab sized by how many tactics it holds.

Structure: a book. Divisions, then entries as leaves, with the tab rail always visible as the table of contents. Reading is one size of serif text at a 62-character measure, and labels are set in a typewriter-style face.

Signature moment: nothing eases. Every change is a two-frame hinge flip in 90 ms, like turning a hard page. That makes it the calmest of the three by nature.

For this reader: excellent reading comfort, and the tab rail answers "how much is there and where am I" at a glance. But boxed-software manuals are a niche, nostalgic object most readers have never held, and a phone at night showing a full screen of saturated violet is bright and conspicuous.

Weighing: product clarity is high; audience recognition is medium. It lost to A because it is less familiar and louder on a phone in the dark. Its best idea, a rail showing each division's real size, is carried into A as the line index.

C · The Cutting Bench

Alternate

World: a film editor's bench flattened onto a screen. A perforated orange rail crosses every page, each tactic is a punched white frame on the rail, a folded tape flag marks where you stopped, and anything you set aside hangs on pins below.

Structure: a strip you scrub. Entries are frames in a row that snap into place, saved items hang on pins, and the flag brings you back to where you were yesterday. State is shown by a mark (cross, flag, pin), not by colour.

Signature moment: scrubbing the rail with inertia until it settles on a frame, while set-aside frames swing gently on their pins.

For this reader: it is genuinely good at "come back tomorrow" and "set two aside to compare". But orange covering a third of every screen clashes with amber as the safety colour, the editing vocabulary is foreign to most readers, and scrubbing a strip is a poor way to read 1,700 words.

Weighing: audience recognition is low and product clarity is medium. It lost to A. Its "flag where you stopped" idea is carried into A as "Continue where you left off", for readers who opt in to saving.

Why Wayfinding wins

It was built for a frightened reader

Signage design is about one problem: someone under stress, in an unfamiliar place, needs to know where they are, where to go and how to get out, in a glance. That is this reader's state exactly. The same rules carry over: few choices at once (Hick's law), big targets where the thumb lands (Fitts's law), mixed-case lettering because word shapes read faster than capitals (the reason Kinneir and Calvert used mixed case on British road signs), colour never carrying meaning alone, and safety colours that mean the same thing everywhere.

The metaphor is true to the content

The night sky placed stars by a layout algorithm, and the chart coordinates (such as 19ʰ 53ᵐ +36.8°) pointed at nothing real. In the network, every mark encodes a fact in the data:

  • Line = the tactic's group (10 lines; 19 to 44 stations each).
  • Neighbouring stations = closely related tactics in the same group. The build orders each line so that adjacent stations are linked in the data (1,047 same-group links to work with).
  • Interchange = a related tactic in another group. 679 such links; 269 of 310 stations have at least one. The busiest pairs are Truth ↔ Blame (70), Control ↔ Pressure (57) and Pressure ↔ Wear (48), so those lines run side by side on the map.
  • Route = one of the 109 documented sequences, averaging 5.9 tactics.

It turns the hardest question into a native feature

"Is it this, or that?" is the reader's hardest question, and v14 answers it with a paragraph. In a transit system, the question has a sign: Change here for. Each station lists its interchanges with their line bullets, and each one has a Compare button that sets the two side by side, using the existing "how to tell it apart" text and both entries' "Is this it?" signs.

Calm without being bland

A metro at night has character: hard black, bright enamel, ten strong colours and one heavy typeface. None of it moves unless you do. That gives the energy you asked for in v13 and v14 without the cost to battery, attention and nerves.

Wayfinding devices, and what each answers

  • Line bullet (coloured circle + letter): "what kind of tactic is this?" It appears beside every tactic name anywhere on the site.
  • Strip map on every station: "where am I in this group, and what is either side?" Previous and next are tappable, and J/K or ←/→ work too.
  • Step bar on every station: "how much of this entry is left?" Four segments, filled as you go.
  • Back sign: "how do I get back to my results?" It names the page: "← Back to your matches".
  • Private status line: "is anything being saved?" It is always visible, in words, not just an icon.
  • Leave sign (if approved): "how do I get out right now?"

The logic behind each screen

Home: the concourse
  • Three signs, not seven links. v14's home offered a search box, four "Try" phrases, three serif links and ten constellations: 18 choices on the first screen. Choice time grows with the number of options (Hick's law), and a frightened reader has less to spend. v15 shows three big directional signs, then the line index below.
  • The guide is the brightest sign. "Help me figure it out" is a white blade with dark text, the only inverted element on the screen. That reverses v14's submerged italic link (measured problem 2).
  • The headline is a sentence, not capitals. "Put a name to what is happening." in 800-weight sign lettering keeps its word shapes; the tracked thin capitals did not.
  • The privacy promise is a status line, always visible: "Private · nothing is saved". When saving is on, it reads "Saving on this device".
Station: answer first
  • Screen one answers the question. Name sign, plain subtitle, the plain-language summary with its first sentence in bold (kept from v14), and two "It can sound like" lines, because hearing your own situation in a quote is often the moment of recognition.
  • Safety outranks everything. On the 105 sensitive entries the amber panel sits between the name and the answer, with its first two sentences visible and "Read the full note" one tap away.
  • Four steps, numbered because they are a real sequence: Is this it? → Understand it → What you can do → Go deeper. On a phone you swipe sideways between them (native scroll snapping) or tap the step bar; on a computer they stack with a sticky step bar.
  • Folding, not cutting. Any list over three items shows the first three and "Show all 6". Open lists stay open while you read. Print expands everything.
  • The strip map replaces the 19/43 pager. It shows the same position, plus the names on either side, and the line's colour ties it to the map.
  • "Change here for" lists related tactics with their line bullets and a Compare button. It replaces a chip row and a paragraph.
  • Ticks never stored, never scored. Readers can tick "signs it might be this" to think it through. Nothing is counted, no verdict appears, and ticks vanish when you leave. That keeps the guide's rule of no verdicts.
Help me figure it out: the information desk
  • Step 1: "Where has this been happening?" Seven sign blades with pictograms, plus "I'm not sure".
  • Step 2: "Which of these sound familiar?" The existing 50 plain feelings, filtered by step 1, as large tick rows. Pick as many as fit.
  • Results: the three closest first, each with the reason it matched ("Matches: walking on eggshells, a relationship"), then "See 9 more". Each answer chip can be removed or changed in place, so you never have to start over.
  • "See them on the map" lights the matches on the network, so you can see they cluster on one or two lines.
  • Why it helps: v14 gave a flat list of up to 18 with no reasons. Reasons build trust ("why is it showing me this?") and make it easy to see that a match is wrong.
Search: the departures board
  • All of v14's search engine stays (everyday words, stemming, synonyms, pasted phrases, fuzzy fallback). What changes is how results are shown.
  • Rows are set like a departures board: line bullet, name, and the exact words that matched, highlighted. "Best match" is separated from "Also".
  • An empty search shows the Try phrases as rows, plus "Not sure what to type? Answer two questions instead".
  • On a computer, ⌘K and / open this same page, not a separate menu, so there is one search to learn.
Map and lines
  • Built, not simulated. The map is an SVG schematic generated by the Python build: lines run straight or at 45°, stations are evenly spaced, and line order follows the data. It is drawn once and needs no animation loop.
  • Semantic zoom: zoomed out you see the ten lines and their names; zoom in for station names; tap a station for a sheet with its summary and interchanges.
  • Interchanges on demand. 679 cross-links would be a hairball, so only the selected station's interchanges are drawn.
  • On a phone, the line diagram comes first. It is a vertical list that reads as both a map and a list, and it is the screen-reader version of the map.
Routes, Saved, Safety, Updates, Settings
  • Routes: each of the 109 sequences drawn as a route crossing the lines it touches, titled "Patterns that often appear together". It is never presented as a prediction.
  • Saved: saving is off by default, so this page explains what turning it on does and offers the switch. When on, it shows saved stations, recent reads and "Continue where you left off".
  • Use this safely: set out like station emergency information. An amber panel says what to do in danger; a green panel explains leaving quickly; plain text covers what the guide is and isn't.
  • Service updates: the version badge and the plain-language changelog, as the CLAUDE.md patch-notes rule requires.
  • Settings: Night / Day / Match my phone; text size (3 steps); line spacing; reduce motion; saving on/off with "Delete everything saved".

Space, shape and surface

  • 8px grid, with sign padding of 16/24 and 48px minimum touch targets (v14's pager buttons were 44).
  • Shapes carry meaning: rectangles are signs and controls, circles are lines and stations, and the warning triangle appears only on safety. v14's 0px geometry stays for signs; circles return for bullets because that is the transit grammar.
  • Opaque panels. No glass and no 40px blur, which is costly on cheap phones and hurts contrast. Depth comes from two panel tones and a 1px rule.
  • Hairlines keep v14's inset-ring technique for board rows; line strokes are 4–6px, like a real map.
  • High-contrast mode (prefers-contrast: more): pure black and white, 2px rules, line names underlined.

Motion budget

The rule: nothing moves unless the reader did something. Signs don't animate; they change state. The whole budget is five movements:

Map interchangeThe selected station's interchange links draw in once320 msShows connections exactly when they are asked for.

Rules from the motion skills: keyboard actions (J/K, arrows, ⌘K) never animate. Exits run about 30% faster than entries. Every movement is a CSS transition or a WAAPI animation on transform and opacity only, so a new tap interrupts it from where it is. Hover effects exist only on devices with a real pointer. The map sheet and map pan are the only gesture-driven pieces; they follow the finger 1:1, project a flick forward and hand its speed to a spring (damping 0.8, response 0.3), with rubber-band resistance at the edges. Theme changes crossfade over 200 ms.

Easing: arrivals cubic-bezier(.23,1,.32,1), the sheet cubic-bezier(.32,.72,0,1), presses ease-out. Off forever: grain, meteors, twinkle, drift, magnetism, pointer light, ripple, veil and blur reveals, the focus veil, the chromatic split and the header frost animation. Reduced motion (the phone setting or Settings): every movement becomes a 120 ms crossfade or an instant change. Idle CPU is zero, because no animation loop exists.

Why there is no history guard

The plan had a two-entry history guard so the trail would stay short. The build dropped it: it would have fought the browser's own back behaviour, and it protects less than making every address and title unreadable does. Back works as people expect, and the history list holds only numbers.

Leave quickly (recommended, pending decision 1)

Leave → A green sign at the top right of every page. One tap replaces the page with a neutral website (for example a weather page) using location.replace and clears everything held in memory. Before it leaves, it steps back over every page opened in this visit, so pressing Back on the weather page does not return to the guide (tested in a real browser). Offline it shows a blank page instead, because the weather page needs a connection. On a keyboard, Escape twice does the same, even with the map sheet open. The same Leave button is inside the map sheet, and sits above the reading pause. Use this safely explains its limits plainly: it doesn't erase a visit the browser already recorded, and a private browsing window is safer still.

Safety rules that stay

  • Safety notes lead on all 105 sensitive entries, and no screen ever suggests confronting someone or announcing a departure.
  • No scores, no verdicts, no labels for people. The ticks in "Is this it?" are never counted.
  • The optional reading pause stays, as a quiet "Take a break" card after about 20 minutes, which can be turned off.

Accessibility

  • Contrast: body text 17.1:1 at night and 18.5:1 by day; second text 8.6 and 8.0; every line colour above 4.5:1 as text. Target AAA for reading text, AA everywhere else.
  • Targets: 48×48px minimum, with the sign bar in the thumb zone.
  • Never colour alone: line letters, the safety triangle and word labels ("Signs it might be this") carry every meaning.
  • Focus: a 3px double ring (enamel and ground) visible on any background, and focus moved to the station name after navigation, as in v14.
  • Steps as tabs: the four steps use the ARIA tabs pattern, with arrow-key movement and a live "Step 2 of 4" announcement.
  • Map equivalent: every line diagram is an ordered list of links, so screen-reader users get the same information as the map.
  • Text size: three steps in Settings on top of browser zoom; layouts tested to 200% with no horizontal scroll.
  • From the accessibility review: arrow keys and 24 px target spacing for the map (2.5.7, 2.5.8); scroll-padding so focus never hides behind the header or sign bar (2.4.11); inactive steps inert and a pre-rendered status region for "Step 2 of 4" and result counts; real checkboxes with labels for the "Is this it?" ticks; the station sheet as a dialog that returns focus on close; outlined letter bullets in forced-colours mode; search input at 16px so iOS doesn't zoom; reflow checked at 320px.
  • Still owed: a pass with real VoiceOver and TalkBack on devices. v14 never had one, and this plan budgets for it.

Performance and engineering

Same rules, smarter file

Still one self-contained offline HTML file: no CDN, no frameworks, embedded fonts and the one approved analytics beacon. What changes is when work happens.

  • Lazy data. v14 parses all 3.35 MB of JSON before showing anything (347 ms on a computer, 836 ms on a throttled phone). v15 splits it inside the same file: a small index (names, subtitles, search words, line, links; about 150 KB) parsed at start, and each line's full entries in their own <script type="application/json"> block, parsed the first time a station on that line opens (around 10–20 ms each).
  • No canvas. The map is SVG, built at build time and inserted only on the Map page. Nothing runs between taps.
  • One stylesheet built from tokens: @layer reset, tokens, base, components, pages, with no override rounds and no !important outside the reduced-motion block. Target 35 KB, from 66 KB with 49 !important.
  • One script, in sections: router, data, search, help, station, map, privacy, settings. Target 60 KB, from 117 KB including the sky engine.
  • content-visibility: auto on long lists such as A–Z and line diagrams.
  • Home is pre-rendered into the HTML at build time, so the first screen paints with no script at all.
  • File order streams: shell, CSS, script and the index first; the ten line data blocks last. The page is usable before the file has finished arriving.
  • Search is indexed in small idle slices, so typing does no heavy work (slowest keystroke 32 ms on a 4× slowdown). Fonts are inline, so text never swaps and shifts the layout.

Build plan

  1. Foundation: tokens, fonts, pictograms, the shell, the one navigation model, lazy data, privacy-by-default and Leave (if approved). Verify suite in the repo from day one.
  2. The reader's path: Home, Search, Station (answer-first with four steps), Help with reasons. This alone covers what most readers ever touch, so it gets tested first.
  3. The network: line diagrams, interchanges, Compare, the map and Routes.
  4. Everything else: All lines, Saved, Use this safely, Service updates, Settings, print, old-link redirects.
  5. Finish: one screenshot round at five widths in both themes, a fresh design review against this document, a DESIGN.md written from the built site, and the versioned changelog (v15) on the page and in sites.json. Then I bring it to you for a deploy decision. Nothing ships without your go-ahead.

What we lose, and the risks

  • The spectacle. The living sky was beautiful and some readers will miss it. v15 trades wonder for clarity and calm. The map is still a moment, but a still one.
  • The current identity. Astra-in-capitals, the cover plate and the constellation-people engravings leave the interface. The brand logo and House seal stay.
  • The journey metaphor could read as "this leads to that". Mitigation: the copy never says "next stop" or "destination", and routes are framed as patterns seen together.
  • Automatic map layout is hard. 310 stations on ten octilinear lines may need hand-tuned anchors for line positions. The plan budgets for a fixed anchor file the build reads.
  • Back gesture in private mode leaves the site (see decision 2).
  • Not yet tested with real readers or real screen readers. Every claim here about readers is a design hypothesis, the same as in the v14 brief.
