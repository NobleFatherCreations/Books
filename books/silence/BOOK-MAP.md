## THE BRIEF

**Audience.** A man in a coercive relationship — controlled, isolated, frightened, sometimes hit — who has almost nowhere else in this subject that speaks to him without either dismissing it or trying to recruit him into something else on the way in.

**Why this book exists, stated once and then never apologised for again.** *Sovereign* is for women and stays for women. *The Offer* covers 13–25. Between them sits a large, underserved population: men in coercive intimate relationships, at every age past twenty-five, for whom almost nothing exists that isn't either a court-mandated program for the *accused*, or manosphere content that will use his real pain to recruit him into misogyny.

**The structural difficulty, named up front.** A book like this is easy to write badly in two directions:
- **Too soft** — so busy proving it isn't manosphere that it fails to actually help a man in danger.
- **Too hard** — so busy asserting "men get abused too" that it becomes a debate about whose suffering counts, which helps nobody and hands ammunition to people who want to defund services for women.

This book must do neither. It states its position once (chapter 3) and then simply *helps*, the way the rest of this library does for everyone else.

**Objective.** That a man reading this can (1) recognize what is happening to him without a framework designed for someone else's body and social position, (2) find real practical help that exists for him, specifically, and (3) not be recruited by the first voice that offers to validate his anger, because that voice is usually selling something worse than what he's escaping.

**The single hardest sentence in the whole book, and the one every chapter is tested against:** *This exists alongside the book for women, not instead of it.* If any chapter could be quoted to argue that women's services should be cut, that chapter needs rewriting.

**Voice.** Plain, direct, unadorned. Less literary than *Sovereign*, on purpose — the audience for this book is, on average, less patient with lyrical prose about pain and more responsive to something that sounds like a friend giving him a straight answer. Short sentences. No therapy-speak where a plain word works. Never "brave." Never "vulnerable" as praise. Say what happened, then say what to do.
